# Notation Registry

- $G, H, K, N$ — groups (finite unless stated otherwise); $N$ is often reserved for a normal subgroup
- $e$, or $1_G$, or just $1$ — the identity element of $G$
- $g^{-1}$ — the inverse of $g \in G$; $g^n$ — the $n$-fold product, with $g^0 = e$ and $g^{-n} = (g^{-1})^n$
- $|G|$ — the **order** of $G$, the number of elements
- $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ — the **order** of an element: the least $n > 0$ with $g^n = e$ (or $\infty$)
- $H \leq G$ — $H$ is a subgroup of $G$
- $H \trianglelefteq G$ (also written $H \triangleleft G$) — $H$ is a normal subgroup of $G$
- $gH$, $Hg$ — the left and right cosets of $H$ containing $g$
- $|G : H|$ — the **index** of $H$ in $G$, the number of left cosets
- $G/H$ — the quotient group (the set of left cosets, when $H \trianglelefteq G$)
- $\langle g \rangle$, $\langle S \rangle$ — the subgroup generated by an element $g$ or a subset $S$
- $\varphi : G \to H$ — a group homomorphism
- $\ker \varphi$, $\operatorname{im} \varphi$ — the kernel and image of $\varphi$
- $G \cong H$ — $G$ and $H$ are isomorphic
- $\{e\}$ or $1$ — the trivial group
- $C_n$ — cyclic group of order $n$; $S_n$ — symmetric group; $A_n$ — alternating group; $D_{2n}$ — dihedral group of order $2n$
- $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ — the usual number systems, here as additive groups
- $G \times H$ — the direct product
- $HK$ — the set $\{hk : h \in H, k \in K\}$

# Motivation

A group is the mathematics of **reversible transformation**. Whenever you have a collection of operations you can compose, where every operation can be undone, and where doing nothing counts as an operation, you have a group. The symmetries of a square, the permutations of a deck of cards, the integers under addition, the invertible matrices — these look like different kinds of object, but the axioms in [[Def - Group]] capture exactly what they share, and any theorem proved from those axioms speaks about all of them at once.

Sections §1.1 is a rapid recap: groups, subgroups, cosets, and the single most important counting fact in finite group theory, [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]]. If you remember one thing from a first course, it is that the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group. This is not a statement about which subgroups *exist* — it is a brutal constraint that rules most candidate subgroups out before you even look.

The real content of this topic is §1.2, and it answers one question: **how do you take a group apart?** A group can be enormous and intricate, and the only hope of understanding it is to break it into smaller pieces. The arithmetic analogy is exact. To understand an integer you factor it into primes; to understand a group you want to factor it into simpler groups. The "factor" operation for groups is the **quotient** $G/N$, which collapses a subgroup $N$ to a single point. But the quotient does not always make sense — coset multiplication is only well-defined when $N$ is **normal** (see [[Def - Normal Subgroup]]). Normality is not an arbitrary technical hypothesis; it is precisely, exactly, the condition under which the division works, and §1.2 derives the definition by demanding that it work.

Once you can divide, you need bookkeeping. [[Def - Homomorphism|Homomorphisms]] are the maps that respect group structure, and the three **isomorphism theorems** are the rules of arithmetic for quotients: the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem|first]] says every homomorphism *is* a quotient map followed by an inclusion; the [[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem|second]] and [[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem|third]] tell you how quotients interact with subgroups and with each other. The chapter closes with the notion of a [[Def - Simple Group|simple group]] — a group with no quotients at all, the analogue of a prime — and the [[Thm - Composition Series|composition series]], which shows every finite group really is built from simple pieces. The classification of those pieces is one of the deepest theorems in mathematics; this topic builds the language in which it is even stated.

# Concept Map

## §1.1 Basic Concepts

- **[[Def - Group]]**
	- A **group** is a set $G$ with an associative binary operation $\cdot : G \times G \to G$, an identity element $e$ satisfying $e \cdot g = g \cdot e = g$, and an inverse $g^{-1}$ for every $g$ with $g \cdot g^{-1} = g^{-1} \cdot g = e$. The axioms encode reversible composition: every element can be undone. Associativity is what makes a product $g_1 g_2 \cdots g_n$ unambiguous without brackets. Canonical examples are $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the symmetric groups $S_n$, and the matrix groups $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$; the identity and each inverse are forced to be unique.

- **[[Def - Subgroup]]**
	- A **subgroup** $H \leq G$ is a subset that is itself a group under the restricted operation: it contains $e$ and is closed under products and inverses. The efficient one-step test is the **subgroup criterion**: $H$ is a subgroup if and only if it is non-empty and $h_1 h_2^{-1} \in H$ for all $h_1, h_2 \in H$. Subgroups are the substructures of a group, and the lattice of all subgroups is the first invariant one studies. Examples: $n\mathbb{Z} \leq \mathbb{Z}$, $\mathrm{SL}_n \leq \mathrm{GL}_n$, and the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ generated by any element.

- **[[Def - Abelian Group]]**
	- A group is **abelian** if its operation is commutative, $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ for all $a, b$. Abelian groups are dramatically simpler than general groups: every subgroup is automatically normal, and the finite ones are completely classified (see [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7]]). The smallest non-abelian group is $S_3$, of order $6$. The name honours Niels Henrik Abel; commutativity is the single most consequential extra hypothesis one can add to the group axioms.

- **[[Def - Coset]]**
	- For $H \leq G$ and $g \in G$, the **left coset** is $gH = \{gh : h \in H\}$. The cosets partition $G$ into disjoint blocks, all of the same size as $H$ — the map $h \mapsto gh$ is a bijection $H \to gH$. Two elements name the same coset exactly when $g^{-1}g' \in H$. Cosets are the translates of $H$; the number of them is the **index** $|G : H|$, and they are the raw material both for [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] and for the [[Def - Quotient Group|quotient construction]].

- **[[Def - Order of a Group and of an Element]]**
	- The **order** $|G|$ of a group is its number of elements. The **order** $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ of an element is the least $n > 0$ with $g^n = e$, or $\infty$ if no such $n$ exists; it equals the order of the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$. For finite $G$, [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] forces $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ to divide $|G|$. Order is the most basic numerical invariant of a group, and much of finite group theory is the study of what the integer $|G|$ alone forces upon $G$.

- **[[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem]]**
	- For a finite group $G$ and a subgroup $H \leq G$, $|G| = |H| \cdot |G : H|$; in particular $|H|$ divides $|G|$. The proof is the observation that the cosets of $H$ partition $G$ into $|G : H|$ blocks each of size $|H|$. Immediate consequences: a group of prime order is cyclic, the order of every element divides $|G|$, and Fermat's little theorem. The converse is false — the group $A_4$ has order $12$ but no subgroup of order $6$ — so Lagrange constrains but does not construct.

## §1.2 Normal Subgroups, Quotients, Homomorphisms, Isomorphisms

- **[[Def - Normal Subgroup]]**
	- A subgroup $N \leq G$ is **normal**, written $N \trianglelefteq G$, if $gNg^{-1} = N$ for every $g \in G$ — equivalently $g^{-1}ng \in N$ for all $n \in N, g \in G$. There are four equivalent faces: conjugation-invariance, the coincidence of left and right cosets ($gN = Ng$), being a union of conjugacy classes, and being the kernel of some homomorphism. Normality is exactly the condition that makes coset multiplication well-defined. In an abelian group every subgroup is normal, which is why abelian groups have so many quotients.

- **[[Def - Quotient Group]]**
	- When $N \trianglelefteq G$, the set $G/N$ of cosets becomes a group under $(g_1 N)(g_2 N) = g_1 g_2 N$, with identity $N$ and inverses $(gN)^{-1} = g^{-1}N$. The quotient **collapses $N$ to a single point**: it is the group of "$G$ modulo the distinction $N$ erases". Its order is the index, $|G/N| = |G : N|$. The quotient is how groups are divided, and the requirement that $N$ be normal is the price of admission — without it the product of cosets depends on the representatives chosen.

- **[[Def - Homomorphism]]**
	- A **homomorphism** is a map $\varphi : G \to H$ with $\varphi(g_1 g_2) = \varphi(g_1)\varphi(g_2)$. It then follows automatically that $\varphi(e_G) = e_H$ and $\varphi(g^{-1}) = \varphi(g)^{-1}$. Homomorphisms are the structure-preserving maps — the morphisms of the category of groups — and most of group theory is the study of them. Examples: $\det : \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}^\times$, the sign $\operatorname{sgn} : S_n \to \{\pm 1\}$, and the quotient map $G \to G/N$.

- **[[Def - Kernel and Image]]**
	- For a homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$, the **kernel** is $\ker\varphi = \{g : \varphi(g) = e_H\}$ and the **image** is $\operatorname{im}\varphi = \{\varphi(g) : g \in G\}$. The kernel is always a *normal* subgroup of $G$; the image is a subgroup of $H$. The kernel measures the failure of injectivity — $\varphi$ is injective if and only if $\ker\varphi = \{e\}$ — and conversely every normal subgroup arises as a kernel, while every subgroup arises as an image. These two subgroups are the data the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem|first isomorphism theorem]] ties together.

- **[[Def - Isomorphism]]**
	- An **isomorphism** is a bijective homomorphism; its set-theoretic inverse is then automatically a homomorphism. Two groups are **isomorphic**, $G \cong H$, if an isomorphism exists between them — they are "the same group with the elements relabelled". Isomorphism is the equivalence relation under which group theory is conducted: to *classify* groups means to list them up to isomorphism. An isomorphism from a group to itself is an **automorphism**.

- **[[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem]]**
	- For any homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$, the map $g\ker\varphi \mapsto \varphi(g)$ is an isomorphism $G/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$. Every homomorphism therefore factors as a surjection onto a quotient followed by an injection. This is the master tool for **identifying an unknown quotient**: to show $G/N \cong Q$, exhibit a surjection $G \to Q$ with kernel exactly $N$. The classic illustration is $\varphi(z) = e^z$, giving $\mathbb{C}/2\pi i\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{C}^\times$.

- **[[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem]]**
	- If $H \leq G$ and $K \trianglelefteq G$, then $HK$ is a subgroup of $G$, $H \cap K$ is normal in $H$, and $HK/K \cong H/(H \cap K)$. This is the "diamond" theorem: it relates an arbitrary subgroup $H$ to a normal subgroup $K$ by comparing $H$ with its image under the quotient map $G \to G/K$. It is proved in one line by applying the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem|first isomorphism theorem]] to the composite $H \hookrightarrow G \to G/K$.

- **[[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem]]**
	- If $K \leq L \leq G$ are both normal in $G$, then $L/K \trianglelefteq G/K$ and $(G/K)\big/(L/K) \cong G/L$. Quotienting in two stages — first by $K$, then by $L/K$ — gives the same result as quotienting by $L$ all at once. It is the "cancellation law" for quotients, the group-theoretic counterpart of $\frac{a/c}{b/c} = \frac{a}{b}$, and like the second theorem it falls straight out of the first.

- **[[Thm - Correspondence Theorem]]**
	- If $K \trianglelefteq G$, the subgroups of the quotient $G/K$ correspond bijectively with the subgroups of $G$ that **contain $K$**, via $X \mapsto \{g \in G : gK \in X\}$. The correspondence preserves inclusion, index, and normality, so normal subgroups of $G/K$ match normal subgroups of $G$ containing $K$. It lets you read the entire subgroup lattice of a quotient off the lattice of $G$, and it is the engine behind the inductive proofs of [[Thm - Composition Series|composition series]] and of the $p$-group results in [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7]].

- **[[Def - Simple Group]]**
	- A non-trivial group $G$ is **simple** if its only normal subgroups are $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself — it has no non-trivial quotients, so it cannot be broken down any further. Simple groups are the atoms of finite group theory. The abelian simple groups are exactly the cyclic groups $C_p$ of prime order; the non-abelian ones — the alternating groups $A_n$ for $n \geq 5$, the groups of Lie type, and $26$ sporadic groups — are the substance of the Classification of Finite Simple Groups.

- **[[Thm - Abelian Simple Groups are Cyclic of Prime Order]]**
	- An abelian group is [[Def - Simple Group|simple]] if and only if it is isomorphic to $C_p$ for some prime $p$. The point is that in an abelian group *every* subgroup is normal, so simplicity collapses to having no proper non-trivial subgroups at all; a short argument with cyclic subgroups and [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange]] then forces the order to be prime. This is the complete classification of abelian simple groups, and it shows the cyclic groups of prime order are the abelian atoms.

- **[[Thm - Composition Series]]**
	- Every finite group $G$ admits a chain $G = H_1 \trianglerighteq H_2 \trianglerighteq \cdots \trianglerighteq H_n = \{e\}$, with each $H_i$ normal in the previous one and each quotient $H_i/H_{i+1}$ [[Def - Simple Group|simple]]. The simple quotients are the **composition factors** of $G$ — its "prime factorization". The Jordan–Hölder theorem (beyond this course) shows the multiset of composition factors is an invariant of $G$, independent of the chain chosen.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Group Representation *(from Representation Theory)*
> A representation of $G$ on a vector space $V$ is nothing but a [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphism]] $G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$. Once you have homomorphisms and the symmetric/matrix groups, the entire subject of representation theory is available — it is the study of homomorphisms from $G$ into matrix groups.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Additive Group of a Ring *(from Ring Theory)*
> A ring is an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]] under $+$ carrying a compatible multiplication. The isomorphism theorems for rings, modules, and vector spaces are these very theorems re-run with "subgroup" replaced by "ideal" or "submodule" — see [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2#Bridges|Bridges]].

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of an introductory group theory section fall into a small number of recurring goals, and recognizing which goal you are chasing is half the battle. The most common is **identifying a group up to isomorphism**: you are handed a quotient $G/N$, or a group defined by a construction, and asked what familiar group it equals. A second is **proving a subgroup is normal**, which is the gateway to forming any quotient at all. A third is **counting** — the order of a group, the number of elements of a given order, the number of subgroups or cosets — where the answer is forced by divisibility. A fourth is **establishing or refuting simplicity**, that is, deciding whether a group can be broken down. Finally there are **structural existence and non-existence claims**: does a group of order $n$ contain an element of order $d$, a subgroup of index $k$, a normal subgroup of a given type. These five — identify, normalize, count, decide simplicity, build or forbid structure — are the targets, and they recur because each is a way of pinning a group down: you understand a finite group when you know its order, its subgroups, its normal subgroups, its quotients, and which standard group each quotient is.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The assumptions in these problems are equally stereotyped. **The order $|G|$ is given**, perhaps with its prime factorization — this is the richest source, because [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange]] turns it instantly into divisibility constraints on every subgroup and element. **A homomorphism is given or can be built** — the moment you have a homomorphism you have a normal subgroup (its kernel) and a quotient (its image), and the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem|first isomorphism theorem]] converts the homomorphism into an isomorphism. **A subgroup of small index is given** — index $2$ forces normality outright, and small index in general lets you act on cosets. **An element of known order is given**, producing a cyclic subgroup of that order. **The group is abelian**, which makes every subgroup normal and every quotient available. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a given order routes through Lagrange to a counting or structure conclusion; a constructed homomorphism routes through the first isomorphism theorem to an identification; a small index routes through normality to a quotient. The [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves that almost every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything here is self-contained: a reader with no group theory background should be able to follow each operation from the description alone.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Partition a group into cosets of a subgroup.** Given any subgroup $H \leq G$, the [[Def - Coset|left cosets]] $gH$ chop $G$ into disjoint blocks, all the same size as $H$. Use this whenever you want to count elements of $G$ or compare $|G|$ with $|H|$: it is the content of [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]], and it is the first thing to reach for when the order of a group or subgroup appears in the problem. The trigger is any divisibility question.

2. **Read off divisibility from Lagrange.** Once a subgroup or element exists, its order *divides* $|G|$. This converts the single integer $|G|$ into a list of constraints. The trigger is: you know $|G|$ and want to limit what subgroups or element orders are possible. For example, in a group of order $15$ every non-identity element has order $3$, $5$, or $15$, because no other divisor is available.

3. **Build a homomorphism to expose structure.** To understand a group $G$, map it somewhere you understand. A [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphism]] $\varphi : G \to H$ immediately hands you a normal subgroup $\ker\varphi$ and a subgroup $\operatorname{im}\varphi$. Standard homomorphisms to keep in stock: the determinant, the sign of a permutation, "reduce mod $n$", projection $G \times H \to G$, and the quotient map $G \to G/N$. The trigger is any request to identify a quotient or to produce a normal subgroup out of thin air.

4. **Apply the first isomorphism theorem to identify a quotient.** If you can write down a *surjective* homomorphism $\varphi : G \to Q$ whose kernel is exactly $N$, then $G/N \cong Q$. This is how an abstract quotient is given a concrete name. The trigger: the problem says "show $G/N \cong \ldots$" or "what is this quotient". The skill is in choosing the target $Q$ and the map.

5. **Pass to a quotient to simplify.** Replacing $G$ by $G/N$ for a well-chosen normal subgroup $N$ produces a strictly smaller group. Combined with the [[Thm - Correspondence Theorem|correspondence theorem]], facts about $G/N$ pull back to facts about $G$. The trigger is an induction on $|G|$: quotient by something normal, solve the smaller problem, lift the conclusion.

6. **Conjugate to test or exploit normality.** Forming $gxg^{-1}$ is "viewing $x$ from the standpoint of $g$". A subgroup is [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal]] precisely when conjugation never leaves it. The trigger: you want to check normality (conjugate a general element and see if you stay inside), or you know a subgroup is normal and want to use $gNg^{-1} = N$ as a rewriting rule.

7. **Use index $2$ to force normality.** A subgroup of index $2$ is always normal — there are only two cosets, the subgroup and its complement, and they must coincide for left and right. The trigger is the literal appearance of a subgroup whose index is $2$; this is the cheapest normality argument there is, requiring no conjugation at all.

8. **Generate a subgroup from elements you possess.** From any element $g$ you get the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ of order $\operatorname{ord}(g)$; from any subset you get the subgroup it generates. The trigger: you have located some elements with known orders and want a subgroup of a particular size to feed into Lagrange or the isomorphism theorems.

9. **Intersect or multiply subgroups.** The intersection $H \cap K$ of two subgroups is always a subgroup; the product $HK$ is a subgroup as soon as one of them is normal. The [[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem|second isomorphism theorem]] then computes $HK/K$. The trigger is any problem featuring two subgroups at once.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Forming the quotient $G/H$ for a non-normal $H$
> It is tempting to divide by any subgroup, since cosets always partition the group. But the product $(g_1 H)(g_2 H) = g_1 g_2 H$ is only **well-defined** when $H$ is [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal]]: with $H = \langle (1\,2) \rangle \leq S_3$, the cosets multiply inconsistently depending on which representatives you pick. The set of cosets $G/H$ still exists as a set, and $G$ still acts on it, but it is not a group. Normality is exactly the missing condition.

> [!warning] 2. Assuming a subgroup of order $d$ exists because $d \mid |G|$
> Lagrange says every subgroup's order divides $|G|$; it does **not** say every divisor is achieved. The standard counterexample is $A_4$, of order $12$, which has no subgroup of order $6$. The converse direction is delicate and partial: Cauchy's theorem rescues the *prime* divisors, and the [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7|Sylow theorems]] rescue prime-power divisors, but no theorem promises a subgroup of every divisor.

> [!warning] 3. Treating "normal" as transitive
> If $K \trianglelefteq H$ and $H \trianglelefteq G$, it is tempting to conclude $K \trianglelefteq G$. This fails: normality is a relation between a subgroup and *one specific* overgroup, not an intrinsic property. In $D_8$, a reflection generates a subgroup normal in a Klein-four subgroup which is normal in $D_8$, yet the reflection's subgroup is not normal in $D_8$. The properties that *do* transfer are tracked carefully in the [[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem|third isomorphism theorem]] and the [[Thm - Correspondence Theorem|correspondence theorem]].

> [!warning] 4. Concluding $G \cong N \times (G/N)$ from a normal subgroup
> A normal subgroup $N$ and its quotient $G/N$ do not reassemble into $G$ by direct product. The group $C_4$ has the normal subgroup $C_2$ with quotient $C_2$, but $C_4 \not\cong C_2 \times C_2$. The quotient remembers the *sizes* of the pieces but forgets how they were glued; recovering $G$ from $N$ and $G/N$ is the extension problem, and it has genuinely many answers.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic are won or lost at the moment you classify what kind of problem you are looking at, so begin there. Almost every exercise is one of five types, and each type has a characteristic assumption pattern and a characteristic route to the answer.

If the problem **gives you the order of a group** and asks for something structural — an element of a certain order, a bound on the number of subgroups, a constraint on element orders — then you are in a counting problem and your primary instrument is [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]]. The route is always the same: factor $|G|$, list the divisors, and observe that every subgroup order and every element order must appear on that list. Often this alone finishes the problem, because the list is short. When it does not, the next move is to find an actual element or subgroup whose order you can pin down — typically by generating a cyclic subgroup from an element you can name — and then play its order against the divisibility constraints. A group of order $15$, for instance, is squeezed so hard by Lagrange that it has no choice but to be cyclic.

If the problem **asks you to identify a quotient** — to put a concrete name to $G/N$ — then the assumption pattern is that you have, or can build, a homomorphism, and the route runs through the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem|first isomorphism theorem]]. This is the single most important technique in the chapter. You do not try to understand $G/N$ directly; instead you guess the answer $Q$, construct a surjective homomorphism $\varphi : G \to Q$, and check that its kernel is exactly $N$. The theorem then certifies $G/N \cong Q$ for free. The entire difficulty is concentrated in the guess and the construction, and the guidance is to look for a property of elements of $G$ that is "blind to $N$" — a feature that two elements share precisely when they differ by an element of $N$. That feature *is* the homomorphism. When the problem instead hands you two subgroups, one of them normal, and asks for a quotient, the [[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem|second isomorphism theorem]] is the pre-packaged answer; when it hands you a tower of normal subgroups, the [[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem|third]] is.

If the problem **asks you to prove a subgroup is normal**, scan first for the cheap wins before doing any work. Is the index $2$? Then it is normal immediately. Is the group abelian? Then every subgroup is normal. Is the subgroup visibly the kernel of some homomorphism, or the centre, or an intersection of normal subgroups? Each of these is normal for a structural reason and needs no computation. Only if all the cheap routes fail should you fall back on the definition and conjugate a general element $gng^{-1}$, checking that it lands back inside. The reason to exhaust the structural arguments first is that they generalize, whereas a brute-force conjugation calculation teaches you nothing reusable.

If the problem **concerns simplicity** — proving a group is simple, or far more often proving it is *not* — the targets and tools split sharply. To prove non-simplicity you must produce a proper non-trivial normal subgroup, and the productive sources of normal subgroups are kernels of homomorphisms and (in later topics) unique Sylow subgroups; the strategy is to manufacture a homomorphism that cannot be injective and cannot be trivial. To prove simplicity is much harder and is usually deferred to a direct assault on the conjugacy class structure, as in the proof that $A_5$ is simple. For abelian groups the question is settled completely and cheaply by [[Thm - Abelian Simple Groups are Cyclic of Prime Order]].

Finally, a meta-strategy threads through all of the above: **when a group is too big to handle, quotient it down.** If you can find any proper non-trivial normal subgroup $N$, then $G/N$ is strictly smaller, and the [[Thm - Correspondence Theorem|correspondence theorem]] guarantees that subgroups, normal subgroups, indices, and quotients of $G/N$ are faithful shadows of the corresponding objects in $G$. This licenses induction on $|G|$, and induction on the order is the backbone of essentially every structural theorem in the next two topics. The reason this strategy is always worth trying is that it cannot make things worse: either the smaller group is tractable and you lift the conclusion, or it is still hard and you have lost nothing.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's Theorem]]**: $|H|$ divides $|G|$, and $|G| = |H|\,|G:H|$. This is the most-used single fact in finite group theory because it is *free*: it costs nothing and applies the instant a subgroup or element is in sight. Reach for it whenever an order or an index appears, whenever you need to bound the possible orders of elements, and whenever a counting argument needs a denominator. Its most powerful disguised use is *negative* — ruling out subgroups and homomorphisms by a divisibility obstruction, for instance showing there is no injective homomorphism from a group of order $10$ into one of order $21$.

- **[[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem|First Isomorphism Theorem]]**: $G/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$. This is the workhorse for *identification*. The recognizable setup is "here is a quotient, what is it?" or "show these two groups are isomorphic". Combine it with a cleverly chosen homomorphism and it converts an opaque quotient into a familiar group in one step. It is also the proof engine for the second and third isomorphism theorems, so internalizing it pays compound interest. Recognize its applicability whenever you can find a structure-preserving map whose kernel is the subgroup you are quotienting by.

- **[[Def - Normal Subgroup|Normality]]** (as kernel-detection): a subgroup is normal if and only if it is a kernel. The reusable move is the equivalence itself — to *produce* a normal subgroup, build a homomorphism and take its kernel; to *use* a normal subgroup, remember it is the kernel of the quotient map $G \to G/N$ and that $gNg^{-1} = N$ is a licence to move $N$ past any element. Normality is the hinge on which every quotient turns.

- **[[Def - Coset|Cosets partition the group]]**: the cosets of $H$ are disjoint, exhaustive, and equinumerous with $H$. This is more reusable than Lagrange itself, because the partition statement holds for *any* subgroup and underlies orbit-counting, the [[Group Theory II — §1.3–1.4|orbit-stabiliser theorem]], and the index calculations in [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7|Sylow theory]]. The typical use is to set up a count: whenever you must enumerate a set on which a group acts, look for a subgroup whose cosets are the pieces.

- **[[Thm - Correspondence Theorem|Correspondence Theorem]]**: subgroups of $G/N$ are exactly the subgroups of $G$ above $N$. Its typical use is in induction: it certifies that a quotient is not just smaller but *structurally transparent*, so any subgroup you find downstairs in $G/N$ corresponds to a known subgroup upstairs. It is the reason quotienting is a safe simplification rather than a loss of information.

# Bridges

1. **Ring theory and linear algebra — the isomorphism theorems recur verbatim.** A ring is an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]] under addition with a compatible multiplication, and a module or vector space is an abelian group with a scalar action. In every one of these settings there is a notion of substructure (ideal, submodule, subspace) playing the role of the normal subgroup, a quotient construction, and a first isomorphism theorem of exactly the same shape: a structure-preserving map factors as a quotient followed by an injection. The reason the theorems look identical is that they *are* identical — they are all instances of the isomorphism theorems for groups with operators, and ultimately of a single statement in any algebraic category. Learning the group case once means the ring and module cases in later topics cost nothing.

2. **Representation theory — homomorphisms into matrix groups.** A linear representation of $G$ is a [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphism]] $\rho : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$ to the invertible linear maps of a vector space. Everything in this topic transfers: $\ker\rho$ is a normal subgroup measuring how much of $G$ the representation forgets, a representation is faithful exactly when that kernel is trivial, and the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem|first isomorphism theorem]] says $G/\ker\rho$ embeds in $\mathrm{GL}(V)$. Representation theory is, structurally, the systematic study of all homomorphisms out of $G$ at once.

3. **Algebraic topology — the fundamental group and covering spaces.** To each topological space $X$ one assigns a group $\pi_1(X)$ built from loops, and to each continuous map a homomorphism. The deepest bridge is the Galois correspondence for covering spaces: the connected covers of a space $X$ correspond to subgroups of $\pi_1(X)$, with normal subgroups corresponding to the symmetric ("regular") covers and the [[Def - Quotient Group|quotient group]] acting as the covering's symmetry group. Normal subgroups and quotients, abstract here, become geometric there.

4. **Number theory — modular arithmetic as a quotient.** The group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is literally the [[Def - Quotient Group|quotient]] of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ by the [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] $n\mathbb{Z}$, and "reduction mod $n$" is the quotient homomorphism. Lagrange's theorem applied to the multiplicative group of units modulo $n$ is Fermat's little theorem and its generalization, Euler's theorem. The first isomorphism theorem applied to $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/m \times \mathbb{Z}/n$ is the Chinese remainder theorem (see [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7]]).

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a group is understood through its homomorphisms, not its elements.** It is tempting to think of a group as a bag of elements with a multiplication table, but the table is unreadable past a handful of elements. The productive viewpoint, and the one §1.2 is built to install, is that a group is understood by the maps into and out of it. A homomorphism *out* of $G$ exposes a quotient and a normal subgroup; a homomorphism *into* $G$ exposes a subgroup. The three isomorphism theorems are the grammar of this language, and the first isomorphism theorem is its central sentence: it says the homomorphism, the normal subgroup, and the quotient are three views of one object. Whenever you are stuck on a group, the question to ask is not "what are its elements" but "what can I map it to, and what maps into it".

**The true name of normality is "kernel".** The textbook definition of a normal subgroup — closed under conjugation — is the right thing to *check* but the wrong thing to *think*. Conjugation-invariance is a verification criterion; it tells you almost nothing about why you would ever care. The operational meaning is that the normal subgroups of $G$ are precisely the kernels of homomorphisms out of $G$, equivalently precisely the subgroups you are allowed to quotient by. So when you see "$N$ is normal", do not picture a conjugation calculation — picture the quotient map $G \to G/N$, of which $N$ is the kernel. Every use of normality in this topic and the next is really a use of that map.

**Quotients are deliberate, controlled forgetting.** Passing from $G$ to $G/N$ throws information away — it declares two elements indistinguishable when they differ by an element of $N$. The art is that the forgetting is controlled: the [[Thm - Correspondence Theorem|correspondence theorem]] guarantees you can still see, inside $G/N$, every subgroup of $G$ that contained $N$, with its index and normality intact. This is why "quotient to simplify" is a safe move and not a reckless one. The same pattern — forget structure you do not need, but forget it in a way that leaves the surviving structure faithfully visible — recurs throughout mathematics: it is what passing to a quotient ring, a quotient space, or a quotient topology all accomplish.

**Lagrange's theorem is a constraint, not a construction, and the gap between the two is the whole subject.** Lagrange tells you the order of every subgroup divides $|G|$. It is enormously powerful as a *filter* — it eliminates impossible subgroups instantly — but it builds nothing. The natural question it provokes is the converse: given a divisor $d$ of $|G|$, must there be a subgroup of order $d$? The answer is no in general ($A_4$, order $12$, has no subgroup of order $6$), yes for prime divisors (Cauchy), and yes for prime-power divisors (Sylow). The entire arc of [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7]] is the project of partially reversing Lagrange, and the reason it is hard — and interesting — is that the obvious converse is simply false.

**Simple groups are an ending, not a beginning.** It is natural to expect that the "simplest" groups are the easy ones, the place a course should start. The opposite is true: [[Def - Simple Group|simple]] groups are the ones that resist all the machinery of this topic, because that machinery is entirely about *breaking groups apart*, and a simple group cannot be broken. The [[Thm - Composition Series|composition series]] says every finite group is assembled from simple composition factors, so the simple groups are the periodic table of finite group theory — and writing that table down took the combined effort of a generation of mathematicians. This topic's tools build *toward* the simple groups by stripping everything else away.
