# Notation Registry

- $G, H, K, N, P, Q$ — groups; $P, Q$ are typically $p$-subgroups
- $p$ — a prime number; $|G|$ — the order of $G$
- $p$-group — a finite group of order $p^n$ for some $n \geq 1$
- $|G| = p^a m$ with $p \nmid m$ — the standard factorization isolating the prime $p$
- $Z(G)$ — the centre of $G$; $C_G(x)$ — the centraliser of $x$; $N_G(H)$ — the normaliser of $H$
- $\langle x \rangle$ — the cyclic subgroup generated by $x$
- $C_n$ — the cyclic group of order $n$; $G \times H$ — the direct product
- A **Sylow $p$-subgroup** — a subgroup of order $p^a$, the largest possible power of $p$
- $\operatorname{Syl}_p(G)$ — the set of all Sylow $p$-subgroups of $G$
- $n_p = |\operatorname{Syl}_p(G)|$ — the number of Sylow $p$-subgroups
- $d_1, \dots, d_r$ — invariant factors, satisfying $d_{i+1} \mid d_i$ (in the classification of abelian groups)
- $\gcd(m,n)$ — the greatest common divisor; $m, n$ coprime means $\gcd(m,n) = 1$

# Motivation

[[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] told us that the order of every subgroup divides $|G|$. It is a constraint of enormous force, but it builds nothing: it does not promise that a subgroup of any given order *exists*, and indeed the converse is false — $A_4$ has order $12$ and no subgroup of order $6$. This topic is the sustained project of **partially reversing Lagrange**: of extracting genuine existence and structure theorems from the single datum of the integer $|G|$.

The project proceeds in three stages of increasing power. The first, §1.5, studies the simplest groups whose order is not prime: the **$p$-groups**, of order a prime power. One might expect these to be featureless, but they are remarkably rigid. The [[Thm - The Class Equation|class equation]] from the previous topic, applied to a $p$-group, forces the [[Def - Centraliser and Centre|centre]] to be non-trivial — and from that one fact a cascade follows: $p$-groups are never [[Def - Simple Group|simple]], every $p$-group has a [[Thm - Subgroups of a p-Group|subgroup of every order dividing $|G|$]], and groups of order $p^2$ are [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian]]. For $p$-groups, Lagrange's converse holds completely.

The second stage, §1.6, takes the abelian case and finishes it entirely. The **classification of finite abelian groups** says every finite abelian group is a [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] of cyclic groups, in an essentially unique way. This is a complete solution: it lists, with no redundancy and no omission, every finite abelian group there is. The classification will be proved in full generality later in the course as a special case of the structure theorem for modules over a principal ideal domain; here it is stated and used, with the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Cyclic Groups|Chinese remainder theorem]] as the tool that converts between its two standard forms.

The third stage, §1.7, is the summit: **Sylow's theorems**. Where Cauchy's theorem rescues the prime divisors of $|G|$ and the $p$-group results handle prime powers in isolation, Sylow's theorems handle the largest prime-power divisor of any $|G|$ at once. They guarantee that a subgroup of order $p^a$ exists (where $p^a$ is the exact power of $p$ in $|G|$), that all such subgroups are [[Def - Conjugacy Class|conjugate]], and that their number $n_p$ is sharply constrained: $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and $n_p$ divides $|G|$. These constraints are so tight that they amount to a near-algorithm for the most common question in elementary group theory — *is there a group of order $n$ that is simple?* — and the answer, for a long list of $n$, is forced to be no. Every Sylow theorem is proved by the technique of [[Group Theory II — §1.3–1.4|the previous topic]]: choose the right action, and apply orbit-stabiliser.

# Concept Map

## §1.5 Finite p-Groups

<details>
<summary><strong>[[Def - p-group]]</strong></summary>

A **$p$-group** is a finite group whose order is a power $p^n$ of a prime $p$, with $n \geq 1$. By [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange]] every element has order a power of $p$, and by [[Group Theory II — §1.3–1.4|Cauchy's theorem]] the converse holds, so $p$-groups are exactly the finite groups in which every element has $p$-power order. They are the simplest groups beyond prime order, and they are unexpectedly rigid: their defining arithmetic forces a non-trivial centre, subgroups of every intermediate order, and abelian-ness in order $p^2$.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>[[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre]]</strong></summary>

Every non-trivial finite $p$-group has non-trivial [[Def - Centraliser and Centre|centre]], $Z(G) \neq \{e\}$. The proof is the [[Thm - The Class Equation|class equation]] read modulo $p$: every non-central conjugacy class has size a non-trivial power of $p$, hence divisible by $p$, and since $|G|$ is too, the count of size-one classes — that is, $|Z(G)|$ — must be divisible by $p$. As $\{e\}$ is one such class, there must be at least $p$ of them. An immediate consequence is that a $p$-group of order $p^n$ with $n \geq 2$ is never [[Def - Simple Group|simple]].

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>[[Thm - Quotient by the Centre and Commutativity]]</strong></summary>

For any group $G$, if the quotient $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic then $G$ is [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian]] — in which case $G/Z(G)$ is in fact trivial. The point is that a generator coset $gZ(G)$ forces every element into the form $g^r z$ with $z$ central, and any two such elements visibly commute. Combined with [[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre|the non-trivial centre]], this yields the clean corollary that **every group of order $p^2$ is abelian**: the centre has order $p$ or $p^2$, and order $p$ would make $G/Z(G)$ cyclic of order $p$, a contradiction.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>[[Thm - Subgroups of a p-Group]]</strong></summary>

A group $G$ of order $p^a$ has a subgroup of order $p^b$ for *every* $0 \leq b \leq a$, and these can be taken normal in $G$. This is Lagrange's converse holding in full for $p$-groups, and it fails for general groups ($A_5$, order $60$, has no subgroup of order $30$). The proof inducts on $a$: a central element of order $p$ generates a normal subgroup $N \cong C_p$, the smaller group $G/N$ has a subgroup of order $p^{b-1}$ by induction, and the [[Thm - Correspondence Theorem|correspondence theorem]] pulls it back to a subgroup of order $p^b$ in $G$.

</details>

## §1.6 Finite Abelian Groups

<details>
<summary><strong>[[Def - Direct Product]]</strong></summary>

The **direct product** $G \times H$ of two groups is the set of pairs $(g, h)$ with componentwise multiplication $(g_1, h_1)(g_2, h_2) = (g_1 g_2, h_1 h_2)$. It has order $|G|\,|H|$, contains $G$ and $H$ as commuting normal subgroups intersecting trivially, and is the construction by which small groups are assembled into larger ones. The classification of finite [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian]] groups says the direct product of cyclic groups is the *only* way abelian groups are built — every one of them is such a product.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>[[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups]]</strong></summary>

Every finite [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian]] group $G$ is isomorphic to a [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] $C_{d_1} \times C_{d_2} \times \cdots \times C_{d_r}$ of cyclic groups, and the $d_i$ may be chosen so that $d_{i+1} \mid d_i$ — the **invariant factors** — in which case the list is unique. Equivalently, $G$ is a product of cyclic groups of prime-power order — the **elementary divisors**. This is a complete and irredundant classification: it solves the isomorphism problem for finite abelian groups outright. It is proved in full later in the course as the case $R = \mathbb{Z}$ of the structure theorem for modules over a principal ideal domain.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>[[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Cyclic Groups]]</strong></summary>

If $m$ and $n$ are coprime, then $C_{mn} \cong C_m \times C_n$. The proof exhibits an element of order $mn$ in $C_m \times C_n$: a generator pair $(g, h)$ has order $\operatorname{lcm}(m, n) = mn$. This is the group-theoretic form of the Chinese remainder theorem, and it is the device that converts between the two faces of the [[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|classification]] — fusing prime-power [[Def - Direct Product|factors]] across distinct primes into invariant factors, or splitting invariant factors back into elementary divisors.

</details>

> [!tip] Unlocked: Smith Normal Form and Modules over a PID *(from Rings and Modules)*
> The [[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|classification]] is the case $R = \mathbb{Z}$ of the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain. Taking $R = k[X]$ instead yields the rational and Jordan canonical forms of a linear operator; the computational engine in all cases is the Smith normal form of an integer or polynomial matrix.

## §1.7 Sylow's Theorems

<details>
<summary><strong>[[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup]]</strong></summary>

Write $|G| = p^a m$ with $p$ prime and $p \nmid m$. A **Sylow $p$-subgroup** of $G$ is a subgroup of order exactly $p^a$ — a $p$-subgroup of the largest order [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange]] permits. It is a [[Def - p-group|$p$-group]] maximal both by order and by inclusion. Sylow $p$-subgroups are the objects whose existence, conjugacy, and number are governed by [[Thm - Sylow's Theorems|Sylow's theorems]]; the example to keep in mind is the group of upper-triangular unipotent matrices, a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z}/p)$.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>[[Thm - Sylow's Theorems]]</strong></summary>

Let $|G| = p^a m$ with $p \nmid m$. **(I, Existence)** $G$ has a [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup|Sylow $p$-subgroup]]. **(II, Conjugacy)** any two Sylow $p$-subgroups are [[Def - Conjugacy Class|conjugate]], and every $p$-subgroup lies inside some Sylow $p$-subgroup. **(III, Count)** the number $n_p$ of Sylow $p$-subgroups satisfies $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and $n_p \mid |G|$ — indeed $n_p \mid m$. Each part is proved by a [[Group Theory II — §1.3–1.4|group action]]: $G$ on its $p^a$-subsets for existence, a $p$-subgroup on cosets for conjugacy, a Sylow subgroup on $\operatorname{Syl}_p(G)$ for the congruence.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>[[Thm - A Unique Sylow Subgroup is Normal]]</strong></summary>

If $G$ has exactly one [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup|Sylow $p$-subgroup]] — that is, if $n_p = 1$ — then that subgroup is [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal]]. The reason is immediate from [[Thm - Sylow's Theorems|Sylow II]]: any conjugate $gPg^{-1}$ is again a Sylow $p$-subgroup, so if there is only one, conjugation must fix it. This is the single most-used consequence of Sylow's theorems in practice: combined with the count $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$, $n_p \mid m$, it is the standard route to proving a group of given order is not [[Def - Simple Group|simple]].

</details>

> [!tip] Unlocked: Borel Subgroups *(from the Theory of Algebraic Groups)*
> The upper-triangular unipotent [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup|Sylow $p$-subgroup]] of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z}/p)$ is the finite shadow of a **Borel subgroup** — a maximal connected solvable subgroup of an algebraic group. Borel's conjugacy theorem ("all Borel subgroups are conjugate") is the exact analogue of [[Thm - Sylow's Theorems|Sylow II]].

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

This topic has one dominant target, so common it deserves to be named: **proving a group of a given order $n$ is not [[Def - Simple Group|simple]]**. Roughly speaking, the exercises hand you an integer $n$ and ask you to show every group of order $n$ has a proper non-trivial [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]]. A close relative is proving a group of order $n$ is [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian]], or cyclic, or a [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] of specified pieces — a stronger structural conclusion drawn from the same machinery. A third target is **counting**: the number of groups of order $n$ up to isomorphism, the number of abelian groups of order $n$, the number of elements of a given order, the number of Sylow subgroups. A fourth, specific to §1.6, is **decomposition**: given an abelian group, finding its invariant factors or elementary divisors. The reason non-simplicity dominates is historical and structural — the [[Thm - Composition Series|composition series]] makes the simple groups the atoms of finite group theory, so the first question about any order is whether a new simple group could hide there, and Sylow's theorems are precisely calibrated to answer it.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The sources are dominated by one: **the order $|G|$ is given as an explicit integer**, and its prime factorization is the entire input. From the factorization $|G| = p^a m$ you read the possible values of $n_p$ off the two Sylow constraints $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and $n_p \mid m$; very often only $n_p = 1$ survives, and you are done. When $|G|$ is a prime power, the source instead feeds the [[Thm - The Class Equation|class equation]] and the $p$-group results. A second source is **a $p$-subgroup or Sylow subgroup is given or constructed**, which routes through Sylow II (conjugacy) and the [[Def - Normaliser|normaliser]]. A third, for abelian problems, is **the group is abelian of known order**, which routes directly into the [[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|classification]]. The recurring move is mechanical and worth internalising: factor $|G|$, list candidate $n_p$ for each prime, and hunt for a prime forced to have $n_p = 1$. When that fails, the backup sources are element-counting and actions on the Sylow sets, described in the strategy below.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in this topic is built from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Factor the order and write down the Sylow constraints.** For each prime $p$ dividing $|G| = p^a m$, the number of [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup|Sylow $p$-subgroups]] satisfies $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and $n_p \mid m$. List the integers meeting both conditions for each prime. This is the first move in essentially every §1.7 problem, and it costs nothing but arithmetic; often the list for some prime is just $\{1\}$, which ends the problem.

2. **Conclude normality from a unique Sylow subgroup.** If the constraints force $n_p = 1$ for some prime $p$, that Sylow $p$-subgroup is [[Thm - A Unique Sylow Subgroup is Normal|normal]], and you have produced a proper non-trivial normal subgroup. The trigger is any non-simplicity problem: get $n_p = 1$ for even one prime and the group is not [[Def - Simple Group|simple]]. This is the workhorse and should always be tried first.

3. **Count elements of prime order.** When a Sylow $p$-subgroup has prime order $p$, distinct Sylow $p$-subgroups intersect trivially, so $n_p$ of them contribute exactly $n_p(p-1)$ elements of order $p$. Summing these counts across several primes and comparing with $|G|$ often yields a contradiction or forces some $n_p = 1$. The trigger is a $|G|$ divisible by several primes to the first power; this is the standard backup when operation 2 alone does not finish.

3 has a partner. **Act on the set of Sylow $p$-subgroups.** The group $G$ acts on $\operatorname{Syl}_p(G)$ by conjugation, giving a [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphism]] $G \to S_{n_p}$. If $|G| \nmid n_p!$ the kernel is a proper non-trivial normal subgroup. The trigger: the element-count is inconclusive but $n_p$ is small relative to $|G|$. This is operation 4.

5. **Apply the class equation.** When $|G|$ is a prime power, write $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum |G : C_G(x_i)|$. Every non-central term is divisible by $p$, so $p \mid |Z(G)|$. The trigger is any [[Def - p-group|$p$-group]] problem; this is the lever behind [[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre|the non-trivial centre]] and everything downstream of it.

6. **Quotient by a central element and induct.** In a $p$-group, take a central element of order $p$ — it exists because the centre is non-trivial — and form the normal subgroup $\langle x \rangle \cong C_p$. The quotient $G/\langle x \rangle$ is a smaller $p$-group, so induction applies, and the [[Thm - Correspondence Theorem|correspondence theorem]] lifts conclusions back. The trigger is any structural claim about $p$-groups proved by induction on the order.

7. **Decompose an abelian group via the classification.** Any finite [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian]] group is $C_{d_1} \times \cdots \times C_{d_r}$ with $d_{i+1} \mid d_i$. Use the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Cyclic Groups|Chinese remainder theorem]] to pass between invariant factors and elementary divisors. The trigger is any problem asserting a group is abelian: the [[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|classification]] reduces it to combinatorics of partitions.

8. **Combine two normal subgroups into a direct product.** If $H, K \trianglelefteq G$ with $H \cap K = \{e\}$ and $|H||K| = |G|$, then $G \cong H \times K$. The trigger: you have found normal Sylow subgroups for *all* primes; coprime orders force trivial intersection, and the group splits as the [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] of its Sylow subgroups. This upgrades "not simple" to a complete structural description.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Expecting a subgroup of every order dividing $|G|$
> The $p$-group result [[Thm - Subgroups of a p-Group]] is so clean that it is tempting to believe Lagrange's converse holds generally. It does not: $A_5$ has order $60$ but no subgroup of order $30$, and $A_4$ has order $12$ with no subgroup of order $6$. Subgroups of every order are guaranteed *only* for prime-power orders — via Sylow subgroups and the $p$-group theorem — never for arbitrary divisors.

> [!warning] 2. Assuming distinct Sylow $p$-subgroups intersect trivially
> The element-counting argument relies on distinct Sylow $p$-subgroups meeting only in $\{e\}$, and this is valid *only when the Sylow subgroups have prime order $p$*. When $|P| = p^a$ with $a \geq 2$, two Sylow $p$-subgroups can share a large subgroup: in a group of order $p^2 q$ two Sylow $p$-subgroups of order $p^2$ may intersect in a subgroup of order $p$. Counting elements of order $p$ then overcounts wildly. Check $a = 1$ before counting.

> [!warning] 3. Reading $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ as $n_p = 1$
> The congruence $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ permits $n_p = 1, 1+p, 1+2p, \dots$; it forces $n_p = 1$ only when no larger value also divides $m$. It is a frequent error to drop the divisibility condition $n_p \mid m$ and conclude uniqueness from the congruence alone, or vice versa. Both conditions must be imposed together, and only their *common* solutions are admissible.

> [!warning] 4. Believing a normal Sylow subgroup makes the group a direct product
> Finding $n_p = 1$ gives a normal Sylow subgroup, but $G$ is the [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] of its Sylow subgroups only if *every* Sylow subgroup is normal. With one normal Sylow subgroup $P$ and a non-normal complement, $G$ is a *semidirect* product, which is generally non-abelian and not a direct product — the group $S_3$, with normal $C_3$ and non-normal $C_2$, is the smallest example. Normality of one Sylow subgroup proves non-simplicity, not splitting.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The overwhelming majority of problems in this topic ask you to show that no group of some order $n$ is [[Def - Simple Group|simple]], and there is a standard playbook for this, to be run in order. Internalising the playbook is the single most valuable outcome of studying §1.7.

The first move is always the same: **factor $n$ and write down, for every prime $p$ dividing $n$, the candidate values of $n_p$** permitted by $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and $n_p \mid m$ where $n = p^a m$. Now scan the primes. If for any prime the only candidate is $n_p = 1$, you are finished immediately: that Sylow subgroup is [[Thm - A Unique Sylow Subgroup is Normal|normal]], so the group is not simple. A surprising fraction of problems — orders like $15$, $20$, $200$ — die at this first step, because the divisibility conditions are simply too tight to allow $n_p > 1$.

If every prime admits $n_p > 1$ as a possibility, move to the second tactic: **element-counting**. This works when the relevant Sylow subgroups have prime order, so that distinct ones intersect trivially. Each prime $p$ with $n_p$ Sylow subgroups of order $p$ then contributes exactly $n_p(p-1)$ elements of order $p$, and these element-sets for different primes are disjoint. Add up the forced counts. If the total exceeds $n$, or leaves too few elements for some remaining Sylow subgroup, you have a contradiction, and the resolution is that some $n_p$ must after all equal $1$. Orders like $30$ and $56$ fall to this tactic: the elements of large prime order are so numerous that the remaining Sylow subgroup has no room to be anything but unique.

If element-counting is inconclusive — because some Sylow subgroup is not of prime order, or the counts do not quite collide — move to the third tactic: **act on the Sylow set**. The group $G$ acts by conjugation on its $n_p$ Sylow $p$-subgroups, and by [[Thm - Sylow's Theorems|Sylow II]] this action is transitive, giving a [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphism]] $G \to S_{n_p}$. If $|G|$ does not divide $n_p!$, the kernel cannot be trivial; and since the action is transitive and non-trivial, the kernel cannot be all of $G$. The kernel is therefore a proper non-trivial normal subgroup. This embedding-obstruction argument handles orders like $36$ and $24$, where the Sylow count is small enough that $S_{n_p}$ is simply too small to contain $G$.

These three tactics — uniqueness, element-counting, the Sylow-set action — settle the non-simplicity of every order an introductory course will pose. A fourth, more refined move, used when all three stall, is to study **normaliser indices**: the number of Sylow $p$-subgroups is $n_p = |G : N_G(P)|$, so a small $n_p$ means a large normaliser, and the normaliser is itself a subgroup whose structure can be exploited.

For the [[Def - p-group|$p$-group]] problems of §1.5, the strategy is different and even more uniform: **everything is the [[Thm - The Class Equation|class equation]] and induction on the order**. To prove a structural fact about $p$-groups, write the class equation to extract a non-trivial centre, take a central element of order $p$ to form a normal $C_p$, pass to the smaller quotient $G/C_p$, apply the inductive hypothesis, and lift the conclusion through the [[Thm - Correspondence Theorem|correspondence theorem]]. This single template proves the subgroup theorem, the order-$p^2$ classification, and more.

For the abelian problems of §1.6, the strategy is to **invoke the [[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|classification]] and count partitions**. The number of abelian groups of order $p^a$ is the number of partitions of $a$; the number of abelian groups of order $n$ is the product of these over the prime-power factors of $n$. Converting a group between invariant-factor and elementary-divisor form is a mechanical rearrangement powered by the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Cyclic Groups|Chinese remainder theorem]]. The conceptual work in abelian problems is entirely front-loaded into the classification theorem; once it is invoked, what remains is bookkeeping.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Sylow's Theorems|Sylow's Theorems]]**: existence, conjugacy, and the count $n_p \equiv 1 \pmod p$, $n_p \mid m$. This is the most reusable result in the topic and arguably in elementary finite group theory. The recognizable setup is the appearance of an explicit order $|G|$: factor it, and the theorems immediately constrain the subgroup structure. The combination most worth memorising is Sylow III with [[Thm - A Unique Sylow Subgroup is Normal]] — together they convert the factorization of $|G|$ directly into a normal subgroup whenever the arithmetic cooperates.

- **[[Thm - The Class Equation|The class equation]]** (inherited from [[Group Theory II — §1.3–1.4]]): $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum |G:C_G(x_i)|$. In this topic its decisive use is on $p$-groups, where divisibility by $p$ of every non-central term forces a non-trivial centre. Reach for it whenever the order is a prime power, and whenever you need to prove a centre is non-trivial — it is the only general tool that produces central elements from nothing but the order.

- **[[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre|$p$-groups have non-trivial centre]]**: $Z(G) \neq \{e\}$. This is the seed from which all $p$-group structure grows. Its typical use is to start an induction: a central element of order $p$ gives a normal subgroup to quotient by, and the [[Thm - Correspondence Theorem|correspondence theorem]] does the rest. Whenever a problem says "$p$-group", this fact is the first thing to write down.

- **[[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|Classification of finite abelian groups]]**: every finite abelian group is a [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] of cyclic groups, uniquely in invariant-factor form. The typical use is total: it converts any question about a finite abelian group into a question about partitions and divisibility, with no group theory left. Recognise its applicability the instant "abelian" and "finite" appear together.

- **[[Thm - A Unique Sylow Subgroup is Normal|A unique Sylow subgroup is normal]]**: $n_p = 1 \Rightarrow$ that Sylow $p$-subgroup is normal. This tiny theorem is the most-invoked step in non-simplicity proofs. Its reusability is that it makes the abstract goal "find a normal subgroup" into the concrete, checkable arithmetic goal "force some $n_p$ to be $1$" — a goal you can attack purely with the Sylow constraints.

# Bridges

1. **Rings and modules — the classification as a module theorem.** The [[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|classification of finite abelian groups]] is the case $R = \mathbb{Z}$ of the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain: every such module is a direct sum of cyclic modules $R/(d_i)$ with $d_i \mid d_{i+1}$. A finite abelian group is exactly a finite $\mathbb{Z}$-module, the invariant factors $d_i$ are the same $d_i$, and the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Cyclic Groups|Chinese remainder theorem]] is the ring isomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/mn \cong \mathbb{Z}/m \times \mathbb{Z}/n$. Taking $R = k[X]$ instead, the same theorem yields the rational and Jordan canonical forms of a matrix — so this section and linear algebra's normal forms are one theorem seen twice.

2. **Galois theory — solvability and the unsolvable quintic.** A finite group is **solvable** if its [[Thm - Composition Series|composition factors]] are all cyclic of prime order. Every finite $p$-group is solvable — indeed nilpotent — because [[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre|the non-trivial centre]] supplies an abelian normal subgroup at every stage. A finite group is in fact nilpotent precisely when it is the [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] of its Sylow subgroups. The payoff is the Galois correspondence: a polynomial is solvable by radicals exactly when its Galois group is solvable, and since the [[Group Theory II — §1.3–1.4|symmetric group $S_n$]] is not solvable for $n \geq 5$, the general quintic has no solution in radicals.

3. **The classification of finite simple groups.** Every non-simplicity exercise in this topic is a hand-verification that a particular order does not support a new [[Def - Simple Group|simple group]]. The systematic version of this is one of the deepest theorems in mathematics: every finite simple group is cyclic of prime order, an alternating group $A_n$ with $n \geq 5$, a group of Lie type, or one of $26$ sporadic groups. Sylow's theorems are the elementary instrument that begins the analysis of which orders can occur, and the [[Thm - Composition Series|Jordan–Hölder theorem]] is what makes the simple groups the meaningful target.

4. **Algebraic groups — Sylow theory becomes Borel theory.** The unipotent upper-triangular [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup|Sylow $p$-subgroup]] of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z}/p)$ is the finite-field shadow of a **Borel subgroup** of the algebraic group $\mathrm{GL}_n$ — a maximal connected solvable subgroup. Borel's theorem that all Borel subgroups are conjugate is the precise analogue of [[Thm - Sylow's Theorems|Sylow's conjugacy theorem]], and the normaliser of the Sylow subgroup corresponds to the Borel itself. The theory of reductive groups, with its Borel subgroups, maximal tori, and Weyl groups, is in large part Sylow theory raised to the setting of algebraic geometry.

# Insights

**The whole topic is the project of reversing Lagrange, and the reversal succeeds exactly for prime powers.** [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange]] gives a one-way implication: a subgroup of order $d$ exists only if $d \mid |G|$. The natural question is the converse, and the honest answer is a graded one. For an arbitrary divisor $d$, the converse fails ($A_4$, no subgroup of order $6$). For a prime divisor it holds — that is Cauchy's theorem. For a prime-power divisor it holds — that is [[Thm - Subgroups of a p-Group]] together with [[Thm - Sylow's Theorems|Sylow I]]. The boundary is sharp and meaningful: prime powers are exactly the orders for which the converse of Lagrange is true, and recognising this tells you precisely when an existence question has a guaranteed positive answer and when it requires a genuine construction.

**The class equation transmits divisibility from $|G|$ to $|Z(G)|$, and that single transmission generates $p$-group theory.** It is worth isolating what actually does the work in §1.5. The [[Thm - The Class Equation|class equation]] $|G| = |Z(G)| + \sum |G:C_G(x_i)|$ is, for a $p$-group, an equation in which the left side and every summand on the right except $|Z(G)|$ are divisible by $p$. Therefore $|Z(G)|$ is too. That is the entire miracle; everything else — non-simplicity, the subgroup theorem, the order-$p^2$ classification — is induction built on top of it. The lesson is general: a congruence that pins one unknown term of a sum is often worth more than a direct attack on that term.

**Sylow's theorems are not three theorems but one technique applied three times.** Existence, conjugacy, and the count look like separate statements, but each is proved by the identical move: choose a set, let a group act on it, and apply [[Group Theory II — §1.3–1.4|orbit-stabiliser]]. Existence acts $G$ on its $p^a$-element subsets and finds an orbit whose stabiliser has the right order. Conjugacy acts a $p$-subgroup on cosets of a Sylow subgroup and uses that a $p$-group action on a set of size coprime to $p$ must have a fixed point. The congruence acts a Sylow subgroup on the set of all Sylow subgroups and counts fixed points modulo $p$. The unifying fact underneath all three is the **fixed-point congruence**: a $p$-group acting on a finite set $X$ has $|X^G| \equiv |X| \pmod p$. Sylow theory is that congruence, deployed against three well-chosen sets.

**Non-simplicity is decidable by arithmetic, and that is why the exercises feel mechanical.** Once Sylow's theorems are in hand, the question "is there a simple group of order $n$" becomes, for small $n$, a finite computation: factor $n$, list the admissible $n_p$, and try the three tactics. There is something philosophically striking here — a question about the existence of an abstract structure has been reduced to divisibility arithmetic on a single integer. This is also why the topic's exercises, for all their variety of $n$, train one skill: the playbook is fixed, and what changes from problem to problem is only which of the three tactics the arithmetic of $n$ happens to require. Recognising which tactic an order calls for, quickly, is the whole game.
