# Notation

Throughout, $G$ and $H$ are groups and $\varphi : G \to H$ is a [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphism]], a map satisfying $\varphi(g_1 g_2) = \varphi(g_1)\varphi(g_2)$. Its [[Def - Kernel and Image|kernel]] is $\ker\varphi = \{g \in G : \varphi(g) = e_H\}$ and its [[Def - Kernel and Image|image]] is $\operatorname{im}\varphi = \{\varphi(g) : g \in G\}$. For a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] $N \trianglelefteq G$, the [[Def - Quotient Group|quotient group]] $G/N$ is the set of cosets $gN$ with multiplication $(g_1 N)(g_2 N) = g_1 g_2 N$. The symbol $\cong$ denotes [[Def - Isomorphism|isomorphism]]. The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]].

---

# Statement

> **First Isomorphism Theorem.** Let $\varphi : G \to H$ be a homomorphism of groups. Then $\ker\varphi$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, and the map
> $$f : G/\ker\varphi \longrightarrow \operatorname{im}\varphi, \qquad g\ker\varphi \longmapsto \varphi(g)$$
> is a well-defined isomorphism. Hence $G/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$.

---

# Motivation

A homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$ is, on its face, just a function that respects multiplication. But it secretly carries a great deal of structural information about $G$, and the first isomorphism theorem is the statement that *extracts* that information in usable form.

Here is the question the theorem answers. Suppose you are handed a [[Def - Quotient Group|quotient group]] $G/N$ — perhaps as the result of some construction — and asked what it *is*: which familiar group does it equal? Working directly with $G/N$ is awkward, because its elements are cosets, which are sets, and reasoning about a multiplication of sets is unpleasant. You would much rather recognise $G/N$ as a group you already know. The first isomorphism theorem says exactly when you may: $G/N$ is isomorphic to $Q$ precisely when there is a surjective homomorphism $G \to Q$ whose kernel is $N$. So instead of dissecting the quotient, you go hunting for a homomorphism.

There is a second, more conceptual reading. Every homomorphism $\varphi$ fails to be injective in a way measured by $\ker\varphi$, and fails to be surjective in a way measured by $\operatorname{im}\varphi$. The theorem says these two failures are not independent: once you quotient out the kernel — that is, once you stop distinguishing elements that $\varphi$ already refuses to distinguish — the resulting map is a *perfect* match onto the image. Every homomorphism therefore factors canonically as

$$G \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{surjection}\;}\; G/\ker\varphi \;\xrightarrow{\;\cong\;}\; \operatorname{im}\varphi \;\xrightarrow{\;\text{inclusion}\;}\; H.$$

A homomorphism is nothing more than a quotient map followed by an inclusion. This is why kernels, images, and quotients are not three separate topics but three views of one phenomenon, and the first isomorphism theorem is the identity that ties them together. The second and third isomorphism theorems, and a great deal else, are corollaries.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The theorem's literal precondition is mild — *any* homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$ will do — so the real "source" question is: when does a problem secretly hand you a homomorphism, even though none is named? Recognising these disguised inputs is the entire skill.

The first disguised source is **a group defined by generators and relations, or by a universal-style property**. If $G$ is generated by elements $x_1, \dots, x_k$ and you wish to identify $G/N$, you can often define a map on generators into a candidate group $Q$ and check it respects the relations; that produces a homomorphism $G \to Q$ even though the problem only gave you a presentation. The non-obvious part is that a well-defined homomorphism out of $G$ exists at all — it must be *checked* against every defining relation. *Example problem:* show that the quotient of the free group on $\{a, b\}$ by the normal closure of $\{a^2, b^2, (ab)^2\}$ is the Klein four-group, by mapping the generators into $C_2 \times C_2$.

The second disguised source is **a group equipped with a natural numerical or combinatorial invariant**. If every element of $G$ carries a number — a sign, a determinant, a winding number, a residue modulo $n$ — that multiplies correctly under the group operation, then "take that invariant" is a homomorphism. The non-obvious step is to *notice* that the invariant is multiplicative and so qualifies. *Example problem:* identify the quotient of the symmetric group $S_n$ by the alternating group $A_n$ by using the sign $\operatorname{sgn} : S_n \to \{\pm 1\}$, whose kernel is exactly $A_n$.

The third disguised source is **a group acting on a set, or a subgroup sitting inside a larger group with a known quotient**. An action of $G$ on a set $X$ is by definition a homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Sym}(X)$; a subgroup $H \leq G$ composed with a quotient map $G \to G/K$ is a homomorphism $H \to G/K$. In both cases the homomorphism is built, not given. The non-obviousness is that composition with an inclusion or a quotient map manufactures a brand-new homomorphism whose kernel and image you can compute — this is exactly how the [[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem|second]] and [[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem|third]] isomorphism theorems arise. *Example problem:* given $H \leq G$ and $K \trianglelefteq G$, build $H \hookrightarrow G \twoheadrightarrow G/K$ and read off $HK/K \cong H/(H \cap K)$.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusion is an isomorphism $G/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$. Combined with other facts it does much more.

Combine the conclusion with **knowledge of the order of $G$**. Since $G/\ker\varphi$ has order $|G|/|\ker\varphi|$, the isomorphism gives $|\operatorname{im}\varphi| = |G|/|\ker\varphi|$, so $|\operatorname{im}\varphi|$ divides $|G|$ — and by [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] it also divides $|H|$. The further result $E$ is a divisibility obstruction: if $|G|$ and $|H|$ are coprime, then $|\operatorname{im}\varphi|$ divides both, so it is $1$ and the *only* homomorphism $G \to H$ is trivial. This is non-obvious because it turns a counting fact into a rigidity statement — there are simply no interesting maps between groups of coprime order.

Combine the conclusion with **simplicity of $G$**. If $G$ is a [[Def - Simple Group|simple group]] then $\ker\varphi$, being normal, is $\{e\}$ or $G$. So every homomorphism out of a simple group is either trivial or injective: a simple group cannot be "partially collapsed". The further result is that any non-trivial homomorphism from a simple group $G$ embeds $G$ into the target, which is the standard first move in proving non-simplicity of a larger group by exhibiting a map it cannot inject under.

Combine the conclusion with **a counting argument on $H$**. If you can independently bound $|\operatorname{im}\varphi|$ from above — say $\operatorname{im}\varphi$ lands in a known small subgroup of $H$ — then $|G|/|\ker\varphi|$ is small, forcing $\ker\varphi$ to be *large*. The further result $E$ is the production of a large normal subgroup of $G$ from nothing but a homomorphism into a small group; this is the engine of most non-simplicity proofs, where a forced large kernel is exactly the proper normal subgroup you wanted.

# Why Is It True

Forget the formal proof and picture what a homomorphism does to $G$. The map $\varphi$ sends $G$ onto $\operatorname{im}\varphi$, and along the way it identifies some elements: it can send $g$ and $g'$ to the same place. Ask which elements get identified. We have $\varphi(g) = \varphi(g')$ if and only if $\varphi(g)^{-1}\varphi(g') = e_H$, which says $\varphi(g^{-1}g') = e_H$, which says $g^{-1}g' \in \ker\varphi$. But "$g^{-1}g' \in \ker\varphi$" is precisely the condition for $g$ and $g'$ to lie in the same coset of $\ker\varphi$. So:

> Two elements of $G$ have the same image under $\varphi$ **exactly when** they lie in the same coset of $\ker\varphi$.

This single observation is the whole theorem. The cosets of $\ker\varphi$ are *literally* the fibres of $\varphi$ — the sets of elements sharing a common image. The kernel measures the redundancy in $\varphi$, and that redundancy is organised perfectly into cosets.

Now the conclusion is forced. The quotient $G/\ker\varphi$ is, by construction, the set of these cosets, that is, the set of fibres. Sending each fibre to the common value of $\varphi$ on it is a bijection onto $\operatorname{im}\varphi$: it is surjective because every value of $\varphi$ is the value on *some* fibre, and injective because *distinct* fibres carry distinct values — that is what the boxed statement says. And it respects multiplication because $\varphi$ does. The map $f$ is not a clever construction one must be lucky to find; it is the only thing $\varphi$ could possibly be once you collapse its redundancy. A homomorphism, looked at the right way, *is* a quotient map. The first isomorphism theorem just says so out loud.

A useful slogan: a homomorphism is a surjection that has forgotten it is a surjection. To remind it, quotient by the kernel.

---

# What Makes This Hard

The conceptual content is easy; the trap is the **well-definedness** of $f$. Because $f$ is defined by the rule "take a coset $g\ker\varphi$, pick the representative $g$, apply $\varphi$", one must check that a *different* representative $g'$ of the same coset yields the same answer — otherwise $f$ is not a function at all. Beginners routinely skip this and "prove" the theorem with a circular argument, or they conflate well-definedness with injectivity (these use the same equation $g^{-1}g' \in \ker\varphi$ but read in opposite directions: well-definedness assumes the cosets equal and concludes the values equal; injectivity assumes the values equal and concludes the cosets equal). The other common slip is forgetting to restrict the codomain to $\operatorname{im}\varphi$: the map $G/\ker\varphi \to H$ is injective but not surjective, so it is an isomorphism only onto its image, not onto all of $H$.

---

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Define the candidate map $f$ on cosets by applying $\varphi$ to a representative. Then verify, in order, the four things any claimed isomorphism needs: it is well-defined, it is a homomorphism, it is surjective, it is injective. Every step is a one-line consequence of the equivalence "$g\ker\varphi = g'\ker\varphi \iff \varphi(g) = \varphi(g')$".

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Kernel is normal.** Show $\ker\varphi \trianglelefteq G$, so the quotient $G/\ker\varphi$ even exists.
   - *Hint:* For $x \in G$ and $g \in \ker\varphi$, compute $\varphi(x^{-1}gx)$ using multiplicativity; the middle factor $\varphi(g)$ is the identity.
   - *Why needed:* Without normality there is no quotient group to be the source of $f$.

2. **Define $f$ and prove it is well-defined.** Set $f(g\ker\varphi) = \varphi(g)$. Show that if $g\ker\varphi = g'\ker\varphi$ then $\varphi(g) = \varphi(g')$.
   - *Hint:* Equal cosets mean $g^{-1}g' \in \ker\varphi$, so $\varphi(g^{-1}g') = e_H$; expand and rearrange to $\varphi(g) = \varphi(g')$.
   - *Why needed:* Until this is checked, $f$ is not a function and nothing below makes sense.

3. **$f$ is a homomorphism.** Show $f$ sends a product of cosets to the product of values.
   - *Hint:* $f((g\ker\varphi)(g'\ker\varphi)) = f(gg'\ker\varphi) = \varphi(gg')$, and now use that $\varphi$ is a homomorphism.
   - *Why needed:* An isomorphism must preserve the operation.

4. **$f$ is surjective.** Show every element of $\operatorname{im}\varphi$ is hit.
   - *Hint:* An element of $\operatorname{im}\varphi$ is $\varphi(g)$ for some $g$, and that is $f(g\ker\varphi)$ by definition. (Surjectivity is automatic once the codomain is $\operatorname{im}\varphi$.)
   - *Why needed:* An isomorphism must be onto its stated codomain.

5. **$f$ is injective.** Show $f(g\ker\varphi) = f(g'\ker\varphi)$ forces $g\ker\varphi = g'\ker\varphi$.
   - *Hint:* The hypothesis says $\varphi(g) = \varphi(g')$; run the well-definedness argument in reverse to get $g^{-1}g' \in \ker\varphi$, hence equal cosets.
   - *Why needed:* A well-defined surjective homomorphism becomes an isomorphism exactly when it is injective.

# Lemma Decomposition

<details>
<summary><strong>Lemma 1: The kernel of a homomorphism is a normal subgroup</strong></summary>

**Statement:** For any homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$, the set $\ker\varphi$ is a subgroup of $G$, and it is normal: $x^{-1}gx \in \ker\varphi$ for all $g \in \ker\varphi$, $x \in G$.

**Hint:** Use the subgroup criterion ($\varphi(gh^{-1}) = e_H$) for the subgroup part; for normality, compute $\varphi(x^{-1}gx)$ and watch the $\varphi(g)$ in the middle become the identity.

**Why needed:** It guarantees $G/\ker\varphi$ is a group, so that the source of $f$ exists. (This lemma is independently the proof that "every kernel is normal".)

<details>
<summary>Full proof</summary>

First, $\ker\varphi$ is non-empty: $\varphi(e_G) = e_H$, so $e_G \in \ker\varphi$. For the subgroup criterion, take $g, h \in \ker\varphi$. Then
$$\varphi(gh^{-1}) = \varphi(g)\,\varphi(h)^{-1} = e_H \cdot e_H^{-1} = e_H,$$
so $gh^{-1} \in \ker\varphi$. Hence $\ker\varphi$ is a subgroup.

For normality, take $g \in \ker\varphi$ and $x \in G$. Then
$$\varphi(x^{-1}gx) = \varphi(x^{-1})\,\varphi(g)\,\varphi(x) = \varphi(x^{-1})\,e_H\,\varphi(x) = \varphi(x^{-1})\,\varphi(x) = \varphi(x^{-1}x) = \varphi(e_G) = e_H.$$
So $x^{-1}gx \in \ker\varphi$, and $\ker\varphi \trianglelefteq G$.

</details>

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Lemma 2: Coset equality criterion</strong></summary>

**Statement:** For a subgroup $N \leq G$ and elements $g, g' \in G$, the cosets are equal, $gN = g'N$, if and only if $g^{-1}g' \in N$.

**Hint:** If $gN = g'N$ then $g' \in gN$ since $g' \in g'N$; conversely if $g^{-1}g' \in N$ then $g' = g(g^{-1}g') \in gN$ and translate.

**Why needed:** This is the algebraic identity used in *both* directions in the proof — for well-definedness and for injectivity of $f$.

<details>
<summary>Full proof</summary>

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $gN = g'N$. Since $e \in N$, we have $g' = g'e \in g'N = gN$, so $g' = gn$ for some $n \in N$, whence $g^{-1}g' = n \in N$.

($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $g^{-1}g' = n \in N$, so $g' = gn$. Then $g'N = gnN = gN$, because $nN = N$ as $n \in N$ and $N$ is closed under multiplication. (Explicitly, $nN \subseteq N$ by closure, and $N = n(n^{-1}N) \subseteq nN$, so $nN = N$.)

</details>

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Lemma 3: A well-defined surjective injective homomorphism is an isomorphism</strong></summary>

**Statement:** A map $f : A \to B$ between groups that is a homomorphism and is bijective is an isomorphism; in particular its set-theoretic inverse is automatically a homomorphism.

**Hint:** For elements $b_1, b_2 \in B$ write $b_i = f(a_i)$ and apply $f^{-1}$ to $f(a_1 a_2) = b_1 b_2$.

**Why needed:** It is the final assembly step: once $f$ is shown to be a well-defined surjective injective homomorphism, this lemma upgrades it to a genuine isomorphism without further work.

<details>
<summary>Full proof</summary>

A bijective homomorphism has a set-theoretic inverse $f^{-1} : B \to A$. We check $f^{-1}$ is a homomorphism. Take $b_1, b_2 \in B$ and write $b_1 = f(a_1)$, $b_2 = f(a_2)$. Since $f$ is a homomorphism, $f(a_1 a_2) = f(a_1)f(a_2) = b_1 b_2$. Applying $f^{-1}$,
$$f^{-1}(b_1 b_2) = a_1 a_2 = f^{-1}(b_1)\,f^{-1}(b_2).$$
So $f^{-1}$ is a homomorphism, and $f$ is an isomorphism with isomorphism inverse $f^{-1}$.

</details>

</details>

# Formal Proof

<details>
<summary><strong>Complete formal proof</strong></summary>

Let $\varphi : G \to H$ be a homomorphism.

**Step 0 — the quotient exists.** By Lemma 1, $\ker\varphi$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, so the quotient group $G/\ker\varphi$ is defined, with multiplication $(g\ker\varphi)(g'\ker\varphi) = gg'\ker\varphi$.

**Step 1 — define the map.** Define
$$f : G/\ker\varphi \longrightarrow \operatorname{im}\varphi, \qquad f(g\ker\varphi) = \varphi(g).$$

**Step 2 — $f$ is well-defined.** The rule for $f$ uses a coset representative, so we must check the output is independent of the choice. Suppose $g\ker\varphi = g'\ker\varphi$. By the coset equality criterion (Lemma 2), $g^{-1}g' \in \ker\varphi$, so $\varphi(g^{-1}g') = e_H$. Then
$$e_H = \varphi(g^{-1}g') = \varphi(g)^{-1}\,\varphi(g'),$$
and multiplying on the left by $\varphi(g)$ gives $\varphi(g) = \varphi(g')$, i.e. $f(g\ker\varphi) = f(g'\ker\varphi)$. Hence $f$ is well-defined. (It lands in $\operatorname{im}\varphi$ by definition of the image.)

**Step 3 — $f$ is a homomorphism.** For cosets $g\ker\varphi$ and $g'\ker\varphi$,
$$f\big((g\ker\varphi)(g'\ker\varphi)\big) = f(gg'\ker\varphi) = \varphi(gg') = \varphi(g)\,\varphi(g') = f(g\ker\varphi)\,f(g'\ker\varphi),$$
using the definition of multiplication in the quotient and that $\varphi$ is a homomorphism.

**Step 4 — $f$ is surjective.** Let $h \in \operatorname{im}\varphi$. By definition $h = \varphi(g)$ for some $g \in G$, and then $h = f(g\ker\varphi)$. So every element of $\operatorname{im}\varphi$ is in the image of $f$.

**Step 5 — $f$ is injective.** Suppose $f(g\ker\varphi) = f(g'\ker\varphi)$, i.e. $\varphi(g) = \varphi(g')$. Then $\varphi(g^{-1}g') = \varphi(g)^{-1}\varphi(g') = e_H$, so $g^{-1}g' \in \ker\varphi$. By the coset equality criterion (Lemma 2), $g\ker\varphi = g'\ker\varphi$. Hence $f$ is injective.

**Step 6 — conclude.** The map $f$ is a bijective homomorphism, hence by Lemma 3 an isomorphism. Therefore
$$G/\ker\varphi \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}\varphi. \qquad \blacksquare$$

</details>

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**The exponential map and the circle.** Consider the homomorphism $\varphi : (\mathbb{R}, +) \to (\mathbb{C}^\times, \times)$ given by $t \mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$. Its image is the unit circle group, and its kernel is $\mathbb{Z}$, so the first isomorphism theorem gives $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} \cong S^1$. This is non-obvious as an *application* of the theorem because the input — the real line with addition — looks nothing like a quotient problem; the property $B$ here is "carries an additive map to a multiplicative group", and recognising that the winding number $t \mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$ is multiplicative is the whole step. The complex version $\varphi(z) = e^z$ gives the closely related $\mathbb{C}/2\pi i\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{C}^\times$.

**Determinant and the special linear group.** The determinant $\det : \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}^\times$ is a homomorphism because $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$, it is surjective (diagonal matrices realise every nonzero value), and its kernel is $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$. The first isomorphism theorem instantly gives $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^\times$. The application is non-obvious because the matrix group is enormous and one would not expect its quotient to be a one-dimensional object; the multiplicativity of $\det$ is the disguised source.

**The abelianisation of a group.** For any group $G$, the commutator subgroup $[G,G]$ is normal, and the quotient $G/[G,G]$ is abelian. If $\varphi : G \to A$ is *any* homomorphism into an abelian group, then $[G,G] \subseteq \ker\varphi$ (commutators die in an abelian target), and the first isomorphism theorem shows $\varphi$ factors through $G/[G,G]$. The non-obvious recognition is that "$A$ is abelian" is a source property forcing the commutator subgroup into the kernel — so the abelianisation is the universal abelian quotient, and every homomorphism to an abelian group is secretly a homomorphism out of it.

**Evaluation of polynomials.** Fix a real number $a$ and consider the additive group $(\mathbb{R}[x], +)$ of polynomials. Evaluation $\operatorname{ev}_a : \mathbb{R}[x] \to \mathbb{R}$, $p \mapsto p(a)$, is an additive homomorphism, surjective (constants hit everything), with kernel the polynomials vanishing at $a$. The theorem gives $\mathbb{R}[x]/\ker(\operatorname{ev}_a) \cong \mathbb{R}$. This is a warm-up to the ring-theoretic version, where evaluation is a ring homomorphism and the same argument identifies a quotient ring; recognising the *additive group* structure first shows the isomorphism theorems are not specific to multiplication.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem|Second Isomorphism Theorem]]** — a direct corollary. Applying the first isomorphism theorem to the composite homomorphism $H \hookrightarrow G \twoheadrightarrow G/K$ produces $H/(H\cap K) \cong HK/K$ in a single line; the kernel of the composite is $H \cap K$ and its image is $HK/K$.

- **[[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem|Third Isomorphism Theorem]]** — also a direct corollary. Applying the first isomorphism theorem to the homomorphism $G/K \to G/L$, $gK \mapsto gL$, produces $(G/K)/(L/K) \cong G/L$; the kernel is $L/K$ and the map is surjective.

- **[[Thm - Correspondence Theorem|Correspondence Theorem]]** — a companion result. Where the first isomorphism theorem identifies the quotient *group*, the correspondence theorem describes the quotient's subgroup *lattice*; together they say everything about $G/N$ in terms of $G$.

- **[[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's Theorem]]** — supplies the counting. The first isomorphism theorem gives $|\operatorname{im}\varphi| = |G|/|\ker\varphi|$, and Lagrange then forces $|\operatorname{im}\varphi|$ to divide both $|G|$ and $|H|$; this is the source of all divisibility obstructions on homomorphisms.

- **Isomorphism theorems for rings, modules, and vector spaces** — the same theorem in other categories. Replace "group" by "ring", "normal subgroup" by "ideal", and "homomorphism" by "ring homomorphism", and the statement and proof transfer verbatim; likewise for modules with "submodule". The group case is the prototype.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Quotient Ring and the Evaluation Map *(from Ring Theory)*
> The first isomorphism theorem for rings says $R/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$ for a ring homomorphism $\varphi$. Combined with evaluation maps it identifies quotient rings such as $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$. See [[Def - Quotient Ring]].

> [!tip] Rank–Nullity Theorem *(from Linear Algebra)*
> For a linear map $T : V \to W$, the first isomorphism theorem gives $V/\ker T \cong \operatorname{im} T$; taking dimensions yields $\dim V = \dim\ker T + \dim\operatorname{im} T$, the rank–nullity theorem. See [[Thm - Rank-Nullity]].
