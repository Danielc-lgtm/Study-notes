# Problem Statement

Let $G$ be a finite group and let $g \in G$.

(a) Show that $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ divides $|G|$.

(b) Deduce that $g^{|G|} = e$.

(c) Deduce **Fermat's little theorem**: if $p$ is a prime and $a$ is an integer not divisible by $p$, then
$$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p.$$

**Recall:**

The **order** of a finite group $G$, written $|G|$, is its number of elements. The **order** of an element $g \in G$, written $\operatorname{ord}(g)$, is the least positive integer $n$ with $g^n = e$. The **cyclic subgroup generated by $g$** is $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \dots\}$; it consists of exactly $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ distinct elements. See [[Def - Order of a Group and of an Element]] and [[Def - Subgroup]].

The theorem the problem hinges on is Lagrange's:

![[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem#Statement]]

For part (c), recall the group of **units modulo $p$**: the set $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{1, 2, \dots, p-1\}$ of nonzero residues, with the operation of multiplication modulo $p$. This is a group — every nonzero residue mod a prime has a multiplicative inverse — and it has $p - 1$ elements.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** Parts (a) and (b) are *counting problems*: the input is the order of a group, and the parent page's [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] prescribes [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] as the instrument whenever the order of a group is the given datum. Part (c) is a *bridge problem*: a statement of number theory that becomes a statement of group theory once you recognize the right group, after which (b) finishes it instantly.

**Assumption pattern.** The assumption in (a) and (b) is minimal — just "$G$ finite, $g \in G$" — which is exactly what makes the result so reusable: it asks for almost nothing. The hidden structure is that the element $g$ generates a subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ whose order *is* $\operatorname{ord}(g)$; once that subgroup is in view, Lagrange supplies the divisibility. In (c) the assumption "$a$ is coprime to $p$" is what guarantees $a$ corresponds to an element of the *group* of units (a non-coprime residue would not be invertible).

**Theorem routing.** For (a): the element $g$ generates the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ with $|\langle g \rangle| = \operatorname{ord}(g)$, and [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] applied to $H = \langle g \rangle$ gives $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid |G|$. For (b): write $|G| = \operatorname{ord}(g) \cdot k$ and compute $g^{|G|} = (g^{\operatorname{ord}(g)})^k = e$. For (c): take $G = (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$, which has order $p - 1$; the residue of $a$ is an element of this group; apply (b).

**Key decision point.** Part (a) hides one subtlety, which is *why* $|\langle g \rangle| = \operatorname{ord}(g)$ — the powers $e, g, \dots, g^{n-1}$ must be shown distinct, and this uses the minimality of $\operatorname{ord}(g)$. Part (c)'s decision point is purely one of recognition: spotting that "integers mod $p$ under multiplication, coprime ones only" is a finite group and that $a^{p-1}$ is just $a$ raised to the group's order.

---

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Generate a subgroup from an element you possess** — the element $g$ produces the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$, whose order equals $\operatorname{ord}(g)$. This is the operation that gives Lagrange a subgroup to constrain.

2. **Read off divisibility from Lagrange** — [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] applied to $\langle g \rangle$ converts "$\langle g \rangle \leq G$" into "$\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid |G|$".

3. **Build a homomorphism / identify a standard group** — in part (c), recognizing $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ as a finite group of order $p - 1$ is the move that imports the number-theoretic statement into the group-theoretic framework.

# Solution

**Step 1 (part a): $\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup of order $\operatorname{ord}(g)$, so $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid |G|$.**

The powers of $g$ form a subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ with exactly $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ elements. By Lagrange's theorem, the order of this subgroup divides $|G|$; hence $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid |G|$.

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

Let $n = \operatorname{ord}(g)$ and set $H = \{e, g, g^2, \dots, g^{n-1}\}$.

*$H$ is a subgroup.* It is non-empty. For closure under products and inverses, apply the subgroup criterion: $g^r (g^s)^{-1} = g^{r-s}$, and reducing the exponent $r - s$ modulo $n$ and using $g^n = e$ rewrites this as one of $e, g, \dots, g^{n-1}$, so it lies in $H$.

*$H$ has exactly $n$ elements.* The listed powers are distinct: if $g^i = g^j$ with $0 \le j \le i \le n - 1$, then $g^{i-j} = e$ with $0 \le i - j < n$, and the minimality of $n$ as the least positive integer with $g^n = e$ forces $i - j = 0$. So $|H| = n = \operatorname{ord}(g)$.

By [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]], $|H|$ divides $|G|$. Therefore $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid |G|$.

</details>

**Step 2 (part b): $g^{|G|} = e$.**

Writing $|G| = \operatorname{ord}(g) \cdot k$ and using $g^{\operatorname{ord}(g)} = e$ gives $g^{|G|} = e$.

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

By part (a), $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ divides $|G|$, so $|G| = \operatorname{ord}(g) \cdot k$ for some positive integer $k$. Then
$$g^{|G|} = g^{\operatorname{ord}(g) \cdot k} = \bigl(g^{\operatorname{ord}(g)}\bigr)^{k} = e^{k} = e,$$
where the third equality uses the defining property $g^{\operatorname{ord}(g)} = e$ of the order.

</details>

**Step 3 (part c): apply (b) to the group of units modulo $p$.**

The nonzero residues modulo a prime $p$ form a group $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ of order $p - 1$. The residue of $a$ is an element of this group, so part (b) gives $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$.

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

Let $G = (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{1, 2, \dots, p-1\}$ with multiplication modulo $p$.

*$G$ is a group.* Multiplication mod $p$ is associative and has identity $1$. It is closed on $G$: if $p \nmid x$ and $p \nmid y$ then $p \nmid xy$, since $p$ is prime — so a product of nonzero residues is a nonzero residue. Each element has an inverse: for $x$ with $p \nmid x$, $\gcd(x, p) = 1$, so Bézout's identity gives integers $u, v$ with $ux + vp = 1$, whence $u x \equiv 1 \pmod p$ and the residue of $u$ inverts $x$.

*The order of $G$.* The set $\{1, 2, \dots, p-1\}$ has exactly $p - 1$ elements, so $|G| = p - 1$.

*Apply (b).* Since $a$ is an integer not divisible by $p$, its residue $\bar a$ lies in $G$. Part (b), applied to the element $\bar a$ of the finite group $G$, gives $\bar a^{\,|G|} = \bar 1$, that is,
$$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p. \qquad\blacksquare$$

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Complete formal solution</strong></summary>

**(a)** Let $n = \operatorname{ord}(g)$ and $H = \{e, g, \dots, g^{n-1}\}$. The set $H$ is non-empty, and for any $g^r, g^s \in H$ the element $g^r(g^s)^{-1} = g^{r-s}$ equals $g^k$ where $k \equiv r - s \pmod n$ and $0 \le k < n$ (using $g^n = e$), so $g^{r-s} \in H$; by the subgroup criterion $H \leq G$. The $n$ listed powers are distinct: $g^i = g^j$ with $i \ge j$ gives $g^{i-j} = e$ with $0 \le i - j < n$, so $i = j$ by minimality of $n$. Hence $|H| = n$. By Lagrange's theorem $|H|$ divides $|G|$, i.e. $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid |G|$.

**(b)** By (a), $|G| = \operatorname{ord}(g)\,k$ for some integer $k \ge 1$. Then $g^{|G|} = (g^{\operatorname{ord}(g)})^k = e^k = e$.

**(c)** The set $G = (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times = \{1, \dots, p-1\}$ with multiplication mod $p$ is a group: multiplication is associative with identity $1$; it is closed because $p$ prime and $p \nmid x, p \nmid y$ imply $p \nmid xy$; and each $x$ is invertible because $\gcd(x,p) = 1$ yields, via Bézout, a residue $u$ with $ux \equiv 1$. Its order is $|G| = p - 1$. Since $p \nmid a$, the residue $\bar a$ lies in $G$, and (b) gives $\bar a^{\,p-1} = \bar 1$, i.e. $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

</details>

# Key Takeaways

**Lagrange applied to a cyclic subgroup is the universal divisibility engine.** Parts (a) and (b) isolate the single most-used corollary of [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]]: in a finite group, the order of any element divides the order of the group, and consequently raising any element to the group's order returns the identity. The reason this is so heavily reused is that its hypothesis is almost vacuous — it asks only for a finite group and an element — so it applies in essentially every finite-group problem. The mechanism worth internalizing is the two-step bridge: an element $g$ is not a subgroup, but it *generates* one, $\langle g \rangle$, whose order equals $\operatorname{ord}(g)$; Lagrange then constrains that subgroup. Any time you have an element and want a divisibility fact, run this bridge. It is also the engine of [[Ex - Groups of prime order are cyclic]], where the divisibility collapses to a single option.

**Number-theoretic congruences are group statements wearing a disguise — learn to undress them.** Part (c) is the template for a recurring move: a statement that appears to be about integers and remainders ("$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$") is really a statement about a finite group ("$g^{|G|} = e$ in the group of units"). The trigger is the presence of modular arithmetic together with multiplication; the move is to ask "what is the group, and what is its order?". Here the group is $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ of order $p - 1$, and Fermat's little theorem is *nothing but* part (b) for that group. The same recognition gives Euler's theorem $a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$ — take instead the group of units $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^\times$, which has order $\varphi(n)$ — and it gives results about orders of matrices over finite fields by viewing $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{F}_q)$ as a finite group. Whenever an exponent in a congruence equals the size of some natural group of units, that congruence is a corollary of Lagrange.

**The coprimality hypothesis is exactly the membership ticket for the group of units.** It is worth noticing *why* part (c) demands $p \nmid a$: a residue divisible by $p$ is the zero residue, which is not invertible and therefore not an element of the *group* $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^\times$ at all. The general lesson is that when you import a number-theoretic problem into group theory, the "coprime" or "invertible" hypotheses in the problem are precisely the conditions that place the objects inside the group rather than merely inside the ambient ring. If a hypothesis like this is missing, the statement genuinely fails — $0^{p-1} \not\equiv 1$ — so the hypothesis is not decoration but the precondition that makes the group-theoretic machine applicable.
