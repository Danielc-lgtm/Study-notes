# Notation

An abelian group is a [[Def - Group|group]] $(G, \cdot, e)$ whose operation is commutative. When a group is known to be abelian the operation is very often written **additively**: the operation is $+$, the identity is $0$, the inverse of $g$ is $-g$, and the $n$-fold sum is $ng$ rather than $g^n$. This additive convention is itself a signal — seeing $+$ for a group operation conventionally announces "this group is abelian". See [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]] for the full registry.

---

# Axiom Motivation

We already have the [[Def - Group|group]] axioms — associativity, identity, inverses — which capture reversible composition. The one feature deliberately left *out* of those axioms is **commutativity**: in a general group $a \cdot b$ and $b \cdot a$ need not agree. Putting on socks then shoes is not the same as shoes then socks; reflecting a triangle then rotating it is not the same as rotating then reflecting. So order of operations genuinely matters in general, and the group axioms are careful not to assume otherwise.

An abelian group is what you get when you *do* add that one extra hypothesis. The motivating question is therefore not "what is the definition" — the definition is forced, it is the single equation $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ — but rather "**why is this one equation worth a name and a whole theory**". The answer is that commutativity is, by a wide margin, the most consequential thing you can add to the group axioms, and the desiderata are about what it buys you.

First desideratum: **the order in which you multiply a list of elements should not matter**. In a general group, associativity already tells you the *bracketing* of $g_1 g_2 \cdots g_n$ is irrelevant, but the *sequence* still matters. Commutativity removes the remaining freedom: in an abelian group you may permute the factors of any product arbitrarily. This is exactly the property that makes arithmetic in $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$ feel effortless — you sum a finite set of numbers without ever asking in which order — and it is the property an abelian group is designed to abstract.

Second desideratum, and the structurally decisive one: **every subgroup should be normal**. Recall ([[Def - Normal Subgroup]]) that a subgroup $N \leq G$ is normal when $gNg^{-1} = N$ for all $g$, and that normality is precisely the condition allowing the [[Def - Quotient Group|quotient]] $G/N$ to be formed. In a general group some subgroups are normal and some are not, and checking normality is real work. But if $G$ is abelian, then for any subgroup $N$ and any $g \in G$ and $n \in N$ we have $gng^{-1} = gg^{-1}n = n$, so conjugation does *nothing* and $gNg^{-1} = N$ automatically. So in an abelian group **every subgroup is normal**, and consequently every subgroup can be quotiented by. This is why abelian groups have an abundance of quotients while non-abelian groups can be quotient-poor, and it is the technical reason the theory of abelian groups is so much more complete. To see what breaks without commutativity: in the non-abelian $S_3$, the subgroup $\langle (1\,2)\rangle$ generated by a transposition is *not* normal, so you cannot form the quotient of $S_3$ by it — a restriction that simply never arises for abelian groups.

Third desideratum: **the class should be classifiable**. One wants, ideally, a complete list of all groups of a given kind. For general finite groups this is hopeless — the number and variety explode. But for *finite abelian* groups it is completely solved: the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups says every one is a direct product of cyclic groups, $C_{n_1} \times C_{n_2} \times \cdots \times C_{n_k}$, and the multiset of factors is an invariant. Commutativity is exactly the hypothesis that tames the classification problem (this theorem belongs to [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7]]). Demanding commutativity is the price of a complete classification.

Now, should we *weaken* the hypothesis? A natural near-variant is to demand only that every subgroup be normal, without full commutativity. This is strictly weaker: the quaternion group $Q_8$ is **non-abelian**, yet every one of its subgroups is normal. So "all subgroups normal" does not recover "abelian"; commutativity is genuinely stronger, and it is commutativity, not the weaker property, that delivers the classification and the permutation-of-factors property. Should we *strengthen* it? There is little room — commutativity is already a single universally quantified equation. One could add finiteness, or being finitely generated, to get the sharpest classification theorems, but those are orthogonal hypotheses, not strengthenings of commutativity itself.

So the definition is the bare equation $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$, and it earns its name because that one equation simultaneously makes products order-independent, makes every subgroup normal and hence quotientable, and makes the finite case completely classifiable.

# The Definition

A [[Def - Group|group]] $(G, \cdot, e)$ is **abelian** (or **commutative**) if its operation is commutative:

$$\text{for all } a, b \in G, \qquad a \cdot b = b \cdot a.$$

A group failing this — for which there exist $a, b$ with $a \cdot b \neq b \cdot a$ — is called **non-abelian**. The term honours Niels Henrik Abel.

---

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

An abelian group is precisely a **$\mathbb{Z}$-module**. A module over a ring $R$ is an abelian group on which $R$ acts by scalar multiplication, compatibly with the ring structure; when $R = \mathbb{Z}$, the scalar action is forced — $n \cdot g$ must be the $n$-fold sum $g + g + \cdots + g$ — so a $\mathbb{Z}$-module carries no information beyond its underlying abelian group, and conversely every abelian group is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module in exactly one way. This identification is not a curiosity: it means the entire theory of abelian groups is the theory of modules over $\mathbb{Z}$, and the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups is the special case $R = \mathbb{Z}$ of the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain. From the other direction, an abelian group is the **additive skeleton of a ring**: every ring is, by definition, an abelian group under its addition, with a multiplication layered on top. The category $\mathbf{Ab}$ of abelian groups is the prototype of an *abelian category*, the setting in which homological algebra is done.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ and the other number groups.** The integers, rationals, reals, and complex numbers under addition are all abelian: $a + b = b + a$ is the familiar commutative law of arithmetic. So are $\mathbb{Q}^\times$, $\mathbb{R}^\times$, $\mathbb{C}^\times$ — the nonzero elements under multiplication. These are the prototypes; the additive notation used for them is the standard signal of commutativity.

**Is an instance: every cyclic group $C_n$.** A cyclic group is generated by a single element $g$, so every element is a power $g^k$, and $g^a \cdot g^b = g^{a+b} = g^b \cdot g^a$ — powers of one element always commute. Hence **every cyclic group is abelian**. The converse fails (the Klein four-group below is abelian but not cyclic), so "cyclic" is strictly stronger than "abelian".

**Is an instance: the Klein four-group $V = C_2 \times C_2$.** The group of order $4$ in which every non-identity element has order $2$. It is abelian — being a direct product of the abelian $C_2$ with itself — and it is the smallest **non-cyclic** abelian group, so it calibrates the gap between "cyclic" and "abelian".

**Is an instance: any direct product of abelian groups.** If $A$ and $B$ are abelian then $A \times B$ is abelian, since multiplication is componentwise and each component commutes. This probes a different aspect: commutativity is preserved by the product construction, which is what makes the classification "every finite abelian group is a product of cyclic groups" a statement entirely within the abelian world.

**Is NOT an instance: the symmetric group $S_3$.** Take the transposition $(1\,2)$ and the $3$-cycle $(1\,2\,3)$. Composing them in the two orders gives $(1\,2)(1\,2\,3) = (1\,3)$ but $(1\,2\,3)(1\,2) = (2\,3)$, and $(1\,3) \neq (2\,3)$. So $S_3$ is non-abelian. Moreover $S_3$, of order $6$, is the **smallest non-abelian group**: every group of order $1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ is abelian (orders $1,2,3,5$ are prime or trivial hence cyclic; order $4$ is $C_4$ or $V$, both abelian). This non-example probes the definition by exhibiting the *minimal* witness to its failure.

**Is NOT an instance: $\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ and $D_{2n}$ for $n \geq 3$.** Matrix multiplication does not commute. For example,

$$\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix},$$

and these are unequal; likewise in the dihedral group a rotation and a reflection fail to commute. These show non-commutativity is the *generic* situation: groups of geometric symmetry are typically non-abelian, which is exactly why the group axioms refuse to assume commutativity.

**Is NOT an instance: the quaternion group $Q_8$.** Here $ij = k$ but $ji = -k$, so $Q_8$ is non-abelian. The instructive point is that **every subgroup of $Q_8$ is nonetheless normal**. So $Q_8$ proves the converse of the corollary below is false: "all subgroups normal" does not imply "abelian". Such groups are called *Hamiltonian*.

**Corollary (every subgroup of an abelian group is normal).** If $G$ is abelian and $N \leq G$, then for any $g \in G$, $n \in N$ we have $g n g^{-1} = g g^{-1} n = n \in N$, so $gNg^{-1} = N$ and $N \trianglelefteq G$ (see [[Def - Normal Subgroup]]). Consequently the [[Def - Quotient Group|quotient]] $G/N$ exists for *every* subgroup $N$. *Calibration check:* if you see why conjugation is trivial in an abelian group, you have understood that normality is a statement about conjugation.

**Corollary (subgroups and quotients of abelian groups are abelian).** A subgroup $H \leq G$ inherits the relation $ab = ba$ from $G$; and in a quotient $G/N$, $(aN)(bN) = abN = baN = (bN)(aN)$. So the abelian property passes both downward to subgroups and across to quotients. *Calibration check:* this closure is why, in proving the structure theorem, one never leaves the abelian category.

**Corollary (in an abelian group, $(ab)^n = a^n b^n$).** The "freshman's dream" exponent law, false in general groups, holds when $G$ is abelian, because the factors of $abab\cdots ab$ may be freely rearranged into $aa\cdots a\, bb\cdots b$. This is the cleanest single test: the identity $(ab)^2 = a^2 b^2$ holds for all $a,b$ **if and only if** $G$ is abelian.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Module and Vector Space *(from Linear Algebra and Ring Theory)*
> An abelian group is exactly a [[Def - Group|group]] flat enough to carry a scalar action: a module is an abelian group with a compatible ring action, and a vector space is a module over a field. The whole of linear algebra is built on the abelian group axiom for vector addition.

> [!tip] The Additive Group of a Ring *(from Ring Theory)*
> A ring is an abelian group under addition with a second, multiplicative operation distributing over it. Once you have abelian groups, the additive half of every ring, integral domain, and field is already in place; ring theory adds only the multiplication.
