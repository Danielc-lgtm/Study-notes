# Notation

For a [[Def - Group|group]] $(G, \cdot, e)$ and a subset $H \subseteq G$, we write $H \leq G$ to mean "$H$ is a subgroup of $G$", and $H < G$ when additionally $H \neq G$ (a **proper** subgroup). The identity is $e$ (or $1_G$), the inverse of $h$ is $h^{-1}$. The two extreme subgroups are the **trivial subgroup** $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself. The subgroup generated by an element $g$ is $\langle g \rangle$, and by a subset $S$ is $\langle S \rangle$. See [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]] for the full registry.

# Axiom Motivation

We have a [[Def - Group|group]] $G$, and we want to name its **substructures** — subsets of $G$ that are themselves groups in their own right, using the *same* operation. The reason to want this is that groups are large and intricate, and the first handle on any group is the collection of smaller groups sitting inside it: the lattice of subgroups is the first invariant one computes, and subgroups are what [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]], [[Def - Coset|cosets]], and [[Def - Quotient Group|quotients]] are all built from.

So the desideratum is sharp: $H \subseteq G$ should be a **subgroup** exactly when $(H, \cdot|_H, e)$ — the set $H$ with the group operation of $G$ restricted to $H$ — is itself a group. The whole question is which subsets pass this test, and the definition is just an unpacking of "the restricted operation makes $H$ a group".

Walk through the group axioms ([[Def - Group]]) one at a time and ask what each demands of $H$.

The first thing that can go wrong is that the operation might *leave* $H$. The group multiplication is a function $\cdot : G \times G \to G$; restricting it to $H \times H$ gives a function into $G$, but for it to be an operation *on* $H$ we need its outputs to land back in $H$. So we must demand **closure under products**: $a, b \in H \implies a \cdot b \in H$. Drop this and the restricted "operation" is not even a function $H \times H \to H$, and $H$ cannot be a group for the trivial reason that there is no operation to speak of. Concretely, the subset $\{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$ of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ is closed under addition, but the subset $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ is not, since $1 + 1 = 2 \notin \{-1,0,1\}$.

Associativity needs no axiom. It is **inherited for free**: if $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$ holds for all elements of $G$, it certainly holds for all elements of the subset $H$. This is the key economy of subgroups — the hardest-looking axiom comes along automatically, because it is a universally quantified equation that survives restriction. Every "for all" identity behaves this way; this is why subobjects are cheap to specify.

Now the identity. A group must have an identity element, so $H$ needs one. We demand $e \in H$, where $e$ is the identity *of $G$*. Could $H$ have its own, different identity? A short argument says no: if $e_H \in H$ satisfies $e_H \cdot e_H = e_H$, then cancelling $e_H$ in $G$ (legal, by the cancellation corollary in [[Def - Group]]) gives $e_H = e$. So any identity for $H$ must be the identity of $G$; demanding $e \in H$ is demanding that $H$ contain that specific element. There is a second, sneaky reason this axiom matters: it rules out the empty set. The empty set vacuously satisfies "closed under products" and "closed under inverses", yet the empty set is not a group — a group must have an identity, hence at least one element. So $e \in H$ does double duty: it supplies the identity and forbids $H = \emptyset$.

Finally inverses. A group requires every element to have an inverse, so we demand **closure under inverses**: $a \in H \implies a^{-1} \in H$. As with the identity, one checks the inverse computed inside $H$ must be the inverse computed inside $G$, by uniqueness of inverses ([[Def - Group]]); so this axiom says exactly "$H$ contains the $G$-inverse of each of its elements". This is the axiom most easily *forgotten* and the one whose failure is most instructive. In an infinite group it is genuinely independent of closure under products: the subset $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ contains $0$ and is closed under addition, yet $1 \in \mathbb{N}$ while $-1 \notin \mathbb{N}$, so it is **not** a subgroup. In a *finite* group this cannot happen — closure under products alone forces closure under inverses, because the powers $a, a^2, a^3, \ldots$ of an element must eventually repeat, which manufactures the inverse — but the definition must handle infinite groups too, so the inverse condition stays.

That gives the three-part definition: contain $e$, be closed under products, be closed under inverses. But now comes a genuine refinement, the **subgroup criterion**. Checking three conditions is wasteful, and there is a single condition equivalent to all three: $H$ is non-empty and for all $h_1, h_2 \in H$, the element $h_1 h_2^{-1}$ lies in $H$. Why does one condition suffice? The expression $h_1 h_2^{-1}$ is engineered to *generate* all three demands. Taking $h_1 = h_2 = h$ for some element $h$ (which exists since $H \neq \emptyset$) gives $h h^{-1} = e \in H$, so the identity is recovered. Taking $h_1 = e$ and $h_2 = h$ gives $e h^{-1} = h^{-1} \in H$, so closure under inverses is recovered. And with inverses in hand, taking $h_1 = a$ and $h_2 = b^{-1}$ gives $a (b^{-1})^{-1} = ab \in H$, so closure under products is recovered. The single combined operation "left element times inverse of right element" is exactly the minimal expression from which identity, inverse, and product all flow. This is the form to use in practice, and it is the lemma proved in the source lecture notes. The non-emptiness clause is not optional: without it the empty set would satisfy the criterion vacuously, and we have already seen the empty set is not a group.

In summary: the definition demands closure under products (so the operation stays on $H$), $e \in H$ (so $H$ has an identity and is non-empty), and closure under inverses (so $H$ has inverses); associativity is inherited gratis; and the three conditions compress into the single subgroup criterion $h_1 h_2^{-1} \in H$ on a non-empty set.

# The Definition

Let $(G, \cdot, e)$ be a [[Def - Group|group]]. A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** of $G$, written $H \leq G$, if:

1. $e \in H$;
2. for all $a, b \in H$, $\quad a \cdot b \in H$ (closure under the operation);
3. for all $a \in H$, $\quad a^{-1} \in H$ (closure under inverses).

Equivalently — and this is condition (3) of the source's definition — $(H, \cdot|_{H \times H}, e)$ is itself a group under the restricted operation. Conditions 1–3 are exactly what is needed for the restricted operation to make $H$ a group, since associativity is inherited from $G$ automatically.

**Subgroup criterion (one-step test).** A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a subgroup if and only if $H$ is **non-empty** and

$$h_1, h_2 \in H \quad \implies \quad h_1 h_2^{-1} \in H.$$

**Proof of the criterion.**

> [!note]- Forward direction: a subgroup satisfies the criterion
> If $H \leq G$ then $e \in H$ so $H \neq \emptyset$. Given $h_1, h_2 \in H$, condition (3) gives $h_2^{-1} \in H$, and then condition (2) gives $h_1 h_2^{-1} \in H$. So the criterion holds.

> [!note]- Reverse direction: the criterion implies the three axioms
> Assume $H \neq \emptyset$ and $h_1 h_2^{-1} \in H$ for all $h_1, h_2 \in H$.
>
> - **Identity.** Since $H \neq \emptyset$, pick any $h \in H$. Apply the criterion with $h_1 = h_2 = h$: then $h h^{-1} = e \in H$.
> - **Inverses.** Given $a \in H$, we now know $e \in H$. Apply the criterion with $h_1 = e$, $h_2 = a$: then $e \, a^{-1} = a^{-1} \in H$.
> - **Closure.** Given $a, b \in H$, we now know $b^{-1} \in H$. Apply the criterion with $h_1 = a$, $h_2 = b^{-1}$: then $a (b^{-1})^{-1} = a b \in H$.
>
> All three axioms hold, so $H \leq G$. Associativity is inherited from $G$.

# Categorical Definition

The clean categorical statement is that a subgroup is the data of an **injective [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphism]]** $\iota : H \hookrightarrow G$, considered up to relabelling of $H$. Recall a homomorphism is a structure-preserving map: $\iota(h_1 h_2) = \iota(h_1)\iota(h_2)$. If $H \leq G$ then the inclusion map $h \mapsto h$ is such an injective homomorphism, since the subgroup conditions say precisely that inclusion preserves products, the identity, and inverses. Conversely, given any injective homomorphism $\iota : H \to G$, its image $\iota(H)$ is a subgroup of $G$ isomorphic to $H$ (this is a special case of [[Def - Kernel and Image]]: the image of a homomorphism is always a subgroup). So "subgroup of $G$" and "isomorphism class of injective homomorphisms into $G$" are the same notion. This is why subgroups are sometimes called **subobjects** in $\mathbf{Grp}$ — they are the monomorphisms into $G$ up to isomorphism of the source, exactly as subsets are the injections into a set and subspaces are the injections into a vector space.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A subgroup is the **same idea as a linear subspace, specialized to the category of groups**. A linear subspace of a vector space is a subset closed under the operations that define a vector space — vector addition and scalar multiplication — and containing $0$; a subgroup is a subset closed under the operations that define a group and containing $e$. In ring theory the analogue is a **subring**, and the closely related **ideal**; in topology, a **subspace**. In every case the recipe is identical: a substructure is a subset on which the ambient operations restrict to give a structure of the same kind, which forces closure under each operation. The recurring economy is that universally quantified *identities* — associativity, distributivity, the vector-space axioms — are inherited for free, so a substructure is always specified purely by *closure* conditions, never by re-checking the identities.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: $n\mathbb{Z} \leq \mathbb{Z}$.** For any integer $n$, the multiples of $n$, $n\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2n, -n, 0, n, 2n, \ldots\}$, form a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$. Check the criterion: $n\mathbb{Z}$ is non-empty (it contains $0$), and if $an, bn \in n\mathbb{Z}$ then $an - bn = (a-b)n \in n\mathbb{Z}$ — recall the operation is addition, so "$h_1 h_2^{-1}$" is $h_1 - h_2$. In fact *every* subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$ has this form, so the $n\mathbb{Z}$ are the complete list of subgroups of the integers.

**Is an instance: $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{R}) \leq \mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$.** The matrices of determinant $1$ form a subgroup of the invertible matrices. By the criterion: the identity matrix has determinant $1$ so the set is non-empty, and if $\det A = \det B = 1$ then $\det(A B^{-1}) = \det A / \det B = 1$. This subgroup probes a different feature than $n\mathbb{Z}$: it is the level set of the determinant homomorphism, foreshadowing that subgroups arise as fibres of [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphisms]] — indeed $\mathrm{SL}_n$ is the kernel of $\det$, see [[Def - Kernel and Image]].

**Is an instance: the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle \leq G$.** For any element $g$ of any group, the set of all powers $\langle g \rangle = \{g^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is a subgroup. It is non-empty ($g^0 = e$), and $g^a (g^b)^{-1} = g^{a-b} \in \langle g \rangle$. This is the smallest subgroup containing $g$, and its size is the order of $g$; see [[Def - Order of a Group and of an Element]]. It shows every single element of a group already generates a subgroup.

**Is an instance: the two trivial cases.** In any group $G$, both $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself are subgroups — the smallest and largest. They satisfy the criterion immediately. A group whose *only* subgroups are these two extremes (and which is non-trivial) is closely related to being a [[Def - Simple Group|simple group]].

**Is NOT an instance: $\mathbb{N} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$.** The non-negative integers $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, \ldots\}$ inside $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$ are non-empty, contain the identity $0$, and are closed under addition — yet they form **no subgroup**, because they are not closed under inverses: $1 \in \mathbb{N}$ but $-1 \notin \mathbb{N}$. Equivalently the criterion fails: $0, 1 \in \mathbb{N}$ but $0 - 1 = -1 \notin \mathbb{N}$. This is the canonical "closed under products but not inverses" non-example, and it works precisely because $\mathbb{Z}$ is **infinite** — in a finite group, closure under products would force closure under inverses, so one *must* go to an infinite group to exhibit this failure.

**Is NOT an instance: a coset $gH$ with $g \notin H$.** Take $H \leq G$ and an element $g \notin H$; the [[Def - Coset|left coset]] $gH$ is a subset of $G$ of the same size as $H$, but it is **not** a subgroup, because it does not contain the identity: if $e \in gH$ then $e = gh$ for some $h \in H$, giving $g = h^{-1} \in H$, contradicting $g \notin H$. This non-example probes condition (1) in isolation — a coset is "$H$ shifted", and shifting destroys exactly the property of containing $e$. It is the reason cosets, despite being the building blocks of [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]], are not themselves groups.

**Corollary (intersections are subgroups).** If $H \leq G$ and $K \leq G$, then $H \cap K \leq G$. Apply the criterion: $e \in H$ and $e \in K$ so $e \in H \cap K$, hence non-empty; and if $h_1, h_2 \in H \cap K$ then $h_1 h_2^{-1}$ lies in $H$ and in $K$ separately, hence in $H \cap K$. More generally an *arbitrary* intersection of subgroups is a subgroup. *Calibration check:* this is what makes "$\langle S \rangle$, the smallest subgroup containing $S$" well-defined — it is the intersection of all subgroups containing $S$.

**Corollary (unions are usually not subgroups).** A union $H \cup K$ of two subgroups is a subgroup *only if* one contains the other. In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the union $2\mathbb{Z} \cup 3\mathbb{Z}$ contains $2$ and $3$ but not $2 + 3 = 5$, so it fails closure. This calibrates the asymmetry: intersection respects the subgroup structure, union does not, because closure is preserved by intersecting "and" conditions but broken by combining them with "or".

**Corollary (transitivity).** If $K \leq H$ and $H \leq G$ then $K \leq G$: a subgroup of a subgroup is a subgroup, since the criterion involves only the operation, which is the same throughout. This is what lets one speak of chains $K \leq H \leq G$, the starting point of [[Thm - Composition Series|composition series]]. Note this transitivity holds for plain subgroups but **fails** for [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroups]].
