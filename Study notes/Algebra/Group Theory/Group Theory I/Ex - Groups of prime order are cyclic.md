# Problem Statement

Let $p$ be a prime number and let $G$ be a group with $|G| = p$. Show that $G$ is cyclic — that is, there is an element $g \in G$ with $\langle g \rangle = G$ — and conclude that $G \cong C_p$, the cyclic group of order $p$.

**Recall:**

The **order** $|G|$ of a finite group is its number of elements, and the **order** $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ of an element $g$ is the least positive integer $n$ with $g^n = e$. The cyclic subgroup **generated by** $g$ is $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \dots\}$, and it has exactly $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ elements. A group is **cyclic** if some single element generates the whole group. See [[Def - Order of a Group and of an Element]] and [[Def - Subgroup]].

A prime $p$ has exactly two positive divisors: $1$ and $p$ itself.

The one external fact this problem needs is Lagrange's theorem and its corollary on element orders:

![[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem#Statement]]

The cyclic group $C_p$ is, up to isomorphism, the unique group generated by a single element of order $p$ — concretely $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}, +)$. Any two cyclic groups of the same order are [[Def - Isomorphism|isomorphic]], by matching generator to generator.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *counting problem*: the only datum is the integer $|G| = p$, and the goal is a structural conclusion (cyclicity). As the parent page's [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] explains, when a problem hands you nothing but the order of a group, your primary instrument is [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] — and more specifically its corollary on the orders of elements.

**Assumption pattern.** The assumption "$|G|$ is prime" is the strongest possible squeeze that Lagrange can exploit. Lagrange forces the order of every element to divide $|G|$; when $|G| = p$ is prime, "divides $p$" allows only the values $1$ and $p$. A prime order leaves the structure of a group almost no freedom, and recognizing that primality is what collapses the divisor list is the key to the whole argument.

**Theorem routing.** The route is: a non-identity element exists (because $p \geq 2$, so $G$ has more than one element) $\to$ its order divides $p$ by the corollary to [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] $\to$ since the order is not $1$, it must be $p$ $\to$ the cyclic subgroup it generates has $p$ elements $\to$ that subgroup, having the same finite size as $G$, *is* $G$.

**Key decision point.** The one move that requires a flicker of thought is the last one: from "$\langle g \rangle$ has $p$ elements" to "$\langle g \rangle = G$". A subgroup of $G$ with as many elements as $G$ must equal $G$, because a subset of a finite set with the full cardinality is the whole set. This is obvious once stated but is the step where a careless solution simply asserts the conclusion.

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Generate a subgroup from an element you possess** — from a single non-identity element $g$ you build the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$, whose size is $\operatorname{ord}(g)$. This is the operation that turns an *element* into a *subgroup* so that Lagrange has something to act on.

2. **Read off divisibility from Lagrange** — applying the corollary to [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] to $g$ gives $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid |G| = p$, which converts the prime $p$ into the two-element list of possible element orders.

# Solution

**Step 1: Produce a non-identity element.**

Since $p$ is prime, $p \geq 2$, so $G$ has at least two elements. Choose any $g \in G$ with $g \neq e$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Every group is non-empty (it contains the identity $e$), and a group of order $p \geq 2$ contains at least one element besides $e$. Pick one and call it $g$. Nothing about $g$ is special yet; the next step extracts information from it.

**Step 2: The order of $g$ is exactly $p$.**

By the corollary to Lagrange's theorem, $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ divides $|G| = p$; the divisors of $p$ are $1$ and $p$; and $\operatorname{ord}(g) \neq 1$ because $g \neq e$. Hence $\operatorname{ord}(g) = p$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The corollary to [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] states that for a finite group $G$ and any $g \in G$, the order $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ divides $|G|$. Here $|G| = p$, so $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid p$.
>
> Because $p$ is prime, its only positive divisors are $1$ and $p$. The order of an element is $1$ exactly when that element is the identity: $\operatorname{ord}(g) = 1$ means $g^1 = e$, i.e. $g = e$. Since we chose $g \neq e$, the value $1$ is excluded, leaving $\operatorname{ord}(g) = p$.

**Step 3: $g$ generates $G$, so $G$ is cyclic.**

The cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ has $\operatorname{ord}(g) = p$ elements. It is a subset of $G$, which also has $p$ elements, so $\langle g \rangle = G$. Thus $g$ generates $G$ and $G$ is cyclic.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By definition, $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \dots, g^{p-1}\}$ is the cyclic subgroup generated by $g$, and it has exactly $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ distinct elements — here $p$ of them.
>
> Now $\langle g \rangle \subseteq G$ and $|\langle g \rangle| = p = |G|$. A subset of a finite set that has the same number of elements as the whole set must equal the whole set. Therefore $\langle g \rangle = G$, which is the definition of $G$ being cyclic with generator $g$.

**Step 4: Identify $G$ as $C_p$.**

A cyclic group of order $p$ is, up to isomorphism, the cyclic group $C_p$. Hence $G \cong C_p$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Two cyclic groups of the same finite order are [[Def - Isomorphism|isomorphic]]: if $\langle g \rangle$ has order $n$ and $\langle h \rangle$ has order $n$, the map $g^k \mapsto h^k$ is a well-defined bijective [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphism]] (well-defined because $g^k = g^{k'}$ if and only if $k \equiv k' \pmod n$ if and only if $h^k = h^{k'}$). The standard model of the cyclic group of order $p$ is $C_p \cong (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}, +)$, generated by the residue $1$. Since our $G$ is cyclic of order $p$, it is isomorphic to this model: $G \cong C_p$.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Let $p$ be prime and $|G| = p$.
>
> Since $p \geq 2$, the group $G$ contains an element $g \neq e$.
>
> By the corollary to Lagrange's theorem (for a finite group $G$ and $g \in G$, $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ divides $|G|$), we have $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid p$. As $p$ is prime, $\operatorname{ord}(g) \in \{1, p\}$. Since $g \neq e$, $\operatorname{ord}(g) \neq 1$, so $\operatorname{ord}(g) = p$.
>
> The cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ has exactly $\operatorname{ord}(g) = p$ elements. As $\langle g \rangle \subseteq G$ and $|\langle g \rangle| = p = |G|$, equality of cardinalities for finite sets gives $\langle g \rangle = G$. Hence $g$ generates $G$, and $G$ is cyclic.
>
> Finally, a cyclic group of order $p$ is isomorphic to the standard cyclic group $C_p$ via the generator-matching map, so $G \cong C_p$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**A prime order is the tightest possible cage for a finite group.** The entire argument is powered by one fact: the divisors of a prime $p$ are just $1$ and $p$. Whenever a problem gives you a group whose order is prime, the corollary to [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] instantly tells you that *every* non-identity element has order $p$ — there is literally no other option. This is the trigger to watch for: "the order of the group is prime" should immediately make you write down "so every non-trivial element generates the whole group". The same collapse-of-options reasoning settles a whole family of questions about groups of prime order in one stroke: such a group has no proper non-trivial subgroups at all (a subgroup would have order dividing $p$), it is automatically abelian (being cyclic), and it is [[Def - Simple Group|simple]]. Prime order is the most information a single integer can give you.

**To make Lagrange bite, first manufacture a subgroup — and a lone element is enough.** Lagrange's theorem is a statement about subgroups, but the problem mentions no subgroup. The reusable move, applicable far beyond this exercise, is that *every element silently carries a cyclic subgroup*: from $g$ you get $\langle g \rangle$, whose size is $\operatorname{ord}(g)$. This is how a problem phrased purely in terms of elements gets routed to a theorem phrased in terms of subgroups. Any time you know an element exists and want to invoke Lagrange, generate its cyclic subgroup first. The companion exercise [[Ex - Order of an element divides the group order]] is the same bridge isolated as its own statement, and it underlies essentially every order-counting argument in finite group theory.

**"Same size as the whole group" is a complete proof of "equals the whole group" — for finite groups.** The step from $|\langle g \rangle| = p$ to $\langle g \rangle = G$ uses nothing more than: a subset of a finite set with full cardinality is the entire set. This little observation is a recurring closing move. Whenever you have located a subgroup and can show it is as large as the ambient group, you are done — the subgroup *is* the group. The pattern generalizes: to prove an element generates $G$, it is enough to show its order equals $|G|$; to prove two finite groups of equal order are equal, exhibit an inclusion. Note the finiteness is essential — for infinite groups a proper subgroup can biject with the whole group, so this move is specific to the finite setting.
