# Notation

$G$ is a group with identity $e$. The **centre** is $Z(G) = \{z \in G : zg = gz \text{ for all } g \in G\}$, a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] of $G$ (see [[Def - Centraliser and Centre]]); its elements are exactly those commuting with everything. The **quotient** $G/Z(G)$ is the [[Def - Quotient Group|group of cosets]] $gZ(G)$ under $(gZ(G))(hZ(G)) = (gh)Z(G)$; this is a group precisely because $Z(G)$ is normal. A group is **cyclic** if it is generated by a single element, $\langle a \rangle = \{a^k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. A group is [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian]] if $xy = yx$ for all $x, y$. We write $p$ for a prime and call $G$ a [[Def - p-group|$p$-group]] when $|G| = p^n$, $n \geq 1$. The full registry is on [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7]].

# Statement

> **Theorem ($G/Z(G)$ cyclic forces $G$ abelian).** Let $G$ be any group. If the [[Def - Quotient Group|quotient]] $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then $G$ is [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian]].
>
> Equivalently — since the centre of an abelian group is the whole group — if $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic then $G/Z(G)$ is the **trivial** group. In particular $G/Z(G)$ is never a non-trivial cyclic group.

> **Corollary (groups of order $p^2$ are abelian).** If $p$ is prime and $|G| = p^2$, then $G$ is [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian]].

The corollary follows by combining the theorem with [[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre|the non-trivial-centre theorem]]: that theorem forces $|Z(G)| \in \{p, p^2\}$, and the case $|Z(G)| = p$ would make $G/Z(G)$ a group of order $p$, hence cyclic, hence (by the theorem) $G$ abelian and $Z(G) = G$ — a contradiction. So $|Z(G)| = p^2$ and $G = Z(G)$ is abelian.

# Motivation

The previous theorem, [[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre|$p$-groups have non-trivial centre]], hands you a centre that is not just $\{e\}$. But "not trivial" is a weak conclusion — the centre could still be small. The natural next question is: *how do you turn a lower bound on the centre into a statement about the whole group?* You want to know when a group is forced all the way to abelian. This theorem answers that with a remarkably clean criterion phrased not about the centre directly but about the **quotient by the centre**.

The quotient $G/Z(G)$ is itself an object of study; it is sometimes called the *central quotient* and it measures "how non-abelian $G$ is", because $G$ is abelian exactly when $G/Z(G)$ is trivial. So one wants to understand which groups can appear as $G/Z(G)$. This theorem is a sharp prohibition: a non-trivial *cyclic* group can never be the central quotient of anything. There is no group $G$ at all with $G/Z(G) \cong C_n$ for $n > 1$. That is a genuinely surprising structural fact — cyclic groups are the simplest groups, and one would naively expect them to be easy to realise as quotients, yet as central quotients they are forbidden except for the trivial one.

The reason this is the criterion worth proving is its leverage in induction. The whole strategy of §1.5 is: in a $p$-group, find a normal subgroup, quotient, induct. The non-trivial-centre theorem gives you the normal subgroup. But to *use* an inductive hypothesis about $G/Z(G)$ you need to know what kinds of quotient are possible — and the smallest non-trivial quotient, the cyclic one, is the case that this theorem rules out for free. The immediate dividend is the corollary: every group of order $p^2$ is abelian, with no case analysis. The order $p^2$ is the first composite prime power, the first place a non-abelian group could conceivably appear, and this theorem says it does not. Non-abelian behaviour does not begin until order $p^3$ (where $D_8$ and $Q_8$ live). Knowing exactly where non-commutativity switches on is structural information of the first importance.

# Sources and Targets

This records the non-obvious ways a problem arrives at the hypothesis ($G/Z(G)$ cyclic) and the non-obvious results that follow from the conclusion ($G$ abelian).

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The hypothesis is "$G/Z(G)$ is cyclic". A problem rarely states this; you must recognise it.

The first source is **the central quotient has prime order**. Property $B$ is "$|G/Z(G)| = q$ for some prime $q$". The bridge is that *every* group of prime order is cyclic — a group of order $q$ has a non-identity element, whose order divides $q$ hence equals $q$, so it generates everything. Thus prime-order central quotient $\implies$ cyclic central quotient $\implies$ (by the theorem) $G$ abelian. The implication is non-obvious because "$|G/Z(G)| = q$" looks like a bare cardinality, not a structural hypothesis; the hidden step is "prime order means cyclic". This is the source that powers the order-$p^2$ corollary.

The second source is **the index of the centre is small**, say $|G : Z(G)| \leq 3$. Property $B$ is "$|G : Z(G)| \in \{1, 2, 3\}$". The bridge is that every group of order $1$, $2$, or $3$ is cyclic (orders $1, 2, 3$ are each $1$ or prime). So a small central index forces a cyclic central quotient and hence forces $G$ abelian — a frequently used lemma in its own right ("if $Z(G)$ has index at most $3$, then $G$ is abelian, so in fact $Z(G) = G$ and the index is $1$"). The non-obviousness is that one expects "small index" to be a mild constraint, whereas here it is total.

The third source is **a group generated by one element together with central elements**. Property $B$ is "$G = \langle a, Z(G) \rangle$ for a single element $a$". The bridge is that the cosets of $Z(G)$ are then all powers of the coset $aZ(G)$, so $G/Z(G) = \langle aZ(G) \rangle$ is cyclic. The implication is non-obvious because "$G$ is generated by $a$ and a bunch of central elements" does not visibly say anything about a quotient — but quotienting out the central elements leaves only powers of $a$. This is the source's most general form, and the previous two are special cases of it.

The fourth source is **a non-abelian group, used contrapositively**. Property $B$ is "$G$ is known to be non-abelian". The contrapositive of the theorem says: if $G$ is non-abelian, then $G/Z(G)$ is *not* cyclic. So whenever you have a non-abelian group, you immediately know its central quotient is non-cyclic — in particular $|G/Z(G)| \neq 1$ and $|G/Z(G)| \neq$ any prime. The implication is non-obvious because it extracts a fact about a quotient from a fact about $G$ itself; it is the standard way to bound $|Z(G)|$ from above for non-abelian $G$.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$G$ is abelian" (equivalently, $G/Z(G)$ is trivial). Combined with one further property $D$ this becomes a sharper structural result $E$.

The first combination is **abelian plus a prime-power order routes to the classification**. The conclusion $C$ "$G$ is abelian" together with property $D$ "$|G| = p^2$" gives, via the [[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|classification of finite abelian groups]], that $G \cong C_{p^2}$ or $G \cong C_p \times C_p$ — these are the *only* two groups of order $p^2$, up to isomorphism. The combination is non-obvious because the theorem on its own only says "abelian"; it is the classification that converts "abelian of order $p^2$" into a complete and finite list. The upshot $E$: there are exactly two groups of each order $p^2$.

The second combination is **abelian forces the centre to be everything, collapsing a contradiction**. The conclusion $C$ "$G$ abelian" is logically equivalent to "$Z(G) = G$". So whenever you have *assumed* $|Z(G)| < |G|$ and then derived that $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, the theorem produces $Z(G) = G$, contradicting the assumption. The result $E$ is a proof by contradiction: $G/Z(G)$ *cannot* be a non-trivial cyclic group. This is non-obvious as a "target" because the theorem's power here is to *forbid* a configuration — it is used to close off a case in a case analysis. This is exactly how the order-$p^2$ corollary eliminates the possibility $|Z(G)| = p$.

The third combination is **abelian plus known generators gives a presentation or an isomorphism type**. The conclusion $C$ "$G$ abelian" together with property $D$ "$G$ is generated by elements of known orders" lets you write $G$ as a direct product of cyclic groups directly, since in an abelian group the subgroup generated by commuting elements of coprime orders is their direct product. The result $E$ is an explicit isomorphism type. The combination is non-obvious because commutativity is precisely the hypothesis that lets generator-and-relation data assemble into a [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] rather than a messier semidirect one.

# Why Is It True

The intuition is that a cyclic central quotient leaves *no room* for non-commutativity, and you can see exactly where the room would have to be and watch it vanish.

Think of what the centre $Z(G)$ does: its elements are the "boring" elements, the ones that commute with everything, so multiplying by a central element never causes a commutativity problem. All the potential non-commutativity of $G$ lives in the *cosets* of $Z(G)$ — in how elements from different cosets multiply. The quotient $G/Z(G)$ is the bookkeeping device that records exactly this coset-level structure, with the central "noise" quotiented away.

Now suppose $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, generated by a single coset $gZ(G)$. Cyclic means *every* coset is a power of this one: every coset has the form $g^r Z(G)$ for some integer $r$. Translate that back down to $G$. Every single element $x \in G$ lies in some coset, and that coset is $g^r Z(G)$ for some $r$, which means $x = g^r z$ for some central element $z$. So the cyclic hypothesis has a very concrete consequence: **every element of $G$ is a power of the one element $g$, times something central.**

Once you know that, commutativity is forced, and here is the picture of *why*. Take two elements $x = g^r z$ and $y = g^s w$, with $z, w$ central. To compute $xy$ you must commute things past each other. The central elements $z$ and $w$ slide past anything without resistance — that is what central means. And the two "non-central parts" are $g^r$ and $g^s$, both powers of the *same* element $g$ — and any element always commutes with its own powers, since $g^r g^s = g^{r+s} = g^s g^r$. So in the product $xy$, *every* pair of factors that needs to be swapped is either a central element (slides freely) or two powers of $g$ (commute automatically). There is simply no pair of factors anywhere in the computation that fails to commute. Hence $xy = yx$.

The deep reason, stated in one line: a cyclic central quotient means $G$ is generated by central elements together with a *single* non-central generator $g$, and one generator cannot fail to commute with itself. Non-commutativity needs *two* independent non-central directions to express itself — two generators that genuinely refuse to commute. A cyclic central quotient permits only one non-central direction, and one direction is always commutative. That is why the theorem is true, and why "cyclic" — meaning "one generator" — is exactly the right hypothesis: drop to *two* generators (central quotient $\cong C_p \times C_p$) and non-abelian groups reappear, as $D_8$ and $Q_8$ show. The boundary "cyclic versus not" is precisely the boundary "one non-central direction versus two".

# What Makes This Hard

The proof is short and the step people miss is the *translation* from the quotient back to $G$ — recognising that "$G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, generated by $gZ(G)$" means every element of $G$ literally has the form $g^r z$ with $z$ central. The common error is to conflate two different statements: "$G/Z(G)$ is cyclic" is *not* the same as "$G$ is cyclic", and assuming $G$ itself is cyclic trivialises the theorem incorrectly (a cyclic group is abelian, but $G$ here need not be cyclic — only its central quotient is). The second pitfall is forgetting to actually *use* centrality of $z$ and $w$: the whole computation $g^r z g^s w = g^r g^s z w$ hinges on $z$ and $w$ sliding past powers of $g$, and a proof that does not invoke "$z, w$ central" has a gap.

# Rederivation Scaffold

This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.

**High-level strategy:**
A generator of the cyclic group $G/Z(G)$ is a coset $gZ(G)$. Since every coset is a power of it, every element of $G$ is $g^r z$ with $z$ central. Multiply two such elements and push the central factors past the powers of $g$ (legal, they are central) and the powers of $g$ past each other (legal, powers of one element commute). The two products agree, so $G$ is abelian.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Every element has the form $g^r z$.** Let $gZ(G)$ generate $G/Z(G)$. Show every $x \in G$ equals $g^r z$ for some integer $r$ and some $z \in Z(G)$.
   - *Hint:* $x$ lies in the coset $xZ(G)$; cyclicity makes that coset $g^r Z(G)$ for some $r$; "$x \in g^r Z(G)$" means $x = g^r z$ for some $z \in Z(G)$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the normal form in which all elements will be compared.

2. **Two such elements commute.** Take $x = g^r z$ and $y = g^s w$ with $z, w \in Z(G)$, and show $xy = yx$.
   - *Hint:* In $xy = g^r z g^s w$, slide $z$ rightward past $g^s$ (central elements commute with everything) and use $g^r g^s = g^s g^r$; you get $g^r g^s z w$, which is symmetric in the pairs $(r,z)$ and $(s,w)$ up to the obvious rearrangement, hence equals $yx$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the definition of abelian, verified for arbitrary elements.

3. **Conclude, and note the equivalent form.** Since $xy = yx$ for all $x, y$, the group $G$ is abelian. Then $Z(G) = G$, so $G/Z(G)$ is trivial — a non-trivial cyclic central quotient is impossible.
   - *Hint:* The centre of an abelian group is the whole group.
   - *Why needed:* This is the statement, plus its sharper "$G/Z(G)$ trivial" reformulation.

# Lemma Decomposition

Each lemma below is independently practiceable in roughly five minutes.

<details>
<summary><strong>Lemma 1: A cyclic central quotient gives every element a normal form</strong></summary>

**Statement:** If $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic with generator coset $gZ(G)$, then every $x \in G$ can be written $x = g^r z$ for some $r \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $z \in Z(G)$.

**Hint:** Push $x$ down to its coset, use cyclicity to name that coset $g^r Z(G)$, then pull back.

**Why needed:** It is the representation of a general group element on which the commuting computation is performed.

<details>
<summary>Full proof</summary>

Each $x \in G$ determines its coset $xZ(G) \in G/Z(G)$. Since $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic generated by $gZ(G)$, every element of $G/Z(G)$ is a power $(gZ(G))^r = g^r Z(G)$ for some $r \in \mathbb{Z}$. So $xZ(G) = g^r Z(G)$ for some $r$. Two cosets are equal if and only if each element of one lies in the other; in particular $x \in g^r Z(G)$, which means $x = g^r z$ for some $z \in Z(G)$.

</details>

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Lemma 2: Powers of one element commute; central elements commute with all</strong></summary>

**Statement:** For any $g$ in any group and any integers $r, s$, $g^r g^s = g^s g^r$. For any $z \in Z(G)$ and any $y \in G$, $zy = yz$.

**Hint:** $g^r g^s = g^{r+s} = g^{s+r} = g^s g^r$; the central statement is the definition of $Z(G)$.

**Why needed:** These are the only two commuting facts the main proof uses — every swap in the computation is one of these two.

<details>
<summary>Full proof</summary>

Powers of a fixed element satisfy $g^r g^s = g^{r+s}$, and since integer addition is commutative, $g^{r+s} = g^{s+r} = g^s g^r$. For the centre: $z \in Z(G)$ means by definition $zy = yz$ for every $y \in G$.

</details>

</details>

# Formal Proof

<details>
<summary><strong>Complete formal proof</strong></summary>

**Theorem.** Let $G$ be any group. If $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, then $G$ is abelian.

*Proof.* Suppose $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, and let $gZ(G)$ be a generator, so that every element of $G/Z(G)$ equals $g^r Z(G)$ for some integer $r$.

Take any $x \in G$. Its coset $xZ(G)$ is some power of the generator, say $xZ(G) = g^r Z(G)$. Then $x \in g^r Z(G)$, so
$$x = g^r z \quad \text{for some } z \in Z(G).$$
Likewise, any other element $\bar x \in G$ can be written
$$\bar x = g^{\bar r} \bar z \quad \text{for some } \bar r \in \mathbb{Z}, \ \bar z \in Z(G).$$

Now compute the two products. Since $z$ and $\bar z$ lie in the centre, they commute with every element of $G$; and since $g^r$ and $g^{\bar r}$ are powers of the same element, they commute with each other ($g^r g^{\bar r} = g^{r + \bar r} = g^{\bar r + r} = g^{\bar r} g^r$). Therefore
$$x \bar x = g^r z \, g^{\bar r} \bar z = g^r g^{\bar r} z \bar z = g^{\bar r} g^r \bar z z = g^{\bar r} \bar z \, g^r z = \bar x x,$$
where each equality moves a central element past a power of $g$, or swaps the two powers of $g$, or recombines — all legal because central elements commute with everything and powers of $g$ commute with each other.

Hence $x \bar x = \bar x x$ for all $x, \bar x \in G$, so $G$ is abelian. $\qquad\blacksquare$

**Equivalent form.** If $G$ is abelian then every element commutes with every other, so $Z(G) = G$ and the quotient $G/Z(G)$ is the trivial group. Thus, if $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic it is trivial: no group has a non-trivial cyclic central quotient.

**Corollary.** If $p$ is prime and $|G| = p^2$, then $G$ is abelian.

*Proof.* Since $Z(G) \leq G$, [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] forces $|Z(G)| \in \{1, p, p^2\}$. By [[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre|the non-trivial-centre theorem]] applied to the $p$-group $G$, $Z(G) \neq \{e\}$, so $|Z(G)| \neq 1$; hence $|Z(G)| \in \{p, p^2\}$.

If $|Z(G)| = p^2$ then $Z(G) = G$, so $G$ is abelian and we are done.

If instead $|Z(G)| = p$, then $|G/Z(G)| = p^2 / p = p$. A group of order $p$ (prime) is cyclic: any non-identity element has order dividing $p$, hence order $p$, hence generates the whole group. So $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, and by the theorem $G$ is abelian — whence $Z(G) = G$ and $|Z(G)| = p^2$, contradicting $|Z(G)| = p$. So this case cannot occur.

Therefore $|Z(G)| = p^2$ and $G$ is abelian. $\qquad\blacksquare$

</details>

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

The aim is to find settings where the theorem applies but is not advertised — testing recognition of the *sources*.

**Counting groups: there are exactly two groups of order $p^2$.** Combine this theorem's corollary ("order $p^2$ implies abelian") with the [[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|classification of finite abelian groups]]: an abelian group of order $p^2$ is $C_{p^2}$ or $C_p \times C_p$, and these are non-isomorphic (one has an element of order $p^2$, the other does not). So the count of groups of order $p^2$ is exactly $2$ — for instance $4$ ($C_4, V$), $9$ ($C_9, C_3 \times C_3$), $25$, $49$. The application is non-obvious because the enumeration problem mentions no centre and no quotient; the hidden first move is "order $p^2$ is a prime square, so the corollary applies".

**Linear algebra: a non-scalar matrix forces a non-cyclic central quotient.** Let $G$ be a group of matrices whose centre $Z(G)$ consists of the scalar matrices in $G$. If $G$ contains a non-scalar matrix and is non-abelian, then $G/Z(G)$ cannot be cyclic — by the contrapositive of the theorem. So for any such matrix group, $|G : Z(G)| \neq 1$ and $|G:Z(G)|$ is not prime; the index of the scalars is at least $4$. The application is non-obvious because the input is geometric ("scalar versus non-scalar matrices") while the theorem is about abstract quotients; the source is the contrapositive direction.

**Number theory / abstract algebra: a group with $|G : Z(G)| = p$ cannot exist for $p$ prime.** Suppose someone claims to construct a group $G$ in which the centre has prime index $p$. The theorem refutes the claim outright: index $p$ means $|G/Z(G)| = p$, a prime, hence $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, hence $G$ is abelian, hence $Z(G) = G$ and the index is $1 \neq p$. So no group has a centre of prime index. The application is non-obvious because it is a *non-existence* result deployed against a plausible-sounding construction — the theorem is being used as an impossibility filter, the same way Lagrange filters out impossible subgroup orders.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre|$p$-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre]]** — the matched partner of this theorem. That one produces a non-trivial centre (a *lower* bound on $|Z(G)|$); this one says what a sufficiently large centre forces (if the quotient is cyclic, $G$ is abelian). Used together they pincer the order-$p^2$ case: the lower bound rules out $|Z(G)| = 1$, and this theorem rules out $|Z(G)| = p$, leaving only $|Z(G)| = p^2$. Neither alone proves "order $p^2$ implies abelian"; the conjunction does.

- **[[Thm - Subgroups of a p-Group|Subgroups of a $p$-Group]]** — both theorems are tools for inductions on the order of a $p$-group, and both rely on understanding $G/Z(G)$ or $G/\langle x \rangle$. This theorem is what tells you the *smallest* non-trivial central quotient ($C_p$) is impossible, sharpening any inductive argument that quotients by the centre.

- **[[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|Classification of Finite Abelian Groups]]** — this theorem reduces a structural problem ("is $G$ abelian?") to its conclusion; the classification then takes over and converts "abelian, of order $n$" into an explicit finite list of isomorphism types. The corollary plus the classification is the complete statement that there are exactly two groups of order $p^2$.

- **The commutator subgroup and abelianisation** — the theorem is really a statement about the commutator subgroup $[G, G]$, the subgroup generated by all $xyx^{-1}y^{-1}$. The proof shows that when $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic every commutator is trivial, i.e. $[G,G] = \{e\}$, which is the definition of abelian. The general slogan is that $G/Z(G)$ cyclic forces $[G,G]$ to collapse — the central quotient and the commutator subgroup are two complementary measures of non-commutativity, and a cyclic value of one kills the other.
