# Problem Statement

Let $m$ and $n$ be positive integers with $\gcd(m, n) = 1$.

1. Prove that $C_{mn} \cong C_m \times C_n$, where $C_k$ denotes the cyclic group of order $k$.
2. Use part 1 to decompose $C_{60}$ into a [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] of cyclic groups of **prime-power** order.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are cyclic groups, their direct product, and the order of an element.

A **cyclic group** $C_k$ is a group of $k$ elements generated by a single element: $C_k = \langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \dots, g^{k-1}\}$, with the generator $g$ having [[Def - Order of a Group and of an Element|order]] exactly $k$. Concretely it is the integers $\{0, 1, \dots, k-1\}$ under addition modulo $k$. A finite group is cyclic if and only if it contains an element whose order equals the order of the whole group — that single element then generates everything.

![[Def - Direct Product#The Definition]]

The [[Def - Order of a Group and of an Element|order]] of an element $x$ is the least positive integer $k$ with $x^k = e$; equivalently, $x^j = e$ holds precisely for the multiples $j$ of this $k$. For an integer $j$, the statement "$x^j = e$" is therefore the statement "$\operatorname{ord}(x) \mid j$".

Two integers $m, n$ are **coprime** when $\gcd(m, n) = 1$. The fact used about coprimality is the divisibility lemma: if $m \mid k$ and $n \mid k$ with $\gcd(m,n) = 1$, then $mn \mid k$. (The least common multiple is $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n) = mn/\gcd(m,n)$, which equals $mn$ exactly when $m, n$ are coprime.)

This statement is the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Cyclic Groups|Chinese remainder theorem]] in group-theoretic form — part 1 *is* a proof of that theorem, so you should treat the theorem as the thing to be established rather than a tool to be cited.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** Part 1 is an *isomorphism* problem — show two groups are the same — and part 2 is a *decomposition* problem, both from the §1.6 family in the topic page's [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7#Sources and Targets|Sources and Targets]]. Part 1 is foundational: it proves the very theorem, the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Cyclic Groups|Chinese remainder theorem for cyclic groups]], that powers every conversion between the two normal forms of a finite abelian group.

**Assumption pattern.** The hypothesis driving everything is $\gcd(m, n) = 1$. Coprimality is the assumption that makes least common multiples collapse: $\operatorname{lcm}(m, n) = mn$ holds *exactly* when $m$ and $n$ are coprime, and that collapse is what pushes the order of a generator pair all the way up to $mn$. Without coprimality the argument fails at this precise point — and rightly so, since $C_4$ is not isomorphic to $C_2 \times C_2$.

**Theorem routing.** For part 1 no prior theorem is needed; the route is the *element-order criterion for cyclicity* — a finite group is cyclic if it possesses an element whose order equals the group's order. The plan is to locate, inside $C_m \times C_n$, an element of order $mn$; since $C_m \times C_n$ has $mn$ elements, that element generates the whole group, making it cyclic of order $mn$, hence isomorphic to $C_{mn}$. For part 2 the route is to invoke part 1 repeatedly, peeling off one prime power at a time.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious move in part 1 is the choice of *which* element of $C_m \times C_n$ to test: take a generator $g$ of $C_m$ and a generator $h$ of $C_n$, and examine the pair $(g, h)$. The order of $(g,h)$ is governed by *both* coordinates at once — $(g,h)^j = (e,e)$ forces $g^j = e$ and $h^j = e$ simultaneously — so its order is the *least* $j$ that is a multiple of both $m$ and $n$, namely $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$. The coprimality hypothesis then converts that $\operatorname{lcm}$ into the full product $mn$. Recognising that the order of a pair is the lowest common multiple of the coordinate orders is the entire idea.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Combine factors across coprime orders via the Chinese remainder theorem** (operation 7). Part 1 is the *proof* that this operation is legal; part 2 then applies it three times in the splitting direction. The trigger is the appearance of a cyclic group whose order factors into coprime pieces.

2. **Test for cyclicity by exhibiting a maximal-order element** (an instance of operation 7's underlying mechanism). The structural fact deployed is that a finite group is cyclic exactly when it has an element of order equal to its size; the proof of part 1 is the construction of such an element inside a direct product.

# Solution

The strategy for part 1 is to find an element of order $mn$ inside $C_m \times C_n$; since the group has $mn$ elements, such an element generates it, forcing it to be cyclic of order $mn$. Part 2 iterates the resulting isomorphism.

**Step 1: Set up a candidate element of $C_m \times C_n$.**

Let $g$ generate $C_m$ and $h$ generate $C_n$, so $\operatorname{ord}(g) = m$ and $\operatorname{ord}(h) = n$. The element to study is the pair $(g, h) \in C_m \times C_n$.

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

By definition a cyclic group has a generator: $C_m = \langle g \rangle$ for some $g$ of [[Def - Order of a Group and of an Element|order]] exactly $m$, and likewise $C_n = \langle h \rangle$ with $\operatorname{ord}(h) = n$. The [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] $C_m \times C_n$ consists of all pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \in C_m$, $b \in C_n$, multiplied coordinatewise, and has order $|C_m| \cdot |C_n| = mn$. Its identity is $(e, e)$. We single out the pair $(g, h)$ and will compute its order.

</details>

**Step 2: Compute the order of $(g, h)$ — it is $\operatorname{lcm}(m, n)$.**

A power $(g,h)^j$ equals the identity exactly when both $m \mid j$ and $n \mid j$. The least positive such $j$ is the lowest common multiple, so $\operatorname{ord}(g, h) = \operatorname{lcm}(m, n)$.

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

Multiplication in the [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] is coordinatewise, so powers are too: $(g, h)^j = (g^j, h^j)$ for every integer $j \geq 0$ (an easy induction — $(g,h)^{j+1} = (g,h)^j (g,h) = (g^j, h^j)(g,h) = (g^{j+1}, h^{j+1})$).

The order of $(g,h)$ is the least positive $j$ with $(g,h)^j = (e,e)$. Now
$$(g^j, h^j) = (e, e) \iff g^j = e \ \text{ and } \ h^j = e.$$
By the order characterisation in [[Def - Order of a Group and of an Element]], $g^j = e$ holds precisely when $\operatorname{ord}(g) \mid j$, that is $m \mid j$; likewise $h^j = e$ holds precisely when $n \mid j$. So $(g,h)^j = (e,e)$ if and only if $j$ is a *common multiple* of $m$ and $n$. The least positive common multiple is by definition $\operatorname{lcm}(m, n)$, hence
$$\operatorname{ord}(g, h) = \operatorname{lcm}(m, n).$$

</details>

**Step 3: Use coprimality to get order $mn$, and conclude $C_m \times C_n \cong C_{mn}$.**

Because $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, the lowest common multiple is the full product: $\operatorname{lcm}(m, n) = mn$. So $(g, h)$ has order $mn$, equal to $|C_m \times C_n|$; therefore $C_m \times C_n$ is cyclic of order $mn$, that is, $C_m \times C_n \cong C_{mn}$.

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

The general identity relating the two is $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n) \cdot \gcd(m,n) = mn$. With the hypothesis $\gcd(m, n) = 1$ this becomes
$$\operatorname{lcm}(m, n) = \frac{mn}{\gcd(m,n)} = \frac{mn}{1} = mn.$$
(Directly: if $m \mid j$ and $n \mid j$ with $m, n$ coprime, then $mn \mid j$ — the coprime divisibility lemma — so every common multiple is a multiple of $mn$, and $mn$ is itself one; hence $mn$ is the least.)

Combining with Step 2, $\operatorname{ord}(g, h) = mn$. The cyclic subgroup $\langle (g,h) \rangle$ generated by $(g,h)$ therefore has $mn$ elements. But the ambient group $C_m \times C_n$ also has exactly $mn$ elements, so
$$\langle (g, h) \rangle = C_m \times C_n.$$
A group equal to the cyclic subgroup generated by one of its elements is cyclic; and a cyclic group is determined up to [[Def - Isomorphism|isomorphism]] by its order. Since $|C_m \times C_n| = mn$, we conclude
$$C_m \times C_n \cong C_{mn}. \qquad \square$$
Equivalently and explicitly, the map $C_{mn} \to C_m \times C_n$ sending the generator of $C_{mn}$ to $(g,h)$ is a well-defined bijective homomorphism.

</details>

**Step 4: Decompose $C_{60}$ into prime-power cyclic factors.**

Factor $60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 = 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$ into pairwise coprime prime powers and apply Step 3 repeatedly:
$$C_{60} \cong C_4 \times C_3 \times C_5.$$

<details>
<summary>Derivation</summary>

The prime factorization is $60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$, so the prime-power factors are $4, 3, 5$, and these are pairwise coprime (distinct primes share no common factor).

Apply part 1, which we have now proved, in its splitting direction. First split $60 = 4 \cdot 15$, and $\gcd(4, 15) = 1$:
$$C_{60} \cong C_4 \times C_{15}.$$
Now split the factor $C_{15}$, using $15 = 3 \cdot 5$ with $\gcd(3, 5) = 1$:
$$C_{15} \cong C_3 \times C_5.$$
Substituting,
$$C_{60} \cong C_4 \times C_3 \times C_5,$$
a [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] of cyclic groups of prime-power order, as required. As a check, the orders multiply correctly: $4 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 = 60$. Note the factor $C_4$ does *not* split further — $4 = 2^2$ is a prime power, and $C_4 \not\cong C_2 \times C_2$ because $2$ and $2$ are not coprime; this is the elementary divisor form, and $C_4$ is an elementary divisor in its own right.

</details>

<details>
<summary><strong>Complete formal solution</strong></summary>

**Part 1.** Let $\gcd(m,n) = 1$. Write $C_m = \langle g \rangle$ with $\operatorname{ord}(g) = m$ and $C_n = \langle h \rangle$ with $\operatorname{ord}(h) = n$, and consider $(g, h) \in C_m \times C_n$.

Multiplication in the [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] is coordinatewise, so $(g,h)^j = (g^j, h^j)$. Hence $(g,h)^j = (e,e)$ if and only if $g^j = e$ and $h^j = e$, which by the [[Def - Order of a Group and of an Element|definition of order]] holds if and only if $m \mid j$ and $n \mid j$. The least positive such $j$ is $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$, so $\operatorname{ord}(g,h) = \operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$.

Since $\gcd(m,n) = 1$, we have $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n) = mn / \gcd(m,n) = mn$. Thus $\operatorname{ord}(g,h) = mn$. The subgroup $\langle (g,h) \rangle$ then has $mn$ elements, equal to $|C_m \times C_n| = mn$, so $\langle (g,h) \rangle = C_m \times C_n$ and the group is cyclic. A cyclic group of order $mn$ is isomorphic to $C_{mn}$, hence
$$C_m \times C_n \cong C_{mn}.$$

**Part 2.** Factor $60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$, giving pairwise coprime prime powers $4, 3, 5$. Applying Part 1:
$$C_{60} \cong C_4 \times C_{15} \quad (\gcd(4,15)=1), \qquad C_{15} \cong C_3 \times C_5 \quad (\gcd(3,5)=1).$$
Therefore
$$C_{60} \cong C_4 \times C_3 \times C_5,$$
a direct product of cyclic groups of prime-power order. The factor $C_4$ does not decompose further, since $4$ is a prime power. $\blacksquare$

</details>

# Key Takeaways

**To prove a group is cyclic, hunt for one element of maximal order — never build the isomorphism by hand.** The structural lever in part 1 is the criterion that a finite group is cyclic exactly when it contains an element whose [[Def - Order of a Group and of an Element|order]] equals the group's size. This reframes "prove $G \cong C_k$" — which sounds like it demands an explicit isomorphism formula — into the far easier "find an element of order $|G|$". The reusable instinct is that *every* cyclicity question should be attacked this way: locate a single high-order element and let the order count do the rest, since a cyclic group is pinned down up to [[Def - Isomorphism|isomorphism]] by its order alone. The same move proves that $(\mathbb{Z}/p)^\times$ is cyclic (it has a primitive root), that a finite subgroup of the multiplicative group of a field is cyclic, and that a direct product of cyclic groups of coprime orders is cyclic — all are "exhibit a generator" arguments wearing different clothes.

**The order of a tuple is the lowest common multiple of its coordinate orders — coprimality is what makes that an honest product.** The computational heart of the proof is that in a [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]], $(g,h)^j = (e,e)$ requires *both* coordinates to die at once, so the order of $(g,h)$ is the least $j$ killing both — the lowest common multiple $\operatorname{lcm}(\operatorname{ord} g, \operatorname{ord} h)$. This rule is general: in any product $G_1 \times \cdots \times G_t$, the order of a tuple is the lcm of the orders of its entries. The coprimality hypothesis enters at exactly one place — it upgrades $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$ to the full product $mn$ — and that is the *only* place it is used, which is why the theorem is sharp: drop coprimality and the order of $(g,h)$ falls short of $mn$, leaving the product non-cyclic. The diagnostic value is high: whenever you see $\operatorname{lcm}$ where you wanted a product, the missing ingredient is a coprimality (or "trivial intersection") hypothesis, and that gap is precisely why $C_4 \not\cong C_2 \times C_2$.

**The Chinese remainder theorem is reversible, and which direction you run it is the difference between the two normal forms.** Part 1 establishes a single isomorphism $C_{mn} \cong C_m \times C_n$, but it is a tool that runs both ways. Run *rightward* — splitting $C_{mn}$ into $C_m \times C_n$ — and iterating down to prime powers produces the **elementary divisor** form, as in part 2's $C_{60} \cong C_4 \times C_3 \times C_5$. Run *leftward* — fusing coprime cyclic factors into one — and you assemble the **invariant factor** form. This one reversible isomorphism is therefore the entire engine of normal-form conversion for finite abelian groups, and recognising a problem as "apply [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Cyclic Groups|CRT]] in direction $X$" handles every such conversion. The boundary of its power is also instructive: $C_4$ in part 2 refuses to split because $4 = 2^2$ is a prime power with no coprime factorization — prime-power cyclic groups are the *atoms* the theorem bottoms out at, which is exactly why "elementary divisor" means "prime-power order".
