# Problem Statement

Let $G$ be a finite group and let $p$ be a prime such that $p$ divides $|G|$ but $p^2$ does not — so that every [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup|Sylow p-subgroup]] of $G$ has order exactly $p$. Write $n_p$ for the number of Sylow $p$-subgroups. Prove that $G$ contains exactly $n_p(p-1)$ elements of order $p$.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are Sylow subgroups, the order of an element, and the elementary arithmetic of subgroups of prime order.

![[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup#The Definition]]

Here $|G| = p^a m$ with $p \nmid m$, and the standing hypothesis $p \mid |G|$ but $p^2 \nmid |G|$ says precisely that $a = 1$. A Sylow $p$-subgroup therefore has order $p^a = p$.

The **order of an element** $g \in G$, written $\operatorname{ord}(g)$, is the smallest positive integer $k$ with $g^k = e$; equivalently it is the order of the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ it generates. By [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] the order of any element divides $|G|$, and the order of any subgroup divides $|G|$.

The single fact about Sylow subgroups this problem needs is the *existence* clause of [[Thm - Sylow's Theorems|Sylow's theorems]] together with the definition of $n_p$ — that $\operatorname{Syl}_p(G)$ is a non-empty finite set and $n_p$ counts it. The deeper conjugacy and congruence clauses are not needed here: this is a pure counting statement.

A subgroup of **prime order** $p$ is automatically cyclic, and every one of its $p - 1$ non-identity elements is a generator, hence has order exactly $p$. This is the small fact the whole exercise turns on.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *counting* problem in the sense of the [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7#Sources and Targets|Sources and Targets]] of the topic page — specifically, counting the elements of a given order. But its real role is infrastructural: it is the lemma that powers the *element-counting tactic* in the non-simplicity playbook. Almost every non-simplicity proof that proceeds by tallying elements (orders $30$, $56$, $132$, and many more) silently invokes the formula proved here. The exercise isolates that formula so the later proofs can cite it cleanly.

**Assumption pattern.** The decisive hypothesis is the small one hiding in plain sight: $p \mid |G|$ but $p^2 \nmid |G|$. This is exactly the condition that Sylow $p$-subgroups have *prime* order $p$. A group of prime order has no proper non-trivial subgroups at all, so the lattice of subgroups it can share with another such group is as thin as possible. That thinness is what makes the count exact rather than an inequality — the assumption $a = 1$ is doing all the work.

**Theorem routing.** The route does not run through a named theorem so much as through [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] applied to an intersection. Two distinct Sylow $p$-subgroups $P \neq Q$ have an intersection $P \cap Q$, which is a subgroup of $P$; by Lagrange $|P \cap Q|$ divides $|P| = p$, so $|P \cap Q| \in \{1, p\}$. The value $p$ would force $P \cap Q = P = Q$, contradicting $P \neq Q$. Hence $|P \cap Q| = 1$: distinct Sylow $p$-subgroups meet only in the identity. The non-identity elements then partition cleanly, and the count is immediate.

**Key decision point.** The one genuinely substantive move is to recognise that *the elements of order $p$ are exactly the non-identity elements of the Sylow $p$-subgroups*, and that this set is partitioned by them. Both halves need an argument. That every order-$p$ element lies in *some* Sylow $p$-subgroup is the existence/conjugacy content of Sylow: the cyclic group $\langle g \rangle$ generated by an order-$p$ element is a $p$-subgroup, hence contained in a Sylow $p$-subgroup — but when $a = 1$, $\langle g \rangle$ already *has* order $p$, so it *is* a Sylow $p$-subgroup. That the union is *disjoint* off the identity is the trivial-intersection fact above. Get both and the formula $n_p(p-1)$ falls out by simple multiplication.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Count elements of prime order** (operation 3). This exercise *is* that operation, proved as a standalone statement. The trigger is a Sylow $p$-subgroup of prime order $p$; the move is to observe that distinct such subgroups intersect trivially and each contributes $p - 1$ elements of order $p$.

2. **Factor the order and read the Sylow constraints** (operation 1), in its mildest form. We use only that $|G| = p^a m$ and that the hypothesis $p^2 \nmid |G|$ pins $a = 1$, so Sylow $p$-subgroups have order $p$. No congruence is invoked.

3. **Apply Lagrange to a subgroup** — the workhorse from [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]]. Lagrange is applied to the intersection $P \cap Q$ of two Sylow $p$-subgroups, viewed as a subgroup of $P$, to force $|P \cap Q| \in \{1, p\}$ and hence $|P \cap Q| = 1$.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> What is the order of a Sylow $p$-subgroup under the hypothesis $p^2 \nmid |G|$? Once you know that order, ask what the subgroup-lattice of such a Sylow $p$-subgroup looks like — a group of prime order is as simple as a group can be.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Take two *distinct* Sylow $p$-subgroups $P$ and $Q$. Their intersection $P \cap Q$ is a subgroup of $P$. Apply [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]]: what are the possible values of $|P \cap Q|$, and which one is ruled out by $P \neq Q$?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Two things to nail down. First: every element of order $p$ lies in some Sylow $p$-subgroup — because $\langle g \rangle$ has order $p$ and so *is* a Sylow $p$-subgroup. Second: in a group of prime order $p$, every one of the $p - 1$ non-identity elements has order exactly $p$. Combine: the order-$p$ elements are the disjoint union, over the $n_p$ Sylow subgroups, of their $p - 1$ non-identity elements.

# Solution

The plan is to show that the set of elements of order $p$ in $G$ is partitioned by the Sylow $p$-subgroups: each contributes exactly $p - 1$ such elements, distinct subgroups contribute disjoint sets, and every order-$p$ element is accounted for. Multiplying gives $n_p(p-1)$.

**Step 1: Each Sylow $p$-subgroup has order $p$ and contributes exactly $p - 1$ elements of order $p$.**

The hypothesis $p \mid |G|$, $p^2 \nmid |G|$ forces the exponent $a$ in $|G| = p^a m$ to equal $1$, so every Sylow $p$-subgroup has order $p$. A group of prime order $p$ is cyclic, and every non-identity element generates it, so each of its $p - 1$ non-identity elements has order exactly $p$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Write $|G| = p^a m$ with $p \nmid m$, the factorization from [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup]]. The hypothesis says $p$ divides $|G|$, so $a \geq 1$, and $p^2$ does not divide $|G|$, so $a < 2$. Hence $a = 1$ and a Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$ has order $p^a = p$.
>
> Now $P$ is a group of prime order. By [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] every element $x \in P$ has order dividing $|P| = p$, so $\operatorname{ord}(x) \in \{1, p\}$. The only element of order $1$ is the identity $e$. Therefore every one of the $|P| - 1 = p - 1$ non-identity elements of $P$ has order exactly $p$. (In particular $P$ is cyclic, generated by any one of them.)
>
> So each Sylow $p$-subgroup contains precisely $p - 1$ elements of order $p$, namely all of its non-identity elements.

**Step 2: Distinct Sylow $p$-subgroups intersect only in the identity.**

If $P \neq Q$ are two Sylow $p$-subgroups, then $P \cap Q$ is a subgroup of $P$, so by Lagrange $|P \cap Q|$ divides $p$. It cannot be $p$ — that would force $P \cap Q = P = Q$ — so $|P \cap Q| = 1$, i.e. $P \cap Q = \{e\}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $P, Q \in \operatorname{Syl}_p(G)$ with $P \neq Q$. The intersection $P \cap Q$ is a subgroup of $G$ (an intersection of subgroups is a subgroup), and it is contained in $P$, so it is a subgroup of $P$.
>
> By [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]], $|P \cap Q|$ divides $|P| = p$. Since $p$ is prime, the only divisors are $1$ and $p$, so $|P \cap Q| \in \{1, p\}$.
>
> Suppose $|P \cap Q| = p$. Then $P \cap Q$ is a subgroup of $P$ of the same order as $P$, hence $P \cap Q = P$; this says $P \subseteq Q$. As $|P| = |Q| = p$, a subset relation between equal-size finite sets is an equality, so $P = Q$ — contradicting $P \neq Q$.
>
> Therefore $|P \cap Q| = 1$, which means $P \cap Q = \{e\}$. Distinct Sylow $p$-subgroups share only the identity.

**Step 3: Every element of order $p$ lies in exactly one Sylow $p$-subgroup.**

If $g$ has order $p$, the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ has order $p$ and so *is* a Sylow $p$-subgroup. By Step 2 it is the unique Sylow $p$-subgroup containing $g$. Hence the order-$p$ elements are exactly the union, over $\operatorname{Syl}_p(G)$, of the non-identity elements — a disjoint union.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $g \in G$ have order $p$. The cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \dots, g^{p-1}\}$ it generates has order $\operatorname{ord}(g) = p$. A subgroup of order $p$ is a $p$-subgroup of the maximum order allowed by $|G| = p^1 m$, so by the definition in [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup]], $\langle g \rangle$ is itself a Sylow $p$-subgroup. So $g$ lies in *at least one* Sylow $p$-subgroup, namely $\langle g \rangle$.
>
> It lies in *at most one*: if $g \in P$ for a Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$, then $\langle g \rangle \subseteq P$, and since $|\langle g \rangle| = |P| = p$ this inclusion is an equality, $P = \langle g \rangle$. So $\langle g \rangle$ is the *unique* Sylow $p$-subgroup containing $g$.
>
> Conversely every non-identity element of a Sylow $p$-subgroup has order $p$, by Step 1. Therefore the set $S$ of elements of order $p$ in $G$ is exactly
> $$S = \bigcup_{P \in \operatorname{Syl}_p(G)} \big(P \setminus \{e\}\big),$$
> and Step 2 makes this a *disjoint* union: if $g$ lay in two different sets $P \setminus \{e\}$ and $Q \setminus \{e\}$, then $g \in P \cap Q = \{e\}$, impossible for $g \neq e$.

**Step 4: Add up — there are $n_p(p-1)$ elements of order $p$.**

The order-$p$ elements form a disjoint union of $n_p$ sets, each of size $p - 1$, so the total is $n_p(p-1)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By Step 3 the set $S$ of elements of order $p$ is the disjoint union $\bigsqcup_{P} (P \setminus \{e\})$ over the $n_p = |\operatorname{Syl}_p(G)|$ Sylow $p$-subgroups. The size of a disjoint union is the sum of the sizes, and by Step 1 each summand $|P \setminus \{e\}| = p - 1$. Hence
> $$|S| = \sum_{P \in \operatorname{Syl}_p(G)} |P \setminus \{e\}| = \sum_{P \in \operatorname{Syl}_p(G)} (p - 1) = n_p \,(p - 1).$$
> That is the claimed count. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Let $|G| = p^a m$ with $p \nmid m$. The hypothesis $p \mid |G|$, $p^2 \nmid |G|$ gives $a = 1$, so every [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup|Sylow p-subgroup]] has order $p$.
>
> *Each Sylow $p$-subgroup contributes $p - 1$ elements of order $p$.* Let $P$ be a Sylow $p$-subgroup, $|P| = p$. By [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange]], every $x \in P$ has $\operatorname{ord}(x) \mid p$, so $\operatorname{ord}(x) \in \{1, p\}$. Only $e$ has order $1$, so each of the $p - 1$ non-identity elements of $P$ has order exactly $p$.
>
> *Distinct Sylow $p$-subgroups meet only in $e$.* If $P \neq Q$ are Sylow $p$-subgroups, then $P \cap Q$ is a subgroup of $P$, so $|P \cap Q| \mid p$ by Lagrange, giving $|P \cap Q| \in \{1, p\}$. If $|P \cap Q| = p$ then $P \cap Q = P = Q$, contradicting $P \neq Q$; hence $P \cap Q = \{e\}$.
>
> *The order-$p$ elements are the disjoint union of the non-identity elements of the Sylow $p$-subgroups.* If $g$ has order $p$, then $\langle g \rangle$ has order $p$ and so is a Sylow $p$-subgroup; thus $g$ lies in a Sylow $p$-subgroup. Conversely every non-identity element of a Sylow $p$-subgroup has order $p$. So the set $S$ of order-$p$ elements satisfies $S = \bigcup_P (P \setminus \{e\})$, and this union is disjoint, since a common element of $P \setminus \{e\}$ and $Q \setminus \{e\}$ with $P \neq Q$ would lie in $P \cap Q = \{e\}$.
>
> *Count.* Therefore
> $$|S| = \sum_{P \in \operatorname{Syl}_p(G)} |P \setminus \{e\}| = n_p (p - 1),$$
> since there are $n_p$ Sylow $p$-subgroups and each contributes $p - 1$ elements. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Prime-order Sylow subgroups partition the order-$p$ elements — this is the engine of element-counting non-simplicity proofs.** The formula $n_p(p-1)$ is not an isolated curiosity; it is the precise tool that converts a Sylow count into a population census of the group. The trigger to reach for it is the arithmetic condition $p \mid |G|$ but $p^2 \nmid |G|$ — equivalently, $p$ appears to the *first power* in the factorization of $|G|$. Whenever you see such a prime, you may immediately assert that $G$ has exactly $n_p(p-1)$ elements of order $p$, with no further argument. In a non-simplicity proof you then run this for every first-power prime, add the counts (they are disjoint, since elements of different prime orders cannot coincide), and compare with $|G|$. If the running total overshoots $|G|$, or crowds out the room a remaining Sylow subgroup needs, some $n_p$ is forced to $1$ and the group is not [[Def - Simple Group|simple]]. The exercises on orders $30$, $56$, and $132$ are all this single move, instantiated.

**Trivial intersection is a Lagrange fact about prime order, and it must be checked, not assumed.** The entire count rests on distinct Sylow $p$-subgroups meeting only in the identity, and the reason is razor-thin: a subgroup of a group of prime order has order $1$ or $p$, and order $p$ collapses the two subgroups into one. This is *special to prime order*. The instant a Sylow $p$-subgroup has order $p^2$ or larger, two of them can share a subgroup of order $p$ — see the warning on the topic page — and the elements of order $p$ are then wildly overcounted, because an order-$p$ element living in the shared subgroup is tallied once for *each* Sylow subgroup containing it. The discipline this exercise installs is to verify the exponent $a = 1$ before counting anything. The clean partition is a privilege of first-power primes; for higher powers the count is an inequality at best, and the element-counting tactic must be abandoned in favour of acting on the Sylow set.

**Cyclic-of-prime-order is the simplest possible building block, and recognising "$\langle g \rangle$ *is* a Sylow subgroup" is the move that ties an element to the Sylow machinery.** A group of prime order has no internal structure at all — no proper non-trivial subgroups — and every non-identity element is a generator. This featurelessness is exactly why the count is exact. The reusable recognition is the identification, when $a = 1$, of the cyclic subgroup $\langle g \rangle$ generated by an order-$p$ element *with* a Sylow $p$-subgroup: an abstract element of order $p$ and a Sylow $p$-subgroup are two views of the same object. This is what lets a statement about *elements* (how many have order $p$) be answered by a statement about *subgroups* (how many Sylow $p$-subgroups there are). The general lesson is that whenever a problem mixes a question about elements of a certain order with the Sylow framework, the bridge is the cyclic subgroup an element generates — pass to $\langle g \rangle$, identify it as a Sylow subgroup if its order is maximal, and the element-level question becomes a subgroup-level one the Sylow theorems can answer.
