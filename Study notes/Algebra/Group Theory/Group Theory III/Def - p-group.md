# Notation

Throughout, $p$ denotes a prime number and $G$ a finite group with identity $e$. The **order** $|G|$ is the number of elements of $G$, and for $g \in G$ the **order of the element** $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ is the least integer $n > 0$ with $g^n = e$. We write $\langle g \rangle = \{e, g, g^2, \dots\}$ for the cyclic subgroup generated by $g$, and $Z(G) = \{x \in G : xg = gx \text{ for all } g \in G\}$ for the [[Def - Centraliser and Centre|centre]]. The phrase "$p$-power order" means order equal to $p^k$ for some integer $k \geq 0$. The full notation registry for this chapter lives on the parent page [[Group Theory III — §1.5–1.7]].

# Axiom Motivation

The motivating question of this whole chapter is a question about the integer $|G|$: *how much does the order of a finite group, on its own, tell you about the group's internal structure?* [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] gives the first answer — the order of every subgroup divides $|G|$ — but that is a constraint, not a construction, and to make progress one wants to start with the *simplest* possible orders and work upward. The simplest orders are the primes, and groups of prime order are already completely understood: a group of order $p$ is forced to be cyclic, because any non-identity element has order dividing $p$, hence order $p$, hence generates everything. So the genuinely first unsolved case, the smallest place where group structure has any room to be interesting, is **order a prime power**. The definition of a $p$-group is simply the decision to give that case a name and study it as a class.

Why insist on a *single* prime? One could imagine defining a "prime-power group" to mean any group whose order is a prime power $p^n$ — but that is exactly what a $p$-group is, with the prime $p$ recorded. The reason for recording $p$ rather than leaving it anonymous is that essentially every theorem about these groups is a divisibility-by-$p$ argument: the [[Thm - The Class Equation|class equation]] read modulo $p$, [[Group Theory II — §1.3–1.4|Cauchy's theorem]] producing an element of order $p$, the count of fixed points of an action congruent modulo $p$. The prime is the active ingredient in every proof, so it belongs in the name. A group of order $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$ is neither a $2$-group nor a $3$-group; it has *features* of both, accessed through its Sylow subgroups, but it is not itself a $p$-group for any single $p$.

Why insist on **finiteness**, and why $n \geq 1$ rather than $n \geq 0$? The condition $n \geq 1$ merely excludes the trivial group of order $p^0 = 1$ from being called a $p$-group; this is a harmless convention that keeps statements like "every $p$-group has non-trivial centre" from needing an exception clause, since the trivial group has trivial centre. Finiteness is the substantive demand. There is an infinite-group notion — a group in which *every element* has $p$-power order, called a $p$-group in the infinite setting too — but for finite groups the two notions coincide (this is the equivalence proved below), and the finite theory is where the rigidity lives. If you dropped finiteness you would lose the class equation outright, because it counts elements, and with it you would lose the non-trivial-centre theorem: the infinite group $\mathbb{Z}/p^\infty$ (the Prüfer group, all $p$-power roots of unity) does have non-trivial centre because it is abelian, but the infinite free product of copies of $C_p$ is a $p$-group with trivial centre. Finiteness is exactly the hypothesis that makes prime-power order *force* structure.

Here is the cleanest way to see that this is the right definition and not a nearby variant. The defining datum is the single equation $|G| = p^n$. From it, and Lagrange, every element $g$ has $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ dividing $p^n$, so $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ is a power of $p$ — the group is one in which **every element has $p$-power order**. Conversely — and this is the non-obvious half — a finite group in which every element has $p$-power order must have order a power of $p$: if some other prime $q$ divided $|G|$, then Cauchy's theorem would manufacture an element of order exactly $q$, a non-$p$-power, a contradiction. So the order-definition and the element-order-definition are *equivalent* for finite groups. This equivalence is what tells you the definition is natural: two genuinely different-looking conditions — one about the global integer $|G|$, one about every individual element — pick out exactly the same class. A definition that did this only for a coincidental reason would be suspect; here both directions are forced, the first by Lagrange and the second by Cauchy, so the class of $p$-groups is robust. Weaken "$|G| = p^n$" to "$p \mid |G|$" and you no longer have a $p$-group at all — $S_3$ has $2 \mid |S_3|$ but contains an element of order $3$ — and you lose every theorem in §1.5.

# The Definition

A **$p$-group** is a finite group $G$ whose order is a power of a prime: $|G| = p^n$ for some prime number $p$ and some integer $n \geq 1$.

Equivalently, by [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] and [[Group Theory II — §1.3–1.4|Cauchy's theorem]], a finite group $G$ is a $p$-group if and only if **every element of $G$ has order a power of $p$**. (Lagrange gives the forward direction: each $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ divides $p^n$, hence is a power of $p$. Cauchy gives the converse: if a prime $q \neq p$ divided $|G|$ there would be an element of order $q$, contradicting $p$-power order.)

A subgroup $H \leq G$ is called a **$p$-subgroup** of an arbitrary finite group $G$ if $H$ is itself a $p$-group, that is, if $|H|$ is a power of $p$ — even when $|G|$ itself is not a prime power. The largest possible $p$-subgroups are the [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup|Sylow $p$-subgroups]].

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The cleanest compression is **arithmetic**: a $p$-group is the group-theoretic analogue of a prime power among the integers. Just as the integers $p^n$ are the multiplicative building blocks from which every integer is assembled by the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, the $p$-groups (one prime at a time) are the pieces from which every finite group is locally assembled — [[Thm - Sylow's Theorems|Sylow's theorems]] make this precise by extracting, for each prime $p$ dividing $|G|$, a maximal $p$-subgroup. A finite group "is" its collection of Sylow $p$-subgroups glued together, much as an integer "is" its prime-power factors multiplied together; the gluing is just more intricate than multiplication.

A second, sharper analogy is with **nilpotent objects**. In linear algebra a nilpotent operator is one some power of which is zero, and such operators are exactly those built by repeated extension from the zero operator; they are the "most degenerate" operators, and they always have a non-trivial kernel. A finite $p$-group is nilpotent in the precise group-theoretic sense (its upper central series reaches the whole group), and the analogue of "non-trivial kernel" is the [[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre|non-trivial centre]]. The slogan is that $p$-groups are to finite groups as nilpotent operators are to all operators: a tractable, layered, "triangular" subclass on which everything can be done by induction up a central series. This is not a loose analogy — the unipotent upper-triangular matrices over $\mathbb{Z}/p$ form a $p$-group, and the strictly-upper-triangular structure *is* the central series.

Against probability, there is no useful analogy: $p$-groups are an arithmetic-structural notion with no measure-theoretic content. Forcing one would mislead.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: the cyclic group $C_{p^n}$.** The cyclic group of order $p^n$ is the simplest $p$-group. Every element has order dividing $p^n$, hence a power of $p$, confirming the element-order characterisation directly. For each $0 \leq b \leq n$ it has a unique subgroup of order $p^b$ — already a small instance of [[Thm - Subgroups of a p-Group|the subgroup theorem]] — and being abelian its centre is everything.

**Is an instance: the Klein four-group $V = C_2 \times C_2$.** Order $4 = 2^2$, so a $2$-group. Every non-identity element has order $2$, a power of $2$. It is the smallest non-cyclic $p$-group, and shows that "$p$-group" does not mean "cyclic": the element-orders here ($1, 2, 2, 2$) do not include $4$.

**Is an instance: the dihedral group $D_8$ and the quaternion group $Q_8$.** Both have order $8 = 2^3$, so both are $2$-groups, and both are *non-abelian*. They are the two non-abelian groups of order $8$, and they illustrate the rigidity claimed below: each has centre of order exactly $2$ (non-trivial, as it must be), and each has subgroups of orders $1, 2, 4, 8$. They are the standard test objects for any statement about non-abelian $p$-groups.

**Is an instance: the unitriangular group $U_n(\mathbb{Z}/p)$.** The $n \times n$ upper-triangular matrices over the field $\mathbb{Z}/p$ with all diagonal entries equal to $1$ form a group of order $p^{\binom{n}{2}}$ — a $p$-group, and the canonical [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup|Sylow $p$-subgroup]] of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z}/p)$. Its central series is visible: conjugation pushes non-zero entries toward the top-right corner.

**Is NOT an instance: the symmetric group $S_3$.** Its order is $6 = 2 \cdot 3$, divisible by *two* distinct primes, so it is not a $p$-group for any $p$. Concretely it contains an element of order $2$ (a transposition) and an element of order $3$ (a $3$-cycle); the presence of orders that are powers of different primes is exactly the obstruction. Note $S_3$ does have $2 \mid |S_3|$, but "$p$ divides $|G|$" is far weaker than "$|G|$ is a power of $p$" — this non-example probes that distinction.

**Is NOT an instance: the cyclic group $C_{12}$.** Order $12 = 2^2 \cdot 3$. Even though it is abelian and very well-behaved, it is not a $p$-group: it has an element of order $3$ and elements of order $4$, and no single prime accounts for all element orders. It does, however, have $p$-subgroups for each relevant prime — a $2$-subgroup of order $4$ and a $3$-subgroup of order $3$ — illustrating that an arbitrary group still carries $p$-subgroups even when it is not itself a $p$-group.

**Corollary (every element has $p$-power order).** In a $p$-group $G$ of order $p^n$, every $g \in G$ has $\operatorname{ord}(g) \in \{1, p, p^2, \dots, p^n\}$. *Calibration check:* this is immediate from [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange]] — $\operatorname{ord}(g)$ is the order of $\langle g \rangle$, which divides $p^n$ — and if you can produce this one-line argument you have understood the order-definition.

**Corollary (subgroups and quotients of $p$-groups are $p$-groups).** If $G$ is a $p$-group and $H \leq G$, then $|H|$ divides $p^n$ by Lagrange, so $H$ is a $p$-group (or trivial); and if $N \trianglelefteq G$ then $|G/N| = |G|/|N|$ is also a power of $p$, so the [[Def - Quotient Group|quotient]] $G/N$ is a $p$-group (or trivial). *Calibration check:* the class of $p$-groups is closed under passing to subgroups and quotients — this closure is precisely what makes induction on $|G| = p^n$ a legal proof technique throughout §1.5.

**Corollary (rigidity — a preview).** A non-trivial $p$-group is far from featureless. It always has a non-trivial [[Def - Centraliser and Centre|centre]] ([[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre]]); for $n \geq 2$ it is never [[Def - Simple Group|simple]]; it has a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal]] subgroup of order $p^b$ for *every* $0 \leq b \leq n$ ([[Thm - Subgroups of a p-Group]]); and if $|G| = p^2$ it is forced to be [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian]] ([[Thm - Quotient by the Centre and Commutativity]]). For $p$-groups, the converse of Lagrange holds completely — every divisor of the order is realised as the order of a subgroup. *Calibration check:* contrast this with $A_5$ of order $60$, which has no subgroup of order $30$; if you see why "$p$-group" is essential to the rigidity and a general group of composite order need not have it, the definition has landed.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Sylow $p$-Subgroup *(from §1.7)*
> Once "$p$-group" is in hand, an arbitrary finite group of order $p^a m$ ($p \nmid m$) can be probed prime-by-prime: a [[Def - Sylow p-Subgroup|Sylow $p$-subgroup]] is a $p$-subgroup of the largest order Lagrange permits, $p^a$. [[Thm - Sylow's Theorems|Sylow's theorems]] govern their existence, conjugacy, and number, turning the local study of $p$-groups into a tool for all finite groups.

> [!tip] Nilpotent and Solvable Groups *(from Galois Theory)*
> Every finite $p$-group is nilpotent — its [[Thm - p-Groups Have Non-Trivial Centre|non-trivial centre]] supplies an abelian normal layer at every stage of a central series — and therefore solvable. A finite group turns out to be nilpotent exactly when it is the [[Def - Direct Product|direct product]] of its Sylow $p$-subgroups. Solvability of the relevant Galois group is what decides whether a polynomial is soluble by radicals.
