# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Throughout, $k$ is a field and $A$ is a $k$-algebra — a ring equipped with a [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]] $k \to A$, so that $A$ is in particular a $k$-vector space. We write $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ for the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] in $n$ indeterminates, the free object: its elements are honest formal polynomials with no relations among the $T_i$. For elements $x_1, \dots, x_n \in A$ we write $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ for the $k$-subalgebra of $A$ they generate (all polynomial expressions in the $x_i$ with coefficients in $k$), and there is always a surjective evaluation homomorphism
$$\operatorname{ev} : k[T_1, \dots, T_n] \twoheadrightarrow k[x_1, \dots, x_n], \qquad T_i \mapsto x_i.$$
For a field extension $k \subseteq L$ we write $k(A)$ for the subfield of $L$ generated by a subset $A$, and $\operatorname{trdeg}_k L$ for the transcendence degree of $L$ over $k$. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

This is a compound page: it defines three interlocking notions — **algebraic independence** of a tuple of elements, a **transcendence basis** of a field extension, and the **transcendence degree** — because they are introduced together and none is fully usable without the others. Algebraic independence is the pointwise condition; a transcendence basis is a maximal algebraically independent set; the transcendence degree is the common size of all such bases.

# Axiom Motivation

The whole subject is built on one analogy, and it is worth stating before any definition: **transcendence theory is linear algebra with "linear combination" replaced by "polynomial relation".** In linear algebra, vectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ are *linearly independent* when no non-trivial linear combination $\sum c_i v_i$ vanishes; a *basis* is a maximal independent set; and the *dimension* is the common size of all bases, well-defined by the exchange lemma. Replace "linear combination $\sum c_i v_i$" by "polynomial expression $p(x_1, \dots, x_n)$" and the entire structure reappears: algebraic independence, transcendence basis, transcendence degree. The reason to make this replacement is that the natural notion of "independence" for elements of a *ring* (or a field over a subfield) is not "no linear relation" but "no polynomial relation" — because a ring lets you multiply, so $x$ and $x^2$ are linearly independent over $k$ yet are clearly not independent in any reasonable sense; the relation $x^2 - x\cdot x = 0$ binds them.

**Why "no polynomial relation" and not "no linear relation".** Suppose we want to say that $x_1, \dots, x_n \in A$ are "as free as indeterminates". The indeterminates $T_1, \dots, T_n$ in $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ satisfy *no* polynomial relation at all: if $p(T_1, \dots, T_n) = 0$ as an element of the polynomial ring, then $p$ is the zero polynomial. That is the gold standard of freedom. So $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are algebraically independent precisely when they share this property: $p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = 0$ forces $p$ to be the zero polynomial. Equivalently — and this is the operational heart — the evaluation map $\operatorname{ev} : k[T_1, \dots, T_n] \to A$, $T_i \mapsto x_i$, is *injective*. Its kernel is exactly the ideal of all polynomial relations among the $x_i$; algebraic independence is the statement that this kernel is zero, so that $k[x_1, \dots, x_n] \cong k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ is an honest polynomial ring. If we had demanded only "no linear relation", we would be measuring vector-space dimension, which is the wrong invariant: $k[x]$ for a single transcendental $x$ is infinite-dimensional as a $k$-vector space but has transcendence degree $1$. Polynomial independence is coarser than linear independence in exactly the way that captures "number of free parameters" rather than "size of the spanning set".

**Why a single condition, and what its failure looks like.** Unlike a group or a ring, this definition has no list of axioms to vary independently — it is one clean condition (injectivity of evaluation) — so the right "failure analysis" is to see what *dependence* buys us. If $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are *not* algebraically independent, there is a nonzero $p$ with $p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = 0$. Collect the terms of $p$ by powers of $x_n$ and you get a nonzero polynomial relation expressing some power of $x_n$ in terms of lower powers with coefficients in $k[x_1, \dots, x_{n-1}]$ — that is, $x_n$ is *algebraic* over $k(x_1, \dots, x_{n-1})$, the field they generate. This is the bridge to [[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|integrality]]: an algebraic dependence is one short step from an integral dependence (clear the leading coefficient), and it is exactly the relation that [[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether normalization]] manufactures and then exploits. So the negation of the definition is not a pathology but the engine of the chapter: dependence means one element is algebraic over the rest, which means a polynomial subalgebra controls the whole thing finitely.

**Why a maximal independent set has a well-defined size.** Having a notion of independence, we want a notion of *dimension*: the number of free parameters in the field $L = k(x_1, x_2, \dots)$. A **transcendence basis** is a maximal algebraically independent subset $B \subseteq L$; equivalently, $B$ is algebraically independent and $L$ is *algebraic* over the subfield $k(B)$ (nothing more is free; everything else is bound by a polynomial relation). The non-obvious fact — and the only theorem hiding inside this definition — is that any two transcendence bases of $L/k$ have the *same cardinality*, proved by a Steinitz exchange argument identical in spirit to the one for vector-space bases: given two bases, swap elements of one into the other one at a time, using that each new element is algebraic over the current set to preserve the spanning property. This common cardinality is the **transcendence degree** $\operatorname{trdeg}_k L$, and its well-definedness is what makes "dimension" meaningful. Without the exchange lemma the notion would depend on a choice of basis and be useless; with it, $\operatorname{trdeg}_k L$ is an honest invariant of the extension, additive in towers ($\operatorname{trdeg}_k E = \operatorname{trdeg}_k L + \operatorname{trdeg}_L E$ for $k \subseteq L \subseteq E$) exactly as dimensions add for vector spaces in a flag.

**Why this is the right notion of dimension for the chapter.** The payoff, made precise later, is that for a [[Def - Finitely Generated Algebra|finitely generated]] $k$-algebra $A$ that is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]], the transcendence degree of its [[Def - Field of Fractions|fraction field]] equals its **Krull dimension** — the longest chain of prime ideals — and equals the dimension of the corresponding **affine variety** as a geometric object. The polynomial ring $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ has transcendence degree $n$ (the indeterminates are a basis), and the variety $\mathbb{A}^n$ has geometric dimension $n$; these agree because both count "free coordinates". Transcendence degree is the algebraic shadow of geometric dimension, and defining it carefully here is what lets Noether normalization read "every variety is a finite cover of some $\mathbb{A}^d$" as "$d$ is the dimension".

# The Definition

Let $k$ be a field and $A$ a $k$-algebra.

## Algebraic independence

Elements $x_1, \dots, x_n \in A$ are **$k$-algebraically independent** if, for every nonzero polynomial $p \in k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$,
$$p(x_1, \dots, x_n) \neq 0.$$
Equivalently, the $k$-algebra homomorphism
$$\operatorname{ev} : k[T_1, \dots, T_n] \to A, \qquad T_i \mapsto x_i,$$
is **injective**, in which case it restricts to an isomorphism $k[T_1, \dots, T_n] \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ onto the subalgebra generated by the $x_i$. A set (finite or infinite) is algebraically independent if every finite subset is.

A single element $x$ is algebraically independent — one says **transcendental** over $k$ — when it satisfies no nonzero polynomial $p \in k[T]$; otherwise it is **algebraic** over $k$.

## Transcendence basis

Now let $k \subseteq L$ be a field extension. A subset $B \subseteq L$ is a **transcendence basis** of $L$ over $k$ if it satisfies one — hence all — of the following equivalent conditions:

1. $B$ is algebraically independent over $k$, and $L$ is algebraic over the subfield $k(B)$.
2. $B$ is algebraically independent over $k$, and $B \cup \{\beta\}$ is algebraically dependent for every $\beta \in L$ (i.e. $B$ is *maximal* algebraically independent).
3. $L$ is algebraic over $k(B)$, but not over $k(B \setminus \{\alpha\})$ for any $\alpha \in B$ (i.e. $B$ is *minimal* spanning).

## Transcendence degree

All transcendence bases of $L$ over $k$ have the same cardinality. This common cardinality is the **transcendence degree** of $L$ over $k$, written $\operatorname{trdeg}_k L$. For a $k$-algebra $A$ that is an integral domain, one sets $\operatorname{trdeg}_k A := \operatorname{trdeg}_k \operatorname{Frac}(A)$.

For a tower of fields $k \subseteq L \subseteq E$, transcendence degree is additive:
$$\operatorname{trdeg}_k E = \operatorname{trdeg}_k L + \operatorname{trdeg}_L E.$$

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The structural content is the parallel with linear algebra, and it is exact enough to be called a *matroid*. A **matroid** is a set with an abstract notion of "independence" satisfying three axioms: the empty set is independent; subsets of independent sets are independent; and the **exchange axiom** — if $B_1, B_2$ are independent with $|B_1| < |B_2|$, then some element of $B_2$ can be added to $B_1$ keeping it independent. Linear independence in a vector space is a matroid (the *linear* or *vector* matroid); algebraic independence over $k$ is also a matroid (the *algebraic matroid*), and the equality of cardinalities of maximal independent sets — the rank of the matroid — is precisely the statement that transcendence degree is well-defined. From this viewpoint, "transcendence basis" is "basis of the algebraic matroid" and $\operatorname{trdeg}_k L$ is "rank". The additivity in towers is the rank formula for the contraction/restriction of a matroid. One does not need matroid language to use the definitions, but it explains *why* the exchange lemma — proved by hand for vector spaces and again for transcendence — is the same lemma twice: both independence notions are instances of one abstract structure.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The cleanest compression: **algebraic independence is linear independence after you allow multiplication, and transcendence degree is the dimension that results.** Vectors are independent when no $\sum c_i v_i = 0$; elements are algebraically independent when no $p(x_i) = 0$; bases and dimension follow formally in both cases from an exchange lemma.

**True name:** the true name of "$x_1, \dots, x_n$ are algebraically independent" is "**the evaluation map $k[T_1, \dots, T_n] \to A$ is injective**", equivalently "$k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is a polynomial ring". This is the form you reach for in proofs: to show independence, show evaluation has zero kernel; to *use* independence, treat $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ as a free polynomial ring with all the rigidity that entails (it is a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|UFD]], it is [[Def - Integral Closure and Normal Domain|integrally closed]], its only units are nonzero scalars). The "no nonzero $p$ vanishes" form is for recognising independence in the wild; the "injective evaluation" form is for manipulation.

The notion also packages a piece of **Galois / field theory**: a transcendence basis splits any field extension $k \subseteq L$ into a *purely transcendental* part $k \subseteq k(B)$ (where $k(B)$ is a field of rational functions, the function field of $\mathbb{A}^{|B|}$) followed by an *algebraic* part $k(B) \subseteq L$. This is the algebraic analogue of writing a map of varieties as a fibration over affine space (transcendental directions) followed by a finite cover (algebraic directions) — exactly the geometry [[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether normalization]] makes explicit.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the indeterminates themselves.** In $A = k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ the elements $T_1, \dots, T_n$ are algebraically independent essentially by definition: $p(T_1, \dots, T_n) = 0$ in the polynomial ring means $p$ is the zero polynomial. So $\operatorname{trdeg}_k k(T_1, \dots, T_n) = n$, and the rational function field $k(T_1, \dots, T_n)$ has transcendence degree $n$. This is the prototype of "$n$ free coordinates".

**Is an instance — a single transcendental.** Over $k = \mathbb{Q}$, the real number $\pi$ is transcendental (Lindemann), so $\{\pi\}$ is a transcendence basis of $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ and $\operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb{Q}} \mathbb{Q}(\pi) = 1$. By contrast $\sqrt{2}$ is *algebraic* (root of $T^2 - 2$), so $\{\sqrt 2\}$ is *not* algebraically independent and $\operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb{Q}} \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2) = 0$.

**Is NOT an instance — $x$ and $x^2$.** For a single transcendental $x$, the pair $\{x, x^2\}$ is *not* algebraically independent: the nonzero polynomial $p(T_1, T_2) = T_2 - T_1^2$ satisfies $p(x, x^2) = x^2 - x^2 = 0$. The evaluation map $k[T_1, T_2] \to k[x]$ has $T_2 - T_1^2$ in its kernel, so it is not injective. This probes the definition: algebraic independence is far stronger than linear independence ($x$ and $x^2$ *are* linearly independent over $k$), and the multiplication is exactly what creates the relation.

**Is NOT an instance — anything over an algebraic extension.** If $L/k$ is algebraic (e.g. $L = \overline{k}$ or $L = \mathbb{C}$ over $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ restricted to algebraic numbers), every element of $L$ is algebraic over $k$, so the only algebraically independent subset is $\varnothing$ and $\operatorname{trdeg}_k L = 0$. Algebraic extensions are "zero-dimensional"; transcendence degree sees only genuinely transcendental directions.

**Corollary — additivity computes towers.** Since $\operatorname{trdeg}_k k(T_1, \dots, T_n) = n$ and an algebraic extension contributes $0$, the function field of any irreducible hypersurface — $\operatorname{Frac}\big(k[T_1, \dots, T_n]/(f)\big)$ for irreducible $f$ — has transcendence degree $n - 1$: dividing by one equation cuts one free coordinate. (This is [[Ex - Noether normalization of a plane curve|exactly the trdeg computation]] for plane curves, where $n = 2$ gives trdeg $1$.)

**Calibration check.** Verify that $T_1, \dots, T_n$ are algebraically independent directly from the definition of the polynomial ring. Confirm that $\{x, x^2\}$ is algebraically dependent by naming the witnessing polynomial. Check that $\operatorname{trdeg}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt 2, \pi)) = 1$ using additivity in the tower $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\pi) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\pi, \sqrt 2)$. If you can also explain why algebraic independence implies linear independence but not conversely, you have separated the two notions correctly.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Dimension of an affine variety *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> For a finitely generated $k$-algebra $A$ that is a domain, the transcendence degree $\operatorname{trdeg}_k \operatorname{Frac}(A)$ equals the **Krull dimension** of $A$ and the **geometric dimension** of the corresponding **affine variety** $X$ with coordinate ring $A$. So transcendence degree is the algebraic definition of the dimension of a variety: $\mathbb{A}^n$ has dimension $n$ because $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ has $n$ algebraically independent generators, and a hypersurface $\{f = 0\}$ in $\mathbb{A}^n$ has dimension $n - 1$. This identity — dimension equals transcendence degree — is one of the foundational theorems organising the **dimension theory** of varieties and schemes, and Noether normalization is the tool that proves it.

> [!tip] The transcendental–algebraic factorisation of a morphism *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> A transcendence basis $B$ of $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$ over $k$ realises the structure map of a variety as a **purely transcendental part** $k \subseteq k(B)$ followed by an **algebraic (finite) part** $k(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Frac}(A)$. Geometrically this is the projection of the variety onto an affine space $\mathbb{A}^{|B|}$ (the free directions) with finite fibres (the bound directions) — the picture that **Noether normalization** upgrades into an honest finite surjection of varieties.
