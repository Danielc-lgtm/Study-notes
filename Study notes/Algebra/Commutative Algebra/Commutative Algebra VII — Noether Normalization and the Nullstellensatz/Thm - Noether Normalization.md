# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Throughout, $k$ is a field and $A \neq 0$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra, $A = k[t_1, \dots, t_\ell]$ for some generators $t_i$. We write $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ for the subalgebra generated by elements $x_i \in A$; these $x_i$ are **algebraically independent** when the evaluation map $k[T_1, \dots, T_n] \to A$, $T_i \mapsto x_i$, is injective ([[Def - Algebraic Independence and Transcendence Degree]]). "$A$ is **finite** over $B$" means $A$ is finitely generated as a $B$-*module* (the strong condition), equivalently — for $A = B[\text{finitely many elements}]$ — that every generator is [[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|integral]] over $B$. A polynomial $f$ is **homogeneous of degree $r$** if every monomial has total degree $r$; $F$ denotes the top-degree homogeneous part of a polynomial $f$. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Noether Normalization).** Let $k$ be a field and $A \neq 0$ a finitely generated $k$-algebra. Then there exist elements $x_1, \dots, x_n \in A$ (with $n \geq 0$), **algebraically independent** over $k$, such that $A$ is a **finite** module over the polynomial subalgebra
> $$A' := k[x_1, \dots, x_n] \cong k[T_1, \dots, T_n].$$
> Equivalently: every finitely generated $k$-algebra is a finite (module-finite) extension of a polynomial ring. The integer $n$ is the [[Def - Algebraic Independence and Transcendence Degree|transcendence degree]] of $A$ (when $A$ is a domain, $n = \operatorname{trdeg}_k \operatorname{Frac}(A)$) and is uniquely determined.

> **Linear-change-of-variables form (infinite $k$).** When $k$ is infinite and $A = k[t_1, \dots, t_\ell]$, the $x_i$ can be taken to be $k$-linear combinations of the $t_j$: there is an invertible matrix $P \in M_{\ell \times \ell}(k)$ (which may be taken upper-triangular with $1$s on the diagonal) such that, setting $(x_1, \dots, x_\ell)^{\mathsf T} = P (t_1, \dots, t_\ell)^{\mathsf T}$, the first $n$ of the $x_i$ are algebraically independent and $A$ is finite over $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. A *general* (generic) $P$ works.

The two forms agree; the second specifies *how* to find the $x_i$ over an infinite field — by a generic linear change of coordinates — while the first is the coordinate-free statement, true over any field (via a polynomial change of variables, Nagata's proof).

# Motivation

Finitely generated algebras over a field are the algebraic objects corresponding to affine varieties, and in that generality they are unwieldy: $A = k[t_1, \dots, t_\ell]/\mathfrak a$ can have complicated ideal structure, mysterious dimension, and no obvious handle. Noether normalization is the structural theorem that tames every one of them at a single stroke. It says: however complicated $A$ looks, it is built on top of an honest polynomial ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ — a ring we understand completely — in the tightest possible way, as a *finite module*. The polynomial ring is the "skeleton"; $A$ is finitely many module-generators of "flesh" hung on it.

Why is this the right notion of "tame"? Because *finite* extensions transfer almost every good property. A finite extension of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian; a finite extension preserves dimension; a finite extension of a domain by a field is a field ([[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|the domain/field criterion]]); finiteness over a polynomial ring makes $A$ a finitely generated module, so linear-algebra and Nakayama-style tools apply. The single most important consequence — proved in the very next breath — is **Zariski's lemma**: if $A$ happens to be a *field*, then it is finite *as a vector space* over $k$, because the polynomial skeleton $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ must itself be a field, forcing $n = 0$. From Zariski's lemma the entire **Nullstellensatz** follows. So Noether normalization is the workhorse underneath the chapter's headline theorems; it is the lemma that makes the geometry–algebra dictionary provable.

Geometrically, the theorem is a statement about *projection*. A variety $X \subseteq \mathbb{A}^\ell$ can be projected onto a linear subspace $\mathbb{A}^n$; for a generic choice of projection direction, the resulting map $X \to \mathbb{A}^n$ is a **finite surjection** — surjective, with finite fibres, mapping closed sets to closed sets. Noether normalization is the precise algebra of "every affine variety is a finite cover of an affine space of the same dimension". The number $n$ of free coordinates after projecting is the dimension of $X$. This is the picture to hold: *flatten the variety onto coordinate space by a generic linear projection, and finitely many sheets cover the target.*

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The theorem's precondition $A$ is "a nonzero finitely generated $k$-algebra". The recognition skill is spotting that an algebra in front of you is of this type and that normalization is the lever.

The first disguised source is **"$A$ is the coordinate ring of an affine variety"**, i.e. $A = k[T_1, \dots, T_\ell]/I(X)$ for an algebraic set $X$. The bridge: a quotient of a polynomial ring is finitely generated as a $k$-algebra (the images of the $T_i$ generate it), so normalization applies. The non-obvious value is that you may now treat *any* coordinate ring as a finite module over a polynomial ring, importing dimension and finiteness. *Example problem:* to prove $\dim X = \operatorname{trdeg}_k \operatorname{Frac}(k[X])$, normalize $k[X]$ over $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ and transfer the chain-of-primes count along the finite extension.

The second disguised source is **"$A$ is a field, finitely generated as a $k$-algebra"**. The bridge: a field is in particular a nonzero finitely generated $k$-algebra, so normalize it. The non-obvious payoff is dramatic — the polynomial skeleton $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ sits inside a field and is integral-closure-related to it, forcing it to be a field too, which can only happen if $n = 0$, so $A$ is *module-finite over $k$*, i.e. a finite field extension. This is [[Thm - Zariski's Lemma|Zariski's lemma]] and the route into the Nullstellensatz. *Example problem:* show that a finitely generated $k$-algebra that is a field is algebraic over $k$.

The third disguised source is **"I need a polynomial subring over which $A$ is module-finite"** — sometimes this is the literal goal (computing integral closures, proving finiteness of normalization, setting up dimension induction). The bridge is that normalization *constructs* exactly such a subring. *Example problem:* in proving the dimension of $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ is $n$, one normalizes a quotient and induces on the transcendence degree.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$A$ is finite over a polynomial ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ with the $x_i$ algebraically independent".

Combine with **the [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|domain/field criterion]]**. If $A$ is a domain and *also* a field, then the subring $A'$, being a domain with $A$ integral (finite) over it and $A$ a field, is itself a field — and a polynomial ring is a field only when $n = 0$. The further result $E$ is **Zariski's lemma**: a finitely generated algebra that is a field is finite-dimensional over $k$. This combination is the single most important use of the theorem.

Combine with **"finite extensions preserve Krull dimension"** ([[Commutative Algebra VIII — Going Up and Going Down|going up / incomparability]]). Since $A$ is finite over $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ and $\dim k[x_1, \dots, x_n] = n$, one gets $\dim A = n = \operatorname{trdeg}_k A$. The further result $E$ is the **dimension = transcendence degree** theorem for finitely generated domains, the foundation of dimension theory. Nonobvious because it equates a purely order-theoretic invariant (longest prime chain) with a field-theoretic one (number of free transcendentals).

Combine with **the Noetherian property**. $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is Noetherian (Hilbert basis), and finite over it implies $A$ is a finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring, hence a Noetherian module, hence $A$ is Noetherian. The further result $E$: normalization gives a second proof that finitely generated algebras are Noetherian, and equips them with the finiteness needed for primary decomposition and the theory of associated primes.

# Why Is It True

The proof is an induction on the number of generators, and the entire idea lives in a single trick: **if your generators satisfy a relation, twist the coordinates so that the relation becomes monic in the last generator.** A monic relation is an integral dependence, and integral dependence is exactly "finite". So each twist converts one algebraic relation into one module-finiteness, peeling off a generator.

**The bolded one-liner: a generic linear change of variables turns the top-degree part of any relation into a nonzero constant times $T_m^r$, making the last generator integral over the rest — so each dependence costs you one generator and buys you finiteness.**

Unpack it. Suppose $A = k[x_1, \dots, x_m]$ and the generators are *not* algebraically independent: some nonzero $f$ has $f(x_1, \dots, x_m) = 0$. We want to show $x_m$ is integral over the subalgebra generated by suitable combinations of the others, so that $A$ becomes finite over a smaller algebra and induction can proceed. Write $f = F + (\text{lower-degree terms})$ where $F$ is the top homogeneous part of degree $r$. Now perform the substitution $x_i \mapsto x_i + c_i x_m$ for $i < m$ (a shear, a linear change of coordinates). After substituting, collect by powers of $x_m$: the coefficient of the *highest* power $x_m^r$ is exactly $F(c_1, \dots, c_{m-1}, 1)$ — a *scalar* in $k$ — and all lower powers have coefficients in the smaller subalgebra. If we can choose the $c_i$ so that this scalar is *nonzero*, then dividing through gives a **monic** polynomial relation for $x_m$ over the subalgebra $k[x_1 - c_1 x_m, \dots, x_{m-1} - c_{m-1}x_m]$: $x_m$ is integral, hence $A$ is finite over that subalgebra, which has one fewer generator. Induction finishes.

The only thing to check is that a good $c$ exists, and this is where "$k$ infinite" enters. The scalar $F(c_1, \dots, c_{m-1}, 1)$ is the value at $(c, 1)$ of the polynomial $F(T_1, \dots, T_{m-1}, 1)$, which is *nonzero* (this is the homogeneity input — [[Ex - Why algebraic closure is needed in the Nullstellensatz|setting the last variable to one in a nonzero homogeneous polynomial leaves a nonzero polynomial]], a step you prove on Example Sheet 1). A nonzero polynomial over an infinite field has a non-root (Schwartz–Zippel / the infinitude of $k$), so a good $c$ exists. *This is why "a general $P$ works": you are avoiding the zero set of one polynomial, and over an infinite field that zero set is a proper subset.* Over a finite field the linear trick can fail, and one replaces the linear substitution by a polynomial one (Nagata) — but the conceptual content is identical: deform coordinates to make the leading term a unit.

The reason the homogeneous *top* part is what matters: integrality is about the *highest*-degree behaviour, the leading coefficient of the relation viewed in $x_m$. The shear sends the leading coefficient to $F(c, 1)$ because only the top-degree monomials of $f$ contribute to the top power of $x_m$ after substitution — lower-degree monomials produce lower powers. So the top homogeneous part $F$ governs whether the relation can be made monic, and homogeneity guarantees $F(\cdot, 1) \neq 0$.

# What Makes This Hard

The conceptual hurdle is seeing that an *algebraic relation* ($f(x) = 0$) can be upgraded to an *integral relation* ($x_m$ monic over a subring) by a change of coordinates — most people do not expect a linear shear to do anything, and miss that it converts the top homogeneous part into the leading coefficient. The non-obvious technical step is that the coefficient of $x_m^r$ after the substitution $x_i \mapsto x_i + c_i x_m$ is exactly $F(c_1, \dots, c_{m-1}, 1)$, and that this is a *nonzero polynomial in the $c_i$* precisely because $F$ is homogeneous (drop homogeneity and $F(\cdot, 1)$ can vanish identically). The most common error is to forget the homogeneity hypothesis or to assume the field is infinite without noting it; the second-most-common is to confuse "finite over $A'$" (module-finite, the strong conclusion) with "finitely generated as an algebra over $A'$" (which is automatic and useless).

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:**
Induct on the minimal number $m$ of $k$-algebra generators of $A$. If the generators are algebraically independent, stop. Otherwise take a relation $f(x_1, \dots, x_m) = 0$, shear the coordinates ($x_i \mapsto x_i + c_i x_m$ for $i < m$) so that $x_m$ becomes integral over the subalgebra spanned by the sheared first $m-1$ generators, making $A$ finite over an $(m-1)$-generator subalgebra; apply the induction hypothesis to that subalgebra and use transitivity of finiteness.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Base case.** If $m = 0$ then $A = k$; take $n = 0$, $A' = A$.
   - *Hint:* Nothing to prove; the empty tuple is vacuously algebraically independent.
   - *Why needed:* Anchors the induction.

2. **Dichotomy.** Either $x_1, \dots, x_m$ are algebraically independent (done, with $n = m$) or there is a nonzero $f$ with $f(x_1, \dots, x_m) = 0$.
   - *Hint:* This is the definition of algebraic (in)dependence.
   - *Why needed:* Splits into the trivial case and the case that drives the induction.

3. **The shear and the leading coefficient.** Substitute $x_i \mapsto x_i + c_i x_m$ ($i < m$); the resulting polynomial $g$ in $x_m$ over $k[x_1 - c_1 x_m, \dots]$ has $x_m^r$-coefficient $F(c_1, \dots, c_{m-1}, 1) \in k$, where $F$ is the top homogeneous part of $f$ and $r = \deg f$.
   - *Hint:* Only top-degree monomials of $f$ feed the top power of $x_m$; expand and collect.
   - *Why needed:* Identifies the obstruction to monicity as a single scalar.

4. **Choose $c$ to make it monic.** $F(T_1, \dots, T_{m-1}, 1)$ is a *nonzero* polynomial (homogeneity), so over the infinite field $k$ there exist $c_i$ with $F(c, 1) \neq 0$; divide by it to get a monic relation, so $x_m$ is integral over $B := k[x_1 - c_1 x_m, \dots, x_{m-1} - c_{m-1}x_m]$.
   - *Hint:* A nonzero polynomial over an infinite field has a non-root (Schwartz–Zippel); homogeneity is what keeps $F(\cdot, 1)$ nonzero.
   - *Why needed:* Converts the algebraic relation into integrality of $x_m$.

5. **Transitivity of finiteness.** $A = B[x_m]$ is finite over $B$ (one integral generator); $B$ has $\leq m-1$ generators, so by induction $B$ is finite over a polynomial ring $A' = k[z_1, \dots, z_n]$; finiteness is transitive, so $A$ is finite over $A'$.
   - *Hint:* Module-finite over module-finite is module-finite ([[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|integral transitivity]]).
   - *Why needed:* Assembles the conclusion.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Setting the last variable to 1 in a nonzero homogeneous polynomial gives a nonzero polynomial
> **Statement:** If $F \in k[T_1, \dots, T_m]$ is nonzero and homogeneous of degree $r$, then $F(T_1, \dots, T_{m-1}, 1) \in k[T_1, \dots, T_{m-1}]$ is nonzero. (Homogeneity is essential: $f = T_1 - T_1$ is a bad non-example only trivially, but e.g. $f = T_1 T_2 - T_1$ is nonzero, non-homogeneous, and $f(T_1, 1) = 0$.)
>
> **Hint:** A homogeneous polynomial is determined by its dehomogenisation: distinct monomials $T^\alpha$ of the same total degree $r$ map to *distinct* monomials $T_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots T_{m-1}^{\alpha_{m-1}}$ under $T_m \mapsto 1$, because $\alpha_m = r - (\alpha_1 + \dots + \alpha_{m-1})$ is recoverable.
>
> **Why needed:** It guarantees the scalar $F(c_1, \dots, c_{m-1}, 1)$ in Step 3 is the value of a *nonzero* polynomial, so a good $c$ can be sought; this is [[Ex - Why algebraic closure is needed in the Nullstellensatz|Example Sheet 1, Q1(b)]].
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Write $F = \sum_{|\alpha| = r} a_\alpha T^\alpha$ with not all $a_\alpha$ zero. Under $T_m \mapsto 1$, the monomial $T^\alpha = T_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots T_m^{\alpha_m}$ becomes $T_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots T_{m-1}^{\alpha_{m-1}}$. Two monomials of degree $r$ collide under this map only if $(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{m-1}) = (\beta_1, \dots, \beta_{m-1})$; but then $\alpha_m = r - \sum_{i<m}\alpha_i = r - \sum_{i<m}\beta_i = \beta_m$, so $\alpha = \beta$. Hence distinct degree-$r$ monomials of $F$ map to distinct monomials, no cancellation occurs, and at least one coefficient $a_\alpha \neq 0$ survives. So $F(T_1, \dots, T_{m-1}, 1) \neq 0$. (Without homogeneity, monomials of different total degrees can collide, e.g. $T_1 T_2$ and $T_1$ both go to $T_1$, allowing cancellation.)

> [!note]- Lemma 2: A nonzero polynomial over an infinite field has a non-root
> **Statement:** If $k$ is infinite and $0 \neq g \in k[T_1, \dots, T_{m-1}]$, then there is $c \in k^{m-1}$ with $g(c) \neq 0$.
>
> **Hint:** Induct on the number of variables; a nonzero one-variable polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots, and $k$ is infinite. (Quantitatively: the zero set in $S^{m-1}$ has at most $d \cdot |S|^{m-2}$ points, where $|S|$ is the size of a finite subset — Schwartz–Zippel.)
>
> **Why needed:** Supplies the existence of the shear parameter $c$ with $F(c, 1) \neq 0$; this is where "$k$ infinite" is used and is [[Ex - Why algebraic closure is needed in the Nullstellensatz|Example Sheet 1, Q1(c)]].
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Induct on $m - 1$. For one variable, $g \in k[T_1]$ nonzero of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots; since $k$ is infinite there is $c_1$ with $g(c_1) \neq 0$. For the inductive step, write $g = \sum_{j} g_j(T_1, \dots, T_{m-2}) T_{m-1}^j$ with some $g_{j_0} \neq 0$. By induction choose $(c_1, \dots, c_{m-2})$ with $g_{j_0}(c) \neq 0$; then $g(c_1, \dots, c_{m-2}, T_{m-1})$ is a nonzero one-variable polynomial, which has a non-root $c_{m-1}$ by the base case. So $g(c_1, \dots, c_{m-1}) \neq 0$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The shear makes the leading coefficient a scalar
> **Statement:** Let $f \in k[T_1, \dots, T_m]$ have degree $r$ and top homogeneous part $F$. After the substitution $T_i \mapsto T_i + c_i T_m$ ($1 \leq i \leq m-1$, $T_m$ fixed), the resulting polynomial $g$, viewed in $T_m$ over $k[T_1, \dots, T_{m-1}]$, has degree $r$ in $T_m$ with leading coefficient $F(c_1, \dots, c_{m-1}, 1) \in k$.
>
> **Hint:** Only the degree-$r$ monomials of $f$ produce $T_m^r$; for a monomial $T^\alpha$ of degree $r$, the substitution sends it to $(c^\alpha) T_m^r + (\text{lower in } T_m)$, and summing gives $F(c, 1) T_m^r$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the computation that identifies the obstruction to integrality as the single scalar $F(c, 1)$, tying Lemmas 1–2 to the proof.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Substitute $T_i = T_i' + c_i T_m$ for $i < m$ (renaming $T_i'$ back to $T_i$ after). A monomial $T_1^{\alpha_1}\cdots T_m^{\alpha_m}$ of $f$ becomes $\prod_{i<m}(T_i + c_i T_m)^{\alpha_i} \cdot T_m^{\alpha_m}$. The highest power of $T_m$ arising is $T_m^{\alpha_1 + \dots + \alpha_m} = T_m^{|\alpha|}$, with coefficient $\prod_{i<m} c_i^{\alpha_i} = c^{\alpha'}$ where $\alpha' = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{m-1})$. So $T_m^r$ receives a contribution exactly from monomials with $|\alpha| = r$, i.e. from $F$, and the total coefficient of $T_m^r$ is $\sum_{|\alpha|=r} a_\alpha \prod_{i<m} c_i^{\alpha_i} = F(c_1, \dots, c_{m-1}, 1)$ (evaluating $F$ at $T_i = c_i$, $T_m = 1$). All other monomials of $f$ have degree $< r$ and contribute only to lower powers of $T_m$. Hence $g$ has degree $r$ in $T_m$ with the claimed leading coefficient.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof (infinite field $k$)
> We prove the theorem for $k$ infinite, by induction on the minimal number $m$ of $k$-algebra generators of $A$. (For finite $k$, replace the linear shear by Nagata's polynomial change of variables; the structure is identical.)
>
> **Step 0 — well-posedness.** $A \neq 0$ is finitely generated, so it has a finite generating set; let $m$ be the minimal size, $A = k[x_1, \dots, x_m]$.
>
> **Base case $m = 0$.** Then $A = k$. Take $n = 0$ and $A' = k = A$; the empty set is vacuously algebraically independent and $A$ is finite (rank $1$) over $A'$.
>
> **Inductive step.** Assume the theorem for all finitely generated $k$-algebras needing $< m$ generators, and let $A = k[x_1, \dots, x_m]$.
>
> *Case 1: $x_1, \dots, x_m$ are algebraically independent.* Then $A \cong k[T_1, \dots, T_m]$, and we take $n = m$, $A' = A$. Done.
>
> *Case 2: there is a relation.* Choose $0 \neq f \in k[T_1, \dots, T_m]$ with $f(x_1, \dots, x_m) = 0$; let $r = \deg f$ and $F$ its top homogeneous part. By **Lemma 1**, $F(T_1, \dots, T_{m-1}, 1) \neq 0$; by **Lemma 2**, since $k$ is infinite there exist $c_1, \dots, c_{m-1} \in k$ with $F(c_1, \dots, c_{m-1}, 1) \neq 0$. Define $g(T_1, \dots, T_m) := f(T_1 + c_1 T_m, \dots, T_{m-1} + c_{m-1}T_m, T_m)$. By **Lemma 3**, $g$ has degree $r$ in $T_m$ over $k[T_1, \dots, T_{m-1}]$ with leading coefficient the nonzero scalar $\lambda := F(c, 1)$.
>
> Set $y_i := x_i - c_i x_m$ for $i < m$, and $B := k[y_1, \dots, y_{m-1}] \subseteq A$. Then $x_i = y_i + c_i x_m$, so substituting into $f(x_1, \dots, x_m) = 0$ gives
> $$g(y_1, \dots, y_{m-1}, x_m) = f(x_1, \dots, x_m) = 0.$$
> Dividing $g$ by $\lambda$, the relation $\lambda^{-1} g(y, x_m) = 0$ exhibits $x_m$ as a root of a **monic** polynomial of degree $r$ in $x_m$ with coefficients in $B$. Hence $x_m$ is [[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|integral]] over $B$, and $A = B[x_m]$ is a finite $B$-module.
>
> Now $B$ is generated by the $m - 1$ elements $y_1, \dots, y_{m-1}$, so by the induction hypothesis there exist algebraically independent $z_1, \dots, z_n \in B$ with $B$ finite over $A' := k[z_1, \dots, z_n] \cong k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$. By **transitivity of finiteness** ($A$ finite over $B$, $B$ finite over $A'$ implies $A$ finite over $A'$ — [[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|integral transitivity]]), $A$ is finite over $A'$. This completes the induction.
>
> Finally, the $x_i = z_i$ are algebraically independent and $n = \operatorname{trdeg}_k A$: a finite extension is algebraic, so $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$ is algebraic over $\operatorname{Frac}(A') = k(z_1, \dots, z_n)$, whence $\operatorname{trdeg}_k \operatorname{Frac}(A) = \operatorname{trdeg}_k k(z_1, \dots, z_n) = n$ by additivity of transcendence degree. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Invariant theory and the Hilbert finiteness of rings of invariants.** For a finite group $G$ acting linearly on $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$, the ring of invariants $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]^G$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra (Noether's bound), and Noether normalization realises it as a finite module over a polynomial subring generated by a *homogeneous system of parameters*. The application is nonobvious because it converts an abstract finiteness statement into the concrete structure "free module over a polynomial ring" (Cohen–Macaulayness in good cases), the starting point of computational invariant theory.

**Number theory: rings of integers as finite modules.** The ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of a number field $K$ is a finite module over $\mathbb{Z}$ — the arithmetic analogue of Noether normalization with $\mathbb{Z}$ in place of $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. While $\mathbb{Z}$ is not a polynomial ring over a field, the *philosophy* (a complicated ring is a finite module over a simple "base") is identical, and the proof that $\mathcal O_K$ is finite over $\mathbb Z$ uses the same integrality machinery. This battle-tests the source "I need a finite-module structure over a simple base".

**Dimension theory and the Hilbert polynomial.** Computing the dimension of a projective variety reduces, via normalization of the affine cone's coordinate ring, to reading off $n$ from a finite extension of $k[x_0, \dots, x_n]$. The application is nonobvious because the geometric "dimension" (longest chain of subvarieties) is computed by an algebraic projection whose target dimension is manifestly $n$; finiteness of the projection is what transfers the dimension count. This is the route to **$\dim = \operatorname{trdeg}$**.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Zariski's Lemma|Zariski's Lemma]]** — the immediate and most important corollary. Apply normalization to a finitely generated $k$-algebra $A$ that *happens to be a field*: $A$ is finite over $A' = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, but a polynomial ring is a field only when $n = 0$, because for $n \geq 1$ the indeterminate $x_1$ is not invertible (its inverse would be a polynomial $p$ with $x_1 p = 1$, impossible by degree). The criterion [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|that a domain with a field integral extension is itself a field]] forces $n = 0$, so $A$ is finite-dimensional over $k$. Zariski's lemma is normalization plus the domain/field criterion.

- **[[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|Integral and finite extensions]]** — the technical engine. Normalization is the assertion that the *algebraic* relations among generators can be repackaged as *integral* (module-finite) relations after a coordinate change; "finite over $A'$" is the conclusion, and every step uses that an integral generator gives a finite module and that finiteness is transitive.

- **[[Def - Algebraic Independence and Transcendence Degree|Transcendence degree]]** — the invariant the theorem computes. The number $n$ of polynomial coordinates equals $\operatorname{trdeg}_k \operatorname{Frac}(A)$ (for $A$ a domain), because a finite extension is algebraic and so does not change transcendence degree. Normalization is thus the bridge from the *abstract* dimension (trdeg) to a *concrete* finite cover of $\mathbb{A}^n$.

- **[[Commutative Algebra VIII — Going Up and Going Down|Going up and dimension]]** — the downstream amplifier. Finite (integral) extensions satisfy lying-over, going-up, and incomparability, so prime chains in $A$ and in $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ have the same maximal length: $\dim A = n$. Normalization plus going-up is how dimension theory gets off the ground.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Finite surjection of a variety onto affine space *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Geometrically, Noether normalization is the statement that **every affine variety $X$ admits a finite surjective morphism $\pi : X \to \mathbb{A}^n$ onto affine space**, realised as a generic linear projection. "Finite" means the corresponding ring map $k[T_1, \dots, T_n] \hookrightarrow k[X]$ is module-finite; it forces $\pi$ to be **surjective** (a finite map is closed and dominant, hence onto), **quasi-finite** (every fibre $\pi^{-1}(y)$ is a finite set of points), and **dimension-preserving** (each irreducible component of $X$ maps onto an irreducible set of the same dimension $n$). The number of sheets is generically the degree of the field extension $k(X)/k(\mathbb{A}^n)$. This is the precise content of "a $d$-dimensional variety is a branched finite cover of $\mathbb{A}^d$", and it is the geometric backbone of dimension theory: the dimension of $X$ is the dimension of the affine space it finitely covers.

> [!tip] Every variety, up to finite cover, is affine space; every irreducible variety embeds as a hypersurface +1 *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Two further geometric facts fall out. First, projecting one dimension *too far* — mapping $k[T_1, \dots, T_{d+1}] \to k[X]$ for $X$ of dimension $d$ — gives a finite but no longer injective map, whose image is an **irreducible hypersurface** in $\mathbb{A}^{d+1}$: so every $d$-dimensional irreducible variety is birational to a hypersurface (the **primitive element theorem** in geometric dress), useful because a hypersurface is cut out by a *single* polynomial. Second, the existence of the finite projection is what makes **Cohen–Macaulay** and **Serre's normality** criteria tractable: a finitely generated algebra is a finite module over a polynomial ring, and freeness of that module is the Cohen–Macaulay condition. Noether normalization is the doorway from "abstract finitely generated algebra" to "concrete finite module over a polynomial ring", where the structural theory of varieties lives.
