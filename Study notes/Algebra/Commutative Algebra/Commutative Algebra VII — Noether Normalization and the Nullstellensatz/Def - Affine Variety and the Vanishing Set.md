# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Throughout this chapter we fix a field $k$ and an **algebraically closed** field $\Omega \supseteq k$ (an algebraically closed field is one in which every nonconstant polynomial has a root; the standing example is $k = \mathbb{Q}$ or $k = \mathbb{R}$ inside $\Omega = \mathbb{C}$, or $k = \Omega = \mathbb{C}$). We write $\Omega^n$ for the set of $n$-tuples $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ with $x_i \in \Omega$ — this is **affine $n$-space**, written $\mathbb{A}^n$ when we view it geometrically. For $f \in k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ and $x \in \Omega^n$, $f(x) \in \Omega$ is the value obtained by substituting $x_i$ for $T_i$. For a prime number we use $p$; for a point we use $x$; the maximal ideal of a point $x \in k^n$ is $\mathfrak{m}_x = (T_1 - x_1, \dots, T_n - x_n)$. The radical of an ideal is $\sqrt{I} = \{f : f^m \in I \text{ for some } m \geq 1\}$. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

This is a compound page: it defines two interlocking notions — the **vanishing set** $V(S)$ of a set of polynomials and the resulting **(affine) algebraic set / variety** — because the vanishing operation is the construction and the algebraic set is its output; neither is usable without the other.

# Axiom Motivation

The single idea here is to **make geometry out of solutions to polynomial equations.** You are handed a collection $S$ of polynomials in $n$ variables, and you ask: what is the set of common zeros? That set lives in $\Omega^n$, and we call it $V(S)$. A circle is $V(T_1^2 + T_2^2 - 1)$; a line is $V(a T_1 + b T_2 + c)$; a pair of crossing lines is $V(T_1 T_2)$; the twisted cubic is the common zero set of $T_2 - T_1^2$ and $T_3 - T_1^3$. The point of the definition is that *every* shape cut out by polynomial equations is captured uniformly, and that the operation $S \mapsto V(S)$ has just enough structure to be the closed sets of a topology and to set up a dictionary with algebra.

**Why $V$ takes a set (or an ideal), not a single polynomial.** One might define a variety as the zero set of one equation, but most interesting geometry needs several: a point in $\Omega^2$ is the simultaneous solution of $T_1 = a$ and $T_2 = b$, not of any single polynomial. So $V$ must accept a whole set $S$ and return the *common* zeros, $V(S) = \{x : f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in S\}$. The first structural observation forces a simplification: $V(S)$ depends only on the *ideal* $\mathfrak{a} = (S)$ generated by $S$, because any $\Omega$-linear-combination $\sum g_i f_i$ of vanishing polynomials also vanishes. So $V(S) = V((S))$, and we lose nothing by always feeding $V$ an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]]. This is the first half of the algebra–geometry bridge: the *geometric* object $V(\mathfrak a)$ is controlled by the *algebraic* object $\mathfrak a$. Were $V$ defined on single polynomials, the dictionary would break — the geometry of "several equations" has no single-polynomial encoding.

**Why algebraically closed $\Omega$ — the non-negotiable hypothesis.** Take the equation $T^2 + 1 = 0$ over $k = \mathbb{R}$. As a real equation it has *no solution*: $V(T^2 + 1) = \varnothing$ in $\mathbb{R}^1$. Yet the ideal $(T^2 + 1)$ is a perfectly good proper ideal — it is even maximal. So over $\mathbb{R}$ the map "ideal $\mapsto$ its zero set" loses information catastrophically: a maximal ideal can have empty zero set, and the dictionary between ideals and geometry collapses. The cure is to work over an algebraically closed field $\Omega$, where every nonconstant polynomial has a root, so a proper ideal always has a nonempty zero set (this is the **weak Nullstellensatz**). Over $\Omega = \mathbb{C}$, $V(T^2 + 1) = \{i, -i\}$ is nonempty, and the equations recover their solutions. The hypothesis "$\Omega$ algebraically closed" is not a convenience; without it [[Thm - The Weak Nullstellensatz|the weak Nullstellensatz]] fails and the whole correspondence between varieties and ideals breaks. We keep the smaller field $k$ around to specify which coefficients we allow (a "$k$-algebraic set" is one cut out by polynomials with coefficients in $k$), but we always look for solutions in the bigger closed field $\Omega$.

**Why this gives a topology, and what the axioms of $V$ are.** The operation $V$ has three formal properties, and they are exactly what is needed to make the $V(\mathfrak a)$ the **closed sets** of a topology — the **Zariski topology** — on $\Omega^n$. First, $V(0) = \Omega^n$ and $V(1) = \varnothing$: the zero ideal vanishes everywhere, the unit ideal nowhere. Second, $V$ turns *sums* of ideals into *intersections* of sets: $V\big(\sum_\alpha \mathfrak a_\alpha\big) = \bigcap_\alpha V(\mathfrak a_\alpha)$, so arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed. Third, $V$ turns *products* (or intersections) of ideals into *unions* of sets: $V(\mathfrak a \cap \mathfrak b) = V(\mathfrak a \mathfrak b) = V(\mathfrak a) \cup V(\mathfrak b)$, so *finite* unions of closed sets are closed. These three facts are the closed-set axioms of a topology, and they are dictated, not chosen — they follow immediately from "a product vanishes iff a factor does" and "everything in a sum vanishes iff each summand does". Defining $V$ on ideals rather than sets is what makes these identities clean; this is why the ideal, not the bare generating set, is the right input.

**Why "variety" sometimes means "irreducible" and sometimes not.** Terminology varies: many authors reserve **variety** for an *irreducible* algebraic set (one not a union of two smaller ones — see [[Def - Irreducible Algebraic Set]]) and use **algebraic set** for the general case. We will use "algebraic set" for any $V(\mathfrak a)$ and reserve "variety" for the irreducible ones when the distinction matters, flagging it explicitly. The reason the distinction exists at all is the third axiom above: $V(\mathfrak a \mathfrak b) = V(\mathfrak a) \cup V(\mathfrak b)$ means algebraic sets can break into pieces, and the indecomposable pieces — the irreducible components — are the genuine geometric atoms. An algebraic set is a finite union of varieties, just as an integer is a product of primes.

# The Definition

Fix a field $k$ and an algebraically closed extension $\Omega \supseteq k$, and an integer $n \geq 0$.

## The vanishing set

For a subset $S \subseteq k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$, the **vanishing set** (or **zero locus**) of $S$ is
$$V(S) = \{\, x \in \Omega^n : f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in S \,\} \subseteq \Omega^n.$$
If $\mathfrak{a} = (S)$ is the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] generated by $S$, then $V(\mathfrak{a}) = V(S)$.

## Algebraic sets and varieties

A subset $X \subseteq \Omega^n$ is a **$k$-algebraic set** (an algebraic set *defined over $k$*) if $X = V(S)$ for some $S \subseteq k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$. When $k = \Omega$ one drops the prefix and speaks simply of an **algebraic set** of $\Omega^n$. An **affine variety** is an irreducible algebraic set (see [[Def - Irreducible Algebraic Set]]); some authors use "variety" for any algebraic set.

## Formal properties of $V$

The operation $V$ satisfies, for ideals $\mathfrak a, \mathfrak b, \mathfrak a_\alpha$ of $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$:

1. $V(0) = \Omega^n$, and $V(1) = V(k[T_1, \dots, T_n]) = \varnothing$.
2. $V$ is **inclusion-reversing**: $\mathfrak a \subseteq \mathfrak b \implies V(\mathfrak a) \supseteq V(\mathfrak b)$.
3. $\displaystyle V\Big(\sum_\alpha \mathfrak a_\alpha\Big) = \bigcap_\alpha V(\mathfrak a_\alpha)$ (arbitrary intersections).
4. $V(\mathfrak a \cap \mathfrak b) = V(\mathfrak a \mathfrak b) = V(\mathfrak a) \cup V(\mathfrak b)$ (finite unions).
5. $V(\mathfrak a) = V(\sqrt{\mathfrak a})$: the vanishing set depends only on the [[Def - Radical of an Ideal and the Nilradical|radical]].

By (1), (3), (4) the sets $V(\mathfrak a)$ are the closed sets of a topology on $\Omega^n$, the **Zariski topology**.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The structural way to see $V$ is as one half of an *antitone Galois connection* between two posets. On the algebra side sits the poset of ideals of $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ ordered by inclusion; on the geometry side sits the poset of subsets of $\Omega^n$ ordered by inclusion. The map $V$ goes from algebra to geometry, and its partner $I$ (the [[Def - The Coordinate Ring and the Ideal of a Set|ideal of a set]]) goes back; both reverse inclusions, and they satisfy the Galois-connection inequalities $\mathfrak a \subseteq I(V(\mathfrak a))$ and $X \subseteq V(I(X))$. A Galois connection always cuts down to an *order-reversing bijection* between its "closed" objects on each side — and identifying those closed objects is exactly the content of the [[Thm - The Nullstellensatz Correspondence (radical ideals and varieties)|Nullstellensatz correspondence]]: the closed ideals are the radical ideals, the closed subsets are the algebraic sets. So this page defines one arrow of the connection; the chapter's main theorem computes which objects it restricts to a bijection between. This is the same formal pattern as the Galois correspondence between subgroups and subfields, and as the closure operators of point-set topology.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The cleanest compression: **$V$ is "take the common solutions", packaged so that algebra acts on geometry.** Feeding it an ideal rather than a polynomial is what makes "intersection of solution sets = sum of ideals" and "union = product of ideals" come out clean.

**True name:** the true name of $V(\mathfrak a)$ is "**the set of points where every function in $\mathfrak a$ vanishes**", and the true name of the Zariski-closed sets is "**the simultaneous solution sets of polynomial systems**". The operational reflex is: a geometric condition phrased with "and" (intersection) corresponds to adding ideals; "or" (union) corresponds to multiplying or intersecting ideals; and shrinking the ideal *grows* the variety.

This is the algebraic-geometry incarnation of a pattern you have already met in [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|localization]]: there, the closed sets of $\operatorname{Spec} R$ were $V(I) = \{\mathfrak p : I \subseteq \mathfrak p\}$, primes *containing* $I$. The present $V$ is the "classical points" version of the same construction — over an algebraically closed field the points of $\Omega^n$ correspond to maximal ideals (the **weak Nullstellensatz**), and $V(\mathfrak a) \subseteq \Omega^n$ is the set of those maximal ideals containing $\mathfrak a$. So the Zariski topology on $\Omega^n$ is the restriction of the Zariski topology on $\operatorname{Spec} k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ to its closed (maximal) points.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — a single point.** For $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n) \in k^n$, the singleton $\{x\}$ is $V(\mathfrak{m}_x)$ where $\mathfrak{m}_x = (T_1 - x_1, \dots, T_n - x_n)$: a point $y$ vanishes on all $T_i - x_i$ exactly when $y_i = x_i$ for all $i$, i.e. $y = x$. So points are algebraic sets, cut out by the linear equations $T_i = x_i$.

**Is an instance — affine space and the empty set.** All of $\Omega^n = V(0)$ and $\varnothing = V(1)$ are algebraic; they are the trivial closed sets of the Zariski topology, the analogues of $X$ and $\varnothing$ in any topology.

**Is an instance — a hypersurface and a union.** $V(T_1 T_2) = V(T_1) \cup V(T_2)$ is the union of the two coordinate axes in $\Omega^2$, illustrating property (4): the product ideal $(T_1)(T_2) = (T_1 T_2)$ gives the union of the two lines. This is a *reducible* algebraic set — it is two varieties glued at the origin.

**Is NOT an instance — an open disc, or any "thick" set.** The set $\{x \in \mathbb{C}^2 : |x| < 1\}$ is not algebraic: a nonzero polynomial has a zero set of *measure zero* (it is a proper closed subset), so it can never equal an open set. More starkly, *no infinite proper subset of $\Omega^1$ is algebraic except by being all of $\Omega^1$*: a nonzero $f \in k[T]$ has finitely many roots, so the proper algebraic subsets of the line are exactly the finite sets. The Zariski topology is far coarser than the metric topology — its closed sets are "thin".

**Is NOT an instance (over a non-closed field) — the empty set from a maximal ideal.** Over $k = \Omega = \mathbb{R}$ (which is *not* algebraically closed), $V(T^2 + 1) = \varnothing$ even though $(T^2 + 1)$ is a proper (indeed maximal) ideal. This violates the weak Nullstellensatz and is exactly why the standing hypothesis requires $\Omega$ algebraically closed; it is the running counterexample of [[Ex - Why algebraic closure is needed in the Nullstellensatz]].

**Corollary — $V$ kills radicals.** Since $f^m(x) = f(x)^m$ and $\Omega$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]], $f^m$ vanishes at $x$ iff $f$ does; hence $V(\mathfrak a) = V(\sqrt{\mathfrak a})$. Geometry cannot distinguish an ideal from its radical — the first sign that the *radical* ideals are the geometrically meaningful ones.

**Calibration check.** Verify $V(T_1 T_2) = V(T_1) \cup V(T_2)$ and $V(T_1, T_2) = \{(0,0)\}$ directly. Confirm that the proper algebraic subsets of $\Omega^1$ are precisely the finite sets, using that a nonzero one-variable polynomial has finitely many roots. Explain why $V(\mathfrak a) = V(\sqrt{\mathfrak a})$ uses that $\Omega$ has no zero divisors. If you can also say why $V(T^2+1) = \varnothing$ over $\mathbb{R}$ but $= \{\pm i\}$ over $\mathbb{C}$, you have located the role of algebraic closure.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The Zariski topology and affine space $\mathbb{A}^n$ *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The closed sets $V(\mathfrak a)$ make $\Omega^n$ into a topological space, **affine space $\mathbb{A}^n$**, whose topology — the **Zariski topology** — is the coarsest in which all polynomials are continuous. Its closed sets are the algebraic sets; its points (over an algebraically closed field) are the maximal ideals. This is the basic local model of all of algebraic geometry: an **affine variety** is a Zariski-closed subset of some $\mathbb{A}^n$, and a general **variety** or **scheme** is glued from affine pieces, exactly as a manifold is glued from charts.

> [!tip] The functor of points and Spec *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Defining $V$ on ideals rather than points is the shadow of the deeper move of modern algebraic geometry: replace the *set* $\Omega^n$ of classical points by the **prime spectrum** $\operatorname{Spec} k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ of *all* prime ideals. The classical points $\Omega^n$ sit inside $\operatorname{Spec}$ as the closed points; the extra non-closed points are the **generic points** of irreducible subvarieties. Working with $\operatorname{Spec}$ instead of $\Omega^n$ is what frees algebraic geometry from needing an algebraically closed field at all, and is the starting point of **scheme theory** (developed from the localization side in [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization]]).
