# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Here $k$ is a field and $K$ is a field that is finitely generated *as a $k$-algebra* — meaning $K = k[t_1, \dots, t_\ell]$ for finitely many $t_i \in K$ (a polynomial expression generating $K$, *not* a rational-function generation). The crucial distinction: "$K$ finitely generated as a $k$-algebra" is *much* stronger than "$K$ finitely generated as a field extension" (the latter allows denominators, written $K = k(t_1, \dots, t_\ell)$). We write $\dim_k K$ for the dimension of $K$ as a $k$-vector space; "$K$ is a **finite** $k$-algebra" means $\dim_k K < \infty$, equivalently $K/k$ is a finite [[Def - Algebraic Independence and Transcendence Degree|algebraic]] field extension. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

---

# Statement

> **Proposition (Zariski's Lemma).** Let $k \subseteq K$ be fields with $K$ finitely generated **as a $k$-algebra**. Then $K$ is a **finite** $k$-algebra: $\dim_k K < \infty$. Equivalently, $K$ is a finite (hence [[Def - Algebraic Independence and Transcendence Degree|algebraic]]) field extension of $k$.

> **Contrapositive / sharp form.** If $K/k$ is a field extension and some element of $K$ is *transcendental* over $k$, then $K$ is *not* finitely generated as a $k$-algebra. In particular the rational function field $k(T)$ is **not** a finitely generated $k$-algebra, even though it is generated by one element as a *field*.

The lemma is the precise statement that "finitely generated $k$-algebra that is a field $\implies$ finite algebraic extension". It is the technical core from which the [[Thm - The Weak Nullstellensatz|weak Nullstellensatz]] — and hence the whole Nullstellensatz — is deduced.

---

# Motivation

The lemma resolves a subtle trap that catches everyone once. You know that $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ is generated *as a field* by the single transcendental $\pi$. It is therefore tempting to think such a field is "small" or "finitely built". Zariski's lemma says the opposite about *algebra* generation: $\mathbb{Q}(\pi)$ — equivalently the rational function field $\mathbb{Q}(T)$ — is **not** finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra. To get all of $\mathbb{Q}(T)$ as polynomial expressions in finitely many elements, you would need infinitely many denominators, and no finite set of generators provides them. The lemma pins down exactly which fields *are* finitely generated as algebras over $k$: precisely the finite algebraic extensions, nothing transcendental allowed. The presence of even one transcendental element destroys finite algebra-generation.

Why does this matter so much that it earns a name and a place at the heart of the chapter? Because it is the engine of the **weak Nullstellensatz**. There, one has a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ of $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ and the quotient $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]/\mathfrak m$ — a field, and *finitely generated as a $k$-algebra* (the images of the $T_i$ generate it). Zariski's lemma instantly says this field is a *finite* extension of $k$. Over an algebraically closed $k = \Omega$, a finite extension of $\Omega$ is $\Omega$ itself, so the quotient field is $\Omega$, the $T_i$ map to scalars $a_i \in \Omega$, and $\mathfrak m = (T_1 - a_1, \dots, T_n - a_n)$ is the ideal of a *point*. That single deduction — "maximal ideals are points" — *is* the weak Nullstellensatz, and it rests entirely on Zariski's lemma. The lemma is the bridge from "finitely generated algebra" to "finite-dimensional vector space", and finite-dimensionality is what lets you embed the field into $\Omega$.

The lemma also explains *why algebraic closure enters*. Zariski's lemma alone gives a finite extension $K/k$; it is only when $k$ is algebraically closed that "finite extension" collapses to "equals $k$", giving the clean point-correspondence. Over $\mathbb{R}$, Zariski's lemma still holds — $\mathbb{R}[T]/(T^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$ is a finite (degree-$2$) extension — but $\mathbb{C} \neq \mathbb{R}$, so the maximal ideal $(T^2+1)$ is *not* a point of $\mathbb{R}^1$. The failure of the Nullstellensatz over non-closed fields is precisely the gap between "finite extension" (Zariski) and "trivial extension" (needs closure).

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition is "$K$ is a field that is finitely generated *as a $k$-algebra*". The recognition skill is spotting that a field-quotient is algebra-finitely-generated.

The first disguised source is **"$\mathfrak m$ is a maximal ideal of a finitely generated $k$-algebra $A$"**. Then $A/\mathfrak m$ is a field, and it is finitely generated as a $k$-algebra because $A$ is and quotients of finitely generated algebras are finitely generated. The bridge from "maximal ideal" to "Zariski's lemma applies" is this two-step: maximal $\Rightarrow$ field quotient ([[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients|via quotients]]), and quotient of f.g. algebra $\Rightarrow$ f.g. algebra. *Example problem:* prove $A/\mathfrak m$ is a finite extension of $k$ for any maximal ideal of a finitely generated algebra — the algebraic backbone of the [[Thm - The Weak Nullstellensatz|weak Nullstellensatz]].

The second disguised source is **"$A$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra that is a domain, and you want to know if it can be a field without being finite over $k$"**. Zariski's lemma answers: no. The bridge is direct — if such a domain is a field, it is finitely generated as an algebra and the lemma forces finiteness. *Example problem:* a finitely generated $k$-algebra is a field iff it is a finite-dimensional $k$-vector space.

The third disguised source is **"a field $K$ is sandwiched $k \subseteq K \subseteq L$ with $L$ finitely generated as a $k$-algebra"**. This is the Artin–Tate situation; one shows $K$ is itself finitely generated as an algebra. The bridge uses the lemma to control intermediate fields. *Example problem:* the Artin–Tate lemma, that an intermediate ring of a finite extension of a Noetherian algebra is finitely generated (ES3 Q4).

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$\dim_k K < \infty$", i.e. $K/k$ is finite algebraic.

Combine with **"$k$ is algebraically closed"**. A finite extension of an algebraically closed field is the field itself, so $K = k$. The further result $E$ is that **every maximal ideal of $\Omega[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ is $(T_1 - a_1, \dots, T_n - a_n)$** — the weak Nullstellensatz. This is the single most consequential combination: Zariski + closure = points are maximal ideals.

Combine with **the embedding of finite extensions into $\Omega$**. Field theory guarantees every finite extension $L/k$ embeds $k$-linearly into any algebraically closed $\Omega \supseteq k$. So for a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ of $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ (not necessarily $k$ closed), the finite field $k[T]/\mathfrak m$ embeds into $\Omega$, producing a $k$-algebra homomorphism $k[T_1, \dots, T_n] \to \Omega$ with kernel $\mathfrak m$ — i.e. a *point* of $\Omega^n$ where everything in $\mathfrak m$ vanishes. The further result $E$ is the weak Nullstellensatz over a general base field $k$ (with $\Omega \supseteq k$ closed): $V(\mathfrak a) = \varnothing \iff 1 \in \mathfrak a$.

Combine with **transcendence degree**. Zariski's lemma is the statement $\operatorname{trdeg}_k K = 0$ for $K$ a finitely generated algebra that is a field. The further result $E$, fed back through Noether normalization, is that a finitely generated $k$-algebra has *finite* transcendence degree equal to its Krull dimension — the lemma is the base case ($\dim 0$) of dimension theory.

# Why Is It True

The proof is four lines once you have [[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether normalization]] and the [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|domain/field criterion]] — the lemma is *designed* to be their corollary.

**The bolded one-liner: a field that is finitely generated as an algebra sits finitely on top of a polynomial skeleton, and a polynomial ring can only be a field if it has zero variables — so the skeleton is just $k$, and the field is finite over it.**

Walk through it. Apply Noether normalization to $K$ (a nonzero finitely generated $k$-algebra): $K$ is *finite* over a polynomial subalgebra $A' = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ with the $x_i$ algebraically independent. Now $K$ is a field, and $A'$ is a domain sitting inside it with $K$ integral (finite) over $A'$. The domain/field criterion says: *if a domain $A'$ has an integral extension $K$ that is a field, then $A'$ is itself a field*. (The criterion's content: integral over a field is a field, and conversely integral-and-field forces the base to be a field — units propagate downward through integral relations.) So $A' = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is a field. But a polynomial ring in $n \geq 1$ variables is *never* a field — the variable $x_1$ has no inverse, since $x_1 \cdot p(x) = 1$ is impossible by comparing degrees. Hence $n = 0$, $A' = k$, and "$K$ finite over $A'$" reads "$K$ finite over $k$": $\dim_k K < \infty$. The whole lemma is "normalize, then kill the variables".

The conceptual reason the lemma is true: **being a field is incompatible with having free polynomial directions.** A free direction (a transcendental $x_i$) is something you can never invert with a polynomial, so a field cannot contain a polynomial subring with free directions over which it is merely *finite* — finiteness would force the free direction to be invertible, a contradiction. Algebra-generation by finitely many elements bounds the number of free directions (Noether normalization), and being a field bounds it to zero. The two bounds meet at $n = 0$.

# What Makes This Hard

The genuine difficulty is conceptual, not technical: realising that **"finitely generated as an algebra" and "finitely generated as a field" are wildly different**, and that the lemma is exactly the statement separating them. The proof itself is short, but it depends on two prior results ([[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether normalization]] and the [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|domain/field criterion]]) that do the heavy lifting; the common error is to try to prove Zariski's lemma "from scratch" (e.g. the Artin–Tate argument) and get lost, when the slick route is to invoke normalization. The non-obvious step is the *last* one — that a polynomial ring in $\geq 1$ variables is not a field — which is trivial but easy to forget to state, and is precisely where the integer $n$ is pinned to $0$.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:**
Normalize $K$ over a polynomial subring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$; use that $K$ is a field together with the domain/field criterion to conclude the polynomial subring is a field; observe a polynomial ring is a field only with zero variables; conclude $K$ is finite over $k$.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Normalize.** $K$ is a nonzero finitely generated $k$-algebra, so by [[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether normalization]] it is finite over $A' = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, $x_i$ algebraically independent.
   - *Hint:* This is the whole reason normalization was proved first.
   - *Why needed:* Produces a polynomial skeleton with $K$ module-finite over it.

2. **The skeleton is a field.** $A'$ is a domain, $K \supseteq A'$ is a field, and $K$ is integral over $A'$; by the [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|domain/field criterion]], $A'$ is a field.
   - *Hint:* "Domain with an integral field extension is a field"; units pull back through integral relations.
   - *Why needed:* Turns the structural fact into a statement about the polynomial ring $A'$.

3. **Kill the variables.** A polynomial ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ with $n \geq 1$ is not a field ($x_1$ is not invertible), so $n = 0$ and $A' = k$.
   - *Hint:* Compare degrees in $x_1 \cdot p = 1$.
   - *Why needed:* Forces the skeleton to be the ground field.

4. **Conclude finiteness.** $K$ finite over $A' = k$ means $\dim_k K < \infty$.
   - *Hint:* "Finite over $k$" is the definition of finite-dimensional.
   - *Why needed:* This is the statement.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: A polynomial ring in at least one variable is not a field
> **Statement:** For $n \geq 1$, $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is not a field; specifically $x_1$ has no multiplicative inverse.
>
> **Hint:** A degree argument: if $x_1 p = 1$, compare degrees in $x_1$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the step that forces $n = 0$ once the skeleton is known to be a field.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Suppose $x_1 p(x_1, \dots, x_n) = 1$ for some polynomial $p$. The left side, as a polynomial in $x_1$, has degree $\geq 1$ in $x_1$ (since $x_1$ is a factor and $p \neq 0$), while the right side has degree $0$. This is a contradiction, so $x_1$ is not invertible and $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is not a field. (More generally, the units of $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ are exactly the nonzero scalars $k^\times$.)

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Reduction to the domain/field criterion
> **Statement:** If a domain $A'$ has a ring extension $K \supseteq A'$ that is a field and is integral over $A'$, then $A'$ is a field.
>
> **Hint:** This is the [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|domain/field criterion from the integral extensions chapter]]; for $0 \neq a \in A'$, write down the integral relation satisfied by $a^{-1} \in K$ and clear denominators to put $a^{-1} \in A'$.
>
> **Why needed:** It transfers "field-ness" from $K$ down to the polynomial skeleton $A'$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $0 \neq a \in A'$. Since $K$ is a field, $a^{-1} \in K$; since $K$ is integral over $A'$, $a^{-1}$ satisfies a monic relation $(a^{-1})^d + c_{d-1}(a^{-1})^{d-1} + \dots + c_0 = 0$ with $c_i \in A'$. Multiply through by $a^{d-1}$:
> > $$a^{-1} = -\big(c_{d-1} + c_{d-2}a + \dots + c_0 a^{d-1}\big) \in A'.$$
> > So every nonzero element of $A'$ is invertible in $A'$: $A'$ is a field. (This is exactly the proof of the domain/field criterion, included for self-containedness.)

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $k \subseteq K$ be fields with $K$ finitely generated as a $k$-algebra.
>
> **Step 0 — applicability.** $K \neq 0$ (it is a field) and is finitely generated as a $k$-algebra, so [[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether normalization]] applies.
>
> **Step 1.** By Noether normalization there are algebraically independent $x_1, \dots, x_n \in K$ such that $K$ is finite — hence integral — over $A' := k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$.
>
> **Step 2.** $A'$ is an integral domain (a polynomial ring over a field), $K \supseteq A'$ is a field, and $K$ is integral over $A'$. By **Lemma 2** (the [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|domain/field criterion]]), $A' = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is a field.
>
> **Step 3.** By **Lemma 1**, a polynomial ring in $n \geq 1$ variables is not a field. Hence $n = 0$, so $A' = k$.
>
> **Step 4.** Therefore $K$ is finite over $A' = k$, i.e. $\dim_k K < \infty$. A finite $k$-algebra that is a field is a finite [[Def - Algebraic Independence and Transcendence Degree|algebraic]] extension of $k$. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Transcendence of $e$ and the structure of $\mathbb{Q}(e)$.** Zariski's lemma gives a clean structural reason that $\mathbb{Q}(e)$ (with $e$ transcendental) is not a finitely generated $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra: any transcendental forces infinitely many algebra-generators. The application is nonobvious because it converts a hard analytic transcendence fact into a soft algebraic finiteness obstruction — *if* $\mathbb{Q}(e)$ were a finitely generated $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra, $e$ would be algebraic.

**Hilbert's Nullstellensatz over $\mathbb{C}$ as a corollary.** The whole point of Zariski's lemma is that, combined with $k = \Omega = \mathbb{C}$ algebraically closed, it gives "maximal ideals of $\mathbb{C}[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ are points". Use it to prove that two polynomial systems over $\mathbb{C}$ with no common solution generate the unit ideal. The application battle-tests the source "field quotient of a finitely generated algebra".

**Embedding finite extensions and counting points mod $p$.** For a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ of $\mathbb{F}_p[T_1, \dots, T_n]$, Zariski's lemma says $\mathbb{F}_p[T]/\mathfrak m$ is a finite field $\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$; the point lives in $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}^n$ but has coordinates in $\mathbb{F}_{p^d}$. This underlies point-counting and the **reduction mod $p$** technology ([[Ex - Why algebraic closure is needed in the Nullstellensatz|ES3 Q8]]). The application is nonobvious because it ties the *degree* of the extension to the *field of definition* of the point.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether Normalization]]** — the lemma's sole engine. Zariski's lemma is normalization specialised to the case where $A$ is a field: the polynomial skeleton $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is forced to be a field, hence $n = 0$. Without normalization the lemma is a genuinely hard theorem (its other proofs, Artin–Tate or the Noetherian induction of Atiyah–Macdonald, are longer); with normalization it is a four-line corollary.

- **[[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|Integral extensions and fields]]** — the second ingredient. The criterion "$A'$ domain, $K$ a field integral over $A'$ $\Rightarrow$ $A'$ a field" is what transfers field-ness from $K$ down to the skeleton. It is the same mechanism that makes "[[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions|integral over a field is a field]]" work, run in the harder direction.

- **[[Thm - The Weak Nullstellensatz|The Weak Nullstellensatz]]** — the lemma's payoff. Feed a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ of $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ to Zariski's lemma: the quotient field $k[T]/\mathfrak m$ is finite over $k$, hence embeds into an algebraically closed $\Omega$, producing a point $x \in \Omega^n$ with $\mathfrak m = \ker(\text{ev}_x)$. The weak Nullstellensatz is this deduction, and the strong form follows by the Rabinowitsch trick.

- **[[Def - Algebraic Independence and Transcendence Degree|Transcendence degree]]** — the invariant interpretation. Zariski's lemma is "$\operatorname{trdeg}_k K = 0$ for $K$ a finitely generated algebra that is a field", the zero-dimensional anchor of the equation $\dim = \operatorname{trdeg}$ for finitely generated domains.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Maximal ideals are points; the residue field is the ground field *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Zariski's lemma is the algebraic statement underneath the geometric slogan **"the points of an affine variety are its maximal ideals"**. For a finitely generated algebra $A = k[X]$ over algebraically closed $k$, every maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ has residue field $A/\mathfrak m$ a finite extension of $k$, hence equal to $k$ — so $\mathfrak m$ is the ideal of functions vanishing at an honest point $x \in X$, namely $\mathfrak m_x$. The bijection $X \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{mSpec} A$, $x \mapsto \mathfrak m_x$, is the foundation of the variety–ring dictionary, and it is *exactly* what fails over a non-closed field, where residue fields can be proper finite extensions (e.g. $\mathbb{R}[T]/(T^2+1) = \mathbb{C}$, a "point" whose residue field is bigger than $\mathbb{R}$).

> [!tip] The Jacobson property and the Nullstellensatz for general base fields *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> Zariski's lemma makes $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ a **Jacobson ring**: every prime ideal is the intersection of the maximal ideals containing it, equivalently the nilradical and Jacobson radical agree for every quotient. This is the abstract property that makes "radical = intersection of maximal ideals" ([[Ex - The radical as the intersection of maximal ideals containing it|ES3 Q5]]) hold, and it is the form of the Nullstellensatz that survives over non-closed and arithmetic base rings (e.g. $\mathbb{Z}$), feeding into arithmetic geometry and the theory of Jacobson schemes.
