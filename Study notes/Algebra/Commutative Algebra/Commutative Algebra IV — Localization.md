# Notation Registry

**Standing convention.** Throughout these commutative algebra notes, *ring* means a commutative ring with a multiplicative identity $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$. *Module* means a unital module. The single new operation of this chapter is **localization**, written $S^{-1}(-)$; it is the act of formally adjoining inverses for a chosen set of elements, and it is the algebraic incarnation of *zooming in* on part of a geometric space. The whole chapter is organised around one slogan, stated again in Motivation: **localizing a ring at a prime is restricting attention to a neighbourhood of the corresponding point.**

- $R, A, B$ — commutative rings with $1$
- $S \subseteq R$ — a **multiplicative subset**: $1 \in S$ and $S$ is closed under multiplication
- $S^{-1}R$ — the **localization** of $R$ at $S$; its elements are formal fractions $\tfrac{r}{s}$ with $r \in R$, $s \in S$
- $S^{-1}M$ — the localization of an $R$-module $M$ at $S$; an $S^{-1}R$-module with elements $\tfrac{m}{s}$
- $\iota = \iota_{S^{-1}R} : R \to S^{-1}R$, $r \mapsto \tfrac{r}{1}$ — the **localization map** (canonical, generally neither injective nor surjective)
- $R_f := S^{-1}R$ for $S = \{1, f, f^2, \dots\}$ — $R$ with the single element $f$ inverted
- $R_{\mathfrak{p}} := (R \setminus \mathfrak{p})^{-1}R$ for $\mathfrak{p}$ a prime ideal — **$R$ localized at $\mathfrak{p}$**; a local ring
- $\mathfrak{p}, \mathfrak{q}$ — prime ideals; $\mathfrak{m}, \mathfrak{n}$ — maximal ideals
- $\operatorname{Spec} R$ — the set of all prime ideals of $R$ (the **prime spectrum**); $\operatorname{mSpec} R$ — the set of maximal ideals
- $(R, \mathfrak{m})$ — a **local ring** with unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$
- $\kappa(\mathfrak{p}) := R_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathfrak{p}R_{\mathfrak{p}} = \operatorname{Frac}(R/\mathfrak{p})$ — the **residue field** of $R$ at $\mathfrak{p}$
- $\mathfrak{a}^e = S^{-1}\mathfrak{a}$ — the **extension** of an ideal $\mathfrak{a} \trianglelefteq R$ to $S^{-1}R$; $\mathfrak{b}^c = \iota^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ — the **contraction** of an ideal $\mathfrak{b} \trianglelefteq S^{-1}R$
- $\sqrt{I} = \{ r \in R : r^n \in I \text{ for some } n \geq 1\}$ — the **radical** of an ideal $I$; $\operatorname{nil} R = \sqrt{(0)}$ — the **nilradical**
- $\operatorname{Ann}_R(m) = \{r \in R : rm = 0\}$ — the **annihilator** of $m$; $\operatorname{Ann}_R(M)$ for a whole module
- $(I : x) = \{r \in R : rx \in I\}$ — the **ideal quotient** (colon ideal)
- $M_{\mathfrak{p}} := (R \setminus \mathfrak{p})^{-1}M$ — the **localization of $M$ at $\mathfrak{p}$**, a module over $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **localization is the construction of $\mathbb{Q}$ from $\mathbb{Z}$, run on an arbitrary ring and with an arbitrary set of denominators, and it is the precise algebraic meaning of "looking at a space near a point".** You already know one instance intimately. Given an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] $R$, the [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ is built by inverting *every* non-zero element, formally adjoining a reciprocal $\tfrac{1}{s}$ for each $s \neq 0$ and declaring $\tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{c}{d}$ when $ad = bc$. Localization keeps the construction but drops two restrictions: $R$ need not be a domain, and you need not invert everything — you choose a set $S$ of elements to make invertible. The price of allowing zero-divisors is one subtlety in the equivalence relation (a "clearing" element $u \in S$ appears), and the reward is a tool of enormous flexibility.

Why would you want to invert only *some* elements? Because inverting a well-chosen set is the same as discarding the part of the ring — equivalently, the part of the associated geometric space — that you do not currently care about, and keeping a magnified view of the rest. Two choices of $S$ dominate everything and they are worth holding in mind from the start. First, invert the single element $f$ and its powers: the localization $R_f = \{1, f, f^2, \dots\}^{-1}R$ is "$R$ with $f$ made a unit", and geometrically it is the ring of functions on the open set where $f \neq 0$ — you have thrown away the locus $f = 0$ and can now divide by $f$ freely. Second, and this is the conceptual heart of the subject, fix a [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime ideal]] $\mathfrak{p}$ and invert *everything outside* it: since $R \setminus \mathfrak{p}$ is multiplicatively closed exactly because $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime, the localization $R_{\mathfrak{p}} = (R \setminus \mathfrak{p})^{-1}R$ is "$R$ with everything not in $\mathfrak{p}$ inverted". This ring sees only what happens *near the point $\mathfrak{p}$*: every function that is non-zero at the point becomes invertible, so only the vanishing behaviour at $\mathfrak{p}$ survives. The result is a **local ring** — a ring with a single maximal ideal — and a single point's worth of algebra is exactly a local ring. This is the dictionary the entire chapter installs:

$$\text{localize at } \mathfrak{p} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \text{restrict to a neighbourhood of the point } \mathfrak{p}; \qquad \text{local ring} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \text{the algebra of a single point.}$$

The deep reason localization matters is that it converts *global* questions into *local* ones, and local questions are easier. A property of a module is called **local** if it holds for $M$ exactly when it holds for every localization $M_{\mathfrak{p}}$; the chapter's punchline — being zero, being injective, being surjective, being exact, being flat are all local — means you may check these one prime at a time, in the simplest possible rings. This is the same move that pervades geometry and analysis: understand a manifold by understanding each tangent space, understand a function by its germ at each point. Localization is how that move is made rigorous in algebra, and it is why one says, with only mild exaggeration, that **commutative algebra is the local study of algebraic geometry.**

The machinery has three layers, and the chapter is built in that order. First the *construction and its universal property*: $S^{-1}R$ is the universal ring receiving a map from $R$ that turns $S$ into units, and this property — not the fractions — is what makes it canonical. Second, *how ideals behave under localization*: the operations of extension $\mathfrak{a} \mapsto \mathfrak{a}^e = S^{-1}\mathfrak{a}$ and contraction, culminating in the single most geometric statement of the chapter — the [[Thm - Prime Ideals of a Localization|prime ideals of $S^{-1}R$ are exactly the primes of $R$ disjoint from $S$]]. Localizing does not scramble the prime spectrum; it cuts out a clean piece of it. Third, *local properties*: the [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local–global principle]] that lets global facts be verified prime-by-prime.

This chapter assumes you are fluent with [[Def - Ideal|ideals]], [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient rings]], [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime and maximal ideals]] and the [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients|domain/field-quotient dictionary]] from the [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings]] notes, with [[Def - Module|modules]], [[Def - Submodule|submodules]], [[Def - Quotient Module|quotients]] and [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finite generation]] from the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|Modules]] notes, and that you have met the [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] construction. Tensor products and exactness (the previous two chapters, [[Commutative Algebra II — Tensor Products]] and [[Commutative Algebra III — Flatness and Exactness]]) are used in the proofs that localization is exact, but the fraction-level statements can be read without them.

# Concept Map

## §4.1–4.2 The Construction and the Universal Property

- **[[Def - Multiplicative Set and Localization]]**
	- A **multiplicative subset** $S \subseteq R$ contains $1$ and is closed under products. The **localization** $S^{-1}M$ of an $R$-module $M$ is the set of fractions $\tfrac{m}{s}$ ($m \in M$, $s \in S$) under the relation $\tfrac{m_1}{s_1} = \tfrac{m_2}{s_2}$ iff $u(s_2 m_1 - s_1 m_2) = 0$ for some $u \in S$; the clearing factor $u$ is the one new feature beyond the field-of-fractions case, forced because $S$ may contain zero-divisors. With $\tfrac{m_1}{s_1} + \tfrac{m_2}{s_2} = \tfrac{s_2 m_1 + s_1 m_2}{s_1 s_2}$ it is an $S^{-1}R$-module, and $S^{-1}R$ (taking $M = R$) is a ring via $\tfrac{r_1}{s_1}\cdot\tfrac{r_2}{s_2} = \tfrac{r_1 r_2}{s_1 s_2}$. The two key cases are $R_f$ (invert one element) and $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ (invert the complement of a prime).
	- $\tfrac{r}{s} = 0$ in $S^{-1}R$ iff $ur = 0$ for some $u \in S$; hence $\ker(\iota : R \to S^{-1}R) = \{r : us r = 0 \text{ for some } u\in S\}$, so $\iota$ is injective exactly when $S$ has no zero-divisors, and $S^{-1}R = 0$ exactly when $0 \in S$.

- **[[Thm - Universal Property of Localization]]**
	- $(S^{-1}R, \iota)$ is universal among rings receiving a map from $R$ that sends $S$ into the units: for any ring $B$ and homomorphism $f : R \to B$ with $f(s) \in B^\times$ for all $s \in S$, there is a unique $h : S^{-1}R \to B$ with $h \circ \iota = f$, namely $h(\tfrac{r}{s}) = f(s)^{-1}f(r)$. This characterises $S^{-1}R$ up to unique isomorphism and is the reason it deserves the name "the universal solution to inverting $S$". The map $\iota$ is always an epimorphism of rings but rarely surjective — the model is $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$.

- **[[Thm - Localization is Exact and the Localization is Flat]]**
	- There is a natural isomorphism $S^{-1}R \otimes_R M \cong S^{-1}M$, so localizing is the same as extending scalars along $\iota$; consequently $S^{-1}(-)$ is an **exact functor** and $S^{-1}R$ is a **flat** $R$-module. Exactness means $S^{-1}(-)$ preserves kernels, images, injections, surjections and short exact sequences — fractions never create or destroy relations beyond those forced by the denominators. This single fact powers nearly every later computation; flatness of $\mathbb{Q}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ is the prototype.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The structure sheaf of an affine scheme *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The localizations $R_f$, one for each $f \in R$, are the rings of functions on the **basic open sets** $D(f) = \{\mathfrak{p} : f \notin \mathfrak{p}\}$ of $\operatorname{Spec} R$. Gluing these together — using that $R_f$ and $R_g$ both localize further to $R_{fg}$ — produces the **structure sheaf** $\mathcal{O}_{\operatorname{Spec} R}$, the object that upgrades the bare set $\operatorname{Spec} R$ into a *scheme*. Localization is the affine-local model for the entire global theory: a scheme is what you get by gluing the spectra of rings along their localizations.

## §4.3 Extension and Contraction of Ideals

- **[[Def - Extension and Contraction of Ideals]]**
	- For any ring map $f : A \to B$, the **contraction** of $\mathfrak{b} \trianglelefteq B$ is $\mathfrak{b}^c = f^{-1}(\mathfrak{b}) \trianglelefteq A$ (always an ideal; the contraction of a prime is prime), and the **extension** of $\mathfrak{a} \trianglelefteq A$ is $\mathfrak{a}^e = (f(\mathfrak{a})) \trianglelefteq B$, the ideal *generated* by $f(\mathfrak{a})$ (the image itself is usually not an ideal). One always has $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$ and $\mathfrak{b} \supseteq \mathfrak{b}^{ce}$, and $e, c$ restrict to a bijection between contracted ideals of $A$ and extended ideals of $B$. For the localization map, $\mathfrak{a}^e = S^{-1}\mathfrak{a}$ explicitly, and every ideal of $S^{-1}R$ is extended.

- **[[Thm - Prime Ideals of a Localization]]**
	- Extension and contraction set up a bijection $\{\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec} R : \mathfrak{p} \cap S = \varnothing\} \leftrightarrow \operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R)$, $\mathfrak{p} \mapsto \mathfrak{p}^e = S^{-1}\mathfrak{p}$. Localizing therefore *carves out* the subset of the prime spectrum consisting of primes that avoid $S$, preserving all inclusions: $\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R)$ is a sub-poset of $\operatorname{Spec} R$. For $S = R\setminus\mathfrak{p}$ this leaves exactly the primes $\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$, so $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ has unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}R_{\mathfrak{p}}$; for $S = \{f^n\}$ it leaves the primes not containing $f$, i.e. the open set $D(f)$.

- **[[Def - The Prime Spectrum (Spec)]]**
	- $\operatorname{Spec} R$ is the set of all prime ideals of $R$, made a topological space by the **Zariski topology**, whose closed sets are $V(I) = \{\mathfrak{p} : I \subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}$. Points are primes; **closed points** are maximal ideals; a non-maximal prime $\mathfrak{p}$ is a **generic point** whose closure is the irreducible set $V(\mathfrak{p})$. Every ring becomes the ring of functions on its own spectrum, with the "value" of $r$ at $\mathfrak{p}$ being its image in the residue field $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$. This is the object localization manipulates: $R_f$ restricts to the open $D(f)$, $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ restricts to a neighbourhood of $\mathfrak{p}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Basic open sets and the Zariski topology *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The sets $D(f) = \operatorname{Spec}(R_f)$ form a basis for the Zariski topology on $\operatorname{Spec} R$, and the [[Thm - Prime Ideals of a Localization|prime-correspondence theorem]] is what makes $\operatorname{Spec}(R_f) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Spec} R$ a homeomorphism onto $D(f)$. This is the precise sense in which **inverting $f$ = restricting to the open set where $f \neq 0$**. The full Zariski-topology / Nullstellensatz story is developed in [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

## §4.4–4.5 Local Rings and Local Properties

- **[[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field]]**
	- A ring is **local** if it has exactly one maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$; one writes $(R, \mathfrak{m})$, and then $R \setminus \mathfrak{m} = R^\times$ (every non-unit lies in $\mathfrak{m}$). The localization $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is always local with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}R_{\mathfrak{p}}$, and its **residue field** is $\kappa(\mathfrak{p}) = R_{\mathfrak{p}}/\mathfrak{p}R_{\mathfrak{p}} = \operatorname{Frac}(R/\mathfrak{p})$ — the field "of values at the point $\mathfrak{p}$". Contrast $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ (sees primes $\subseteq \mathfrak{p}$, i.e. a neighbourhood) with $R/\mathfrak{p}$ (sees primes $\supseteq \mathfrak{p}$, i.e. the subvariety through $\mathfrak{p}$); they meet in $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$.

- **[[Def - Radical of an Ideal and the Nilradical]]**
	- The **radical** $\sqrt{I} = \{r : r^n \in I \text{ for some } n\}$ is the largest ideal with the same zero-set as $I$; an ideal is **radical** if $\sqrt I = I$, equivalently $R/I$ is **reduced** (no non-zero nilpotents). The **nilradical** $\operatorname{nil} R = \sqrt{(0)}$ collects the nilpotent elements. Radicals are exactly the ideals expressible as intersections of primes — geometrically, $\sqrt I$ is the largest ideal cutting out the same variety as $I$, the algebraic shadow of "functions cannot tell $f$ from $f^2$ on a reduced space".

- **[[Thm - The Radical is the Intersection of the Primes Above It]]**
	- $\sqrt{I} = \bigcap_{I \subseteq \mathfrak{p}} \mathfrak{p}$, the intersection of all primes containing $I$; in particular $\operatorname{nil} R = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec} R}\mathfrak{p}$. The "$\subseteq$" direction is trivial (a prime swallows roots); the "$\supseteq$" direction is the only place the chapter *needs* a prime to exist, and it is produced by localizing at $x$ and using that a non-zero ring has a maximal ideal — a beautiful application of "$R_x \neq 0 \iff x$ not nilpotent". Geometrically: a function vanishes on all of $V(I)$ iff a power of it lies in $I$.

- **[[Thm - The Local-Global Principle]]**
	- For a module $M$: $M = 0$ iff $M_{\mathfrak{p}} = 0$ for all primes $\mathfrak{p}$ iff $M_{\mathfrak{m}} = 0$ for all maximal $\mathfrak{m}$; and likewise *injective*, *surjective*, *exact*, and *flat* are all **local properties** — true globally iff true after localizing at every (maximal) prime. The engine is "being zero is local", proved with annihilators: if $M_{\mathfrak{m}} = 0$ for all $\mathfrak{m}$ then no annihilator $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ can sit inside a maximal ideal, forcing $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = R$, i.e. $m = 0$. This is the formal license for "check it one point at a time". Freeness, by contrast, is *not* local.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Passing to a germ / the stalk at a point *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is the **stalk** of the structure sheaf at the point $\mathfrak{p}$: it is the ring of germs of functions defined on *some* neighbourhood of $\mathfrak{p}$, two functions identified when they agree near $\mathfrak{p}$. Its unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}R_{\mathfrak{p}}$ consists of germs vanishing at the point, and $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$ is the field of values there. The [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local–global principle]] is then the statement that a sheaf is zero iff all its stalks vanish — the foundational local-to-global mechanism of sheaf theory, here proved by pure algebra.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Vector bundles are not detected locally *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> That **freeness is not a local property** is the algebraic seed of the existence of non-trivial vector bundles: a finitely generated projective module is locally free (free at every $\mathfrak{p}$) yet need not be free, exactly as a vector bundle is locally trivial yet globally twisted. The non-free locally-free ideal $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5}) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is a line bundle on $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ with non-trivial class — the **Serre–Swan** correspondence between projective modules and vector bundles in embryo.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring goals of this chapter cluster into five types. The first is **identifying a localization concretely** — recognising $R_f$, $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$, or a general $S^{-1}R$ as a ring you already know ($\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, the dyadic rationals $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$, a residue field, a product collapsing to one factor). The universal property is the standard route: exhibit a map out of $R$ inverting $S$ and check it is universal, or use the quotient presentation $R[T]/(uT - 1)$. The second is **computing the prime spectrum of a localization** — listing which primes survive, which is settled mechanically by "$\mathfrak{p}$ survives iff $\mathfrak{p}\cap S = \varnothing$" via the [[Thm - Prime Ideals of a Localization|prime-correspondence theorem]]. The third, and the chapter's signature, is **proving a property is local** — reducing a statement about a module or map over a general ring to the same statement over local rings, then solving the local case (often with [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama]]). The fourth is **establishing radical/nilpotence facts** — showing an element is nilpotent, that a radical equals an intersection of primes, that being reduced is local; the lever is almost always "$R_x \neq 0 \iff x$ is not nilpotent" plus the existence of maximal ideals. The fifth is **tracking ideals through extension and contraction** — proving formulas like $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}^{\,e} = \sqrt{\mathfrak{a}^e}$, or that a $\mathfrak p$-primary ideal is contracted from $R_{\mathfrak p}$. These are the natural targets because localization is fundamentally a *comparison* tool: every theorem says "this feature of $R$ is faithfully reflected (or cleanly modified) in $S^{-1}R$".

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A multiplicative set is named** — most often $S = R\setminus\mathfrak{p}$ (localize at a prime, get a local ring), $S = \{f^n\}$ (invert one element, restrict to $D(f)$), or $S = R \setminus \bigcup \mathfrak p_i$; recognising *which* $S$ is in play tells you which geometric picture applies. **A property is to be checked on a module** — and the move is to localize at every maximal ideal, because [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|the property is local]] and local rings are simpler. **A prime or maximal ideal is given** — switching on the local ring $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$, its residue field $\kappa(\mathfrak{p})$, and the fact that $R\setminus\mathfrak p$ is multiplicatively closed. **The ring is local $(R,\mathfrak m)$** — so non-units $=\mathfrak m$, and Nakayama becomes available for finitely generated modules. **An element is suspected nilpotent / an ideal radical** — routing through $R_x$ and $\sqrt I = \bigcap \mathfrak p$. The discipline is to match source to target along the chapter's spine: a named $S$ routes through the universal property or the prime correspondence; a "prove it's local" target routes through the local–global principle to the residue field; a nilpotence question routes through $R_x$ to a maximal ideal. The [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every localization problem is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

1. **Clear denominators with a single element of $S$.** Two fractions are equal, $\tfrac{m_1}{s_1} = \tfrac{m_2}{s_2}$, exactly when some $u \in S$ kills the cross-difference: $u(s_2 m_1 - s_1 m_2) = 0$. So any equation in $S^{-1}M$ can be pulled back to an *honest* equation in $M$ at the cost of multiplying by one clearing factor $u \in S$. *Trigger:* you have a relation among fractions and want a relation among numerators. *Pattern:* "$\tfrac{m}{s} = 0 \Rightarrow um = 0$ for some $u \in S$" — then work in $M$.

2. **Invoke the universal property instead of fractions.** To build a map *out of* $S^{-1}R$, do not chase fractions: produce a map $f : R \to B$ sending $S$ to units, and read off the unique extension $h(\tfrac{r}{s}) = f(s)^{-1}f(r)$ from [[Thm - Universal Property of Localization]]. To *identify* $S^{-1}R$ with a known ring $A$, show $A$ has the same universal property. *Trigger:* "construct a homomorphism from a localization" or "prove two localizations are isomorphic". *Pattern:* check that everything in $S$ already becomes a unit in the target, then the map exists for free.

3. **Localize an exact sequence.** Because $S^{-1}(-)$ is [[Thm - Localization is Exact and the Localization is Flat|exact]], you may apply it term-by-term to any exact sequence and stay exact; in particular $S^{-1}(\ker f) = \ker(S^{-1}f)$, $S^{-1}(\operatorname{im} f) = \operatorname{im}(S^{-1}f)$, and $S^{-1}(M/N) = S^{-1}M/S^{-1}N$. *Trigger:* a kernel, image, quotient, or submodule appears and you want its localization. *Pattern:* "apply $S^{-1}$ to $0 \to N \to M \to M/N \to 0$".

4. **Reduce a global statement to local rings.** When the target property is local — zero, injective, surjective, exact, flat — replace "prove $P$ for $M$ over $R$" by "prove $P$ for $M_{\mathfrak{m}}$ over $R_{\mathfrak{m}}$ for every maximal $\mathfrak{m}$", via [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle]]. *Trigger:* the property to be shown is on the local list and the ring is complicated. *Pattern:* "it suffices to check after localizing at an arbitrary maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$".

5. **Test for zero with annihilators against maximal ideals.** To show $m = 0$, show its annihilator $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ lies in no maximal ideal, hence equals $R$, hence contains $1$. Localization supplies the annihilator: if $\tfrac{m}{1} = 0$ in $M_{\mathfrak{m}}$ then $um = 0$ for some $u \notin \mathfrak{m}$, so $\operatorname{Ann}(m) \not\subseteq \mathfrak m$. *Trigger:* proving a module or element vanishes. *Pattern:* "$\operatorname{Ann}(m) \subseteq \mathfrak m$ for some maximal $\mathfrak m$ — or else $1 \in \operatorname{Ann}(m)$".

6. **Read primes off the disjointness condition.** The primes of $S^{-1}R$ are exactly the $\mathfrak p$ with $\mathfrak p \cap S = \varnothing$, extended; to find $\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R)$, just list the primes of $R$ avoiding $S$. *Trigger:* "what are the primes / the dimension / the maximal ideal of this localization?" *Pattern:* "$\mathfrak p$ survives $\iff \mathfrak p \cap S = \varnothing$"; for $R_{\mathfrak p}$ the survivors are the primes $\subseteq \mathfrak p$.

7. **Detect nilpotence by collapsing a localization.** $R_f = 0 \iff f$ is nilpotent, and more generally $S^{-1}R = 0 \iff 0 \in S$. To prove $f$ nilpotent, prove $R_f$ is the zero ring (often: show it has no primes); to prove $f$ *not* nilpotent, produce a prime avoiding $f$. *Trigger:* a nilpotence claim, or a need to manufacture a prime ideal avoiding a given element. *Pattern:* "$R_x \neq 0$, so it has a maximal ideal, pulling back to a prime of $R$ missing $x$".

8. **Pass between $R_{\mathfrak p}$, $R/\mathfrak p$, and $\kappa(\mathfrak p)$.** Localization commutes with quotients: $R_{\mathfrak p}/\mathfrak q R_{\mathfrak p} \cong (R/\mathfrak q)_{\mathfrak p}$, and at $\mathfrak q = \mathfrak p$ this is the residue field $\kappa(\mathfrak p) = \operatorname{Frac}(R/\mathfrak p)$. *Trigger:* you need the "value at a point" or want to combine a quotient with a localization. *Pattern:* "tensor with $\kappa(\mathfrak p) = R_{\mathfrak p}/\mathfrak p R_{\mathfrak p}$ to evaluate at the point".

9. **Use that localization commutes with finite operations.** $S^{-1}$ commutes with finite sums, finite intersections, quotients, and tensor products of submodules/modules: $S^{-1}(N + P) = S^{-1}N + S^{-1}P$, $S^{-1}(N \cap P) = S^{-1}N \cap S^{-1}P$, $S^{-1}M \otimes_{S^{-1}R} S^{-1}N \cong S^{-1}(M \otimes_R N)$. *Trigger:* a localization of a built-up module. *Pattern:* "push $S^{-1}$ through the construction".

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming the localization map is injective
> Because $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ is injective, it is tempting to treat $R \to S^{-1}R$, $r \mapsto \tfrac r1$, as an embedding and to identify $R$ with a subring of $S^{-1}R$. This fails precisely when $S$ contains a **zero-divisor**: the kernel is $\{r : ur = 0 \text{ for some } u \in S\}$. Concretely, in $R = \mathbb{Z}/6$ with $S = \{1, 2, 4\}$, the element $3$ maps to $0$ because $2\cdot 3 = 0$; indeed $S^{-1}R \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$. The map is injective **exactly when $S$ contains no zero-divisor**, which is automatic when $R$ is a domain and $0 \notin S$ — that is why the field-of-fractions case lulls you into the wrong habit.

> [!warning] 2. Believing freeness, or being an integral domain, is a local property
> Localization is so good at reflecting properties that one expects *every* reasonable property to be local. Two important ones are not. **Freeness is not local:** over $R = \mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$ every localization is a field (so every module is locally free), yet the ideal $M = \mathbb{C}\times\{0\}$ is not a free $R$-module — $(0,1)$ annihilates it, so it has no basis. **Being a domain is not local either:** the same $R = \mathbb C \times \mathbb C$ has every $R_{\mathfrak p} \cong \mathbb C$ a field (hence a domain), but $R$ itself has zero-divisors $(1,0)(0,1) = 0$. The repair: freeness *does* follow from "locally free" once you add **finitely generated + finitely presented**, where it becomes projectivity; "domain" follows from "locally a domain" once you add that $R$ is **reduced and connected** (irreducible Spec). The general lesson — local data may fail to glue — is exactly what makes vector bundles and schemes interesting.

> [!warning] 3. Treating the image $f(\mathfrak a)$ of an ideal as an ideal
> Under a ring map $f : A \to B$, the contraction $f^{-1}(\mathfrak b)$ is always an ideal, so by symmetry one expects $f(\mathfrak a)$ to be one too. It need not be: for $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$, the image of $2\mathbb{Z}$ is $\{2n : n \in \mathbb Z\}$, which is not closed under multiplication by $\tfrac13 \in \mathbb Q$. That is why the **extension** is *defined* as the ideal *generated* by the image, $\mathfrak a^e = (f(\mathfrak a))$, not the image itself. The asymmetry between $e$ and $c$ — only one is "automatic" — is the source of the one-sided inclusions $\mathfrak a \subseteq \mathfrak a^{ec}$ and $\mathfrak b \supseteq \mathfrak b^{ce}$, which become equalities only for contracted/extended ideals respectively.

> [!warning] 4. Confusing $R_{\mathfrak p}$ with $R/\mathfrak p$
> Both "do something at $\mathfrak p$", so it is tempting to blur them. They are opposite operations. $R/\mathfrak p$ *quotients*, keeping the primes $\supseteq \mathfrak p$: geometrically it is the **subvariety** $V(\mathfrak p)$ cut out by $\mathfrak p$, and it is a domain. $R_{\mathfrak p}$ *localizes*, keeping the primes $\subseteq \mathfrak p$: geometrically it is a **neighbourhood** of the point $\mathfrak p$, and it is a local ring. In $\mathbb Z$ at $\mathfrak p = (p)$: $\mathbb Z/(p) = \mathbb F_p$ is the point's residue field, while $\mathbb Z_{(p)}$ is the infinite local ring of fractions with denominator prime to $p$. They agree only after combining: $\kappa(\mathfrak p) = R_{\mathfrak p}/\mathfrak p R_{\mathfrak p} = \operatorname{Frac}(R/\mathfrak p)$.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any localization problem is to name the *role* localization is playing, because there are only three, and each has a characteristic route.

If the problem **asks you to construct or identify a localization** — to build a map out of $S^{-1}R$, or to recognise some explicitly given ring as $S^{-1}R$ — abandon fractions and reach for the [[Thm - Universal Property of Localization|universal property]]. A map out of $S^{-1}R$ is the *same data* as a map out of $R$ that happens to send $S$ to units; so to define $h : S^{-1}R \to B$, define $f : R \to B$, check $f(S) \subseteq B^\times$, and let the property hand you $h(\tfrac rs) = f(s)^{-1}f(r)$. To prove $S^{-1}R \cong A$, show $A$ receives a universal $S$-inverting map from $R$ — uniqueness of universal objects does the rest. The quotient presentation $R_f \cong R[T]/(fT - 1)$ is the same idea in disguise and is often the cleanest way to compute a single-element localization. The recurring trap here is to try to verify well-definedness of fraction formulas by hand; the universal property exists precisely to spare you that.

If the problem **asks about ideals or primes of a localization**, the controlling fact is that *localization carves a clean piece out of the prime spectrum*. Every ideal of $S^{-1}R$ is extended, so ideals of $S^{-1}R$ correspond to certain ideals of $R$; and the [[Thm - Prime Ideals of a Localization|prime-correspondence theorem]] says the primes are exactly the $\mathfrak p$ with $\mathfrak p \cap S = \varnothing$, with all inclusions preserved. So to find the maximal ideal of $R_{\mathfrak p}$, find the largest prime $\subseteq \mathfrak p$, namely $\mathfrak p$ itself, giving $\mathfrak p R_{\mathfrak p}$; to find $\operatorname{Spec}(R_f)$, list the primes not containing $f$; to compute a dimension after localizing, count chains among the surviving primes. The disjointness condition $\mathfrak p \cap S = \varnothing$ is the whole game, and translating "$S = R\setminus\mathfrak p$" into "survivors are the primes inside $\mathfrak p$" or "$S = \{f^n\}$" into "survivors avoid $f$" is the recognition step.

If the problem **asks you to prove something holds in general by checking it locally**, you are using the [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local–global principle]], and the strategy is a fixed two-step descent. Step one: confirm the property is on the local list (zero, injective, surjective, exact, flat) — if it is, you may replace $R$ by $R_{\mathfrak m}$ and $M$ by $M_{\mathfrak m}$ for an arbitrary maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$, throughout. Step two: solve the now-local problem, where the ring has a unique maximal ideal and the heavy tools ([[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama]], the residue field $\kappa(\mathfrak m)$, "non-units form an ideal") are available. The base case under everything is "being zero is local", proved by the annihilator argument: a non-zero element would have a proper annihilator, hence one inside some maximal ideal, contradicting vanishing of that localization. Almost every "it suffices to check at each prime" argument bottoms out here.

A fourth, more computational, family **concerns radicals and nilpotents**, and these route through the identity $\sqrt I = \bigcap_{I \subseteq \mathfrak p}\mathfrak p$ together with the collapse criterion $R_x = 0 \iff x$ nilpotent. To show an element is nilpotent, show the localization $R_x$ has no prime (so it is the zero ring); to manufacture a prime avoiding a given non-nilpotent $x$, localize at $x$, take a maximal ideal of the non-zero ring $R_x$, and contract. This "localize to force a prime into existence" manoeuvre is the one genuinely non-formal idea in the chapter, and it is worth memorising as a unit.

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "what does $R$ look like near the locus I am inverting away from?"** Inverting $f$ asks what $R$ looks like away from $\{f = 0\}$; inverting $R\setminus\mathfrak p$ asks what $R$ looks like at the single point $\mathfrak p$; checking a property locally asks whether a global feature is already visible point-by-point. Decide which locus you are zooming toward, and the right tool — universal property, prime correspondence, or local–global principle — is determined.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Universal Property of Localization|The universal property]]**: every map out of $S^{-1}R$ is a map out of $R$ inverting $S$. This is the most reused fact because it converts *all* questions about morphisms from a localization into questions about morphisms from $R$ with a side condition, which are concrete. Its standard uses are constructing homomorphisms ($\tfrac rs \mapsto f(s)^{-1}f(r)$), proving two constructions of a localization agree (both satisfy the property, so they are uniquely isomorphic), and recognising a familiar ring as a localization. Reach for it whenever a localization appears as the *source* of a map.

- **[[Thm - Localization is Exact and the Localization is Flat|Exactness of $S^{-1}(-)$ / flatness of $S^{-1}R$]]**: fractions preserve kernels, images, injections, surjections, and short exact sequences. Its reusability is that it lets you *localize any diagram* and keep it exact — so kernels, cokernels, quotients, and submodules all commute with localization. This is the technical backbone of every local-property proof and of the identities $S^{-1}(M/N) = S^{-1}M/S^{-1}N$ and $S^{-1}(\ker f) = \ker S^{-1}f$. Whenever an exact sequence and a localization are both in play, this is the first move.

- **[[Thm - Prime Ideals of a Localization|The prime-correspondence theorem]]**: $\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R) = \{\mathfrak p : \mathfrak p \cap S = \varnothing\}$. This is the geometric workhorse: it says localization restricts the spectrum to a sub-poset, and it is what makes $R_{\mathfrak p}$ local (unique maximal $\mathfrak p R_{\mathfrak p}$) and $R_f$ the ring of the open set $D(f)$. Use it for every question about primes, maximal ideals, dimension, or height after localizing — and, downstream, it is the homeomorphism $\operatorname{Spec} R_f \cong D(f)$ that builds the structure sheaf.

- **[[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|The local–global principle]]**: zero/injective/surjective/exact/flat are local. Its reusability is as a *proof strategy* more than a fact: it licenses "reduce to the local case", which simplifies the ring to one maximal ideal and unlocks Nakayama. The trigger is any of those five targets over a complicated ring. The base lemma "being zero is local" — via annihilators not lying in any maximal ideal — is itself reused directly to prove modules and maps vanish.

- **The collapse criterion $R_x = 0 \iff x$ nilpotent, and $\sqrt I = \bigcap_{I\subseteq\mathfrak p}\mathfrak p$**: these two together are the toolkit for radicals and nilpotence. The first turns "is $x$ nilpotent?" into "does $R_x$ have a prime?"; the second turns "is $f \in \sqrt I$?" into "does $f$ lie in every prime over $I$?". Their joint use — localize at $x$ to force a prime into existence when $x$ is *not* nilpotent — is the standard manufacture of primes, and it reappears whenever a proof needs "choose a prime avoiding this element".

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — localization is restriction to an open set, and a local ring is the algebra of a point.** To a ring $R$ one attaches the space [[Def - The Prime Spectrum (Spec)|$\operatorname{Spec} R$]] of prime ideals, and $R$ becomes the ring of functions on it. Inverting an element $f$ produces $R_f$, the ring of functions on the open subset $D(f) = \{\mathfrak p : f \notin \mathfrak p\}$ where $f$ is non-vanishing — you have literally restricted to the region where division by $f$ is legal. Inverting the complement of a prime $\mathfrak p$ produces the local ring $R_{\mathfrak p}$, which sees only the primes $\subseteq \mathfrak p$ — an arbitrarily small neighbourhood of the point — and whose maximal ideal records the functions vanishing there. The construction that glues the rings $R_f$ over all $f$ into a single sheaf of functions, the **structure sheaf**, upgrades $\operatorname{Spec} R$ to an *affine scheme*; gluing affine schemes gives general schemes. So this chapter is the affine-local model for all of scheme theory, and "commutative algebra is the local study of algebraic geometry" is the literal statement that the geometry is reconstructed from the localizations.

2. **Sheaf theory and analysis — $R_{\mathfrak p}$ is a stalk, and the local–global principle is "a sheaf is zero iff its stalks vanish".** In topology and complex analysis the **germ** of a function at a point is its equivalence class under "agree on some neighbourhood", and the **stalk** is the ring of germs. The local ring $R_{\mathfrak p}$ is exactly this for the structure sheaf: a fraction $\tfrac rs$ with $s \notin \mathfrak p$ is a function defined wherever $s \neq 0$, i.e. near $\mathfrak p$, and two such agree as germs iff they agree after clearing some $u \notin \mathfrak p$ — the localization equivalence relation *is* the germ relation. Under this dictionary the [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local–global principle]] ("$M = 0$ iff every $M_{\mathfrak p} = 0$") becomes the foundational fact of sheaf theory that a section is zero iff all its germs vanish. The algebra proves, by an annihilator argument, what analysis usually takes as a definition.

3. **Number theory — $\mathbb Z_{(p)}$ isolates the prime $p$, and the adeles assemble all localizations.** Localizing $\mathbb Z$ at the prime ideal $(p)$ gives $\mathbb Z_{(p)} = \{\tfrac ab : p \nmid b\}$, the local ring that remembers only the divisibility behaviour at $p$ while making every other prime invertible — the algebraic act of "studying one prime at a time" that organises elementary and algebraic number theory. The residue field is $\kappa((p)) = \mathbb F_p$. Inverting $\{p^n\}$ instead gives $\mathbb Z[\tfrac1p]$, the functions away from $p$. The systematic comparison of a global ring with all its localizations — the local–global philosophy made into a technology — is the **Hasse principle** and, completed $p$-adically, the **adeles**; both are this chapter's "check it at every prime" elevated to the central method of modern number theory.

4. **Linear algebra and module theory — flatness of $S^{-1}R$ is why fractions never break exactness, and projectivity is local freeness.** A flat module is one whose tensor product preserves injections; [[Thm - Localization is Exact and the Localization is Flat|$S^{-1}R$ is flat over $R$]] is the statement that passing to fractions cannot manufacture a linear relation that was not already there. This is the abstract reason that solving a linear system over $R$ and then localizing gives the localized solution set, with no surprises — the same robustness that makes $\mathbb Q$-linear algebra govern $\mathbb Z$-linear algebra up to torsion. Pushed one step further, the failure of *freeness* to be local while *projectivity* is exactly "locally free", and the identification of finitely generated projective modules with vector bundles ([[Def - Free Module|free]] = trivial bundle, projective = locally trivial bundle), is the **Serre–Swan** correspondence — the bridge from this chapter's local–global subtleties to the topology of bundles.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: localization is a microscope, and the magnification is the set you invert.** Every construction in this chapter is one setting of the same instrument. Invert nothing and you have $R$; invert everything non-zero in a domain and you have the field of fractions, the maximal magnification that throws away all arithmetic and keeps only the generic behaviour; invert $\{f^n\}$ and you focus on the open region $f \neq 0$; invert $R\setminus\mathfrak p$ and you focus all the way down to the single point $\mathfrak p$. The deep payoff is that the instrument is *faithful on the prime spectrum*: it never distorts, it only restricts — $\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R)$ is literally a sub-poset of $\operatorname{Spec} R$, the primes that survive being exactly those disjoint from $S$. This is why localization is the right tool for "local" study: it isolates a piece of the geometry without changing what it isolates. The slogan to carry is that **passing to $R_{\mathfrak p}$ does to a ring what passing to a tangent space does to a manifold** — it discards everything except the infinitesimal behaviour at one point, and the unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak p R_{\mathfrak p}$ is the algebraic record of "vanishing at that point".

**The true name of a prime ideal, revisited: it is a point, and localizing at it is standing at the point.** The [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings]] notes gave the true name of a prime ideal as "the kernel of a map to a domain". Localization gives the geometric true name: **a prime ideal is a point of $\operatorname{Spec} R$, and $R_{\mathfrak p}$ is the ring you see when you stand at that point and look only at an arbitrarily small neighbourhood.** This reorganises the two operations on a prime that students constantly confuse. Quotienting by $\mathfrak p$ looks *outward along the subvariety through the point* — it keeps the primes containing $\mathfrak p$, the things $\mathfrak p$ is a face of — and produces a domain $R/\mathfrak p$. Localizing at $\mathfrak p$ looks *inward at the point itself* — it keeps the primes contained in $\mathfrak p$, the things specialising to it — and produces a local ring $R_{\mathfrak p}$. The two are dual halves of "studying $R$ at $\mathfrak p$", and they are reconciled in the residue field $\kappa(\mathfrak p) = R_{\mathfrak p}/\mathfrak p R_{\mathfrak p} = \operatorname{Frac}(R/\mathfrak p)$ — the field of *values at the point*, computed either by localizing then quotienting or quotienting then taking fractions. Knowing which direction a problem looks tells you instantly whether to reach for $R_{\mathfrak p}$ or $R/\mathfrak p$.

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "prove it locally" means "localize at an arbitrary maximal ideal, then use Nakayama".** When a target property is on the local list — zero, injective, surjective, exact, flat — the words "it suffices to check at each maximal ideal" should fire automatically, because [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|the local–global principle]] guarantees the reduction is lossless and the local ring $R_{\mathfrak m}$ is dramatically simpler: it has one maximal ideal, its non-units form that ideal, and finitely generated modules over it are governed by [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama's lemma]] and the residue-field dimension $\dim_{\kappa(\mathfrak m)} M/\mathfrak m M$. The complementary pattern is the manufacture of primes: when a proof needs "a prime ideal avoiding the element $x$", the reaction is "localize at $x$; if $x$ is not nilpotent then $R_x \neq 0$, so it has a maximal ideal, which contracts to a prime of $R$ missing $x$". These two reflexes — *localize at a maximal ideal to simplify*, and *localize at an element to force a prime into existence* — between them solve the large majority of the chapter's problems, and both are pure consequences of the single fact that a non-zero ring has a maximal ideal.
