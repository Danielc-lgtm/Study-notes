# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$; all modules unital. Fix an $R$-module $M$ and write $T_M = M \otimes_R(-)$. A module $N$ is **finitely generated** if it has a finite generating set; for [[Def - Tensor Product of Modules|tensor products]] we use that any element of $M \otimes N$ is a *finite* sum $\sum m_i \otimes n_i$, and any relation already holds in a [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] piece. $I \trianglelefteq R$ denotes an ideal, $IM$ the submodule of $M$ generated by $\{im : i \in I, m \in M\}$, and $\mu_r : R \to R$ multiplication by $r$. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra III — Flatness and Exactness]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Characterization of flat modules).** For an $R$-module $M$, the following are equivalent:
>
> 1. $T_M$ preserves the exactness of *all* exact sequences (i.e. $T_M$ is an exact functor).
> 2. $T_M$ preserves the exactness of all *short* exact sequences.
> 3. $M$ is [[Def - Flat Module|flat]]: $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ is injective for every injective $f : N' \to N$.
> 4. $M$ is **flat for finitely generated modules**: $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ is injective for every injective $f : N \to N'$ with $N, N'$ finitely generated.

> **Companion criterion (Atiyah–Macdonald).** $M$ is flat if and only if, for every finitely generated ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$, the natural map $I \otimes_R M \to IM$, $i \otimes m \mapsto im$, is injective (equivalently an isomorphism). In homological terms, this holds if and only if $\operatorname{Tor}_1^R(R/I, M) = 0$ for all finitely generated $I$.

The cheap implications are $(1) \Rightarrow (2) \Rightarrow (3) \Rightarrow (4)$; the substance is $(4) \Rightarrow (3)$ (reduction to finitely generated modules) and $(2) \Rightarrow (1)$ (splicing).

---

# Motivation

The definition of [[Def - Flat Module|flat]] quantifies over *all* injections $f : N' \to N$ — an unmanageable infinitude, with $N$ ranging over every module of every size. Taken literally, proving a module flat would require checking arbitrarily large $N$. This theorem is what makes flatness *checkable*: it collapses the universal quantifier down to **finitely generated** modules, and further down to **finitely generated ideals**. After this theorem, a flatness proof is a finite computation, and that is what makes the notion usable in practice.

The result also unifies the several faces of flatness that appear in different contexts. Sometimes you meet flatness as "tensoring preserves all exact sequences" (the functorial face, condition 1); sometimes as "preserves short exact sequences" (the homological-algebra face, condition 2, the one that says $T_M$ has no first derived functor); sometimes as the raw "preserves injections" (the definition, condition 3); and sometimes as a finite criterion you can actually verify (condition 4, and the ideal criterion). The theorem says these are one notion, so you may switch freely to whichever face a problem hands you. The equivalence $(2) \Leftrightarrow (1)$ in particular encodes a general principle worth internalizing: *a functor that preserves short exact sequences preserves all exact sequences*, because every exact sequence is spliced together from short ones.

Finally, the ideal criterion $I \otimes M \to IM$ is the form in which flatness is *actually tested* in commutative algebra and the bridge to homological algebra. It reduces flatness to a statement about a single, very concrete map for each ideal, and its homological reformulation $\operatorname{Tor}_1(R/I, M) = 0$ is the doorway into the derived theory where flatness becomes the vanishing of an invariant.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The theorem's precondition is "$M$ is flat", possibly in disguise, or a flatness check is wanted.

The first disguised source is **a flatness check that seems to require infinite modules**. The property $B$ is "I must verify $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ injective but $N$ is huge." The bridge: condition (4) says it suffices to test finitely generated $N, N'$, because any element of a kernel is a finite sum and lives in a finitely generated submodule. The non-obvious value: the reduction is *lossless*, so an infinite check becomes finite. *Example problem:* prove a module flat where the natural injection is into an infinitely generated module — pull the relevant tensor relation into a finitely generated piece.

The second disguised source is **a single ideal $I$ with $I \otimes M \to IM$ controllable**. The property $B$ is "for each finitely generated ideal $I$, the map $I \otimes M \to IM$ is injective." The bridge: the companion criterion upgrades this single-ideal condition to full flatness. The non-obvious value: flatness, an all-injections condition, is detected by inclusions of ideals alone. *Example problem:* over a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]], every ideal is $(d) \cong R$, so $I \otimes M \cong M \to IM = dM$ is the iso $m \mapsto dm$ exactly when $M$ is torsion-free — recovering "torsion-free $=$ flat over a PID."

The third disguised source is **"$T_M$ preserves short exact sequences"** arising from a homological computation. The property $B$ is condition (2). The bridge: $(2) \Rightarrow (1)$ by splicing, so $T_M$ preserves *all* exactness. The non-obvious value: you never have to separately check long exact sequences. *Example problem:* having shown $T_M$ has no $\operatorname{Tor}_1$, conclude it commutes with every kernel and cokernel.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$M$ is flat, in all four equivalent senses."

Combine flatness with **a short exact sequence** to get a short exact sequence after tensoring. The result $E$ is that $0 \to M \otimes N' \to M \otimes N \to M \otimes N'' \to 0$ stays exact, the basic computational use. This is what makes localization exact and base change along flat maps preserve exactness. Nonobvious because right-exactness alone gives only the back two-thirds.

Combine flatness with **the contrapositive against torsion**. Flat $\Rightarrow$ torsion-free, so $E$: any torsion element refutes flatness. The combination is the standard *refutation* tool — find a non-zero-divisor killing a non-zero element and flatness is dead. Nonobvious in that a single element decides an all-injections property.

Combine the ideal criterion with **homological algebra**. $I \otimes M \to IM$ injective $\iff \operatorname{Tor}_1(R/I, M) = 0$, so $E$: flatness becomes the vanishing of a derived functor, computable from a free resolution of $R/I$. This is nonobvious and is the entire bridge from this chapter to homological algebra.

# Why Is It True

The intuition has two parts, one for each non-trivial implication.

For **$(4) \Rightarrow (3)$, the reduction to finitely generated modules**: a kernel element of $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ is a *finite* sum $\sum m_i \otimes n_i$, and the equation $\sum m_i \otimes f(n_i) = 0$ in $M \otimes N'$ — being a single relation among finitely many pure tensors — is *witnessed* inside a finitely generated chunk. Tensor products are built from finite data: an element is a finite sum, and any relation that makes it zero is a finite combination of the defining relations, hence already holds in the tensor product of finitely generated submodules. So the whole infinite apparatus collapses: if injectivity holds for finitely generated modules, the finitely generated witness forces the kernel element to be zero there, and therefore zero everywhere. **The slogan: tensoring sees only finitely generated data, so flatness need only be checked on finitely generated data.**

For **$(2) \Rightarrow (1)$, splicing**: any exact sequence is a concatenation of short exact sequences glued along their images. Cut $A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C$ into the three short exact sequences $0 \to \ker f \to A \to \operatorname{im} f \to 0$, $0 \to \operatorname{im} f \to B \to \operatorname{im} g \to 0$ (using $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$), $0 \to \operatorname{im} g \to C \to C/\operatorname{im} g \to 0$. If $T_M$ preserves each short piece's exactness, the tensored images and kernels match up across the splices, and exactness of the long sequence reassembles from the short ones. **The slogan: short exact sequences are the atoms of exactness; preserve the atoms and you preserve everything.**

The cheap chain $(1) \Rightarrow (2) \Rightarrow (3)$ is immediate ((1) is stronger than (2); (2) applied to $0 \to N' \to N \to N/N' \to 0$ gives the injection of (3)), and $(3) \Rightarrow (4)$ is just specializing to finitely generated modules.

---

# What Makes This Hard

The whole difficulty is concentrated in $(4) \Rightarrow (3)$ and specifically in the **finite-witness lemma**: that a vanishing tensor relation $\sum m_i \otimes f(n_i) = 0$ in the possibly-huge $M \otimes N'$ already holds in $M_0 \otimes N_0'$ for finitely generated $M_0, N_0'$. Getting this right means knowing that the kernel of a surjection from a free module presenting the tensor product is generated by the standard relations, each involving finitely many terms, so any particular relation uses only finitely many generators — and then enlarging $N_0'$ to contain $f(N_0)$ so the map $f$ restricts. The common error is to forget to enlarge the target to $N_0' + f(N_0)$, or to assume the witness submodules can be taken on only one side. The splicing $(2) \Rightarrow (1)$ is conceptually clean but error-prone in bookkeeping: one must verify the tensored short sequences glue, which is where keeping $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$ visible matters.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:**
Prove the easy cycle $(1) \Rightarrow (2) \Rightarrow (3) \Rightarrow (4)$ by inclusion/specialization. Close the loop with the two hard steps: $(4) \Rightarrow (3)$ by pulling any kernel relation into finitely generated submodules (a finite-witness argument), and $(2) \Rightarrow (1)$ by splicing a general exact sequence into short exact sequences and reassembling.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Easy implications.** Show $(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow(3)\Rightarrow(4)$.
   - *Hint:* (1)⊃(2) trivially; (2) on $0\to N'\to N\to N/N'\to 0$ yields the injection of (3); (4) is (3) restricted to finitely generated modules.
   - *Why needed:* Establishes three of the four equivalences cheaply.

2. **Finite witness.** Show any $\xi = \sum m_i \otimes n_i \in \ker(\operatorname{id}_M\otimes f)$ already vanishes in $M_0 \otimes (N_0' + f(N_0))$ for finitely generated $M_0, N_0$.
   - *Hint:* The relation $\sum m_i \otimes f(n_i) = 0$ is a finite combination of defining tensor relations; collect the finitely many elements appearing into finitely generated submodules.
   - *Why needed:* It is the engine of $(4)\Rightarrow(3)$.

3. **$(4) \Rightarrow (3)$.** Conclude flatness from finitely generated flatness.
   - *Hint:* Apply (4) to $\operatorname{id}_{M_0} \otimes (f|_{N_0})$; the finite witness forces $\xi = 0$ in $M_0 \otimes N_0$, hence in $M \otimes N$.
   - *Why needed:* Removes the size restriction.

4. **Splice.** Cut a general exact $A \to B \to C$ into three short exact sequences and tensor each.
   - *Hint:* Use $0\to\ker f\to A\to\operatorname{im} f\to 0$, $0\to\operatorname{im} f\to B\to\operatorname{im} g\to 0$, $0\to\operatorname{im} g\to C\to C/\operatorname{im} g\to 0$.
   - *Why needed:* Reduces long exactness to short.

5. **$(2) \Rightarrow (1)$.** Reassemble exactness of the tensored long sequence from the short pieces.
   - *Hint:* Chase images: $\operatorname{im}(M\otimes A\to M\otimes B) = \operatorname{im}(M\otimes\operatorname{im} f\to M\otimes B) = \ker(M\otimes B\to M\otimes\operatorname{im} g) = \ker(M\otimes B\to M\otimes C)$.
   - *Why needed:* Completes the loop, giving all four equivalences.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Easy implications $(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow(3)\Rightarrow(4)$
> **Statement:** Conditions (1), (2), (3), (4) satisfy $(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow(3)\Rightarrow(4)$.
>
> **Hint:** Each is a restriction or specialization of the previous.
>
> **Why needed:** Three of the four equivalences are immediate, isolating where the work is.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > $(1)\Rightarrow(2)$: short exact sequences are exact sequences. $(2)\Rightarrow(3)$: apply (2) to $0 \to N' \xrightarrow{f} N \to N/N' \to 0$; preservation of exactness includes injectivity of $\operatorname{id}_M\otimes f$, which is (3). $(3)\Rightarrow(4)$: (4) is (3) with $N, N'$ restricted to finitely generated modules.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Finite-witness lemma
> **Statement:** If $\sum_{i=1}^k m_i \otimes f(n_i) = 0$ in $M \otimes_R N'$ (with $f : N \to N'$, $n_i \in N$), then there are finitely generated submodules $M_0 \subseteq M$ and $N_0 \subseteq N$ with all $m_i \in M_0$, $n_i \in N_0$, such that the same relation holds in $M_0 \otimes_R (N_0' )$ where $N_0' := f(N_0) \subseteq N'$.
>
> **Hint:** A vanishing element of a tensor product is a finite combination of the bilinearity relations; only finitely many module elements occur, so collect them.
>
> **Why needed:** It localizes an infinite tensor relation to a finitely generated arena, the crux of $(4)\Rightarrow(3)$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Recall $M \otimes_R N'$ is the free module on symbols $m \otimes n'$ modulo the bilinearity relations. The equation $\sum_i m_i \otimes f(n_i) = 0$ means $\sum_i m_i \otimes f(n_i)$ is a finite $R$-linear combination of finitely many bilinearity relations $\rho_1, \dots, \rho_t$, each of which involves only finitely many elements of $M$ and of $N'$. Let $M_0$ be the finitely generated submodule of $M$ generated by the $m_i$ and the $M$-entries of the $\rho_j$, and let $N_0$ be the finitely generated submodule of $N$ generated by the $n_i$ and (preimages under $f$ of) the $N'$-entries of the $\rho_j$ — enlarging so that $N_0' := f(N_0)$ contains all $N'$-entries appearing. Then every symbol and relation in the witnessing combination lives in $M_0 \otimes (N_0')$, so $\sum_i m_i \otimes f(n_i) = 0$ already holds there.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Splicing reduces long exactness to short
> **Statement:** $A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C$ is exact if and only if the three sequences $0 \to \ker f \to A \to \operatorname{im} f \to 0$, $0 \to \operatorname{im} f \to B \to \operatorname{im} g \to 0$, $0 \to \operatorname{im} g \to C \to C/\operatorname{im} g \to 0$ are short exact and $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$.
>
> **Hint:** These are the canonical short exact sequences attached to the maps; exactness at $B$ is $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$.
>
> **Why needed:** It lets condition (2) (short sequences) deliver condition (1) (all sequences).
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Each displayed sequence is short exact by construction (kernel–image and image–cokernel decompositions). The middle one uses $\ker g = \operatorname{im} f$, which holds precisely when $A \to B \to C$ is exact at $B$. Conversely, given the three short exact sequences with $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$, exactness of $A\to B\to C$ at $B$ is immediate.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> By Lemma 1 we have $(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow(3)\Rightarrow(4)$. It remains to prove $(4)\Rightarrow(3)$ and $(3)\Rightarrow(1)$ (equivalently $(2)\Rightarrow(1)$); together these close the loop.
>
> **$(4)\Rightarrow(3)$.** Let $f : N \to N'$ be injective and let $\xi = \sum_{i=1}^k m_i \otimes n_i \in M \otimes N$ lie in $\ker(\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f)$, so $\sum_i m_i \otimes f(n_i) = 0$ in $M \otimes N'$. By Lemma 2 there are finitely generated $M_0 \subseteq M$, $N_0 \subseteq N$ with $m_i \in M_0$, $n_i \in N_0$, and the relation $\sum_i m_i \otimes f(n_i) = 0$ holds in $M_0 \otimes (N_0')$ where $N_0' = f(N_0)$. The restriction $f|_{N_0} : N_0 \to N_0'$ is injective, and $N_0, N_0'$ are finitely generated, so by (4) the map $\operatorname{id}_{M_0} \otimes (f|_{N_0}) : M_0 \otimes N_0 \to M_0 \otimes N_0'$ is injective. The element $\sum_i m_i \otimes n_i \in M_0 \otimes N_0$ is sent to $0$, so it is $0$ in $M_0 \otimes N_0$. The natural map $M_0 \otimes N_0 \to M \otimes N$ sends it to $\xi$, hence $\xi = 0$. Thus $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ is injective, and $M$ is flat.
>
> **$(3)\Rightarrow(1)$ (via $(2)\Rightarrow(1)$).** First, (3) gives (2): for a short exact $0 \to N' \xrightarrow{u} N \xrightarrow{v} N'' \to 0$, [[Thm - Tensoring is Right Exact|right-exactness]] makes $M\otimes N \to M\otimes N'' \to 0$ exact, and flatness (3) makes $\operatorname{id}_M\otimes u$ injective, so the tensored sequence is short exact. Now $(2)\Rightarrow(1)$: let $A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C$ be exact. By Lemma 3 form the short exact sequences
> $$0 \to \ker f \to A \to \operatorname{im} f \to 0,\quad 0 \to \operatorname{im} f \to B \to \operatorname{im} g \to 0,\quad 0 \to \operatorname{im} g \to C \to C/\operatorname{im} g \to 0,$$
> and tensor each with $M$; by (2) each stays short exact. In particular $M\otimes \ker f \to M \otimes A \to M\otimes\operatorname{im} f \to 0$ is exact, so $M\otimes A \to M\otimes\operatorname{im} f$ is surjective; $0 \to M\otimes\operatorname{im} f \to M\otimes B \to M\otimes\operatorname{im} g \to 0$ is exact; and $0 \to M\otimes\operatorname{im} g \to M\otimes C$ is exact (left map injective). Then
> $$\operatorname{im}(M\otimes A \to M\otimes B) = \operatorname{im}(M\otimes\operatorname{im} f \to M\otimes B) = \ker(M\otimes B \to M\otimes\operatorname{im} g) = \ker(M\otimes B \to M\otimes C),$$
> the last equality because $M\otimes\operatorname{im} g \to M\otimes C$ is injective. Hence $M\otimes A \to M\otimes B \to M\otimes C$ is exact, proving (1). $\blacksquare$
>
> **Companion criterion (sketch).** Necessity: the inclusion $I \hookrightarrow R$ tensored with flat $M$ gives an injection $I \otimes M \to R \otimes M = M$ with image $IM$, so $I \otimes M \to IM$ is an isomorphism. Sufficiency reduces, via the finitely generated criterion (4) and the structure of finitely generated modules, to ideals; the homological reformulation comes from the long exact $\operatorname{Tor}$ sequence of $0 \to I \to R \to R/I \to 0$, where $\operatorname{Tor}_1(R/I, M) = \ker(I \otimes M \to M)$.

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Torsion-free equals flat over a PID.** Over a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] every ideal is $(d) \cong R$, so the criterion map $I \otimes M \to IM$ is $M \xrightarrow{\times d} dM$, injective exactly when $M$ has no $d$-torsion. Running over all $d$ gives "torsion-free $\iff$ flat" over a PID. The application is nonobvious because the general ideal criterion specializes to the elementary torsion condition exactly when ideals are principal — pinpointing why the tower's bottom two rungs merge over a PID.

**Flatness of localization in one line.** For a multiplicative set $S$, the criterion map $I \otimes S^{-1}R \to I\cdot S^{-1}R = S^{-1}I$ is an isomorphism because localization commutes with the ideal product and is exact. So $S^{-1}R$ is flat by the companion criterion, with no computation. The application is nonobvious because it derives flatness of localization — usually proved via exactness of $S^{-1}$ — directly from the ideal criterion.

**Reducing a homological vanishing to finite ideals.** In computing $\operatorname{Tor}_1^R(R/I, M)$ from a presentation, the theorem says it suffices to consider finitely generated $I$, so for a Noetherian ring *all* ideals qualify and flatness is detected by finitely many $\operatorname{Tor}$ computations on the (finitely generated) ideals. The application is nonobvious because it turns an infinite homological check into a finite one over Noetherian rings — the practical form used to verify flatness of families in algebraic geometry.

# Bridges

- **[[Def - Flat Module|Flat Module]]** — this theorem is the toolkit that makes the definition usable: it replaces "all injections" by "finitely generated injections" and then by "inclusions of finitely generated ideals", turning an unverifiable definition into a finite test.

- **[[Thm - Tensoring is Right Exact|Tensoring is Right Exact]]** — supplies the back half used throughout: in proving $(3)\Rightarrow(2)$ and in the splicing argument, right-exactness gives surjectivity and cokernels for free, so flatness need only contribute the injections. The two theorems together say flat $=$ right-exact $+$ injection-preserving $=$ fully exact.

- **$\operatorname{Tor}_1$ and homological algebra** — the companion criterion $I \otimes M \to IM$ injective $\iff \operatorname{Tor}_1(R/I, M) = 0$ is the bridge to derived functors. The map $I \otimes M \to M$ has kernel exactly $\operatorname{Tor}_1(R/I, M)$, read off the long exact $\operatorname{Tor}$ sequence of $0 \to I \to R \to R/I \to 0$; flatness is the vanishing of this $\operatorname{Tor}$ for all $I$.

- **Finitely presented modules and faithful flatness** — the finite-witness lemma is the same mechanism that makes flatness *local* and that underlies *faithfully flat descent*: tensor relations are finitely supported, so flatness can be checked on finite data and descended along faithfully flat maps.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Flatness is a local property *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> Because flatness reduces to finitely generated data, $M$ is flat over $R$ iff $M_{\mathfrak p}$ is flat over $R_{\mathfrak p}$ for every prime $\mathfrak p$ — flatness can be checked one prime at a time. Combined with the local criterion, this is what makes flatness a *fibrewise* condition on families and is the technical heart of the local theory of flat morphisms.

> [!tip] The local flatness criterion and deformation theory *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Over a Noetherian local ring, the ideal criterion sharpens to the **local criterion of flatness**: a module is flat iff $\operatorname{Tor}_1(R/\mathfrak m, M) = 0$, checkable on a single ideal. This is the computational engine of **deformation theory** — it is exactly the condition that a family stays flat as one deforms, controlling when a moduli problem is unobstructed.
