# Notation Registry

**Standing convention.** Throughout these commutative algebra notes, *ring* means a commutative ring with a multiplicative identity $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$; *module* means a unital module. The new operation of this chapter is **completion**: the act of building a ring (or module) out of *compatible sequences of approximations* to its elements, written $\widehat{R} = \varprojlim R/\mathfrak{a}^n$. There is one slogan to carry from the first line, repeated in Motivation and used everywhere: **$\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion is the algebra of an infinitely thin tubular neighbourhood of the locus $\mathfrak{a} = 0$ — the formal disk at a point — and it is finer than localizing.** Two pieces of notation are easy to confuse and are kept distinct on purpose: the inverse-limit *arrow* convention is that the maps run *down* the system, $\varprojlim$ collecting the bottom; the direct-limit arrow runs *up*, $\varinjlim$ collecting the top.

- $R, A, B$ — commutative rings with $1$; $M, N$ — $R$-modules
- $(I, \leq)$ — a **directed set**: a poset in which any two elements $a, b$ have a common upper bound $c$ ($a \leq c$, $b \leq c$)
- $D = \big((X_i)_{i \in I}, (f_{ij})_{i \leq j}\big)$ — a **direct system**: objects $X_i$ with morphisms $f_{ij} : X_i \to X_j$ for $i \leq j$, satisfying $f_{ii} = \mathrm{id}$ and $f_{jk}\circ f_{ij} = f_{ik}$
- $E = \big((Y_i)_{i \in I}, (h_{ij})_{i \leq j}\big)$ — an **inverse system**: objects $Y_i$ with morphisms $h_{ij} : Y_j \to Y_i$ for $i \leq j$, satisfying $h_{ii} = \mathrm{id}$ and $h_{ij}\circ h_{jk} = h_{ik}$
- $\varinjlim X_i$ — the **direct limit** (colimit) of $D$; its elements are equivalence classes $[x_i]$ of elements $x_i \in X_i$, with $x_i \sim f_{ij}(x_i)$
- $\varprojlim Y_i$ — the **inverse limit** of $E$; its elements are compatible sequences $(y_i)$ with $y_i = h_{ij}(y_j)$ for $i \leq j$
- $\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{m}$ — an ideal of $R$ ($\mathfrak{m}$ when maximal); $\mathfrak{a}^n$ — its $n$-th power, with $\mathfrak{a}^0 = R$
- $\widehat{R} = \widehat{R}^{\,\mathfrak{a}} := \varprojlim_n R/\mathfrak{a}^n$ — the **$\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion** of $R$
- $\widehat{M} = \widehat{M}^{\,\mathfrak{a}} := \varprojlim_n M/\mathfrak{a}^n M$ — the **$\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion** of an $R$-module $M$
- $(M_n)_{n \geq 0}$ — a **filtration** of $M$: a descending chain $M = M_0 \supseteq M_1 \supseteq \cdots$ of submodules; $\widehat{M} = \varprojlim M/M_n$ is the completion w.r.t. it
- $\varphi = \varphi_M : M \to \widehat{M}$, $m \mapsto (m + \mathfrak{a}^n M)_n$ — the **completion map**; $\ker\varphi = \bigcap_{n} \mathfrak{a}^n M$
- $\mathbb{Z}_p := \varprojlim_n \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ — the ring of **$p$-adic integers** (for $p$ prime)
- $k[[T]], k[[T_1, \dots, T_n]]$ — the ring of **formal power series** over a field $k$
- $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ — the **Prüfer $p$-group**, a direct limit of cyclic groups
- $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ — the finite field with $p^n$ elements; $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p} = \varinjlim_n \mathbb{F}_{p^{n!}}$ — its algebraic closure

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **a completion is what you get by remembering an element only through its sequence of finite-precision approximations, and gluing those approximations back into a single new element — and the inverse limit is the machine that does the gluing.** You already know one instance, and it is the one to keep in mind. A $p$-adic integer is "an integer written in base $p$ with digits running infinitely to the left": you cannot store it as a finite number, but you can store, for every $n$, what it looks like modulo $p^n$, and these snapshots are compatible — reducing the mod-$p^{n+1}$ snapshot mod $p^n$ gives the mod-$p^n$ one. A compatible sequence of snapshots *is* the new number. That is the whole idea. Replace "$\mathbb{Z}$ and the powers of $p$" by "any ring $R$ and the powers of an ideal $\mathfrak{a}$", and the snapshots $R/\mathfrak{a}^n$ assemble into the **$\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion** $\widehat{R} = \varprojlim R/\mathfrak{a}^n$.

Why would anyone want this? Because completion is the algebraic incarnation of *zooming in infinitely far at a point* — finer than the localization of the previous chapters, and in a precise sense it is the bridge from algebra to analysis. Localizing $R$ at a prime $\mathfrak{p}$ keeps a whole Zariski-neighbourhood of the point; it inverts the functions that do not vanish there but it still sees a positive-dimensional chunk of the variety. Completing at $\mathfrak{m}$ throws away everything except the *infinitesimal* behaviour: $R/\mathfrak{m}^n$ is the ring of "functions known to order $n-1$", i.e. their Taylor expansions truncated at degree $n-1$, and $\widehat{R}$ is the ring of *full Taylor series*, convergent or not. This is why $k[T]$ completed at $(T)$ is the formal power series ring $k[[T]]$: a power series is exactly a compatible family of polynomial truncations. The geometric picture to install now and never let go of:

$$\text{localize at } \mathfrak{p} \;\rightsquigarrow\; \text{a Zariski neighbourhood}; \qquad \text{complete at } \mathfrak{m} \;\rightsquigarrow\; \text{the } \textbf{formal disk} \text{ — an infinitely thin tube around the point}.$$

The reason this matters technically is that the formal disk is *analytically simple* even when the variety is globally complicated: two varieties that look the same near a point have isomorphic completions, so completion is the tool that detects and proves *local analytic* facts — that a node looks like $xy = 0$, that a smooth point looks like affine space, that Hensel's lemma lets you solve equations by successive approximation exactly as Newton's method does over $\mathbb{R}$. The cost of passing to this convenient ring is paid up front by two theorems we must trust: completion *preserves Noetherianity* and is *exact on finitely generated modules*. Without those, $\widehat{R}$ might be a wild object; with them, it is as well-behaved as $R$ and you may compute in it freely.

The chapter has two layers and is built in that order. First, the **limit machinery** itself — direct and inverse limits, defined by universal properties so they are canonical rather than ad hoc, with the directed-set bookkeeping that makes them well-defined. The direct limit is a generalised union ("glue objects along compatible identifications", the algebraic-closure-of $\mathbb{F}_p$ and the Prüfer group being the models); the inverse limit is a generalised intersection-of-approximations ("collect compatible threads", $\mathbb{Z}_p$ being the model). Second, **completion** as the canonical inverse limit $\varprojlim R/\mathfrak{a}^n$, its incarnations $\mathbb{Z}_p$, $k[[T]]$, $k[[T_1, \dots, T_n]]$, and the package of theorems (Noetherian-preservation, exactness, and the corollary that formal power series over a Noetherian ring are Noetherian) that make it safe to use. The single thread tying the two layers together is that **completion is the most important inverse limit there is**, and everything proved about inverse limits in the abstract is there to be cashed out for $\widehat{R}$.

This chapter assumes you are fluent with [[Def - Ideal|ideals]], [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient rings]], and the powers $\mathfrak{a}^n$ of an ideal; with [[Def - Module|modules]], [[Def - Submodule|submodules]] and [[Def - Quotient Module|quotients]] and [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finite generation]] from the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|Modules]] notes; with [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian rings]] and [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]] from [[Rings IV — §4.1–4.3|Rings IV]]; and with [[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field|local rings]] from [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization]]. Two of the hard theorems quoted here — the [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees lemma]] and the [[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull intersection theorem]] — are proved in the next chapter, [[Commutative Algebra XI — Graded Rings and the Artin-Rees Lemma]]; the [[Def - Filtration and Stable Filtration|filtration]] language they need is introduced there in full. We use them here as black boxes for the injectivity of the completion map.

# Concept Map

## §11.1 Limits and Directed Systems

- **[[Def - Directed Set and Direct System]]**
	- A **directed set** $(I, \leq)$ is a poset in which any two elements have a common upper bound; this is exactly the property needed for "compatible identifications eventually agree". A **direct system** over $I$ is a family of objects $X_i$ with transition maps $f_{ij} : X_i \to X_j$ for $i \leq j$ that compose coherently ($f_{ii} = \mathrm{id}$, $f_{jk}f_{ij} = f_{ik}$); an **inverse system** is the same with the maps reversed, $h_{ij} : Y_j \to Y_i$. The motivating directed set is $(\mathbb{N}, \leq)$, giving sequences of objects $X_0 \to X_1 \to \cdots$ (direct) or $\cdots \to Y_2 \to Y_1 \to Y_0$ (inverse). The systems live in any of Sets, Groups, Rings, $R$-modules, $R$-algebras.

- **[[Def - Direct and Inverse Limits]]**
	- The **direct limit** $\varinjlim X_i = \big(\coprod_i X_i\big)/\sim$ glues the objects, identifying $x_i$ with all its images $f_{ij}(x_i)$; concretely $x_i \sim x_j$ iff they have a common image $f_{ik}(x_i) = f_{jk}(x_j)$ somewhere up the system. The **inverse limit** $\varprojlim Y_i = \{(y_i) \in \prod_i Y_i : y_i = h_{ij}(y_j)\}$ collects the compatible threads. Each is characterised by a **universal property** — the direct limit is the universal *receiver* of compatible maps out of the $X_i$, the inverse limit the universal *source* of compatible maps into the $Y_i$ — which pins it down up to unique isomorphism and is the form actually used in proofs. The two are dual: reverse every arrow and $\varinjlim$ becomes $\varprojlim$.

- **[[Ex - A direct limit of cyclic groups (Prufer group)]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Build the Prüfer $p$-group $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty) = \varinjlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ along the inclusions $\mathbb{Z}/p^n \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/p^{n+1}$ (multiplication by $p$), identify it with $\mathbb{Z}[1/p]/\mathbb{Z}$, and contrast it with $\mathbb{Z}_p = \varprojlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ formed from the *same* objects with the *opposite* arrows.

- **[[Ex - The p-adic integers as an inverse limit]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Construct $\mathbb{Z}_p = \varprojlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$, show every element has a unique base-$p$ digit expansion running to the left, compute $-1 = \dots 4444_5$ in $\mathbb{Z}_5$, and verify $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a domain whose units are the threads with non-zero first digit.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The absolute Galois group as a profinite limit *(from Galois Theory / Number Theory)*
> An inverse limit of finite groups along surjections is a **profinite group** — compact, totally disconnected, the natural home of infinite Galois theory. The Galois group of $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}/\mathbb{F}_p$ is $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}} = \varprojlim \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the inverse limit of all the finite cyclic Galois groups $\mathrm{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^n}/\mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$; the direct limit $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p} = \varinjlim \mathbb{F}_{p^{n!}}$ of the fields and the inverse limit of their Galois groups are the two halves of the *same* Galois correspondence, run in opposite directions. Every absolute Galois group $\mathrm{Gal}(\overline{K}/K)$ is built this way.

## §11.2 Completions

- **[[Def - The I-adic Completion]]**
	- The **$\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion** of $R$ is $\widehat{R} = \varprojlim_n R/\mathfrak{a}^n$, the inverse limit of the truncation rings along the natural projections $R/\mathfrak{a}^{n+1} \twoheadrightarrow R/\mathfrak{a}^n$; for a module, $\widehat{M} = \varprojlim_n M/\mathfrak{a}^n M$, an $\widehat{R}$-module. An element of $\widehat{R}$ is a compatible family of truncations — a "Taylor series" — and the completion map $\varphi : R \to \widehat{R}$ sends $r$ to its family of reductions, with kernel $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n$. The headline incarnations are $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}^{\,(p)} = \mathbb{Z}_p$, $\widehat{k[T]}^{\,(T)} = k[[T]]$, and $\widehat{k[T_1,\dots,T_n]}^{\,(T_1,\dots,T_n)} = k[[T_1,\dots,T_n]]$. The $\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion is the special case (filtration $= (\mathfrak{a}^n M)$) of completion with respect to a general [[Def - Filtration and Stable Filtration|filtration]].

- **[[Thm - The Inverse Limit and Completeness]]**
	- The completion map $\varphi : M \to \widehat{M}$ has kernel $\ker\varphi = \bigcap_{n \geq 0} \mathfrak{a}^n M$, so it is injective exactly when this intersection vanishes — and the [[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull intersection theorem]] guarantees it does when $R$ is Noetherian and $\mathfrak{a}$ is contained in the [[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field|Jacobson radical]] (in particular when $R$ is a Noetherian local ring and $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{m}$, or a Noetherian domain and $\mathfrak{a}$ proper). The completion $\widehat{R}$ is itself **complete**: completing it again changes nothing, $\widehat{\widehat{R}} = \widehat{R}$. This is the algebraic version of "the reals are complete and completing them again gives the reals".

- **[[Thm - The Completion of a Noetherian Ring is Noetherian]]**
	- If $R$ is Noetherian then $\widehat{R}$ is Noetherian, and $\widehat{R} \otimes_R (-)$ is an exact functor; moreover for a finitely generated $M$ the natural map $\widehat{R} \otimes_R M \to \widehat{M}$ is an isomorphism. This is the load-bearing safety theorem: it says passing to the formal disk does not break the finiteness that all of dimension theory relies on. The proof (in Atiyah–Macdonald Ch.10) goes through the associated graded ring and the [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees lemma]]; we quote it and use it.

- **[[Thm - Formal Power Series over a Noetherian Ring are Noetherian]]**
	- If $R$ is Noetherian then $R[[T_1, \dots, T_n]]$ is Noetherian. This is the formal-power-series analogue of [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]], and it falls out instantly: $R[[T_1,\dots,T_n]]$ is the $\mathfrak{m}$-adic completion of the (Noetherian, by Hilbert) ring $R[T_1,\dots,T_n]$ at $\mathfrak{m} = (T_1,\dots,T_n)$, so it is Noetherian by the previous theorem. In particular $k[[T_1,\dots,T_n]]$ is Noetherian — the basic local ring of $n$-dimensional formal geometry.

- **[[Thm - Exactness Properties of Completion]]**
	- For a Noetherian $R$, the functor $M \mapsto \widehat{M}$ is **exact on finitely generated modules**: it preserves short exact sequences, kernels and cokernels, and $\widehat{M} = \widehat{R}\otimes_R M$ flatly. Concretely, completing a submodule inclusion $N \hookrightarrow M$ keeps it an inclusion and $\widehat{M}/\widehat{N} = \widehat{M/N}$ — the formal disk of a quotient is the quotient of the formal disks. This is the exact analogue of localization being exact, and it is what licenses computing with $\widehat{R}$-modules by completing $R$-modules.

- **[[Ex - The formal power series ring as a completion]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove $\varprojlim k[T]/(T^n) \cong k[[T]]$ by matching a compatible family of polynomial truncations to a single power series, identify the maximal ideal and units of $k[[T]]$ ($f$ is a unit iff $f(0) \neq 0$), and extend to $k[[T_1,\dots,T_n]]$ via $\mathfrak{a}^i = (T_1,\dots,T_n)^i$.

- **[[Ex - Completion of a local ring at its maximal ideal]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show $\widehat{R}^{\,\mathfrak{m}}$ is a local ring with maximal ideal $\widehat{\mathfrak{m}} = \mathfrak{m}\widehat{R}$ and the *same* residue field $\widehat{R}/\widehat{\mathfrak{m}} \cong R/\mathfrak{m}$, so completion is invisible to the residue field but resolves the infinitesimal structure; illustrate with $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)} \rightsquigarrow \mathbb{Z}_p$ and $k[x,y]_{(x,y)} \rightsquigarrow k[[x,y]]$.

- **[[Ex - Hensel-style lifting in the p-adics]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Use successive approximation to lift a simple root of $f \bmod p$ to a root in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ — Newton's method made exact by completeness — proving e.g. that $\mathbb{Z}_p$ contains a primitive $(p-1)$-th root of unity and a square root of any unit that is a QR mod $p$, with $\sqrt{-1} \in \mathbb{Z}_5$ as the worked case.

- **[[Ex - The completion map and the Krull intersection]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Identify $\ker\varphi = \bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$ explicitly, use Artin–Rees to prove this intersection is killed by an element of $1 + \mathfrak{a}$, and deduce injectivity of $\varphi$ for $R$ Noetherian local (ES4 Q15) — the precise statement of "no non-zero element is infinitely divisible by $\mathfrak{a}$".

> [!tip] Unlocked: The formal disk and formal schemes *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Completing the local ring $\mathcal{O}_{X,x}$ of a variety at a point $x$ produces the **complete local ring** $\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,x}}$, whose spectrum is the **formal neighbourhood** (or **formal disk**) of $X$ at $x$ — a scheme so infinitesimally thin that every Taylor series at $x$ converges on it. Two varieties are *formally isomorphic at* $x$ iff their complete local rings agree, and this is the right notion of "look the same near $x$": a smooth point has $\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,x}} \cong k[[T_1,\dots,T_d]]$, a node on a plane curve has $k[[x,y]]/(xy)$. Gluing formal disks along a variety gives a **formal scheme**, the object underlying formal and rigid-analytic geometry and the deformation theory that controls how varieties can be perturbed.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Henselian rings and the implicit function theorem in algebra *(from Algebraic Geometry / Number Theory)*
> The successive-approximation lifting of roots that completeness makes possible is **Hensel's lemma**, and a ring in which it holds is called a **Henselian** ring. Complete local rings are Henselian, which is why $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and $k[[T]]$ behave like rings of convergent functions: a simple root mod $\mathfrak{m}$ lifts uniquely to an honest root, exactly as the implicit function theorem lifts an approximate solution to a true one over $\mathbb{R}$. This is the algebraic engine behind the étale and **étale-local** structure theory: Henselian local rings are the algebraic stand-in for "small analytic neighbourhood", and the strict Henselisation is the algebraic stand-in for the contractible analytic ball.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The local zeta function and $p$-adic analysis *(from Number Theory)*
> Once $\mathbb{Z}_p$ exists as a complete ring, $\mathbb{Q}_p = \mathrm{Frac}(\mathbb{Z}_p)$ becomes a complete *field* carrying a genuine analysis — a non-archimedean absolute value $|x|_p = p^{-v_p(x)}$, convergent power series, integration against Haar measure. Counting solutions of polynomial equations mod $p^n$ for all $n$ at once is a computation in $\mathbb{Z}_p$, packaged by the **local zeta function**; the product of these over all $p$, glued with the archimedean place, is the adelic picture and the analytic heart of modern number theory. The $\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion of this chapter is the first step onto that road.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring goals of this chapter cluster into five types. The first is **identifying a limit concretely** — recognising an inverse limit as $\mathbb{Z}_p$, $k[[T]]$, $\widehat{\mathbb{Z}}$, or a direct limit as $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$ or a Prüfer group; the standard route is the [[Def - Direct and Inverse Limits|universal property]] (exhibit a candidate object with compatible maps and show it is universal) or, for completions, the explicit digit/Taylor description. The second, and the chapter's signature, is **proving the completion map is injective** — showing $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M = 0$, which is exactly the [[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull intersection theorem]] and routes through [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]]; this is what lets you regard $R$ as a subring of $\widehat{R}$. The third is **lifting a solution by successive approximation** — Hensel-style, where you have a root mod $\mathfrak{a}$ and want one in $\widehat{R}$; the lever is completeness plus a non-degeneracy (simple-root) condition, and the route is "improve the approximation one power of $\mathfrak{a}$ at a time and take the limit". The fourth is **transporting a finiteness or exactness property to $\widehat{R}$** — Noetherianity, exactness, flatness — settled by the package [[Thm - The Completion of a Noetherian Ring is Noetherian|completion preserves Noetherian]] / [[Thm - Exactness Properties of Completion|completion is exact on f.g. modules]]. The fifth is **comparing completion with localization or with the original ring** — same residue field, finer than $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$, computing $\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}$, deciding whether $\varphi$ is an isomorphism (it is iff $R$ was already complete). These are the natural targets because completion is fundamentally a *comparison*-and-*approximation* tool: every theorem says either "this feature of $R$ survives passage to the formal disk" or "this object is reconstructed from its finite approximations".

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A directed system is named**, and the move is to decide direct vs inverse from the arrow direction and reach for the matching universal property; an inverse system of surjections of finite objects is a profinite/completion situation, a direct system of inclusions is a union/colimit situation. **An ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ and its powers are in play**, signalling $\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion: the snapshots are $R/\mathfrak{a}^n$ and the topology is the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic one. **$R$ is Noetherian** — the single most important source, because it unlocks the entire safety package (Artin–Rees, Krull intersection, Noetherian-preservation, exactness); almost nothing in the second half is true without it, and the lecture notes flag this explicitly. **$R$ is local $(R,\mathfrak{m})$ or a domain** — so $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathrm{Jac}(R)$ and Krull intersection applies, giving injective $\varphi$. **A simple root mod $\mathfrak{a}$ is given**, i.e. $f(a) \equiv 0$ and $f'(a)$ a unit — the trigger for Hensel lifting. The discipline is to match source to target along the chapter's spine: a named system routes through a universal property; an "is $\varphi$ injective?" target routes through Krull intersection, which demands Noetherian; a "solve this congruence in $\mathbb{Z}_p$" target routes through Hensel, which demands a simple root. The [[Commutative Algebra X — Completions and Limits#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every limit-and-completion problem is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

1. **Invoke the universal property instead of chasing elements.** To build a map *into* an inverse limit $\varprojlim Y_i$, produce a compatible family $(g_i : B \to Y_i)$ with $h_{ij}g_j = g_i$ and read off the unique $g : B \to \varprojlim Y_i$; to build a map *out of* a direct limit $\varinjlim X_i$, produce a compatible family $(g_i : X_i \to A)$ with $g_j f_{ij} = g_i$ and read off the unique $g : \varinjlim X_i \to A$. To *identify* a limit with a known object, show the known object satisfies the same universal property. *Trigger:* "construct a homomorphism to/from a limit" or "prove two limits are isomorphic". *Pattern:* compatible families in, unique arrow out — never verify well-definedness of an explicit formula by hand. See [[Thm - The Inverse Limit and Completeness]].

2. **Represent an element as a compatible thread / Taylor series.** An element of $\varprojlim Y_i$ *is* a sequence $(y_i)$ with $y_i = h_{ij}(y_j)$; an element of $\widehat{R}$ is a family $(r + \mathfrak{a}^n)$ of truncations. To prove two such elements are equal, check equality at every level $n$; to construct one, specify it level by level *compatibly*. *Trigger:* a computation inside $\mathbb{Z}_p$ or $k[[T]]$. *Pattern:* "work modulo $\mathfrak{a}^n$ for each $n$, check the levels glue".

3. **Reduce modulo $\mathfrak{a}^n$ to get a finite/simpler problem, then take the limit.** Many statements about $\widehat{R}$ are proved by proving them in each $R/\mathfrak{a}^n$ (where the ring is often Artinian/finite) and passing to the inverse limit. *Trigger:* a property of $\widehat{R}$ you cannot attack directly. *Pattern:* "establish the statement in $R/\mathfrak{a}^n$ uniformly in $n$, then lift".

4. **Lift a root by successive approximation (Hensel).** Given an approximate solution $a_n$ to $f = 0$ modulo $\mathfrak{a}^{n}$ with $f'(a_n)$ a unit, correct it to $a_{n+1} = a_n - f(a_n)/f'(a_n)$ solving $f = 0$ modulo $\mathfrak{a}^{n+1}$; the $a_n$ form a compatible thread converging to a root in $\widehat{R}$. *Trigger:* "solve a polynomial congruence in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ / show a power series has a root". *Pattern:* Newton's iteration, made exact because $\widehat{R}$ is complete. See [[Ex - Hensel-style lifting in the p-adics]].

5. **Compute the kernel of completion as $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$.** The completion map's failure of injectivity is measured exactly by the elements lying in *every* power $\mathfrak{a}^n M$. To prove $\varphi$ injective, prove this intersection is zero; to find a counterexample, exhibit a non-zero infinitely-$\mathfrak{a}$-divisible element. *Trigger:* "is the completion map injective?" *Pattern:* "$\ker\varphi = \bigcap \mathfrak{a}^n M$; now kill it with Krull/Artin–Rees".

6. **Apply Krull intersection (needs Noetherian + $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathrm{Jac}$).** Over a Noetherian ring with $\mathfrak{a}$ inside the Jacobson radical (e.g. local + $\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{m}$, or a Noetherian domain + proper $\mathfrak{a}$), $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M = 0$ for finitely generated $M$. *Trigger:* you have reduced injectivity to "$\bigcap \mathfrak{a}^n M = 0$" and the ring is Noetherian local/domain. *Pattern:* "by [[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull]], the intersection vanishes". See [[Ex - The completion map and the Krull intersection]].

7. **Transport finiteness/exactness through the safety package.** If $R$ is Noetherian, freely use that $\widehat{R}$ is Noetherian, that $\widehat{R}$ is flat over $R$, that $\widehat{R}\otimes_R M \cong \widehat{M}$ for f.g. $M$, and that $M \mapsto \widehat{M}$ is exact on f.g. modules. *Trigger:* a finiteness or exactness claim about $\widehat{R}$ or $\widehat{M}$. *Pattern:* "$R$ Noetherian, so [[Thm - The Completion of a Noetherian Ring is Noetherian|the package]] applies".

8. **Present a completion as a quotient of a power-series ring.** $\widehat{R[T_1,\dots,T_n]}^{(T)} = R[[T_1,\dots,T_n]]$, and many completions are of the form $k[[T_1,\dots,T_n]]/I$; recognising this presentation lets you import the Noetherian/exactness facts and compute generators. *Trigger:* a completion of a finitely generated algebra at a maximal ideal. *Pattern:* "write $R = k[T]/J$, complete to $k[[T]]/\widehat{J}$".

9. **Recognise units of $\widehat{R}$ by their residue.** In a complete local ring, an element is a unit iff its image in the residue field $\widehat{R}/\widehat{\mathfrak{m}}$ is non-zero — equivalently, in $\widehat{R}^{\,\mathfrak{a}}$, $x$ is a unit iff $x \bmod \mathfrak{a}$ is a unit, by geometric-series inversion $1/(1-y) = \sum y^n$ for $y \in \widehat{\mathfrak{m}}$. *Trigger:* "is this power series / $p$-adic number invertible?" *Pattern:* "check the constant term / first digit is a unit; the rest is summable".

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming the completion map is injective
> Because $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}_p$ and $k[T] \hookrightarrow k[[T]]$ are injective, it is tempting to treat $\varphi : M \to \widehat{M}$ as always an embedding and identify $M$ with a submodule of $\widehat{M}$. The kernel is $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$, and this can be non-zero. Concretely, take $R = k[x]_{(x)} \times k$ — no, simplest is the *non-Noetherian* or *non-local* obstruction: in $R = \prod_{n\geq 1} k$ with $\mathfrak{a} = \bigoplus_n k$ (or any setting where Krull's hypotheses fail) one finds elements infinitely divisible by $\mathfrak{a}$. The clean classroom example: for the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion of $M = \mathbb{Z}$ with $\mathfrak{a} = (6)$ versus $(2)\cap(3)$, or — sharper — $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n \neq 0$ whenever $\mathfrak{a}$ contains a non-unit idempotent-like obstruction. The map is injective **exactly when $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M = 0$**, which the [[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull intersection theorem]] *guarantees only* when $R$ is Noetherian and $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathrm{Jac}(R)$ (e.g. local, or a domain with proper $\mathfrak{a}$). Drop Noetherian or drop "$\mathfrak{a}$ in the radical" and injectivity can fail.

> [!warning] 2. Confusing the direct limit with the inverse limit because they share the same objects
> The Prüfer group $\varinjlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n$ and the $p$-adic integers $\varprojlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n$ are built from the *identical* sequence of objects $\mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$ — only the arrows differ (inclusions up vs projections down). They could not be more different: $\mathbb{Z}(p^\infty)$ is a divisible torsion group, countable, every element killed by some $p^n$; $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is a torsion-free compact ring, uncountable, no non-zero element killed by any power of $p$. The error is reading "$\varprojlim$" off the objects rather than the arrows. The repair: always identify the *transition maps* first. Up-maps (inclusions, extensions) with $\varinjlim$ give a generalised union; down-maps (projections, truncations) with $\varprojlim$ give a generalised intersection of approximations. A correct slogan: **direct limits enlarge, inverse limits refine.**

> [!warning] 3. Believing $\varprojlim$ is exact (that it preserves surjections)
> Tensor product is right exact and localization is exact, so one expects $\varprojlim$ to preserve exactness too. It does *not*: $\varprojlim$ is only **left exact** in general. A short exact sequence $0 \to A_n \to B_n \to C_n \to 0$ of inverse systems gives $0 \to \varprojlim A_n \to \varprojlim B_n \to \varprojlim C_n$ exact, but the last map need *not* be surjective — the obstruction is the derived functor $\varprojlim^1$. The classic counterexample is the system $\mathbb{Z} \xleftarrow{p} \mathbb{Z} \xleftarrow{p} \cdots$ whose $\varprojlim$ is $0$ but whose $\varprojlim^1$ is non-zero, breaking a surjection. The operation **becomes legal** when the inverse system satisfies the **Mittag-Leffler condition** (the images $h_{ij}(B_j)$ stabilise) — which holds automatically for systems of surjections, e.g. $R/\mathfrak{a}^{n+1} \twoheadrightarrow R/\mathfrak{a}^n$. That stabilisation is exactly why completion of f.g. modules over a Noetherian ring *is* exact: Artin–Rees forces Mittag-Leffler.

> [!warning] 4. Treating $k[[T]]$ as the field of fractions or as convergent series
> Two false identifications. First, $k[[T]]$ is *not* a field — it is a local domain with maximal ideal $(T)$; its field of fractions is the Laurent series field $k((T)) = k[[T]][T^{-1}]$, where you additionally allow finitely many negative powers. The unit criterion "$f$ invertible iff $f(0)\neq 0$" is precisely what makes $k[[T]]$ local, not a field. Second, "power series" here means *formal* power series — no convergence is required or available, since $k$ carries no topology and $T$ is an indeterminate, not a number. The completion topology lives on the *coefficients-truncation* side ($\mathfrak{a}^n$-adic), not on evaluation. Forgetting this leads to nonsense like "plug in $T = 2$"; the only legal substitution is $T \mapsto$ another element of a complete ring's maximal ideal.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any problem of this chapter is to name *which limit* you are facing and *which direction* it runs, because the entire toolkit forks on that.

If the problem **gives you a system and asks you to identify or build a map to/from its limit**, abandon element-chasing and reach for the [[Def - Direct and Inverse Limits|universal property]]. A map into an inverse limit is the *same data* as a compatible family of maps into the stages: to define $g : B \to \varprojlim Y_i$, define each $g_i : B \to Y_i$, check $h_{ij}g_j = g_i$, and the property hands you $g$. Dually a map out of a direct limit is a compatible family of maps out of the stages. To prove $\varprojlim Y_i \cong A$ (e.g. $\varprojlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n \cong \mathbb{Z}_p$, or $\varprojlim k[T]/(T^n) \cong k[[T]]$), exhibit $A$ with the same universal property; uniqueness of universal objects finishes it. The recurring trap is to verify a fraction-or-series formula is well-defined by hand; the universal property exists to spare you that. The recognition step is reading the arrows: projections/truncations down the system mean $\varprojlim$ (a completion or profinite object), inclusions/extensions up the system mean $\varinjlim$ (a union or algebraic closure).

If the problem **asks whether the completion map is injective**, you are being asked to compute $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$, and the strategy is a fixed two-step descent. Step one: write $\ker\varphi = \bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$ — this is automatic from [[Thm - The Inverse Limit and Completeness|the kernel formula]]. Step two: show the intersection vanishes, which over a Noetherian ring is the [[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull intersection theorem]], proved via [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]]. The hypotheses you must verify are exactly Krull's: $R$ Noetherian and $\mathfrak{a}$ inside the Jacobson radical — satisfied when $R$ is local with $\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{m}$, or a domain with $\mathfrak{a}$ proper (ES4 Q15). If those hypotheses fail, *do not assume injectivity*: hunt for an infinitely-divisible element instead.

If the problem **asks you to solve an equation in $\widehat{R}$ — a congruence in $\mathbb{Z}_p$, a root of a power series**, you are in Hensel territory, and the strategy is Newton's iteration made exact by completeness. Find an approximate root $a_0$ modulo $\mathfrak{a}$ with $f'(a_0)$ a unit (the *simple-root* condition — this is the only delicate hypothesis), then improve it: $a_{n+1} = a_n - f(a_n)f'(a_n)^{-1}$ solves $f \equiv 0$ one power of $\mathfrak{a}$ deeper, the corrections form a compatible thread, and completeness supplies the limiting root. The whole difficulty is checking the non-degeneracy condition $f'(a_0)\in\widehat{R}^\times$; without it (a multiple root mod $\mathfrak{a}$) the iteration stalls and a root may genuinely fail to exist.

If the problem **asks you to push a finiteness or exactness property onto $\widehat{R}$ or $\widehat{M}$**, the controlling fact is the safety package, and the only real work is checking $R$ is Noetherian. Once it is, [[Thm - The Completion of a Noetherian Ring is Noetherian|$\widehat{R}$ is Noetherian]], $\widehat{R}$ is flat, $\widehat{R}\otimes_R M = \widehat{M}$ for f.g. $M$, and [[Thm - Exactness Properties of Completion|completion is exact on f.g. modules]]. Corollaries like "$R[[T_1,\dots,T_n]]$ is Noetherian" are then one line: realise the power-series ring as a completion and quote the theorem. The recognition step is spotting that a ring is *secretly a completion* — every formal-power-series ring is, and so is every complete local ring of a variety.

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "what does $R$ look like to infinite order at the locus $\mathfrak{a} = 0$, and can I reconstruct that from finite-order data?"** Building a limit assembles the infinite-order object from its finite-order snapshots; injectivity of $\varphi$ asks whether the snapshots lose any information; Hensel lifting asks whether an approximate solution refines to an exact one; the safety package asks whether the good finite-order properties survive to infinite order. Decide which of these four you are doing, and the right tool — universal property, Krull intersection, Newton iteration, or the Noetherian package — is determined.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Def - Direct and Inverse Limits|The universal properties of $\varinjlim$ and $\varprojlim$]]**: a map out of $\varinjlim$ is a compatible family out of the stages; a map into $\varprojlim$ is a compatible family into the stages. This is the most reused fact because it converts every question about morphisms involving a limit into a question about morphisms at the finite stages, which are concrete. Its standard uses are constructing homomorphisms (e.g. $R \to \widehat{R}$, or $\mathbb{Z}_p \to \mathbb{Z}/p^n$), proving two constructions of a limit agree (both satisfy the property, so are uniquely isomorphic), and identifying a completion with a familiar ring. Reach for it whenever a limit appears as the source or target of a map.

- **[[Thm - The Inverse Limit and Completeness|The kernel formula $\ker\varphi = \bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$]]**: the completion map's injectivity is governed entirely by this intersection. Its reusability is that it turns the soft question "does completion lose information?" into the hard but tractable question "is any non-zero element infinitely $\mathfrak{a}$-divisible?", which Krull/Artin–Rees answer. It is the first move in every injectivity problem and the precise diagnostic for when $R \hookrightarrow \widehat{R}$.

- **[[Thm - The Completion of a Noetherian Ring is Noetherian|Completion preserves Noetherian, and $\widehat{R}$ is flat]]**: passing to the formal disk does not break finiteness. This is the technical backbone that makes $\widehat{R}$ safe to compute in — it is used to deduce that formal power series rings are Noetherian, that complete local rings of varieties are Noetherian, and (via flatness) that completing an exact sequence of f.g. modules keeps it exact. Whenever you want to do dimension theory or module theory on the formal disk, this is what guarantees the machinery still runs.

- **[[Thm - Exactness Properties of Completion|Exactness of completion on f.g. modules]]**: $M \mapsto \widehat{M}$ preserves short exact sequences and $\widehat{R}\otimes_R M \cong \widehat{M}$. Its reusability is that it lets you *complete any diagram* of f.g. modules and keep it exact — kernels, cokernels, quotients, submodules all commute with completion. This is the exact analogue of localization's exactness and the reason $\widehat{R}$-module computations reduce to $R$-module computations followed by completion.

- **Hensel's lemma / successive approximation**: a simple root mod $\mathfrak{a}$ lifts uniquely to a root in a complete ring. Its reusability is as a *solving* tool — it manufactures roots, idempotents, and roots of unity in $\mathbb{Z}_p$ and $k[[T]]$ that have no closed form, and it is the algebraic implicit function theorem. The trigger is any equation to be solved over a complete local ring with a non-degenerate approximate solution; the reaction is Newton's iteration, which the completeness of $\widehat{R}$ converts from "converges in the limit" to "lands on an exact element".

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — completion is the passage to the formal disk, the infinitesimal neighbourhood of a point.** To a variety $X$ and a point $x$ one attaches the [[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field|local ring]] $\mathcal{O}_{X,x}$ of functions defined near $x$; localizing kept a whole Zariski-open neighbourhood, still positive-dimensional. Completing at the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_x$ produces $\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,x}} = \varprojlim \mathcal{O}_{X,x}/\mathfrak{m}_x^n$, whose elements are full Taylor series at $x$ and whose spectrum is the **formal neighbourhood** or **formal disk** — a scheme so infinitesimally thin that it sees only how $X$ curves and self-intersects right at $x$. The decisive feature is that this completion is a *local-analytic invariant*: a smooth point gives $\widehat{\mathcal{O}_{X,x}} \cong k[[T_1,\dots,T_d]]$, every node $xy=0$ gives $k[[x,y]]/(xy)$, so two singularities are "the same kind" precisely when their complete local rings agree. The example $\widehat{k[T]}^{(T)} = k[[T]]$ is literally the formal germ of a smooth curve at the origin. Gluing formal disks along $X$ gives a **formal scheme**, and this is the entry point to formal geometry, rigid-analytic geometry, and deformation theory — the study of how a variety can be infinitesimally perturbed, governed entirely by its complete local rings.

2. **Number theory — $\mathbb{Z}_p$ completes $\mathbb{Z}$ at the prime $p$, and assembling all completions is the adelic philosophy.** Completing $\mathbb{Z}$ at $(p)$ gives the $p$-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p = \varprojlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n\mathbb{Z}$, the ring that remembers the divisibility behaviour at $p$ to infinite order; its fraction field $\mathbb{Q}_p$ carries a complete non-archimedean absolute value $|x|_p = p^{-v_p(x)}$ and a genuine $p$-adic analysis. This is the *other* completion of $\mathbb{Q}$ — alongside the archimedean one that gives $\mathbb{R}$ — and Ostrowski's theorem says these are all of them. The systematic technology of solving a problem over $\mathbb{Q}$ by solving it over $\mathbb{R}$ and over every $\mathbb{Q}_p$ and gluing (the **Hasse principle**, the **adeles** $\mathbb{A}_\mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{R}\times\prod'_p \mathbb{Q}_p$) is this chapter's "reconstruct from finite approximations" elevated to the central method of modern number theory. Hensel's lemma is the local solvability criterion that makes the $p$-adic factor computable.

3. **Analysis — completion is the algebraic form of Cauchy completion, and $\varprojlim$ is "take limits of Cauchy sequences".** The reals are built from the rationals by adjoining limits of Cauchy sequences; equivalently $\mathbb{R} = $ the metric completion of $\mathbb{Q}$. The $\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion does exactly this with the metric $d(x,y) = 2^{-\sup\{n : x-y\in\mathfrak{a}^n\}}$: a Cauchy sequence is one whose successive differences sink into ever-higher powers of $\mathfrak{a}$, a compatible thread $(y_n)\in\varprojlim R/\mathfrak{a}^n$ is its limit, and $\widehat{R}$ is the completed metric space made into a ring. The statement [[Thm - The Inverse Limit and Completeness|$\widehat{\widehat{R}} = \widehat{R}$]] is the algebraic "completing a complete space changes nothing". Where analysis takes the metric as primary and the completion as derived, here the *ideal filtration* is primary and supplies the metric — but the two constructions are the same universal "freely adjoin all the limits", and convergence of a power series $\sum a_n T^n$ in $k[[T]]$ is convergence in this $\mathfrak{a}$-adic metric, not in any field-valued sense.

4. **Homological algebra — $\varprojlim$ is only left exact, and its failure is the derived functor $\varprojlim^1$.** The direct limit $\varinjlim$ over a directed set is *exact* — it commutes with kernels and cokernels, which is why $\varinjlim$ of an injective system of injections is injective and the Prüfer group inherits divisibility cleanly. The inverse limit $\varprojlim$ is only **left exact**: it preserves kernels but a surjection of inverse systems need not give a surjection of limits, and the precise obstruction is the first right derived functor $\varprojlim^1$, fitting into $0 \to \varprojlim^1 A_n \to \varprojlim B_n \to \varprojlim C_n \to 0$. The vanishing of $\varprojlim^1$ — guaranteed by the **Mittag-Leffler condition** that the transition images stabilise — is exactly what makes completion of finitely generated modules over a Noetherian ring exact, and [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]] is the theorem that forces Mittag-Leffler in the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic setting. This bridge is why "[[Thm - Exactness Properties of Completion|completion is exact]]" needs the Noetherian and finite-generation hypotheses: they are precisely what kill $\varprojlim^1$.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a completion stores an element as the totality of its finite-precision shadows, and the inverse limit is the apparatus that recovers the element from the shadows.** Every construction in the second half of this chapter is one setting of the same instrument. A $p$-adic integer is the totality of its residues mod $p^n$; a power series is the totality of its polynomial truncations; a function on a formal disk is the totality of its Taylor coefficients to each order. The inverse limit is precisely "the set of consistent shadow-systems", and the magic is that this set is *richer* than the original ring — it contains limiting elements ($-1$ in $\mathbb{Z}_p$, $\sqrt{-1}$ in $\mathbb{Z}_5$, $\frac{1}{1-T}=\sum T^n$ in $k[[T]]$) that had no finite description but whose every shadow is legitimate. The deep payoff is that the instrument is *faithful when nothing is infinitely divisible*: the completion map loses information exactly along $\bigcap_n\mathfrak{a}^n$, and the [[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull intersection theorem]] says this is zero in the Noetherian local world, so $R$ embeds in its formal disk. The slogan to carry: **completing at $\mathfrak{m}$ does to a ring what taking a Taylor expansion does to a function** — it discards everything except the infinitesimal jet at the point, and an element of $\widehat{R}$ is a jet of infinite order.

**The true name of an inverse limit is "compatible threads", and of a direct limit "eventually-stable germs".** Students meet $\varprojlim$ and $\varinjlim$ as opaque formulas; the operational names are what you actually use. The true name of $\varprojlim Y_i$ is **the set of compatible sequences** — to specify or compare an element, work level by level and check the levels glue under the transition maps. The true name of $\varinjlim X_i$ is **the set of elements modulo eventual identification** — two elements are equal once they become equal somewhere up the system, so to compute in $\varinjlim$ you may freely push everything into a single late stage $X_k$ where the question becomes finite. This reorganises the two operations that share the same objects but oppose in arrows. The inverse limit *refines*: it adds limiting elements and the result is bigger and more rigid (compact, like $\mathbb{Z}_p$). The direct limit *enlarges by union*: it absorbs the stages into one big object and the result is the increasing union (divisible, like the Prüfer group or $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$). Knowing which way the arrows run tells you instantly whether you are refining to a formal disk or unioning to an algebraic closure.

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "solve a congruence in $\mathbb{Z}_p$" means "find a simple root mod $p$, then run Newton".** When a problem asks for a root of a polynomial in a complete local ring — a $p$-adic square root, a root of unity, an idempotent splitting — the words "Hensel's lemma" should fire automatically, because completeness converts Newton's iteration from an approximation scheme into an existence proof. The reflex is: reduce mod $\mathfrak{a}$, find an approximate root $a_0$, *check $f'(a_0)$ is a unit* (this single check is the whole content — it is the non-degeneracy that the implicit function theorem also demands), then the corrections $a_{n+1}=a_n - f(a_n)/f'(a_n)$ form a thread converging to an exact root. The complementary reflex governs injectivity: when asked whether $R\hookrightarrow\widehat{R}$, the reaction is "$\ker = \bigcap\mathfrak{a}^n$; is the ring Noetherian local? then Krull kills it". These two reflexes — *Newton-lift to manufacture solutions*, and *Krull intersection to certify faithfulness* — between them solve the large majority of the chapter's problems, and both are pure consequences of the single fact that $\widehat{R}$ is complete: every Cauchy thread has a limit.

**Why Noetherian is non-negotiable here.** The lecture notes flag that *every* part of the completion theorem assumes $R$ Noetherian, and it is worth saying why this is not a technical convenience but the load-bearing hypothesis. Three separate good properties of completion — that $\varphi$ is injective, that $\widehat{R}$ is Noetherian, that completion is exact — all fail without it, and they all fail *for the same reason*: each is secretly the vanishing of the obstruction $\varprojlim^1$ or the stabilisation of an ideal filtration, and the theorem that forces that stabilisation is [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]], which is *only true* over a Noetherian ring. So the single fact "Noetherian rings have stable filtrations" propagates into the entire safety package. When a completion problem misbehaves, the first thing to check is whether Noetherianity has quietly been lost — passing to a non-finitely-generated module, an infinite product, or a non-Noetherian base — because that is where every pathology enters.
