# Problem Statement

Let $A$ be a [[Def - Dedekind Domain|Dedekind domain]] with fraction field $K$. Prove that the set $\mathcal{I}(A)$ of nonzero [[Def - Fractional Ideal and the Ideal Class Group|fractional ideals]] of $A$ is an **abelian group** under multiplication, with identity $A$ and inverse $\mathfrak{a}^{-1} = (A : \mathfrak{a}) = \{x \in K : x\mathfrak{a} \subseteq A\}$. Show moreover that $\mathcal{I}(A)$ is the **free abelian group on the set of nonzero prime ideals**: every $\mathfrak{a} \in \mathcal{I}(A)$ is uniquely $\prod_\mathfrak{p} \mathfrak{p}^{n_\mathfrak{p}}$ with $n_\mathfrak{p} \in \mathbb{Z}$ almost all zero. Finally, observe that invertibility of every nonzero fractional ideal *characterizes* Dedekind domains among Noetherian domains.

**Recall:**

![[Def - Fractional Ideal and the Ideal Class Group#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Fractional Ideal and the Ideal Class Group|fractional ideal]] is a nonzero $A$-submodule $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq K$ with $d\mathfrak{a} \subseteq A$ for some $d \neq 0$. Multiplication $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}$ is the submodule generated by products; the identity is $A$. The candidate inverse is $(A : \mathfrak{a}) = \{x \in K : x\mathfrak{a} \subseteq A\}$, always satisfying $\mathfrak{a}(A:\mathfrak{a}) \subseteq A$.

By [[Thm - A Dedekind Domain has Unique Factorization of Ideals|unique factorization of ideals]], every nonzero *integral* ideal of $A$ is uniquely a product of prime powers. By [[Thm - Localization of a Dedekind Domain is a DVR|localization]], each $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a DVR, where every fractional ideal is $(\pi^n)$ for a unique $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *verify-a-group-structure* problem, and the only nontrivial axiom is the existence of inverses — associativity, commutativity, and the identity are formal. As the [[Commutative Algebra XIII — Dedekind Domains and DVRs#Legal Operations|topic strategy]] records, invertibility is "the Dedekind miracle", so the heart of the exercise is showing $\mathfrak{a}(A:\mathfrak{a}) = A$ (not merely $\subseteq A$).

**Assumption pattern.** Two facts unlock everything. First, unique factorization gives every integral ideal as $\prod \mathfrak{p}^{e_\mathfrak{p}}$, so it suffices to invert *prime* ideals (then extend multiplicatively). Second, localization to the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$ turns "is $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b} = A$?" into "do the local exponents add to zero at every prime?" — and a fractional ideal is determined by its local exponents. So invertibility becomes the trivial DVR fact that $(\pi^n)(\pi^{-n}) = A_\mathfrak{p}$.

**Theorem routing.** The route is: (i) reduce to inverting primes via unique factorization; (ii) prove $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) = A$ either directly (the inclusion $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) \subseteq A$ is automatic, and "$= A$" is shown by ruling out "$= \mathfrak{p}$"), or locally (check at each prime that exponents cancel); (iii) conclude $\mathcal{I}(A) = \bigoplus_\mathfrak{p}\mathbb{Z}$ is free abelian, with the exponent map $\mathfrak{a} \mapsto (v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}))_\mathfrak{p}$ an isomorphism onto $\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}$. The characterization "invertible $\Rightarrow$ Dedekind" is the converse, noted for completeness.

**Key decision point.** The crux is proving $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) = A$ and not just $\subseteq A$. The cleanest argument is *local*: $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p})$ and $A$ are both fractional ideals, and two fractional ideals are equal iff they are equal after localizing at every prime; locally $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{q}$ is either $A_\mathfrak{q}$ (if $\mathfrak{q} \neq \mathfrak{p}$) or $\pi_\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}$ (if $\mathfrak{q} = \mathfrak{p}$), and in the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$ the product $(\pi)(\pi^{-1}) = A_\mathfrak{p}$ is immediate. The non-obvious decision is to **prove the equality locally rather than globally** — globally, "$= A$ not $= \mathfrak{p}$" requires an extra argument; locally it is the trivial DVR cancellation.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Commutative Algebra XIII — Dedekind Domains and DVRs#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Invert a fractional ideal (operation 5).** $\mathfrak{a}^{-1} = (A:\mathfrak{a})$, with $(\prod\mathfrak{p}^{n_\mathfrak{p}})^{-1} = \prod\mathfrak{p}^{-n_\mathfrak{p}}$ once invertibility is known.

2. **Localize at a prime to reach a DVR, then contract (operation 2).** Check $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{a}^{-1} = A$ by checking the local equality at each prime, where it is the DVR cancellation $(\pi^n)(\pi^{-n}) = A_\mathfrak{p}$.

3. **Read off valuation arithmetic (operation 1).** A fractional ideal is the exponent-vector $(v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}))_\mathfrak{p}$; multiplication adds, inversion negates.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Associativity, commutativity, and the identity $A$ are formal. The only real axiom is inverses: you must show $\mathfrak{a}(A:\mathfrak{a}) = A$, where the inclusion $\subseteq A$ is automatic. Reduce to the case $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{p}$ a prime, using that every ideal is a product of primes.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> To show $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) = A$, do not work globally. Two fractional ideals are equal iff they agree after localizing at every prime $\mathfrak{q}$. So localize $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p})$ at each $\mathfrak{q}$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> At $\mathfrak{q} \neq \mathfrak{p}$: $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{q} = A_\mathfrak{q}$ (since $\mathfrak{p} \not\subseteq \mathfrak{q}$), so the local product is $A_\mathfrak{q}$. At $\mathfrak{q} = \mathfrak{p}$: $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a DVR with $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p} = (\pi)$, and $(A_\mathfrak{p} : (\pi)) = (\pi^{-1})$, so $(\pi)(\pi^{-1}) = A_\mathfrak{p}$. Either way the localization is the full local ring, so the product is $A$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Once primes are invertible, every $\mathfrak{a} = \prod\mathfrak{p}^{e_\mathfrak{p}}$ has inverse $\prod\mathfrak{p}^{-e_\mathfrak{p}}$, so $\mathcal{I}(A)$ is a group. Unique factorization says the exponent map $\mathfrak{a} \mapsto (v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}))_\mathfrak{p}$ is a bijection onto $\bigoplus_\mathfrak{p}\mathbb{Z}$, and it is a homomorphism — so $\mathcal{I}(A)$ is free abelian on the primes.

# Solution

The proof has three movements. Step 1 dispatches the formal group axioms and reduces inverses to the prime case. Step 2 proves the key equality $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) = A$ by localizing, where it becomes the trivial DVR cancellation. Step 3 assembles the free abelian group structure via the exponent map and notes the converse characterization.

**Step 1: the formal axioms, and reduction to primes.**

Multiplication of fractional ideals is associative and commutative with identity $A$; it remains to produce inverses, and by unique factorization it suffices to invert prime ideals.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **Well-defined product.** If $d_1\mathfrak{a} \subseteq A$ and $d_2\mathfrak{b} \subseteq A$, then $d_1d_2(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}) \subseteq A$, so $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}$ is again a fractional ideal. The product is associative and commutative (inherited from multiplication in $K$), and $A\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{a}$, so $A$ is the identity. Thus $\mathcal{I}(A)$ is a commutative monoid.
>
> **Reduction to primes.** Any fractional ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ is $\tfrac1d\mathfrak{b}$ for an integral ideal $\mathfrak{b} = d\mathfrak{a}$ and $d \in A$. By [[Thm - A Dedekind Domain has Unique Factorization of Ideals|unique factorization]], $\mathfrak{b} = \prod_i\mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i}$ and $(d) = \prod_j\mathfrak{q}_j^{f_j}$, so $\mathfrak{a} = \prod_i\mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i}\cdot\prod_j\mathfrak{q}_j^{-f_j}$ is a product of integer powers of primes. Hence if every prime $\mathfrak{p}$ is invertible (with inverse a fractional ideal), so is every $\mathfrak{a}$, with inverse the product of the corresponding inverse powers. So it suffices to show $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) = A$ for each nonzero prime $\mathfrak{p}$.

**Step 2: primes are invertible — $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) = A$.**

For each nonzero prime $\mathfrak{p}$, $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) = A$, proved by checking equality after localizing at every prime.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The inclusion $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) \subseteq A$ holds by definition of $(A:\mathfrak{p})$. Both $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p})$ and $A$ are fractional ideals; to show they are equal it suffices (by the local–global principle for submodules of $K$) to show $\big(\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p})\big)A_\mathfrak{q} = A_\mathfrak{q}$ for every nonzero prime $\mathfrak{q}$. Localization commutes with products and with $(A:-)$ for finitely generated $\mathfrak{p}$:
> $$\big(\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p})\big)A_\mathfrak{q} = (\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{q})\,(A_\mathfrak{q} : \mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{q}).$$
>
> **Case $\mathfrak{q} \neq \mathfrak{p}$.** Since $\mathfrak{p}, \mathfrak{q}$ are distinct maximal ideals, $\mathfrak{p} \not\subseteq \mathfrak{q}$, so $\mathfrak{p}$ meets $A\setminus\mathfrak{q}$ and $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{q} = A_\mathfrak{q}$. Then $(A_\mathfrak{q} : A_\mathfrak{q}) = A_\mathfrak{q}$, and the product is $A_\mathfrak{q}$. ✓
>
> **Case $\mathfrak{q} = \mathfrak{p}$.** By [[Thm - Localization of a Dedekind Domain is a DVR|localization]], $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a DVR with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p} = (\pi)$, $\pi$ a uniformizer. In a DVR, $(A_\mathfrak{p} : (\pi)) = \{x \in K : x\pi \in A_\mathfrak{p}\} = \{x : v(x) \geq -1\} = (\pi^{-1})$. So $(\pi)(\pi^{-1}) = (\pi\cdot\pi^{-1}) = A_\mathfrak{p}$. ✓
>
> In every case the localization is the full local ring $A_\mathfrak{q}$, which equals $AA_\mathfrak{q}$. By the local–global principle, $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) = A$. Hence $\mathfrak{p}$ is invertible with $\mathfrak{p}^{-1} = (A:\mathfrak{p})$, and by Step 1 every fractional ideal is invertible. So $\mathcal{I}(A)$ is an abelian group.

**Step 3: $\mathcal{I}(A)$ is free abelian on the primes; the converse.**

The map $\mathfrak{a} \mapsto (v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}))_\mathfrak{p}$ is a group isomorphism $\mathcal{I}(A) \xrightarrow{\sim} \bigoplus_\mathfrak{p}\mathbb{Z}$. Conversely, a Noetherian domain in which every nonzero fractional ideal is invertible is Dedekind.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Define $v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a})$ as the exponent of $\mathfrak{p}$ in the factorization of $\mathfrak{a}$ (equivalently, the valuation of $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}$ in the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$). Unique factorization (extended to fractional ideals via Step 1) says every $\mathfrak{a}$ is uniquely $\prod_\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{p}^{v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a})}$ with finitely many nonzero exponents, so $\mathfrak{a} \mapsto (v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}))_\mathfrak{p}$ is a *bijection* onto $\bigoplus_\mathfrak{p}\mathbb{Z}$. It is a homomorphism: $v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}) = v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}) + v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{b})$ (valuations add under products in the DVR) and $v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}^{-1}) = -v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a})$. Hence
> $$\mathcal{I}(A) \cong \bigoplus_{\mathfrak{p}}\mathbb{Z},$$
> the free abelian group on the set of nonzero primes.
>
> **Converse.** If $A$ is a Noetherian domain in which every nonzero fractional ideal is invertible, then in particular every nonzero prime $\mathfrak{p}$ is invertible. Invertible ideals are finitely generated and, after localizing, principal — so each $A_\mathfrak{p}$ has principal maximal ideal, hence is a DVR (condition (3) of the [[Thm - Characterization of Discrete Valuation Rings|characterization]]), forcing $A$ integrally closed of dimension $1$, i.e. Dedekind. So invertibility of all fractional ideals *characterizes* Dedekind domains among Noetherian domains.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Let $A$ be Dedekind. Multiplication of fractional ideals is associative, commutative, with identity $A$; products of fractional ideals are fractional ideals. By [[Thm - A Dedekind Domain has Unique Factorization of Ideals|unique factorization]], every fractional ideal is a product of integer powers of primes, so it suffices to invert primes.
>
> For a prime $\mathfrak{p}$, $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p}) \subseteq A$ by definition. Localizing at any prime $\mathfrak{q}$: if $\mathfrak{q} \neq \mathfrak{p}$ then $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{q} = A_\mathfrak{q}$ and the local product is $A_\mathfrak{q}$; if $\mathfrak{q} = \mathfrak{p}$ then $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a DVR with $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p} = (\pi)$, and $(\pi)(\pi^{-1}) = A_\mathfrak{p}$. So $\mathfrak{p}(A:\mathfrak{p})$ localizes to $A_\mathfrak{q}$ everywhere, hence equals $A$. Thus every fractional ideal is invertible and $\mathcal{I}(A)$ is an abelian group.
>
> The exponent map $\mathfrak{a} \mapsto (v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}))_\mathfrak{p}$ is a group isomorphism onto $\bigoplus_\mathfrak{p}\mathbb{Z}$ (bijection by unique factorization, homomorphism since valuations add), so $\mathcal{I}(A)$ is free abelian on the primes. Conversely, a Noetherian domain with all fractional ideals invertible has every $A_\mathfrak{p}$ a DVR, hence is Dedekind. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Illegal but tempting: assuming $(A:\mathfrak{a})$ is always an inverse
> For a *general* domain, $(A:\mathfrak{a})$ need not satisfy $\mathfrak{a}(A:\mathfrak{a}) = A$ — only $\subseteq A$. In $k[x,y]$ the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m} = (x,y)$ has $(A:\mathfrak{m}) = A$, so $\mathfrak{m}(A:\mathfrak{m}) = \mathfrak{m} \neq A$: not invertible. The equality $\mathfrak{a}(A:\mathfrak{a}) = A$ is *exactly* the Dedekind property (dimension $1$, normal), which is why the proof localizes to DVRs — only there is the cancellation $(\pi)(\pi^{-1}) = A_\mathfrak{p}$ automatic. Invertibility is the defining miracle, not a free lunch.

# Key Takeaways

**Invertibility of fractional ideals is the Dedekind property, and it is proved by localizing to DVRs where cancellation is trivial.** The single hard axiom — that $(A:\mathfrak{a})$ is a genuine inverse — fails in general domains and holds in Dedekind domains precisely because each $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a DVR. The technique is to verify the global equality $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{a}^{-1} = A$ *locally*: a fractional ideal is determined by its localizations, and at each prime the equation reduces to the one-line DVR fact $(\pi^n)(\pi^{-n}) = A_\mathfrak{p}$. The transferable principle: **to prove an equality of fractional ideals, check it after localizing at every prime, where the DVR structure makes everything a comparison of integers.** This is the same local-to-global reduction that proves unique factorization, and it is the universal method for ideal identities in Dedekind domains.

**A fractional ideal is an exponent-vector, and the group of fractional ideals is free abelian on the primes.** Once invertibility is established, unique factorization upgrades to fractional ideals: every $\mathfrak{a}$ is $\prod\mathfrak{p}^{n_\mathfrak{p}}$ with $n_\mathfrak{p} \in \mathbb{Z}$, so the data of a fractional ideal is exactly its vector $(v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}))_\mathfrak{p}$ of valuations. Multiplication adds the vectors, inversion negates them, containment is coordinatewise $\geq$ — all of ideal arithmetic becomes linear algebra over $\mathbb{Z}$. The trigger to deploy this: whenever a problem involves products, quotients, gcds, or lcms of ideals in a Dedekind domain, replace each ideal by its exponent-vector and compute in $\bigoplus_\mathfrak{p}\mathbb{Z}$. This is what makes the [[Def - Fractional Ideal and the Ideal Class Group|class group]] computable — it is the cokernel of $K^\times$ inside this free group.

**The converse — invertibility characterizes Dedekind domains — is the cleanest definition for some purposes.** Among Noetherian domains, "every nonzero fractional ideal is invertible" is equivalent to being Dedekind, and this is often taken as *the* definition, because it makes the group structure manifest and avoids mentioning dimension or normality directly. The diagnostic value: to recognize a Dedekind domain, you may either check the three axioms (Noetherian, integrally closed, dimension $1$) or check that ideals are invertible; the equivalence means these are interchangeable. This perspective also explains why $\operatorname{Pic}$ and the class group are the natural invariants — they live in the group $\mathcal{I}(A)$ whose existence *is* the Dedekind condition.
