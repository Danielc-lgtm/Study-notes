# Notation Registry

**Standing convention.** Throughout these commutative algebra notes, *ring* means a commutative ring with a multiplicative identity $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$; *module* means a unital module. The two starring assumptions of this chapter are **finite generation** of the module and **smallness of the ideal** — small in the sense of lying inside the Jacobson radical, and largest when the ring is local. Almost every result has the shape "for a *finitely generated* module, an ideal $\mathfrak a$ that is *small enough* cannot fill the module unless the module is already trivial." The single slogan to carry, restated in Motivation, is: **over a local ring, a finitely generated module is controlled by its reduction modulo the maximal ideal — a vector space.**

- $R, A, B$ — commutative rings with $1$
- $M, N$ — finitely generated $R$-modules unless stated otherwise
- $\mathfrak a, \mathfrak b$ — ideals of $R$; $\mathfrak p, \mathfrak q$ — prime ideals; $\mathfrak m, \mathfrak n$ — maximal ideals
- $(R, \mathfrak m)$ — a **local ring** with unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$, residue field $k = R/\mathfrak m$
- $\mathfrak a M = \{\sum_i a_i m_i : a_i \in \mathfrak a,\ m_i \in M\}$ — the **submodule generated by products** $a m$ with $a \in \mathfrak a$, $m \in M$
- $J(R) = \bigcap_{\mathfrak m \text{ maximal}} \mathfrak m$ — the **Jacobson radical**, the intersection of all maximal ideals
- $\operatorname{End}_R(M) = \operatorname{Hom}_R(M, M)$ — the (noncommutative) $R$-algebra of $R$-linear endomorphisms of $M$
- $\operatorname{adj}(Q)$ — the **adjugate** (classical adjoint) of a square matrix $Q$, satisfying $\operatorname{adj}(Q)\,Q = (\det Q)\,I$
- $M/\mathfrak m M$ — the **reduction of $M$ mod $\mathfrak m$**, a vector space over $k = R/\mathfrak m$
- $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ — the **cotangent space** of $(R, \mathfrak m)$, a vector space over $k$; its dimension is the **embedding dimension**
- $\dim_k(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)$ — the minimal number of generators of $\mathfrak m$ (in the Noetherian local case)
- $R^n$ — the free $R$-module of rank $n$; $e_1, \dots, e_n$ — its standard basis
- $f, \varphi$ — $R$-linear maps, usually endomorphisms $M \to M$
- $T$ — a formal indeterminate, used to make $M$ into an $R[T]$-module by letting $T$ act as a chosen endomorphism

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **Nakayama's lemma says that a finitely generated module over a local ring cannot be filled up by its own maximal ideal — and so the module is determined, up to "negligible" corrections, by the vector space you get when you reduce modulo that ideal.** It is a small lemma with a colossal reach. The statement is almost embarrassingly short — "if $M$ is finitely generated and $\mathfrak a M = M$ with $\mathfrak a$ inside the Jacobson radical, then $M = 0$" — and yet it is the tool that lets you transfer information from a *field* (where you have all of linear algebra) back up to a general local ring (where you have very little). The reason vector spaces are easy and modules are hard is the existence of bases; Nakayama is the bridge that lets a basis of the reduced vector space $M/\mathfrak m M$ control the whole module $M$.

To see why such a thing should be true, start with the integers' analogue and watch it fail, because the failure is instructive. Take $R = \mathbb Z$, $\mathfrak a = (p)$, and $M = \mathbb Z[\tfrac1p]/\mathbb Z$ — the $p$-power-torsion "Prüfer" module. Then $pM = M$: every element is already $p$ times something. Yet $M \neq 0$. What went wrong? $M$ is *not finitely generated*. Finite generation is precisely the hypothesis that prevents an ideal from refilling the module forever by descending into ever-smaller denominators. The whole chapter is the working-out of this single tension: a small ideal $\mathfrak a$ tries to "use up" the module by writing every generator as an $\mathfrak a$-combination of the others, and finite generation pins the generators down so that this can only happen if there were nothing there to begin with.

The engine underneath is one clean idea, the **determinant trick**, due to Cayley and Hamilton in this generality. If $f : M \to M$ is linear and $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$, then writing each $f(m_i)$ as an $\mathfrak a$-combination of a chosen finite generating set turns "$f$" into a matrix $P$ with entries in $\mathfrak a$, and the relation $(T\cdot I - P)\mathbf m = 0$ — multiplied through by the adjugate — forces $f$ to satisfy its own characteristic polynomial $f^n + a_1 f^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$ with $a_i \in \mathfrak a$. Specialise $f = \operatorname{id}$ and the equation becomes $(1 + a_1 + \dots + a_n)\operatorname{id} = 0$; if the $a_i$ live in the Jacobson radical, that coefficient $1 + (\text{radical})$ is a unit, and a unit times the identity being zero means the module is zero. That is the whole proof. Everything else in the chapter is a corollary or a re-use of the matrix.

The chapter's structural backbone is the chain of implications

$$\text{Cayley–Hamilton} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\mathfrak a M = M \Rightarrow (1{-}a)M=0} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{Nakayama} \;\Longrightarrow\; \big(\text{generators of } M \text{ lift from } M/\mathfrak m M\big),$$

and the payoff, stated for a local ring $(R,\mathfrak m)$ with residue field $k$, is the dictionary **a minimal generating set of $M$ is the same data as a $k$-basis of $M/\mathfrak m M$**. The number of generators you genuinely need is $\dim_k M/\mathfrak m M$, computable by linear algebra. Applied to $M = \mathfrak m$ itself, this number $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is the **embedding dimension** of the ring, the algebraic measure of how many "coordinates" the point cut out by $\mathfrak m$ requires — the first appearance of the cotangent space, which is where this chapter touches geometry most directly.

This chapter assumes you are fluent with [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated modules]], [[Def - Submodule|submodules]] and [[Def - Quotient Module|quotient modules]] from the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|Modules]] notes, with [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] from the [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2|Rings]] notes, and — most importantly — with [[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field|local rings, residue fields]] and the localization machinery from [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization]], where the [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local–global principle]] already advertised that "reduce to a local ring, then use Nakayama" is the central manoeuvre of the subject. The determinant trick uses the adjugate identity $\operatorname{adj}(Q)Q = (\det Q)I$, valid over any commutative ring, which you met in linear algebra ([[Thm - Cofactor Expansion and Cramer's Rule|cofactor expansion]]); no field is needed for it.

# Concept Map

## §5.1 Cayley–Hamilton and the Jacobson Radical

- **[[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)]]**
	- For a finitely generated $R$-module $M$, an $R$-linear $f : M \to M$, and an ideal $\mathfrak a$ with $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$, there exist $n \geq 1$ and $a_1, \dots, a_n \in \mathfrak a$ with $f^n + a_1 f^{n-1} + \dots + a_n \operatorname{id} = 0$ in $\operatorname{End}_R(M)$. The proof makes $M$ an $R[T]$-module with $T$ acting as $f$, writes the generators' images as $T\mathbf m = P\mathbf m$ for a matrix $P$ over $\mathfrak a$, and multiplies $(T I - P)\mathbf m = 0$ by the adjugate to get $\det(TI - P)\cdot m = 0$ for all $m$. The coefficients of $T^0, \dots, T^{n-1}$ in this monic characteristic polynomial lie in $\mathfrak a$. This is the load-bearing engine of the whole chapter and is reused for **integral extensions** (the same matrix proves "integral $=$ module-finite") and in **dimension theory**.

- **[[Def - The Jacobson Radical]]**
	- $J(R) = \bigcap_{\mathfrak m \text{ maximal}} \mathfrak m$ is the intersection of all maximal ideals of $R$. For a local ring $(R,\mathfrak m)$ it is just $\mathfrak m$ — as large as possible — which is why Nakayama is most powerful there. For $\mathbb Z$ it is $(0)$, since no nonzero integer is divisible by every prime, which is why Nakayama is useless over $\mathbb Z$. It always contains the nilradical, and consists exactly of the elements $x$ such that $1 - xy$ is a unit for every $y$ — the operational characterisation that makes it interact with the determinant trick.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The tangent and cotangent space at a point *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The Jacobson radical of the **local ring of a point** is its maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$, the functions vanishing at the point. The reduction $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is the **cotangent space** $T^*_p$, and its dual $(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)^*$ is the **Zariski tangent space** $T_p$. This is the infinitesimal geometry of a point made algebraic: a function vanishing at $p$ is determined "to first order" by its class in $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$, exactly as a smooth function's differential at a point lives in the cotangent space.

## §5.2 Nakayama and Minimal Generators

- **[[Thm - The Jacobson Radical via Units]]**
	- $x \in J(R)$ if and only if $1 - xy$ is a unit of $R$ for every $y \in R$. The forward direction: if some $1 - xy$ were a non-unit it would lie in a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$, but $x \in J(R) \subseteq \mathfrak m$ forces $1 = (1-xy) + xy \in \mathfrak m$, absurd. The converse manufactures, from $x \notin \mathfrak m$, an equation $t + xy = 1$ with $t \in \mathfrak m$, exhibiting $1 - xy = t$ as a non-unit. This is the bridge that converts "$\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$" into "$1 + (\text{element of } \mathfrak a)$ is a unit", which is exactly what Nakayama needs to invert.

- **[[Thm - Nakayama's Lemma]]**
	- If $M$ is a finitely generated $R$-module and $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ is an ideal with $\mathfrak a M = M$, then $M = 0$. The proof: Cayley–Hamilton with $f = \operatorname{id}$ gives $a \in \mathfrak a$ with $(1-a)m = 0$ for all $m$, and $1 - a$ is a unit because $a \in J(R)$, so $m = 0$. The submodule form: if $N \subseteq M$ with $\mathfrak a M + N = M$, then $N = M$ — apply Nakayama to $M/N$. Finite generation is essential (the Prüfer module $\mathbb Z[\tfrac1p]/\mathbb Z$ shows what goes wrong without it), and $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ is essential ($\mathbb Z$ with $\mathfrak a = (p)$ would otherwise lie).

- **[[Def - Minimal Generating Set and the Cotangent Space]]**
	- Over a local ring $(R,\mathfrak m)$ with residue field $k$, a generating set $\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ of a finitely generated $M$ is **minimal** iff the images $\bar x_i$ form a $k$-basis of $M/\mathfrak m M$; hence every minimal generating set has the same size $\dim_k M/\mathfrak m M$. Applied to $M = \mathfrak m$, the vector space $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is the **cotangent space** and $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is the **embedding dimension** — the minimal number of generators of $\mathfrak m$. This is the unique feature distinguishing modules over local rings from modules over general rings, where "minimal number of generators" is ill-behaved.

- **[[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal]]**
	- Let $(R,\mathfrak m)$ be local, $M$ finitely generated, and $x_1,\dots,x_n \in M$ with images spanning $M/\mathfrak m M$ over $k = R/\mathfrak m$. Then $x_1,\dots,x_n$ generate $M$. The proof sets $N = Rx_1 + \dots + Rx_n$; spanning $M/\mathfrak m M$ says $M = N + \mathfrak m M$, and the submodule Nakayama gives $N = M$. This is the statement that does the actual work in practice: it lets you *lift a basis from the residue field*, turning a linear-algebra computation into a generating set for the module.

> [!tip] Unlocked: A coherent sheaf is generated by finitely many germs at a point *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Reading $M$ as the stalk of a **coherent sheaf** $\mathcal F$ at a point with local ring $(R,\mathfrak m)$ and residue field $k$, the fibre is $\mathcal F \otimes k = M/\mathfrak m M$. The lifting theorem says **sections that span the fibre at $p$ already generate the stalk near $p$** — finitely many germs that fill the fibre determine the sheaf in a neighbourhood. This is Nakayama's geometric content and underlies "the rank of a coherent sheaf is upper semicontinuous" and the construction of a minimal free resolution.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Regular local rings and smooth points *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> A Noetherian local ring is **regular** when its embedding dimension equals its Krull dimension, $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2 = \dim R$ — the algebraic definition of a **smooth point** of a variety. The cotangent space $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ computed in this chapter is exactly the obstruction: a singular point is one where the variety needs *more* coordinates than its dimension, i.e. $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2 > \dim R$. The full theory lives in **Commutative Algebra XII — Dimension Theory**.

### Exercises (interleaved)

- **[[Ex - Generators lifting from M over mM]]** (⭐)
	- Over a local ring $(R,\mathfrak m)$, if $x_1,\dots,x_n \in M$ (finitely generated) have images spanning the $k$-vector space $M/\mathfrak m M$, show they generate $M$. The clean application of submodule Nakayama: $M = N + \mathfrak m M \Rightarrow N = M$.

- **[[Ex - Minimal generators of the maximal ideal and the cotangent space]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For a Noetherian local ring $(R,\mathfrak m)$, show $a_1,\dots,a_n$ generate $\mathfrak m$ iff their images span $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ over $k$, and deduce $\mathfrak m$ needs exactly $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ generators, no fewer. Introduces the cotangent space and embedding dimension.

- **[[Ex - A surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module is injective]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For $\varphi : M \to M$ with $M$ finitely generated, surjective $\Rightarrow$ injective, via making $M$ an $R[T]$-module with $T \mapsto \varphi$ and applying Cayley–Hamilton with $\mathfrak a = (T)$. Show finite generation is necessary and the converse fails.

- **[[Ex - Tensor of nonzero finitely generated modules over a local ring is nonzero]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For finitely generated $M, N$ over a local ring $(A,\mathfrak m)$: if $M \otimes_A N = 0$ then $M = 0$ or $N = 0$. Reduce mod $\mathfrak m$ to the field case ($V \otimes_k W = 0 \Rightarrow V=0$ or $W=0$), then lift with Nakayama.

- **[[Ex - A module with mM equal to M that is nonzero]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Over $\mathbb Z_{(p)}$, find $M \neq 0$ with $(p)M = M$ (take $M = \mathbb Q$), and explain why Nakayama is not contradicted: $M$ is not finitely generated. The counterexample that isolates the finiteness hypothesis.

- **[[Ex - Every generating set of size n for R^n is a basis]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- For a nonzero ring $R$: any $n$ elements that generate $R^n$ form a basis; deduce $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m \Rightarrow n \leq m$. Uses the surjective-endomorphism result and the determinant trick to control rank without a notion of dimension.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Vector bundles have a well-defined rank *(from Algebraic Geometry / Topology)*
> That every size-$n$ generating set of $R^n$ is a basis, and that $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$ forces $n \le m$, is the algebraic statement that **free modules have invariant rank over any nonzero commutative ring** — the seed of "a vector bundle has a well-defined rank" via the **Serre–Swan** correspondence. The determinant trick supplies the rank invariance that, over a field, comes for free from dimension.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The goals of this chapter cluster into five recognisable types. The first is **showing a finitely generated module is zero** — the bare Nakayama conclusion. The route is always: exhibit $\mathfrak a M = M$ for a small ideal $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$, then conclude $M = 0$ via [[Thm - Nakayama's Lemma]]. The second, and the one that does the most daily work, is **producing or counting generators of a module** — proving that a candidate list generates, or that no shorter list can. Over a local ring this routes through [[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal]]: reduce mod $\mathfrak m$, do linear algebra in the vector space $M/\mathfrak m M$, and lift. The third is **proving two submodules coincide**, $N = M$, when $N$ accounts for everything modulo a small ideal: this is the submodule form $\mathfrak a M + N = M \Rightarrow N = M$. The fourth is **deducing injectivity or rank facts from surjectivity**, the most surprising application — a surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module is automatically injective, and free modules have invariant rank — both proved by feeding the endomorphism into [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|the determinant trick]] with $\mathfrak a = (T)$ in $R[T]$. The fifth is **computing the embedding dimension** $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ of a local ring, the cotangent-space invariant that detects singularity. These are the natural targets because Nakayama is fundamentally a *rigidity* statement: it says finitely generated modules over local rings are stiff enough that residue-field data controls them.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are stereotyped, and recognising them is half the battle. **The module is finitely generated** — this is non-negotiable; the moment it appears, the determinant trick and all of Nakayama become available, and the moment it is absent, expect a counterexample (a non-finitely-generated module is the standard way to break every theorem here). **The ring is local, or the ideal lies in $J(R)$** — locality makes $\mathfrak m = J(R)$ as large as possible; recognising "$(R,\mathfrak m)$" should trigger "reduce mod $\mathfrak m$ to a vector space". **An endomorphism, or a surjection, of a module onto itself or a free module** — this routes to the $R[T]$-module construction and Cayley–Hamilton with $\mathfrak a = (T)$; the trigger is "$\varphi : M \to M$" or "$R^n \twoheadrightarrow R^n$". **A spanning condition modulo an ideal**, "$M = N + \mathfrak a M$" or "images span $M/\mathfrak m M$" — this is the submodule-Nakayama or lifting input. **An element is suspected to be a unit because it is $1 + (\text{small})$** — this routes through [[Thm - The Jacobson Radical via Units]], $1 - xy \in R^\times$ for $x \in J(R)$. The discipline is to match the source to the target along the chapter's spine: a "show $M=0$" target wants $\mathfrak a M = M$ with $\mathfrak a$ small; a "count generators" target wants the residue-field reduction; a "surjective $\Rightarrow$ injective" target wants the $R[T]$ trick.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every Nakayama problem is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

1. **Write the action of a map on generators as a matrix over the ideal.** If $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_n$ and $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$, then each $f(m_i) = \sum_j P_{ij} m_j$ with $P_{ij} \in \mathfrak a$, so $f$ becomes a matrix $P \in M_n(R)$ with entries in $\mathfrak a$. This is the universal first step of the determinant trick. *Trigger:* a linear map satisfies $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$ (or $\subseteq M$, with $\mathfrak a = R$). *Pattern:* "pick generators, build $P$, form $TI - P$".

2. **Hit a relation $(TI - P)\mathbf m = 0$ with the adjugate.** From $(TI-P)\mathbf m = 0$ over $R[T]$, left-multiply by $\operatorname{adj}(TI-P)$ to get $\det(TI - P)\cdot m_i = 0$ for every generator, hence $\det(TI-P)$ annihilates all of $M$. The adjugate identity $\operatorname{adj}(Q)Q = (\det Q)I$ holds over any commutative ring — no field needed. *Trigger:* a square matrix annihilates a generating vector. *Pattern:* "multiply by $\operatorname{adj}$; read off that $\det$ kills the module".

3. **Specialise the characteristic polynomial at $f = \operatorname{id}$.** When $\mathfrak a M = M$, run the trick with $f = \operatorname{id}_M$: the equation $f^n + a_1 f^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$ becomes $(1 + a_1 + \dots + a_n)\operatorname{id} = 0$, so $(1 - a)\operatorname{id} = 0$ with $a = -(a_1 + \dots + a_n) \in \mathfrak a$. *Trigger:* you have $\mathfrak a M = M$ and want a single annihilating $1 - a$. *Pattern:* "Cayley–Hamilton at the identity gives $am = m$ for one $a \in \mathfrak a$".

3'. **Make $M$ into an $R[T]$-module by letting $T$ act as $\varphi$.** To apply the determinant trick to a bare endomorphism $\varphi : M \to M$, regard $M$ as a module over $R[T]$ with $T \cdot m := \varphi(m)$. If $\varphi$ is surjective, then $(T) M = M$ as an $R[T]$-module, so Cayley–Hamilton applies with the ideal $\mathfrak a = (T)$. *Trigger:* "$\varphi$ surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module". *Pattern:* "let $T$ act as $\varphi$; surjectivity is $TM = M$".

4. **Invert $1 + (\text{Jacobson element})$.** If $a \in J(R)$ then $1 - a$ is a unit, by [[Thm - The Jacobson Radical via Units]]. So an equation $(1-a)m = 0$ with $a \in J(R)$ collapses to $m = 0$. *Trigger:* an annihilator of the form $1 - a$ with $a$ small. *Pattern:* "$a \in J(R) \Rightarrow 1 - a \in R^\times \Rightarrow m = 0$".

5. **Reduce modulo $\mathfrak m$ to land in a vector space.** Over a local $(R,\mathfrak m)$, tensoring with $k = R/\mathfrak m$ (equivalently quotienting by $\mathfrak m M$) turns the finitely generated module $M$ into the finite-dimensional $k$-vector space $M/\mathfrak m M$, where bases, dimension and linear independence are available. *Trigger:* a local ring and a question about generation or freeness. *Pattern:* "work in $M/\mathfrak m M$, then lift by [[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal|the generator-lifting theorem]]".

6. **Lift a spanning set from the residue field to a generating set.** A list whose images span $M/\mathfrak m M$ generates $M$ — [[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal]]. So choose a $k$-basis of $M/\mathfrak m M$, lift each vector to $M$ arbitrarily, and you have a minimal generating set. *Trigger:* "construct a (minimal) generating set over a local ring". *Pattern:* "basis of $M/\mathfrak m M$ lifts to minimal generators".

7. **Pass to the quotient $M/N$ to use the submodule form.** To prove $N = M$ from $\mathfrak a M + N = M$, observe $\mathfrak a(M/N) = M/N$ and apply Nakayama to the finitely generated module $M/N$. *Trigger:* a spanning-modulo-$N$ hypothesis. *Pattern:* "$\mathfrak a M + N = M \Rightarrow \mathfrak a(M/N) = M/N \Rightarrow M/N = 0$".

8. **Compute the cotangent space $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$.** To find the embedding dimension of $(R,\mathfrak m)$, present $\mathfrak m$, quotient by $\mathfrak m^2$, and take the $k$-dimension; this is the minimal number of generators of $\mathfrak m$ by Nakayama applied to $M = \mathfrak m$ (which is finitely generated because $R$ is Noetherian). *Trigger:* "how many elements generate $\mathfrak m$?" or "is this point smooth?". *Pattern:* "$\#\{\text{minimal generators of } \mathfrak m\} = \dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$".

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Applying Nakayama without finite generation
> The conclusion "$\mathfrak a M = M \Rightarrow M = 0$" is so clean that one forgets it requires $M$ finitely generated. It is false otherwise. Over $R = \mathbb Z_{(p)}$ with $\mathfrak m = (p) = J(R)$, the module $M = \mathbb Q$ (or $\mathbb Z[\tfrac1p]/\mathbb Z$ over $\mathbb Z$) satisfies $pM = M$ — every rational is $p$ times a rational — yet $M \neq 0$. The determinant trick breaks because there is no *finite* generating set to build the matrix $P$ from; the module keeps refilling itself through ever-deeper denominators. The operation becomes legal exactly when $M$ is **finitely generated**, which is what bounds the size of the characteristic polynomial and pins the generators down.

> [!warning] 2. Using Nakayama over a ring whose Jacobson radical is too small
> Because Nakayama is stated for "$\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$", and because over a local ring $J(R) = \mathfrak m$ is huge, one is tempted to apply it with any maximal ideal over any ring. But over $\mathbb Z$, $J(\mathbb Z) = (0)$ — the intersection of all $(p)$ is zero — so the *only* ideal Nakayama accepts is $(0)$, and the lemma says nothing. Concretely $\mathfrak a = (p)$ is *not* inside $J(\mathbb Z)$, so "$pM = M$" carries no conclusion; indeed $M = \mathbb Z/q$ for a different prime $q$ has $pM = M$ and $M \neq 0$. The operation is legal exactly when $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ — guaranteed when $\mathfrak a = \mathfrak m$ and $R$ is **local**, and that is why one always localizes first.

> [!warning] 3. Believing a surjective endomorphism is an isomorphism for granted, or that injective $\Rightarrow$ surjective
> Nakayama gives "surjective $\Rightarrow$ injective" for a finitely generated module, so it is tempting to expect the symmetric statement, "injective $\Rightarrow$ surjective", and to conflate the two into "any one-sided inverse is two-sided". The reverse implication is *false*: multiplication by $2$ on $\mathbb Z$ is an injective $\mathbb Z$-module endomorphism that is not surjective. The asymmetry is real and comes from the determinant trick only seeing *surjectivity* as the input "$(T)M = M$". The operation "surjective $\Rightarrow$ injective" is legal precisely with **finite generation** (over $R = k[x_1,x_2,\dots]$, the shift endomorphism of $M = R$ is... still injective; but the standard non-finitely-generated counterexamples to "surjective $\Rightarrow$ injective" require dropping finite generation, e.g. the left shift on $\bigoplus_{\mathbb N} k$).

> [!warning] 4. Confusing $\mathfrak m M$ with $\mathfrak m \cap M$ or with $\mathfrak m \cdot (\text{ambient ring})$
> The submodule $\mathfrak m M = \{\sum a_i m_i : a_i \in \mathfrak m, m_i \in M\}$ is generated by *products of ideal elements with module elements*; it is not an intersection and not the ideal $\mathfrak m$ sitting inside $M$. For $M = R$ it does equal the ideal $\mathfrak m$, which is the source of the confusion, but for $M = R^2$ it is $\mathfrak m \oplus \mathfrak m$, not $\mathfrak m$. Getting this wrong corrupts the vector space $M/\mathfrak m M$: for $M = R^2$ over a local ring, $M/\mathfrak m M = k^2$, dimension $2$, and the module needs two generators — read it as $\mathfrak m$ and you would wrongly predict one.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any Nakayama problem is to identify which of the three faces of the lemma you are looking at, because each has a fixed route.

If the problem **asks you to show a finitely generated module is zero**, you are using bare [[Thm - Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama]], and the entire task is to manufacture the hypothesis $\mathfrak a M = M$ for a small ideal. The recognition step is spotting that "every element of $M$ is already an $\mathfrak a$-combination" — this is what $\mathfrak a M = M$ means — and the small ideal is almost always the maximal ideal of a local ring, so the move is to *localize first* if you are not already local. Once $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ and $M$ is finitely generated, the conclusion is automatic. The classic trap is forgetting to check finite generation; if $M$ is not finitely generated, the right instinct is to *look for a counterexample*, not a proof.

If the problem **asks about generators** — proving a list generates, finding a minimal generating set, or counting — the controlling fact is that *over a local ring, generation is a residue-field phenomenon*. Reduce $M$ modulo $\mathfrak m$ to the vector space $M/\mathfrak m M$, where you have all of linear algebra: a list generates $M$ iff its images span $M/\mathfrak m M$ ([[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal]]), and a minimal generating set corresponds to a basis, so the minimal number of generators is $\dim_k M/\mathfrak m M$. To produce a minimal generating set, take a basis of $M/\mathfrak m M$ and lift. To prove "no fewer generators exist", note any generating set must span $M/\mathfrak m M$, hence has at least $\dim_k M/\mathfrak m M$ elements. Applied to $M = \mathfrak m$, this computes the embedding dimension. The recognition step is "local ring + question about generators $\Rightarrow$ pass to $M/\mathfrak m M$".

If the problem **asks you to extract injectivity, rank, or an isomorphism from a surjection**, you are using the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]] in its sharpest form, and the strategy is the $R[T]$-module construction. Given a surjective endomorphism $\varphi : M \to M$, regard $M$ as an $R[T]$-module with $T$ acting as $\varphi$; surjectivity reads as $(T)M = M$, so Cayley–Hamilton gives a polynomial relation $\varphi^n + a_1 \varphi^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$ with $a_i \in (T)$, i.e. $a_i = T b_i(T)$. Rearranged, this says $\operatorname{id} = \varphi \circ (\text{something})$, exhibiting a two-sided inverse, hence $\varphi$ is injective. For rank statements about free modules — every size-$n$ generating set of $R^n$ is a basis, $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m \Rightarrow n \leq m$ — the same trick controls the rank without any notion of dimension, which is why it works over an arbitrary nonzero commutative ring. The recognition step is "endomorphism or self-map of a free module $\Rightarrow$ let $T$ act, then Cayley–Hamilton".

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "how much of this finitely generated module survives reduction modulo a small ideal?"** Bare Nakayama is the case where nothing survives ($\mathfrak a M = M$ means the reduction is zero, so the module is zero). The generator theorems are the case where the reduction is a vector space and you read the module off its basis. The endomorphism applications are the case where you reduce the *map* and use that a surjection of vector spaces between equal dimensions is bijective, then lift the bijectivity. Decide what reduction you are taking and which residue-field fact you want to lift, and the right tool is determined.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|The determinant trick]]**: a finitely generated module's endomorphism with $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$ satisfies a monic polynomial with coefficients in $\mathfrak a$. This is the most reused result not just in this chapter but downstream: it *is* the proof of Nakayama (take $f = \operatorname{id}$), it is the proof that "integral over $R$" equals "module-finite" in [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]] (take $f = $ multiplication by the candidate integral element), and it controls rank in the free-module exercises. Reach for it whenever a linear map "compresses" a finitely generated module into an ideal multiple of itself.

- **[[Thm - Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama's lemma]]**: $\mathfrak a M = M$ with $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ and $M$ finitely generated forces $M = 0$. Its reusability is as a *finishing move*: any time a local computation produces "$\mathfrak a M = M$" or "$\mathfrak a M + N = M$", Nakayama closes it instantly. It is the workhorse behind every "it suffices to check at the residue field" reduction in commutative algebra and the reason the [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local–global principle]] has teeth.

- **[[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal|The generator-lifting theorem]]**: over a local ring, a spanning set of $M/\mathfrak m M$ generates $M$. This is the single most-used consequence in practice, because it converts module-generation (hard) into vector-space-spanning (easy). Its standard uses are constructing minimal free resolutions, computing the number of generators of an ideal, and proving that a map is surjective by checking surjectivity on fibres. Whenever a local ring and a generation question meet, this is the first move.

- **[[Thm - The Jacobson Radical via Units|The unit characterisation of J(R)]]**: $x \in J(R) \iff 1 - xy \in R^\times$ for all $y$. Its reusability is as the *bridge* that makes Nakayama's algebra go through: it is exactly what turns "$a \in \mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$" into "$1 - a$ is invertible", the inversion that collapses $(1-a)m = 0$ to $m = 0$. It is also the cleanest way to recognise the Jacobson radical in computations.

- **The residue-field reduction $M \rightsquigarrow M/\mathfrak m M$**: over a local ring, tensoring with $k$ replaces a module by a vector space. This is reusable as a *strategy* more than a single fact: it is the move that imports all of linear algebra — dimension, bases, rank, linear independence — into module theory, and Nakayama is the licence to lift the conclusions back. The trigger is any local ring together with a structural question (generation, freeness, rank), and the base fact under everything is that $k$-vector spaces are classified by dimension.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is the cotangent space, its dual is the Zariski tangent space, and Nakayama is the infinitesimal geometry of a point.** To a point $p$ on a variety one attaches the [[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field|local ring]] $(\mathcal O_p, \mathfrak m_p)$ of functions defined near $p$, with $\mathfrak m_p$ the functions vanishing at $p$ and residue field $k = \mathcal O_p/\mathfrak m_p$ the field of values. A function vanishing at $p$ has a well-defined *differential* at $p$ — its first-order behaviour — and the space of such differentials is precisely $\mathfrak m_p/\mathfrak m_p^2$: squaring kills second-order-and-higher vanishing, so $\mathfrak m_p/\mathfrak m_p^2$ records exactly the linear part. This is the **cotangent space** $T^*_p$, and its $k$-dual $(\mathfrak m_p/\mathfrak m_p^2)^*$ is the **Zariski tangent space** $T_p$ — the algebraic definition of "tangent vectors at $p$" valid even when the variety is singular and has no smooth chart. The minimal-generators theorem of this chapter says $\dim_k \mathfrak m_p/\mathfrak m_p^2$ is the smallest number of equations needed to cut out $p$ locally, the **embedding dimension**; the point is **smooth** exactly when this equals the dimension of the variety. So the linear algebra of $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ done here is literally the differential calculus of varieties.

2. **Sheaf theory — Nakayama is "a coherent sheaf is generated near $p$ by sections that fill the fibre at $p$".** A **coherent sheaf** $\mathcal F$ on a space has, at each point $p$, a stalk $\mathcal F_p$ (a finitely generated module over the local ring $\mathcal O_p$) and a fibre $\mathcal F_p \otimes_{\mathcal O_p} k = \mathcal F_p/\mathfrak m_p \mathcal F_p$ (a finite-dimensional $k$-vector space — the value of the sheaf "at $p$"). The generator-lifting theorem $\big($[[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal]]$\big)$ says sections of $\mathcal F$ whose values span the fibre at $p$ already generate the stalk, hence generate $\mathcal F$ on a whole neighbourhood. This is why the rank of a coherent sheaf (the fibre dimension) is **upper semicontinuous** — it can only jump up on closed sets — and it is the construction principle of the **minimal free resolution**: at each stage you lift a basis of a fibre to a minimal generating set, and Nakayama guarantees minimality. A section vanishing to first order that nonetheless fills the fibre is determined, by Nakayama, from finitely many germs.

3. **Number theory and the local–global method — localize to make $J(R)$ large, then Nakayama bites.** Over a global ring like $\mathbb Z$ or a Dedekind domain, the Jacobson radical is often $(0)$, so Nakayama is inert. The fix is the central technique of the subject: [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|localize]] at a prime $\mathfrak p$ to pass to $(R_{\mathfrak p}, \mathfrak p R_{\mathfrak p})$, where the maximal ideal *is* the Jacobson radical and Nakayama becomes maximally powerful, then assemble the local conclusions by the [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local–global principle]]. This "$R_{\mathfrak p}$ first, Nakayama second, glue third" pattern is how one proves a finitely generated projective module is locally free, computes ranks of modules over Dedekind domains, and studies ramification one prime at a time. Nakayama is the local engine the global theory feeds.

4. **Linear algebra — Cayley–Hamilton over a ring, and rank invariance without dimension.** The classical Cayley–Hamilton theorem says a matrix over a field satisfies its characteristic polynomial; here the same adjugate argument $\operatorname{adj}(Q)Q = (\det Q)I$ runs over an arbitrary commutative ring and proves the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton (Minimal-Polynomial Form)|module form]]. The striking payoff is that a notion that *seems* to need a field — the invariance of rank, "you cannot generate $R^n$ with fewer than $n$ elements", "$R^n \hookrightarrow R^m \Rightarrow n \leq m$" — survives over any nonzero commutative ring, because the determinant trick substitutes for the dimension-counting that a field would supply. This is the algebraic backbone of the statement that a finite-rank free module has a well-defined rank, the module-theoretic shadow of "a vector bundle has a well-defined rank".

# Insights

**The unifying frame: Nakayama is the device that lets a vector space control a module.** Everything in this chapter is one instrument with one purpose — to transport conclusions across the boundary between the residue field $k = R/\mathfrak m$, where you have bases and dimension and all of linear algebra, and the local ring $R$, where you have almost nothing. The reduction $M \mapsto M/\mathfrak m M$ throws away the ring structure and leaves a $k$-vector space; that is the *easy* direction, and it loses information. Nakayama is the licence to go *back*: it says that for finitely generated modules, the lost information is "small enough" ($\mathfrak m M$, a Jacobson-radical multiple) that residue-field facts lift to module facts — spanning lifts to generating, a basis lifts to a minimal generating set, a surjection of fibres lifts to a surjection of modules. The deep reason it works is the determinant trick, which converts "$\mathfrak m M = M$" into a *unit* $1 - a$ that can be inverted, so the gap $\mathfrak m M$ between $M$ and its reduction can never be the whole module. The slogan to carry: **reduce mod $\mathfrak m$ to compute, use Nakayama to lift.**

**The true name of Nakayama: "a finitely generated module is not its own maximal-ideal multiple."** The official statement quantifies over ideals $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$, but the operational form you actually reach for is the local-ring special case: for $(R,\mathfrak m)$ and finitely generated $M$, **$\mathfrak m M = M \Rightarrow M = 0$**, equivalently $M \neq 0 \Rightarrow M \neq \mathfrak m M \Rightarrow M/\mathfrak m M \neq 0$. In words: a nonzero finitely generated module always has a nonzero reduction; it can never hide entirely inside $\mathfrak m M$. This is the form that powers the generator theorems (a nonzero $M$ has a nonzero fibre, hence a generator that survives reduction) and the geometric picture (a coherent sheaf with nonzero stalk has nonzero fibre). The contrapositive — "if the fibre is zero the stalk is zero" — is exactly "a coherent sheaf supported away from $p$ has zero fibre at $p$".

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module" should fire "let $T$ act as $\varphi$, then Cayley–Hamilton with $\mathfrak a = (T)$".** This is the chapter's most surprising and most transferable move, because it proves something that looks like it needs finiteness of *dimension* using only finiteness of *generation*. The reaction is mechanical: make $M$ an $R[T]$-module via $T \cdot m = \varphi(m)$; surjectivity of $\varphi$ is the statement $(T)M = M$; the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]] then yields $\varphi^n + a_1\varphi^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$ with $a_i \in (T)$, and factoring out the $T$ that every $a_i$ carries exhibits an explicit inverse of $\varphi$. The same reaction, applied to the identity map $R^n \to R^n$ written through a generating set, gives rank invariance. The complementary reflex is the localization reflex inherited from the previous chapter: when the ideal is not inside $J(R)$, *localize first* to make the maximal ideal the Jacobson radical, and only then apply Nakayama. These two reflexes — *let $T$ act and run the determinant trick*, and *localize to enlarge $J(R)$* — between them solve the overwhelming majority of the chapter's problems.
