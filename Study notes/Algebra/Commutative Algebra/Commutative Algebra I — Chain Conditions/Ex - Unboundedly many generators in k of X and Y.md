# Problem Statement

Let $k$ be a field and consider the ideal of $k[X, Y]$ generated by all degree-$n$ monomials:
$$\mathfrak{a}_n = \big(X^n,\ X^{n-1}Y,\ X^{n-2}Y^2,\ \dots,\ XY^{n-1},\ Y^n\big) = \big(X^i Y^{n-i} : 0 \leq i \leq n\big).$$
Prove that **any generating set for $\mathfrak{a}_n$ has at least $n+1$ elements**.

So although $k[X, Y]$ is Noetherian (every ideal *is* finitely generated, by [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem (Algebra Form)|Hilbert]]), there is no *uniform* bound on the number of generators: as $n \to \infty$, the minimal number of generators of $\mathfrak{a}_n$ grows without bound.

(This is Example Sheet 1, Question 7. *Hint from the sheet:* consider the image of $\mathfrak{a}_n$ in $k[X, Y]/\mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$.)

**Recall:**

![[Def - Noetherian Ring#The Definition]]

By [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem (Algebra Form)|Hilbert's basis theorem]], $k[X, Y]$ is Noetherian, so $\mathfrak{a}_n$ *is* finitely generated — the question is the minimal *number* of generators. The ideal $\mathfrak{a}_n$ is **homogeneous** (generated by degree-$n$ monomials), and the natural invariant is the dimension of the $k$-vector space $\mathfrak{a}_n / \mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$, where $\mathfrak{m} = (X, Y)$ — the "minimal generators modulo the maximal ideal" count. The hint's quotient $\mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$ is a clean way to access this.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *lower-bound-on-generators* problem — a quantitative refinement of finite generation, in [[Commutative Algebra I — Chain Conditions#Problem-Solving Strategy|the chapter's finiteness family]]. Hilbert guarantees *finitely* many generators; this exercise asks *how many*, and shows the count is unbounded across the family $\mathfrak{a}_n$. The technique is to find a vector-space quotient of the ideal on which the generators must be a spanning set, and count its dimension.

**Assumption pattern.** The structure to exploit is that $\mathfrak{a}_n$ is generated *entirely in degree $n$*, and the degree-$n$ part of $k[X,Y]$ is $(n+1)$-dimensional (the monomials $X^n, X^{n-1}Y, \dots, Y^n$). The recognisable trigger is "homogeneous ideal generated in a single degree": passing to the quotient by the next degree ($\mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$, or equivalently $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$) kills all higher-degree consequences of the generators, leaving exactly the degree-$n$ piece, on which any generating set must project onto a spanning set.

**Theorem routing.** The route is: (1) form the quotient $V = \mathfrak{a}_n / \mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$ (or $\mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$), a $k$-vector space; (2) show $V$ is exactly the span of the images of the degree-$n$ monomials, with $\dim_k V = n+1$; (3) show that the images of any generating set of $\mathfrak{a}_n$ must *span* $V$, because multiplying generators by elements of $\mathfrak{m}$ (positive degree) lands in $\mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$, contributing nothing new to $V$; (4) conclude any generating set has at least $\dim_k V = n+1$ elements, since a spanning set of an $(n+1)$-dimensional space has at least $n+1$ members.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious move is passing to the quotient $\mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$ (realised here via $\mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$) to *kill the contribution of the coefficients*. A generating set $\{g_1, \dots, g_r\}$ of $\mathfrak{a}_n$ means every element is $\sum h_i g_i$ with $h_i \in k[X,Y]$; the danger is that the coefficients $h_i$ could "create" generators, making $r$ small. The resolution is that the constant terms of the $h_i$ are all that survive modulo $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$ — any positive-degree part of $h_i$ pushes $h_i g_i$ into $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$. So modulo $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$, only $k$-linear combinations of the $g_i$ survive, and they must span the $(n+1)$-dimensional $\mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$. This **Nakayama-flavoured** reduction (minimal generators of an ideal = dimension of $\mathfrak{a}/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}$) is the heart of the argument; the natural error is to try to bound $r$ by manipulating the polynomial coefficients directly.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Commutative Algebra I — Chain Conditions#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Pass to the quotient $\mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$ (a degree-stripping device).** Kill the higher-degree consequences of the generators by working modulo $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n = \mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$, leaving a finite-dimensional vector space whose dimension counts minimal generators.

2. **Grade by total degree.** Use that $\mathfrak{a}_n$ is generated in degree $n$ and that the degree-$n$ part of $k[X,Y]$ is $(n+1)$-dimensional, so $\mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$ has dimension $n+1$.

3. **A spanning-set lower bound.** Use that the images of any generating set must span the $(n+1)$-dimensional quotient, so there are at least $n+1$ generators (a spanning set of a $d$-dimensional space has $\geq d$ elements).

# Solution

The proof passes to the quotient $\mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$, shows it is an $(n+1)$-dimensional $k$-vector space spanned by the degree-$n$ monomials, and observes that the images of any generating set must span it — forcing at least $n+1$ generators. The crux is that modulo $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$, only the constant terms of the coefficients survive.

**Step 1: $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n = \mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$, and the quotient $\mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$ is a $k$-vector space of dimension $n+1$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $\mathfrak{m} = (X, Y)$, the maximal ideal of polynomials vanishing at the origin. Since $\mathfrak{a}_n$ is generated by the degree-$n$ monomials $X^i Y^{n-i}$ ($0 \leq i \leq n$), the product ideal $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$ is generated by $\{X \cdot X^iY^{n-i},\ Y \cdot X^iY^{n-i}\}$, i.e. by all degree-$(n+1)$ monomials $X^j Y^{n+1-j}$ ($0 \leq j \leq n+1$):
> $$\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n = \mathfrak{a}_{n+1}.$$
> (Every degree-$(n+1)$ monomial is $X$ or $Y$ times a degree-$n$ monomial, and conversely.)
>
> The quotient $V = \mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$ is annihilated by $\mathfrak{m}$, so it is a module over $k[X,Y]/\mathfrak{m} \cong k$ — a $k$-vector space. As a $k$-vector space, $\mathfrak{a}_n$ has $k$-basis the monomials of degree $\geq n$ lying in $\mathfrak{a}_n$, namely all monomials of degree $\geq n$ (these are exactly the monomials divisible by some $X^iY^{n-i}$). Modulo $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n = \mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$ (the monomials of degree $\geq n+1$), the surviving classes are exactly the images of the degree-$n$ monomials. There are $n+1$ of them ($X^n, X^{n-1}Y, \dots, Y^n$), and they are $k$-linearly independent in $V$ (distinct monomials, none in $\mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$). Hence
> $$\dim_k V = \dim_k\big(\mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{a}_{n+1}\big) = n+1.$$

**Step 2: The images of any generating set of $\mathfrak{a}_n$ span $V$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $g_1, \dots, g_r$ generate $\mathfrak{a}_n$ as an ideal of $k[X, Y]$. Take any $f \in \mathfrak{a}_n$; then $f = \sum_{i=1}^r h_i g_i$ with $h_i \in k[X, Y]$. Decompose each coefficient $h_i = c_i + h_i'$ into its constant term $c_i \in k$ and its part $h_i' \in \mathfrak{m}$ of positive degree. Then
> $$f = \sum_{i=1}^r c_i g_i + \sum_{i=1}^r h_i' g_i,$$
> and the second sum lies in $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$ (each $h_i' \in \mathfrak{m}$ and $g_i \in \mathfrak{a}_n$). Reducing modulo $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$,
> $$\bar{f} = \sum_{i=1}^r c_i \bar{g}_i \quad \text{in } V = \mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n,$$
> a $k$-linear combination of $\bar{g}_1, \dots, \bar{g}_r$. Since $f \in \mathfrak{a}_n$ was arbitrary, the images $\bar{g}_1, \dots, \bar{g}_r$ **span** $V$.

**Step 3: Therefore $r \geq n+1$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> A spanning set of a $k$-vector space of dimension $d$ has at least $d$ elements. By Step 1, $\dim_k V = n+1$; by Step 2, the $r$ images $\bar{g}_i$ span $V$. Hence
> $$r \geq \dim_k V = n+1.$$
> So any generating set of $\mathfrak{a}_n$ has at least $n+1$ elements. Since $\{X^iY^{n-i} : 0 \leq i \leq n\}$ is a generating set of size exactly $n+1$, the minimal number of generators of $\mathfrak{a}_n$ is exactly $n+1$, which grows without bound — there is no uniform bound on generators across $k[X,Y]$.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** Every generating set of $\mathfrak{a}_n = (X^iY^{n-i} : 0 \leq i \leq n) \trianglelefteq k[X,Y]$ has $\geq n+1$ elements.
>
> Let $\mathfrak{m} = (X, Y)$. Then $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n = \mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$, and $V = \mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$ is a $k$-vector space (annihilated by $\mathfrak{m}$) spanned by the images of the $n+1$ degree-$n$ monomials, which are linearly independent; so $\dim_k V = n+1$.
>
> If $g_1, \dots, g_r$ generate $\mathfrak{a}_n$, then for $f = \sum h_i g_i \in \mathfrak{a}_n$, writing $h_i = c_i + h_i'$ ($c_i \in k$, $h_i' \in \mathfrak{m}$), we get $\bar f = \sum c_i \bar g_i$ in $V$; so $\{\bar g_i\}$ spans $V$, whence $r \geq \dim_k V = n+1$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**The minimal number of generators of an ideal is $\dim_k(\mathfrak{a}/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a})$ — pass to the quotient by $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}$ to count generators.** This is the operative principle, and it is a form of **Nakayama's lemma**: minimal generators of $\mathfrak{a}$ correspond to a basis of the vector space $\mathfrak{a}/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}$ over the residue field $k = R/\mathfrak{m}$. The reusable move whenever you must bound (above or below) the number of generators of a module or ideal: quotient by $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}$ (or $\mathfrak{m}M$), which kills everything reachable by multiplying generators with non-unit coefficients, leaving a vector space whose dimension is exactly the minimal generator count. The trigger is "how many generators does this need?"; the response is "compute $\dim \mathfrak{a}/\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}$". This is the cotangent-space count that recurs in [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma]], where for a local ring $\dim_{R/\mathfrak{m}} \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ counts minimal generators of the maximal ideal.

**Noetherian guarantees finitely many generators but never a uniform bound — generator counts can grow without limit.** This exercise draws a precise boundary: Hilbert's basis theorem says every ideal of $k[X,Y]$ is finitely generated, but the family $\mathfrak{a}_n$ shows the *number* needed is unbounded ($n+1 \to \infty$). The lesson is to separate "finitely generated" (a qualitative, always-true-here statement) from "boundedly generated" (a quantitative statement that fails). The number of generators is governed by *geometry* — here, the fattening of the origin as a scheme (the ideal $\mathfrak{a}_n$ cuts out the origin with multiplicity, and the generator count measures the embedding dimension of the fat point). Whenever Noetherianity is invoked, remember it gives finiteness, not uniformity; quantitative control needs the extra structure of degree or the local dimension.

**Working modulo $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}$ strips the coefficients to their constant terms — this is why only $k$-linear combinations of generators survive.** The technical engine of Step 2 is that any coefficient $h_i$ splits as constant $+$ positive-degree, and the positive-degree part pushes $h_i g_i$ into $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}_n$, contributing nothing to the quotient. So although a generating set lets coefficients range over the whole ring, modulo $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}$ the only freedom left is *scalar* multiples of the generators. This "constant terms are all that survive modulo $\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{a}$" phenomenon is the same mechanism that makes Nakayama's lemma work and that appears in the degree arguments for non-finite-generation (such as [[Ex - A subalgebra of a Noetherian algebra need not be Noetherian]]); recognising it lets you convert any ideal-generation question into a clean linear-algebra dimension count over the residue field.
