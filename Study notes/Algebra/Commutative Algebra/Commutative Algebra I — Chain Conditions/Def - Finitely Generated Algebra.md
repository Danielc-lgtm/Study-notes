# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Let $R$ be a ring and $A$ an [[Def - Algebra over a Ring (R-algebra)|R-algebra]] with structural map $\rho : R \to A$; write $rx := \rho(r)x$. We write $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ for the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial algebra]] in $n$ variables, $p(x_1, \dots, x_m)$ for the element of $A$ obtained by substituting $x_i \in A$ into a polynomial $p \in R[T_1, \dots, T_m]$, and $R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ for the subalgebra of $A$ generated by $x_1, \dots, x_n$. A **finitely generated module** allows only $R$-linear combinations of its generators; a **finitely generated algebra** allows all polynomial combinations — the distinction this page exists to draw. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra I — Chain Conditions]].

# Axiom Motivation

The goal is to say what it means for an algebra to be "small" — built from finitely much data over $R$ — and the entire subtlety is that there are *two* reasonable meanings of "built from finitely many elements", one for modules and one for algebras, and they are genuinely different. Getting the algebra version right, and seeing exactly how it differs from the module version, is the content of this page.

**Why "finitely generated" must mean *polynomial* combinations, not linear ones.** A [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated module]] over $R$ is $M = R m_1 + \cdots + R m_k$ — every element is an $R$-*linear* combination $\sum r_i m_i$ of the generators. If we tried to copy this definition for an algebra, we would be asking that every element of $A$ be an $R$-linear combination of $x_1, \dots, x_n$ — but that is just the finitely-generated-*module* condition, and it ignores the one feature an algebra has that a module does not: its *own multiplication*. The right notion of "generated" for an algebra must let us *multiply* the generators together, forming $x_1^2$, $x_1 x_2$, $x_2^3$, and so on, and take $R$-linear combinations of all such products. The result is that an element of the generated subalgebra is a *polynomial* $p(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ with coefficients in $R$. So an algebra is finitely generated when finitely many elements, *together with the ring operations and $R$-scalars*, produce everything. The defining move is exactly "allow products of generators", and it is forced by the demand that the generated object be a *subring*, not merely a submodule.

**Why this is the same as being a quotient of a polynomial algebra.** The polynomial algebra $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ is the *free* $R$-algebra on $n$ generators: by its universal property, choosing $n$ elements $x_1, \dots, x_n \in A$ is exactly choosing an $R$-algebra homomorphism $\varphi : R[T_1, \dots, T_n] \to A$ with $\varphi(T_i) = x_i$, and the image of $\varphi$ is precisely the subalgebra $R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ generated by the $x_i$. Therefore "$A$ is generated as an $R$-algebra by $x_1, \dots, x_n$" means "$\varphi$ is surjective", i.e. $A \cong R[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I$ for the ideal $I = \ker \varphi$. This presentation is the working form of the definition: a finitely generated algebra is a polynomial algebra modulo relations. It is also why Hilbert's basis theorem reduces to polynomial rings — every finitely generated algebra is a quotient of one, and quotients of Noetherian rings are Noetherian.

**Why the distinction from "finitely generated module" is the whole point, with a witness.** The two finiteness notions are *not* equivalent, and the gap is exactly the gap between linear and polynomial combinations. Consider $A = k[T]$ as a $k$-algebra. As an *algebra* it is finitely generated — by the single element $T$, since every polynomial is $p(T)$. As a *module* over $k$ it is *not* finitely generated: a $k$-linear combination of any finite set $\{f_1, \dots, f_m\}$ of polynomials spans only a finite-dimensional space, but $k[T]$ is infinite-dimensional ($1, T, T^2, \dots$ are linearly independent). The mechanism is *unbounded degree*: algebra generation lets degree grow without bound (you keep multiplying $T$ by itself), while module generation caps the degree at $\max_i \deg f_i$. This single example shows the definitions diverge precisely because algebras can iterate multiplication. (See [[Ex - A finitely generated algebra need not be a finitely generated module]].)

**When the two notions *do* coincide, and why.** The gap closes exactly when the generators are *integral* over $R$ — each $x_i$ satisfies a monic polynomial relation $x_i^{d} + r_{d-1} x_i^{d-1} + \cdots + r_0 = 0$ with $r_j \in R$. A monic relation lets you rewrite $x_i^d$, and hence every higher power, as an $R$-linear combination of $1, x_i, \dots, x_i^{d-1}$, capping the degree and collapsing polynomial combinations back to linear ones. So "finitely generated algebra $+$ integral generators $=$ finitely generated module"; this is the content of [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions|integral extensions]], and it explains why $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[i]$ is finite as an $\mathbb{R}$-module ($i^2 = -1$ is monic) while $k[T]$ is not ($T$ satisfies no monic relation over $k$). The integrality hypothesis is exactly what is needed to bound the degree.

# The Definition

Let $R$ be a ring and $A$ an [[Def - Algebra over a Ring (R-algebra)|R-algebra]].

$A$ is a **finitely generated $R$-algebra** (or **of finite type** over $R$) if there exist finitely many elements $x_1, \dots, x_n \in A$ such that every element of $A$ is a polynomial in them with coefficients in $R$:
$$A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n] = \{\, p(x_1, \dots, x_n) : p \in R[T_1, \dots, T_n] \,\}.$$
The elements $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are called **algebra generators** of $A$.

Equivalently, $A$ is finitely generated as an $R$-algebra if and only if it is a **quotient of a polynomial algebra**: there is a surjective $R$-algebra homomorphism
$$R[T_1, \dots, T_n] \twoheadrightarrow A, \qquad T_i \mapsto x_i,$$
so that $A \cong R[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I$ for some ideal $I$. (The equivalence is the universal property of the polynomial algebra: a choice of $n$ elements of $A$ is the same as an $R$-algebra map out of $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$, and the algebra is finitely generated exactly when some such map is onto.)

**Contrast with finitely generated module.** $A$ is a [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated R-module]] if every element is an $R$-*linear* combination $\sum r_i a_i$ of finitely many fixed $a_i$. Finitely-generated-as-module $\implies$ finitely-generated-as-algebra (linear combinations are special polynomials), but not conversely.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

In the category $R\text{-}\mathbf{Alg}$, the polynomial algebras $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ are the **free objects** (left adjoint to the forgetful functor to sets, on $n$ generators), and a **finitely generated** $R$-algebra is precisely a **quotient of a finitely-generated free object** — the algebra-level analogue of "finitely generated module $=$ quotient of $R^{\oplus n}$". The parallel is exact and worth stating side by side:

| | Free object | Finitely generated $=$ |
|---|---|---|
| Modules | $R^{\oplus n}$ | quotient of $R^{\oplus n}$ |
| Algebras | $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ | quotient of $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ |

The map "choose images of generators $\rightsquigarrow$ homomorphism" is the universal property in both rows; the only difference is which free object you start from, and that difference — $R^{\oplus n}$ records *linear* data, $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ records *polynomial* data — is the entire distinction between the two finiteness notions. This is why finite type is the natural smallness condition in algebraic geometry: $\operatorname{Spec}$ of a finitely generated $k$-algebra is a finite-type scheme over $k$, i.e. one cut out by finitely many equations in finitely many variables.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The cleanest compression: **a finitely generated algebra is a polynomial ring modulo relations, $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I$** — finitely many generators (the variables) and, when $R$ is Noetherian so that $I$ is finitely generated, finitely many relations. This is the algebraic incarnation of "an object presented by generators and relations", the same template as a finitely presented group or a finitely presented module.

**True name:** for problem-solving, a finitely generated $R$-algebra is **"a quotient of $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$"** — whenever you must prove something about a finitely generated algebra (Noetherianity, dimension, that it is a coordinate ring), present it as a polynomial-ring quotient and push the property down from the polynomial ring through the quotient.

In algebraic geometry, finitely generated $k$-algebras are exactly the **coordinate rings of affine varieties**, and finite type is the smallness condition making varieties manageable. In number theory, a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra is an **arithmetic scheme** of finite type, the objects of arithmetic geometry. The contrast with finitely generated *module* recurs as the contrast between **finite type** and **finite** morphisms: a finite-type morphism is locally a polynomial-quotient, a finite morphism is locally a module-finite extension, and the gap between them is integrality — exactly the gap [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions|integral extensions]] measures. The unifying frame is that "finitely generated algebra" is the polynomial-degree-unbounded notion, "finitely generated module" is the degree-bounded notion, and integrality is the bridge.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance (algebra, not module) — $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$.** Generated as a $k$-algebra by $T_1, \dots, T_n$, but not as a $k$-module (the monomials are an infinite linearly independent set). The canonical example of the gap; see [[Ex - A finitely generated algebra need not be a finitely generated module]].

**Is an instance (both) — $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[i]$.** Generated as an $\mathbb{R}$-algebra by $i$, and finitely generated as an $\mathbb{R}$-module by $\{1, i\}$. The two notions agree because $i$ is integral over $\mathbb{R}$: the monic relation $i^2 + 1 = 0$ caps the degree, rewriting $i^2 = -1$ and all higher powers in terms of $1, i$.

**Is an instance — a coordinate ring $k[T_1, T_2]/(T_2 - T_1^2)$.** This is the coordinate ring of the parabola $\{(t, t^2)\}$, a finitely generated $k$-algebra presented by two generators and one relation; it is in fact isomorphic to $k[T_1]$ (solve $T_2 = T_1^2$), so it is generated by one element. Illustrates the "generators and relations" presentation.

**Is NOT an instance — $k(T)$, the field of rational functions, as a $k$-algebra.** Although $k(T) = \operatorname{Frac}(k[T])$ looks small, it is *not* a finitely generated $k$-algebra. If it were generated by finitely many rational functions $f_1/g_1, \dots, f_n/g_n$, then every element would have denominator dividing a power of $g_1 \cdots g_n$, but there are infinitely many irreducible polynomials in $k[T]$ (for infinite $k$, or by Euclid's argument), so some $\tfrac{1}{p}$ with $p$ coprime to all $g_i$ is unreachable. This probes that "finitely generated algebra" is a genuine restriction even on familiar rings — fields of fractions are usually not of finite type.

**Is NOT an instance — the subalgebra $k[T_1 T_2, T_1 T_2^2, T_1 T_2^3, \dots] \subseteq k[T_1, T_2]$.** This subalgebra needs infinitely many generators (no finite subset of the $T_1 T_2^j$ generates the rest as an algebra), so it is not a finitely generated $k$-algebra — even though it sits inside the finitely generated $k[T_1, T_2]$. This probes that *subalgebras of finitely generated algebras need not be finitely generated*, the failure behind [[Ex - A subalgebra of a Noetherian algebra need not be Noetherian]].

**Calibration check.** Verify that $k[T]$ is generated by one element as an algebra but needs infinitely many as a module. Confirm that $\mathbb{C}$ is finitely generated as an $\mathbb{R}$-module *because* $i$ satisfies a monic relation. Present $k[T_1, T_2]/(T_1 T_2 - 1)$ as a polynomial-ring quotient and recognise it as $k[T_1, T_1^{-1}]$, the Laurent polynomials (finitely generated as an algebra by $T_1, T_1^{-1}$, not as a module). If you can explain why $k(T)$ fails to be a finitely generated $k$-algebra, you have understood the definition as a real constraint.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Finite-type schemes and varieties *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The spectrum of a finitely generated $k$-algebra is a **finite-type affine scheme** over $k$; gluing these gives finite-type schemes, the geometric objects of the theory. The finiteness of generators corresponds to "embeddable in some affine space $\mathbb{A}^n$", and the finiteness of relations (when $k$ is a field, by Hilbert) to "cut out by finitely many equations". This is the formal home of the algebra–geometry dictionary in [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

> [!tip] Noether normalization *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> Every finitely generated algebra over a field is, after a change of generators, **module-finite over a polynomial subalgebra** $k[y_1, \dots, y_d]$ — this is **Noether normalization**, and the number $d$ is the Krull dimension. It is the structural theorem that controls finitely generated algebras, reducing them to polynomial rings plus a finite (integral) extension, and it powers the Nullstellensatz and dimension theory.

> [!tip] Finite versus finite-type morphisms *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The distinction "finitely generated algebra versus finitely generated module" globalizes to **finite-type versus finite morphisms** of schemes. A finite morphism has finite fibres and is module-finite locally; a finite-type morphism only requires polynomial generation. The gap is integrality, and recognising which one a problem gives you decides whether the fibres are finite — central to [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]] and going-up/going-down.
