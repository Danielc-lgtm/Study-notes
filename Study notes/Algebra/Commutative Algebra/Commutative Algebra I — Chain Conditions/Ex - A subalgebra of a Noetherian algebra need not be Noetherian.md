# Problem Statement

Is every subalgebra of a Noetherian $k$-algebra itself Noetherian? Show that the answer is **no** by proving that the subalgebra
$$B = k\big[\,T_1 T_2,\ T_1 T_2^2,\ T_1 T_2^3,\ \dots\,\big] \;\subseteq\; k[T_1, T_2]$$
of the (Noetherian, by Hilbert) polynomial ring $A = k[T_1, T_2]$ is **not Noetherian**.

(This is Example Sheet 1, Question 8. Equivalently, $B = k[\,T_1 T_2^j : j \geq 1\,]$, the $k$-subalgebra generated by the elements $u_j := T_1 T_2^{\,j}$.)

The intended route: exhibit an ideal of $B$ that is not finitely generated, or equivalently a strictly ascending chain of ideals of $B$ that never stabilises. The natural candidate is the ideal $\mathfrak{b} = (u_1, u_2, u_3, \dots)$ of $B$ generated by *all* the $u_j$.

**Recall:**

![[Def - Noetherian and Artinian Module#Noetherian module]]

A [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian ring]] satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals, equivalently every ideal is finitely generated. By [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem (Algebra Form)|Hilbert's basis theorem]], $A = k[T_1, T_2]$ is Noetherian. A [[Def - Algebra over a Ring (R-algebra)|subalgebra]] $B \subseteq A$ is a subring containing $k$ and closed under the $k$-action. The crux: Hilbert applies to *finitely generated* algebras, and $B$ — a subalgebra of a finitely generated algebra — need **not** itself be finitely generated, which is exactly why it can fail to be Noetherian.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *refute-Noetherian* problem at the [[Commutative Algebra I — Chain Conditions#Problem-Solving Strategy|algebra/module boundary]], and it is the cautionary counterexample to a tempting over-application of Hilbert's basis theorem. The target is to show a specific ring is not Noetherian by exhibiting a non-finitely-generated ideal; the deeper point is that "subalgebra of Noetherian" does not imply "Noetherian", because subalgebras of finitely generated algebras need not be finitely generated.

**Assumption pattern.** The structure to exploit is the **grading by $T_1$-degree** (or total degree). Each generator $u_j = T_1 T_2^j$ has $T_1$-degree exactly $1$, and a product $u_{j_1} \cdots u_{j_m}$ has $T_1$-degree $m$. So an element of $B$ of $T_1$-degree $1$ must be a $k$-linear combination of the $u_j$ alone (degree-$1$ monomials), since products of two or more $u_j$ have $T_1$-degree $\geq 2$. The recognisable trigger is "generators all in one graded layer, with products landing in higher layers" — the bottom layer cannot be reconstructed from products, so the ideal generated by the bottom layer needs all of them.

**Theorem routing.** The route is: (1) grade $B$ by $T_1$-degree; identify the degree-$1$ part of $B$ as $\operatorname{span}_k\{u_j : j \geq 1\}$; (2) consider the ideal $\mathfrak{b} = (u_1, u_2, \dots) \trianglelefteq B$, and show $u_{n+1} \notin (u_1, \dots, u_n)$ by a degree argument — any element of $(u_1, \dots, u_n)$ of $T_1$-degree $1$ is a $k$-combination of $u_1, \dots, u_n$, but $u_{n+1}$ is linearly independent from these; (3) conclude $(u_1) \subsetneq (u_1, u_2) \subsetneq \cdots$ is a non-stabilising ascending chain of ideals, so $B$ is not Noetherian.

**Key decision point.** The genuinely tricky move is the **degree argument showing $u_{n+1} \notin (u_1, \dots, u_n)$ as an ideal of $B$**. The subtlety is that the ideal $(u_1, \dots, u_n)$ of $B$ contains not just $k$-combinations of the $u_i$ but all $B$-multiples $\sum b_i u_i$ — and one must show none of these equals $u_{n+1}$. The resolution is the $T_1$-grading: an element $\sum b_i u_i$ with $b_i \in B$ has, in $T_1$-degree $1$, only the contribution from the *constant* parts of the $b_i$ (since $u_i$ has $T_1$-degree $1$ and any non-constant $b_i \in B$ has $T_1$-degree $\geq 1$, pushing $b_i u_i$ to $T_1$-degree $\geq 2$). So the degree-$1$ part of $(u_1, \dots, u_n)$ is exactly $\operatorname{span}_k\{u_1, \dots, u_n\}$, which omits $u_{n+1}$. Recognising that *only the constant terms of the coefficients reach degree $1$* is the crux; the natural error is to forget that $B$-multiples can be analysed layer by layer.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Commutative Algebra I — Chain Conditions#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Operation 4 (refute a chain condition with one explicit chain).** Exhibit the strictly ascending chain of ideals $(u_1) \subsetneq (u_1, u_2) \subsetneq (u_1, u_2, u_3) \subsetneq \cdots$ of $B$, which never stabilises.

2. **Grade by $T_1$-degree (a structural separation device).** Decompose $B$ and its ideals by $T_1$-degree; the generators $u_j$ all live in degree $1$, while products live in degree $\geq 2$, so the degree-$1$ layer is controlled by $k$-linear combinations of the $u_j$ alone.

3. **Operation 7 / leading-degree tracking, adapted.** Use that an element of the ideal $(u_1, \dots, u_n)$ has its $T_1$-degree-$1$ part equal to a $k$-combination of $u_1, \dots, u_n$ only, so $u_{n+1}$ (linearly independent) cannot lie in it.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Hilbert's basis theorem requires the algebra to be *finitely generated*. The subalgebra $B$ is generated by infinitely many elements $u_j = T_1 T_2^j$. Suspect that $B$ is not finitely generated as an algebra and, more strongly, not Noetherian. To prove not-Noetherian, find an ideal of $B$ that is not finitely generated.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Grade everything by the degree in $T_1$. Each $u_j = T_1 T_2^j$ has $T_1$-degree $1$. What is the $T_1$-degree of a product $u_{j_1} u_{j_2} \cdots u_{j_m}$? What does this say about which elements of $B$ have $T_1$-degree exactly $1$?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> A product of $m$ generators has $T_1$-degree $m$. So the elements of $B$ with $T_1$-degree $1$ are exactly the $k$-linear combinations of the $u_j$ themselves — no product of two or more $u_j$ reaches down to degree $1$. Now consider the ideal $\mathfrak{b} = (u_1, u_2, \dots)$ of $B$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Claim: $u_{n+1} \notin (u_1, \dots, u_n)$ in $B$. An element of $(u_1, \dots, u_n)$ is $\sum_{i=1}^n b_i u_i$ with $b_i \in B$. Look at its $T_1$-degree-$1$ part: $u_i$ has $T_1$-degree $1$, so only the *constant term* of each $b_i$ contributes to degree $1$. Thus the degree-$1$ part is a $k$-combination of $u_1, \dots, u_n$, which cannot equal $u_{n+1}$ (the $u_j$ are $k$-linearly independent). So the chain $(u_1) \subsetneq (u_1, u_2) \subsetneq \cdots$ is strict.

# Solution

The proof grades $B$ by $T_1$-degree, isolates the degree-$1$ layer as the $k$-span of the generators $u_j$, and uses this to show the ideal chain $(u_1) \subsetneq (u_1, u_2) \subsetneq \cdots$ is strictly ascending. The crux is that $B$-multiples of the $u_i$ only reach $T_1$-degree $1$ through the constant terms of their coefficients, so the degree-$1$ part of $(u_1, \dots, u_n)$ omits $u_{n+1}$.

**Step 1: Grade $B$ by $T_1$-degree; the generators live in degree $1$, products in higher degree.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> The ring $A = k[T_1, T_2]$ is graded by $T_1$-degree: $A = \bigoplus_{d \geq 0} A_d$ where $A_d$ is the $k$-span of monomials $T_1^d T_2^e$ ($e \geq 0$). Each generator $u_j = T_1 T_2^j$ lies in $A_1$ (it has $T_1$-degree exactly $1$). A product of generators
> $$u_{j_1} u_{j_2} \cdots u_{j_m} = T_1^m\, T_2^{\,j_1 + \cdots + j_m}$$
> has $T_1$-degree exactly $m$. Therefore $B = k[u_1, u_2, \dots]$ is a graded subalgebra of $A$: $B = \bigoplus_{m \geq 0} B_m$ with $B_m = B \cap A_m$, where $B_0 = k$ (constants), and
> $$B_1 = \operatorname{span}_k\{u_1, u_2, u_3, \dots\}.$$
> Indeed every element of $B$ is a $k$-combination of products of $u_j$, and the degree-$1$ part collects exactly the single-factor products, i.e. the $u_j$ themselves; no product of two or more generators contributes to degree $1$. The $u_j$ are $k$-linearly independent (the monomials $T_1 T_2^j$ are distinct), so $\{u_j\}$ is a $k$-basis of $B_1$ and $\dim_k B_1 = \infty$.

**Step 2: $u_{n+1} \notin (u_1, \dots, u_n)$ as an ideal of $B$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $\mathfrak{a}_n = (u_1, \dots, u_n) \trianglelefteq B$. A general element of $\mathfrak{a}_n$ is $\sum_{i=1}^n b_i u_i$ with $b_i \in B$. Decompose each $b_i = \beta_i + b_i'$ into its constant term $\beta_i \in k = B_0$ and its higher-degree part $b_i' \in \bigoplus_{m \geq 1} B_m$. Then
> $$\sum_{i=1}^n b_i u_i = \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i u_i}_{\in\, B_1} + \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^n b_i' u_i}_{\in\, \bigoplus_{m \geq 2} B_m},$$
> because $\beta_i u_i \in B_1$ (degree $1$) while $b_i' u_i$ has $T_1$-degree $\geq 1 + 1 = 2$ (the $u_i$ contributes degree $1$, the non-constant $b_i'$ contributes degree $\geq 1$). Hence the **$T_1$-degree-$1$ component** of any element of $\mathfrak{a}_n$ is a $k$-linear combination $\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i u_i$ of $u_1, \dots, u_n$ only.
>
> Now $u_{n+1} \in B_1$ is itself purely of degree $1$. If $u_{n+1} \in \mathfrak{a}_n$, its degree-$1$ component (namely $u_{n+1}$) would be a $k$-combination of $u_1, \dots, u_n$. But $u_1, \dots, u_n, u_{n+1}$ are $k$-linearly independent (distinct monomials), so $u_{n+1}$ is not in $\operatorname{span}_k\{u_1, \dots, u_n\}$. Contradiction. Therefore $u_{n+1} \notin \mathfrak{a}_n = (u_1, \dots, u_n)$.

**Step 3: The ascending chain of ideals never stabilises, so $B$ is not Noetherian.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Consider the chain of ideals of $B$
> $$(u_1) \subseteq (u_1, u_2) \subseteq (u_1, u_2, u_3) \subseteq \cdots, \qquad \mathfrak{a}_n = (u_1, \dots, u_n).$$
> By Step 2, $u_{n+1} \in \mathfrak{a}_{n+1} \setminus \mathfrak{a}_n$, so each inclusion $\mathfrak{a}_n \subsetneq \mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$ is strict. This is a strictly ascending chain of ideals of $B$ that never stabilises, so $B$ fails the ascending chain condition: $B$ is **not Noetherian**.
>
> Equivalently, the ideal $\mathfrak{b} = (u_1, u_2, u_3, \dots) = \bigcup_n \mathfrak{a}_n$ is not finitely generated: any finite generating set would lie in some $\mathfrak{a}_n$ (each generator involves finitely many $u_j$, all below some index), but then $\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{a}_n$ would omit $u_{n+1}$ — contradiction.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $B = k[T_1 T_2, T_1 T_2^2, \dots] \subseteq k[T_1, T_2]$ is not Noetherian, even though $k[T_1, T_2]$ is.
>
> Set $u_j = T_1 T_2^j$. Grade by $T_1$-degree: $u_{j_1} \cdots u_{j_m} = T_1^m T_2^{j_1 + \cdots + j_m}$ has $T_1$-degree $m$, so the degree-$1$ part of $B$ is $\operatorname{span}_k\{u_j : j \geq 1\}$, with the $u_j$ a $k$-basis.
>
> Let $\mathfrak{a}_n = (u_1, \dots, u_n) \trianglelefteq B$. For $\sum_{i \leq n} b_i u_i \in \mathfrak{a}_n$, writing $b_i = \beta_i + b_i'$ ($\beta_i \in k$, $b_i'$ of positive degree), the degree-$1$ part is $\sum_{i \leq n} \beta_i u_i \in \operatorname{span}_k\{u_1, \dots, u_n\}$. Since $u_{n+1}$ is degree-$1$ and linearly independent of $u_1, \dots, u_n$, we get $u_{n+1} \notin \mathfrak{a}_n$.
>
> Thus $(u_1) \subsetneq (u_1, u_2) \subsetneq \cdots$ is a strictly ascending chain of ideals of $B$; ACC fails, so $B$ is not Noetherian. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Hilbert's basis theorem needs the algebra *finitely generated* — subalgebras of finitely generated algebras need not be, and so can fail to be Noetherian.** The cautionary lesson is that Noetherianity is *not* inherited by subalgebras (unlike by quotients, localisations, and finite extensions). The mechanism: Hilbert says finitely generated algebras over a Noetherian ring are Noetherian, but a subalgebra $B \subseteq A$ of a finitely generated $A$ can require infinitely many algebra generators, dodging the hypothesis. The reusable diagnostic is to check whether a subalgebra is finitely generated *as an algebra* before assuming it is Noetherian — and the way subalgebras fail to be finitely generated is exactly this graded pattern, where generators occupy one layer and products escape to higher layers, so no finite subset of generators reaches the rest. Recognising "is this subalgebra finitely generated?" as a genuine, often-negative question is the takeaway.

**Grading by a degree is the universal tool for proving an ideal is not finitely generated.** The entire proof runs on the $T_1$-grading: it lets you isolate the bottom layer (degree $1$), identify it with the $k$-span of the generators, and show that $B$-multiples cannot manufacture new degree-$1$ elements because coefficients of positive degree push products upward. The transferable technique: to show an ideal $\mathfrak{b}$ is not finitely generated, find a grading in which $\mathfrak{b}$ has generators spread across infinitely many degrees (or a single degree that products cannot reach), and show that finitely many generators would only span finitely many of them. This "degree confinement" argument is how one proves non-finite-generation throughout commutative algebra — the same move shows the ideal of constant-term-zero polynomials in $k[T_1, T_2, \dots]$ is not finitely generated, and it is the graded shadow of the ascending-chain refutation.

**The chain refutation and the non-finitely-generated-ideal refutation are the same fact, viewed through ACC versus finite generation.** This exercise can be phrased two ways — "the chain $(u_1) \subsetneq (u_1, u_2) \subsetneq \cdots$ never stabilises" (ACC fails) or "the ideal $(u_1, u_2, \dots)$ is not finitely generated" — and these are *literally* the equivalence [[Thm - Noetherian iff Every Submodule is Finitely Generated|ACC ⟺ every ideal finitely generated]] in action. The non-stabilising chain *is* the witness that the union ideal cannot be generated by any finite stage. Carrying both descriptions is useful: the chain form is cleaner to *state* (just exhibit strict inclusions), while the finite-generation form is cleaner to *connect* to Hilbert (the failure is precisely that $B$ is not finitely generated). When refuting Noetherianity, pick whichever description makes the strictness or the non-generation most visible — here the grading makes both transparent at once.
