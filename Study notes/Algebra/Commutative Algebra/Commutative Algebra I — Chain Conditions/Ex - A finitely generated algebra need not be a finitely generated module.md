# Problem Statement

Let $k$ be a field. Prove that the polynomial algebra $A = k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ (with $n \geq 1$) is:

- a **finitely generated $k$-algebra** (generated by $T_1, \dots, T_n$), but
- **not a finitely generated $k$-module**.

This is the canonical example separating the two notions of "finitely generated". Already the case $n = 1$, $A = k[T]$, exhibits the gap: $A = k[T]$ is generated as an algebra by the single element $T$, yet as a $k$-vector space it is infinite-dimensional, hence not finitely generated.

**Recall:**

![[Def - Finitely Generated Algebra#The Definition]]

![[Def - Finitely Generated Module#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated k-module]] is a finite-dimensional $k$-vector space: $M = k m_1 + \cdots + k m_r$ means every element is a $k$-*linear* combination of the $m_i$, so $\dim_k M \leq r < \infty$. A [[Def - Finitely Generated Algebra|finitely generated k-algebra]] allows *polynomial* combinations of the generators, in particular arbitrary products and powers. The whole exercise is the difference between "$k$-linear span of finitely many elements" (module) and "$k$-subalgebra generated by finitely many elements" (algebra).

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is an *algebra-versus-module separation* problem from [[Commutative Algebra I — Chain Conditions#Problem-Solving Strategy|the chapter's algebra/module-boundary family]]. The target is to show one finiteness notion holds while a strictly stronger-looking one fails, and the mechanism is always *unbounded degree*: algebra generation permits arbitrarily high powers of a generator, module generation caps the degree at the maximum degree among finitely many fixed elements.

**Assumption pattern.** The exploitable structure is that $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ is a *graded* ring — it splits by total degree, and the degree-$d$ part is non-zero for every $d$. The recognisable trigger is "a generator whose powers are linearly independent": the powers $1, T, T^2, \dots$ of a single variable are $k$-linearly independent, so no finite-dimensional subspace contains them all. This is exactly what makes the module fail to be finitely generated while the algebra succeeds.

**Theorem routing.** The route splits cleanly. For the algebra claim: $T_1, \dots, T_n$ generate by *definition* of the polynomial algebra — every polynomial is a polynomial in the $T_i$. For the module refutation: suppose for contradiction $A = k f_1 + \cdots + k f_r$ as a $k$-module; let $d = \max_i \deg f_i$; then every $k$-linear combination has degree $\leq d$, but $T_1^{d+1} \in A$ has degree $d + 1 > d$, a contradiction. The non-obvious recognition is that *module generation bounds degree* — this is the single fact that does all the work.

**Key decision point.** The one decision is *which invariant separates the two notions*, and the answer is **degree**. A $k$-linear combination of finitely many polynomials cannot exceed the largest degree present, so the module they generate is degree-bounded; the algebra they generate is not, because multiplication adds degrees. Picking the witness $T_1^{d+1}$ (or, equivalently, observing $\{1, T_1, T_1^2, \dots\}$ is infinite and linearly independent) is the entire content. The natural wrong turn is to try to count generators or argue abstractly; the degree bound makes it a one-line contradiction.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Commutative Algebra I — Chain Conditions#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Operation 6 (reduce an algebra to a polynomial-ring quotient), in reverse.** The polynomial algebra *is* the free object, so it is generated as an algebra by the variables $T_1, \dots, T_n$ — the algebra-generation claim is immediate from the universal property / definition.

2. **A degree-bounding argument (the separating device for the module/algebra boundary).** Bound the degree of every $k$-linear combination of a finite set by the maximum degree in the set, then exhibit a polynomial of higher degree to contradict finite module generation. This is the concrete form of "unbounded degree breaks module finiteness".

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> The two claims are easy for opposite reasons. The *algebra* claim is essentially a restatement of the definition of $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$. The *module* claim is a statement about $k$-linear combinations — what invariant of a polynomial is bounded when you take a $k$-linear combination of finitely many fixed polynomials?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Degree. A $k$-linear combination $\sum_i c_i f_i$ (with $c_i \in k$) has degree at most $\max_i \deg f_i$. So the $k$-span of $\{f_1, \dots, f_r\}$ lives inside the polynomials of degree $\leq \max_i \deg f_i$ — a *finite-dimensional* space.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Suppose $k[T_1, \dots, T_n] = k f_1 + \cdots + k f_r$ as a $k$-module. Let $d = \max_i \deg f_i$. Find a polynomial of degree $> d$ — for instance $T_1^{d+1}$ — and observe it cannot be a $k$-linear combination of the $f_i$. Contradiction.

# Solution

The proof is two independent verifications. The algebra claim is the definition of the polynomial ring. The module refutation is a degree-counting contradiction: a finite generating set as a module would bound all degrees, but polynomials of every degree exist. The only idea is that *$k$-linear combination preserves the degree bound while algebra multiplication does not*.

**Step 1: $A = k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra, generated by $T_1, \dots, T_n$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> By definition of the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]], every element of $A$ is a polynomial $p(T_1, \dots, T_n)$ with coefficients in $k$ — that is, a $k$-linear combination of monomials $T_1^{e_1} \cdots T_n^{e_n}$, each of which is a *product* of the generators $T_i$. So every element of $A$ is a polynomial expression in $T_1, \dots, T_n$ with coefficients in $k$, which is exactly the condition that $T_1, \dots, T_n$ generate $A$ as a [[Def - Finitely Generated Algebra|k-algebra]]. Equivalently, the identity map $k[T_1, \dots, T_n] \to A$ is the surjection from a polynomial algebra witnessing finite algebra generation. Hence $A$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra (with $n$ generators).

**Step 2: $A$ is not a finitely generated $k$-module.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Suppose, for contradiction, that $A$ is finitely generated as a $k$-module: $A = k f_1 + \cdots + k f_r$ for some $f_1, \dots, f_r \in A$. Set
> $$d = \max_{1 \leq i \leq r} \deg f_i < \infty.$$
> Every element of $k f_1 + \cdots + k f_r$ is a $k$-linear combination $\sum_i c_i f_i$ with $c_i \in k$, and
> $$\deg\Big(\sum_i c_i f_i\Big) \leq \max_i \deg(c_i f_i) \leq \max_i \deg f_i = d$$
> (scaling by $c_i \in k$ does not raise degree, and the degree of a sum is at most the maximum of the degrees). So every element of $A$ would have degree $\leq d$.
>
> But the monomial $T_1^{d+1} \in A$ has degree $d + 1 > d$, so it is *not* a $k$-linear combination of $f_1, \dots, f_r$. This contradicts $A = k f_1 + \cdots + k f_r$. Hence no finite set generates $A$ as a $k$-module: $A$ is not a finitely generated $k$-module.
>
> *(Equivalently: the powers $1, T_1, T_1^2, T_1^3, \dots$ are $k$-linearly independent — a nonzero polynomial relation $\sum c_j T_1^j = 0$ would force all $c_j = 0$ — so $\dim_k A = \infty$, and an infinite-dimensional $k$-vector space is never a finitely generated $k$-module.)*

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $A = k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ ($n \geq 1$) is a finitely generated $k$-algebra but not a finitely generated $k$-module.
>
> *Algebra.* Every element of $A$ is by definition a polynomial in $T_1, \dots, T_n$ with coefficients in $k$, so $T_1, \dots, T_n$ generate $A$ as a $k$-algebra.
>
> *Module.* Suppose $A = k f_1 + \cdots + k f_r$ and put $d = \max_i \deg f_i$. Any $k$-linear combination of the $f_i$ has degree $\leq d$, yet $T_1^{d+1} \in A$ has degree $d+1$; contradiction. So $A$ is not finitely generated as a $k$-module. (Indeed $\dim_k A = \infty$, the monomials being a $k$-basis.) $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**The dividing line between "finitely generated module" and "finitely generated algebra" is *bounded degree*, and the universal witness is a single variable's powers.** A module over $k$ is the $k$-linear span of its generators, and linear combinations never raise degree, so a finitely generated module is degree-bounded, hence finite-dimensional. An algebra is the subring generated by its generators, and multiplication *adds* degrees, so algebra generation reaches every degree. The reusable diagnostic: whenever you must decide whether an object is finitely generated as a module or only as an algebra, look for an element whose powers are linearly independent — if the powers of some generator escape every finite-dimensional subspace, the object is at most a finitely generated algebra, never a finitely generated module. This single test settles $k[T]$, $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$, and the Laurent polynomials $k[T, T^{-1}]$.

**The two notions coincide exactly when the generators are integral, because a monic relation caps the degree.** The gap closes precisely when each generator $x_i$ satisfies a monic polynomial $x_i^{d} + r_{d-1}x_i^{d-1} + \cdots + r_0 = 0$ over the base: such a relation rewrites $x_i^d$, and hence all higher powers, in terms of $1, x_i, \dots, x_i^{d-1}$, so degree no longer escapes and polynomial combinations collapse to linear ones. This is why $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[i]$ is finite as an $\mathbb{R}$-module ($i^2 = -1$ is monic) while $k[T]$ is not ($T$ satisfies no monic relation over $k$, being transcendental). The trigger for "finitely generated algebra $\Rightarrow$ finitely generated module" is *integrality of the generators*, the subject of [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]]; recognising whether your generators satisfy monic relations tells you instantly whether the two finiteness notions agree.

**Grading makes degree arguments rigorous and is the right framework for "size escapes upward".** The clean reason the module fails is that $k[T_1, \dots, T_n] = \bigoplus_{d \geq 0} A_d$ is a graded ring with each homogeneous piece $A_d \neq 0$, and a $k$-linear combination of finitely many elements lives in $\bigoplus_{d \leq D} A_d$ for some finite $D$. Any time a structure is graded with infinitely many non-zero pieces, it cannot be finitely generated by elements of bounded degree as a module over the degree-zero part. This perspective recurs throughout commutative algebra — Hilbert functions, the Rees algebra, associated graded rings — where "finitely generated in degree one" versus "finitely generated as an algebra" is exactly the present distinction in graded clothing, developed in [[Commutative Algebra XI — Graded Rings and the Artin-Rees Lemma]].
