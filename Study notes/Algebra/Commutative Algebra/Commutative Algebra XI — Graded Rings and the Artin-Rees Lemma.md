# Notation Registry

**Standing convention.** Throughout these commutative algebra notes, *ring* means a commutative ring with multiplicative identity $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$; *module* means a unital module. This chapter studies two passages between **filtered** and **graded** objects. A *filtration* is a descending tower of submodules measuring "depth"; a *grading* is a direct-sum decomposition by degree with multiplication adding degrees. The two constructions that convert one into the other — the **associated graded ring** $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = \bigoplus \mathfrak{a}^n/\mathfrak{a}^{n+1}$ and the **Rees algebra** $R^* = \bigoplus \mathfrak{a}^n$ — are the engines of the chapter; the slogan, stated again in Motivation, is **the Rees algebra proves finiteness, the associated graded ring extracts geometry**.

- $R, A, B$ — commutative rings with $1$
- $\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b} \trianglelefteq R$ — [[Def - Ideal|ideals]]; $\mathfrak{m}, \mathfrak{n}$ — [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|maximal ideals]]; $(R, \mathfrak{m})$ — a local ring
- $\mathfrak{a}^n$ — the $n$-th power of $\mathfrak{a}$ ($\mathfrak{a}^0 = R$); $\mathfrak{a}^n M = \{\sum a_i m_i : a_i \in \mathfrak{a}^n, m_i \in M\}$
- $A = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} A_n$ — a **[[Def - Graded Ring and Graded Module|graded ring]]**; $A_n$ the degree-$n$ component; $A_0$ the degree-zero subring
- $A_+ = \bigoplus_{n \geq 1} A_n$ — the **irrelevant ideal** (kernel of $A \to A_0$)
- $M = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} M_n$ — a **graded $A$-module**; $\deg(x) = n$ for $x \in M_n$ homogeneous
- $(M_n)_{n \geq 0}$ — a **[[Def - Filtration and Stable Filtration|filtration]]** of $M$: $M = M_0 \supseteq M_1 \supseteq \cdots$
- **$\mathfrak{a}$-filtration**: $\mathfrak{a}M_n \subseteq M_{n+1}$; **stable**: $\mathfrak{a}M_n = M_{n+1}$ for all large $n$
- $(\mathfrak{a}^n M)$ — the **$\mathfrak{a}$-adic filtration** (the canonical stable $\mathfrak{a}$-filtration)
- $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = \bigoplus_{n} \mathfrak{a}^n/\mathfrak{a}^{n+1}$ — the **[[Def - The Associated Graded Ring and the Rees Algebra|associated graded ring]]** (Becker: $G_{\mathfrak{a}}(R)$)
- $\operatorname{gr}(M) = \bigoplus_n M_n/M_{n+1}$ — the **associated graded module**; $\operatorname{gr}_n(M) = M_n/M_{n+1}$
- $R^* = \bigoplus_{n} \mathfrak{a}^n$ — the **[[Def - The Associated Graded Ring and the Rees Algebra|Rees algebra]]**; $M^* = \bigoplus_n M_n$ — the **Rees module**
- $\hat{M} = \varprojlim_n M/\mathfrak{a}^n M$ — the **$\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion**; $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$ — the stable (infinitely-divisible) submodule
- $\operatorname{Jac}(R)$ — the [[Def - The Jacobson Radical|Jacobson radical]] (intersection of maximal ideals; $= \mathfrak{m}$ if local)
- $P(t) = \sum_n (\dim_k M_n) t^n$ — the Poincaré (Hilbert) series of a graded $k$-module

# Motivation

Here is the whole chapter in one sentence: **a filtration is an analytic notion of "smallness" on a module, and the way to do algebra with it is to turn it into a graded ring — either by stacking the levels (the Rees algebra) or by collapsing to the layers between them (the associated graded ring).** The motivating filtration is the most familiar one in mathematics. Near a prime $p$, the integers come sorted into $\mathbb{Z} \supseteq p\mathbb{Z} \supseteq p^2\mathbb{Z} \supseteq \cdots$, each integer assigned a depth by how many times $p$ divides it. Near a point of a variety, functions come sorted by their order of vanishing: $R \supseteq \mathfrak{m} \supseteq \mathfrak{m}^2 \supseteq \cdots$. These descending towers of submodules are *filtrations*, and the questions one wants to ask of them — does the tower shrink to zero? does it behave well under taking submodules? what is the "leading term" of an element? — are not directly algebraic. The chapter's program is to make them algebraic by passing to a graded ring.

There are exactly two honest ways to do the conversion, and they organise everything. The **Rees algebra** $R^* = \bigoplus_n \mathfrak{a}^n$ stacks the filtration levels into one graded ring, with $\mathfrak{a}$ sitting in degree one. Its purpose is *finiteness*: when $R$ is [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian]], $R^*$ is generated in degree one by generators of $\mathfrak{a}$, hence Noetherian, and a filtration is *stable* exactly when its Rees module is *finitely generated*. The Rees algebra converts the analytic word "stable" into the algebraic word "finitely generated", where Noetherian machinery applies. The **associated graded ring** $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = \bigoplus_n \mathfrak{a}^n/\mathfrak{a}^{n+1}$ instead collapses to the layers between successive levels. Its purpose is *geometry*: over $R/\mathfrak{a}$ (a field, when $\mathfrak{a}$ is maximal) it is the coordinate ring of the **tangent cone**, the cone of limiting directions of the variety at the point, and its Hilbert function feeds dimension theory. The structural relationship between the two is one equation, $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = R^*/\mathfrak{a}R^*$.

$$\underbrace{\text{filtration } (M_n)}_{\text{analytic}} \;\xrightarrow{\ \text{Rees}\ }\; \underbrace{R^* = \textstyle\bigoplus \mathfrak{a}^n,\quad M^* = \textstyle\bigoplus M_n}_{\text{finiteness: stable } \iff \text{ finitely generated}} \;\xrightarrow{\ /\mathfrak{a}R^*\ }\; \underbrace{\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = \textstyle\bigoplus \mathfrak{a}^n/\mathfrak{a}^{n+1}}_{\text{geometry: the tangent cone}}$$

The chapter's central theorem is the **Artin–Rees Lemma**: over a Noetherian ring, a stable filtration cuts a stable filtration on every submodule. This is precisely the statement that the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic filtration "behaves well" under taking submodules, and it is proved by the Rees-algebra translation — package the filtration into a module over the Noetherian $R^*$, and the conclusion is just "submodules of Noetherian modules are finitely generated". From Artin–Rees flow the two facts that make the entire $\mathfrak{a}$-adic / completion theory work. The **Krull Intersection Theorem** — that $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{m}^n M = 0$ in a Noetherian local ring, so no nonzero element is infinitely divisible by $\mathfrak{m}$ — makes the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic topology separated and the completion injective. And the theorem that **all stable filtrations induce the same topology** makes "the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic topology" and "the completion $\hat M$" well-defined phrases, and (with Artin–Rees) makes the subspace topology on a submodule equal its intrinsic one.

This chapter assumes you are fluent with [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] and their powers, [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian rings]] and [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]] from the [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8|Rings]] notes, [[Def - Module|modules]], [[Def - Submodule|submodules]], [[Def - Quotient Module|quotients]], and [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finite generation]] from the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|Modules]] notes, and [[Thm - Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama's lemma]] from [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma]]. The $\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion of the previous chapter, [[Commutative Algebra X — Completions and Limits|completions and limits]], is the consumer of this chapter's results; dimension theory consumes the associated graded ring and its Hilbert function — its [[Def - The Hilbert Function and Hilbert Polynomial|Hilbert function]] and [[Def - Krull Dimension and Height|Krull dimension]] pages build directly on this chapter. The polynomial ring as a graded ring is the running example, so [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial rings]] should be at hand.

# Concept Map

## §12.1 Graded Rings and Filtrations

- **[[Def - Graded Ring and Graded Module]]**
	- A graded ring is a ring $A = \bigoplus_{n \geq 0} A_n$ (internal direct sum of additive subgroups) with $A_m A_n \subseteq A_{m+n}$ — "degrees add". Then $A_0$ is automatically a subring containing $1$ (a one-line degree-matching argument), each $A_n$ is an $A_0$-module, and the irrelevant ideal $A_+ = \bigoplus_{n \geq 1}A_n$ is the kernel of $A \to A_0$. A graded module $M = \bigoplus M_n$ satisfies $A_m M_n \subseteq M_{m+n}$. The prototype is $k[T_1, \dots, T_r]$ graded by total degree; the operational content is "compare homogeneous components degree by degree".

- **[[Def - Filtration and Stable Filtration]]**
	- A filtration is a descending tower $M = M_0 \supseteq M_1 \supseteq \cdots$ of submodules. It is an $\mathfrak{a}$-filtration if $\mathfrak{a}M_n \subseteq M_{n+1}$ (multiplying by $\mathfrak{a}$ pushes down a level), and stable if $\mathfrak{a}M_n = M_{n+1}$ for all large $n$ (eventually $\mathfrak{a}$-driven). The canonical example is the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic filtration $(\mathfrak{a}^n M)$, stable with index $0$. Any $\mathfrak{a}$-filtration satisfies $\mathfrak{a}^n M \subseteq M_n$ (the adic one is finest); stability is the bounded reverse domination. Non-stable $\mathfrak{a}$-filtrations (e.g. $(x^{2^n})$ in $k[x]$) plunge faster and give strictly finer topologies.

- **[[Def - The Associated Graded Ring and the Rees Algebra]]**
	- Two constructions turning a filtration into a graded object. The associated graded ring $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = \bigoplus \mathfrak{a}^n/\mathfrak{a}^{n+1}$ keeps the *layers* (quotients of successive levels), with multiplication $\bar x \cdot \bar y = \overline{xy}$ well-defined because $\mathfrak{a}^{m+1}\mathfrak{a}^n \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^{m+n+1}$; it is an algebra over $R/\mathfrak{a}$ and is the coordinate ring of the tangent cone. The Rees algebra $R^* = \bigoplus \mathfrak{a}^n$ keeps the *levels*, generated in degree one over $R$ by generators of $\mathfrak{a}$; it is the coordinate algebra of the blowup. They are linked by $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = R^*/\mathfrak{a}R^*$.

- **[[Thm - A Graded Ring is Noetherian iff Finitely Generated in Degree One]]**
	- A graded ring $A$ is Noetherian $\iff$ $A_0$ is Noetherian and $A$ is finitely generated as an $A_0$-algebra (by homogeneous elements of positive degree). The hard direction is a degree induction: homogeneous generators of the irrelevant ideal $A_+$ generate all of $A$ over $A_0$, because projecting $y = \sum r_i x_i$ onto its own degree replaces the coefficients $r_i$ by lower-degree ones. The corollary: a graded $k$-algebra generated in degree one is a quotient of $k[T_0,\dots,T_r]$, so its Proj embeds in $\mathbb{P}^r$ — generation in degree one *is* a projective embedding.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Projective varieties and the Proj construction *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> A graded ring finitely generated in degree one over a field $A_0 = k$ is the **homogeneous coordinate ring** of a **projective variety** $\operatorname{Proj} A \hookrightarrow \mathbb{P}^r$, with the degree-one piece $A_1$ supplying the homogeneous coordinates. The model is $A = k[T_0, \dots, T_r]$, $\operatorname{Proj} A = \mathbb{P}^r$. The grading implements "scale all coordinates together and get the same point" — the algebra of the $\mathbb{G}_m$-action. The Noetherian criterion is what makes $\operatorname{Proj} A$ quasi-compact and of finite type, hence a genuine variety.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The tangent cone and weighted projective space *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The associated graded ring $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ is the coordinate ring of the **tangent cone** of $\operatorname{Spec} R$ at the point $\mathfrak{m}$, with $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ the **cotangent space**. A smooth point has tangent cone equal to its tangent space (a linear $k[t_1,\dots,t_d]$); a node has two crossing lines, a cusp a doubled line — $\operatorname{gr}$ detects and classifies singularity. A *nonstandard* (weighted) grading gives the coordinate ring of a **weighted projective space**, whose Hilbert function is only quasi-polynomial.

## §12.2 Artin-Rees and Krull Intersection

- **[[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma]]**
	- Over a Noetherian ring $R$, with $M$ finitely generated, a stable $\mathfrak{a}$-filtration $(M_n)$, and $N \subseteq M$ a submodule, the induced filtration $(N \cap M_n)$ is a stable $\mathfrak{a}$-filtration of $N$. Classical form: $\exists c$ with $\mathfrak{a}^n M \cap N = \mathfrak{a}^{n-c}(\mathfrak{a}^c M \cap N)$ for $n \geq c$, so $\mathfrak{a}^n M \cap N \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^{n-c}N$. The proof packages the filtration into the Rees module $M^*$ over the Noetherian Rees algebra $R^*$, where stability $\iff$ finite generation, and "submodules of Noetherian modules are finitely generated" gives the uniform bound $c$ for free.

- **[[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem]]**
	- For Noetherian $R$, ideal $\mathfrak{a}$, finitely generated $M$, the submodule $N = \bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$ satisfies $\mathfrak{a}N = N$, so $(1+a)N = 0$ for some $a \in \mathfrak{a}$. Hence if $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(R)$ — in particular if $(R,\mathfrak{m})$ is local — then $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M = 0$; and in a Noetherian domain $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n = 0$ for proper $\mathfrak{a}$. The mechanism is the two-stroke "Artin–Rees gives $N = \mathfrak{a}N$, Nakayama gives $N = 0$". This is the separatedness of the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic topology: no nonzero element is infinitely divisible, so completion is injective.

- **[[Thm - Stable Filtrations Induce the Same Topology]]**
	- Every stable $\mathfrak{a}$-filtration of $M$ is equivalent (bounded within a finite shift) to the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic filtration, so any two stable $\mathfrak{a}$-filtrations induce the same topology. One inclusion $\mathfrak{a}^n M \subseteq M_n$ is free; the other $M_{n+n_0} = \mathfrak{a}^n M_{n_0} \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^n M$ is exactly where stability is spent. With Artin–Rees (making the induced filtration on a submodule stable), this gives the corollary that the subspace topology on $N$ equals its intrinsic $\mathfrak{a}$-adic topology — the input to exactness of completion.

- **[[Ex - The Artin-Rees lemma and the subspace topology]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic subspace topology on a submodule equals its intrinsic $\mathfrak{a}$-adic topology, by Artin–Rees (induced filtration is stable) plus the comparison theorem. Example Sheet 4 Q14.

- **[[Ex - Krull intersection for a Noetherian local ring]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{m}^n M = 0$ in a Noetherian local ring via Artin–Rees $+$ Nakayama, deduce the completion map is injective, and show by an idempotent counterexample that the local hypothesis is essential.

- **[[Ex - Stable filtrations of a finitely generated module]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Prove "$(M_n)$ stable $\iff$ Rees module finitely generated" via the freeze-then-continue-adically chain, then derive Artin–Rees and the equivalence of stable filtrations as corollaries — the structural heart of the chapter.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Separatedness, completion, and the blowup *(from Commutative Algebra X and Algebraic Geometry)*
> Krull intersection makes the **$\mathfrak{a}$-adic completion** $\hat M = \varprojlim M/\mathfrak{a}^n M$ injective (separated) on finitely generated modules over Noetherian local rings — the kernel $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$ vanishes, so $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_p$, $k[x]_{(x)} \hookrightarrow k[[x]]$. Artin–Rees additionally makes completion **exact** on f.g. modules. Geometrically, $\operatorname{Proj}$ of the Rees algebra $R^* = \bigoplus \mathfrak{a}^n$ is the **blowup** of $\operatorname{Spec} R$ along $V(\mathfrak{a})$, and $\operatorname{Proj}\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ is its **exceptional divisor** — the projectivized tangent cone. The chapter is the algebra under both completion and blowing-up.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring goals of this chapter cluster into five types. The first is **identifying an associated graded ring or Rees algebra concretely** — recognising $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ as a known graded ring (a polynomial ring for a regular point, a quotient by the initial ideal for a singular one) or $R^*$ as a blow-up algebra. The standard route is to use a pre-existing grading or to compute the initial forms of the defining equations. The second is **certifying a graded ring is Noetherian** — almost always by exhibiting it as finitely generated in degree one over a Noetherian base and invoking [[Thm - A Graded Ring is Noetherian iff Finitely Generated in Degree One|the graded criterion]] / [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]]; this is how the Rees algebra and associated graded ring are tamed. The third, and the chapter's signature, is **proving a filtration is stable** — reducing to "the Rees module is finitely generated" over the Noetherian Rees algebra, where Noetherian-ness supplies the stabilization bound; [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]] is the headline instance. The fourth is **proving a stable submodule vanishes** — showing $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M = 0$ by the Artin–Rees / Nakayama two-stroke ([[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull intersection]]), the lever being "infinitely divisible $\Rightarrow$ fixed by $\mathfrak{a}$ $\Rightarrow$ zero". The fifth is **comparing two adic topologies** — showing they coincide by proving the defining filtrations equivalent (never equal), using [[Thm - Stable Filtrations Induce the Same Topology|that stable filtrations induce the same topology]]. These are the natural targets because the chapter is fundamentally a *translation* tool: every theorem converts a filtration question into a graded-module finiteness question.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **The ring is Noetherian** — the single most-used assumption, because it makes the Rees algebra Noetherian and hence makes finite generation pass to submodules; almost every theorem here fails without it. **A module is finitely generated** — so that its submodules are finitely generated and Nakayama applies; the input type of Artin–Rees. **A filtration is stable** (or you must prove it is) — routing through the Rees-module finiteness dictionary. **A grading is present, or generation is in degree one** — routing through the graded Noetherian criterion and, geometrically, through a projective embedding or tangent cone. **The ring is local, or $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(R)$** — the Nakayama hypothesis, what turns Artin–Rees's $\mathfrak{a}N = N$ into $N = 0$; recognising it is what lets Krull intersection fire. The discipline is to match source to target along the chapter's spine: a "prove stable" target routes through the Rees module; a "prove an intersection vanishes" target routes through Artin–Rees to Nakayama; a "topologies coincide" target routes through filtration equivalence; a "compute the tangent cone" target routes through initial forms of the associated graded ring. The [[Commutative Algebra XI — Graded Rings and the Artin-Rees Lemma#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every graded-ring / filtration problem is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

1. **Compare homogeneous components degree by degree.** An equation between graded elements is infinitely many equations, one per degree: project $a = \sum_n a_n$ onto a fixed degree $d$, using $(ab)_d = \sum_{m+n=d} a_m b_n$. This is the graded analogue of "compare coefficients" and is the single most-used move with graded rings. *Trigger:* an equation in a graded ring or module. *Pattern:* "project onto degree $d$ and read off a relation among the degree-$d$ parts".

2. **Verify a grading by checking degrees/weights add, and the decomposition is direct.** To confirm $A = \bigoplus A_n$ is graded, check the monomial (or other) basis splits uniquely by degree (directness) and that the degree function is additive under multiplication ($A_m A_n \subseteq A_{m+n}$). *Trigger:* "show this is a graded ring". *Pattern:* additivity of $\deg$ on generators plus uniqueness of homogeneous decomposition.

3. **Generate a graded ring from its low-degree pieces.** A graded ring is finitely generated over $A_0$ by homogeneous elements of positive degree; for the Rees algebra and associated graded ring these live in *degree one* (generators of $\mathfrak{a}$). Then $A$ is a quotient of a polynomial ring and Hilbert's basis theorem applies. *Trigger:* "is this graded ring Noetherian / finitely generated?" *Pattern:* "the irrelevant ideal's homogeneous generators generate the whole algebra; for blow-up algebras they sit in degree one".

4. **Translate a filtration into its Rees module and read stability off finite generation.** Pass from $(M_n)$ to $M^* = \bigoplus M_n$ over $R^* = \bigoplus \mathfrak{a}^n$; then "$(M_n)$ stable $\iff M^*$ finitely generated over $R^*$". Over a Noetherian $R^*$, finite generation passes to submodules, which is exactly [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]]. *Trigger:* "prove a filtration is stable" or "control the filtration on a submodule". *Pattern:* "$M^*$ finitely generated $\Rightarrow$ Noetherian $\Rightarrow$ its submodule $N^*$ finitely generated $\Rightarrow$ induced filtration stable".

5. **Compute $\operatorname{gr}$-multiplication via leading (initial) forms.** The product $\bar x \cdot \bar y$ in $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R)$ is the leading form of $xy$. To find $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ for $R = k[\underline{x}]/I$, take the *initial ideal* $\operatorname{in}(I)$ of lowest-degree forms: $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R) = k[\underline x]/\operatorname{in}(I)$. *Trigger:* "compute the tangent cone / associated graded ring". *Pattern:* "replace each defining equation by its lowest-degree part".

6. **Diagnose Hilbert–Serre / tangent-cone behaviour by the degree-one hypothesis.** A Hilbert polynomial exists iff the graded ring is generated in degree one; weighted gradings give only quasi-polynomials. A smooth point gives $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ a polynomial ring; singularity is the failure thereof. *Trigger:* "why does/doesn't a Hilbert polynomial exist" or "is this point smooth?" *Pattern:* "check generation in degree one (Hilbert–Serre) or whether $\operatorname{gr}$ is a polynomial ring (smoothness)".

7. **Count a graded piece combinatorially and package as a Poincaré series.** $\dim_k A_n$ is a lattice-point count; the Poincaré series $\sum_n (\dim_k A_n) t^n$ factors over the generators as $\prod_i \frac{1}{1 - t^{w_i}}$ (with relations contributing numerator factors). *Trigger:* "compute the Hilbert function / series". *Pattern:* "count monomials of degree $n$, then recognise the generating function".

8. **Collapse an infinite intersection to a fixed-point equation, then apply Nakayama.** For $N = \bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$, note $N \cap \mathfrak{a}^n M = N$, feed Artin–Rees to get $N = \mathfrak{a}N$, then Nakayama (needing $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(R)$) gives $N = 0$. *Trigger:* "show a deep / stable submodule is zero". *Pattern:* "Artin–Rees gives $N = \mathfrak{a}N$, Nakayama gives $N = 0$".

9. **Replace "topologies coincide" by "filtrations equivalent".** Two filtration topologies agree iff the filtrations are equivalent (each bounded within a finite shift of the other), which is cofinality of neighbourhood bases. Recognise a subspace topology as the induced filtration $(N \cap \mathfrak{a}^n M)$. *Trigger:* "two adic topologies coincide". *Pattern:* "find a shift $c$ with $M_{n+c} \subseteq M_n'$ and conversely; never try to prove equality of filtrations".

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming $N \cap \mathfrak{a}^n M = \mathfrak{a}^n N$ for a submodule
> Because $\mathfrak{a}^n N \subseteq N \cap \mathfrak{a}^n M$ always holds, it is tempting to assume equality — it would make the subspace topology equal the intrinsic adic topology *on the nose*. This is **false**. The two differ because $N \cap \mathfrak{a}^n M$ collects elements of $N$ that are $n$-fold $\mathfrak{a}$-divisible *using witnesses in $M$ outside $N$*, while $\mathfrak{a}^n N$ requires witnesses *in $N$*. Concretely, even in nice rings the inclusion is strict for small $n$. The correct statement is the bounded comparison $N \cap \mathfrak{a}^{n+c}M \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^n N$, which holds for one fixed shift $c$ — and the only tool that produces $c$ is [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]]. The operation becomes legal precisely when you replace "equal" by "equivalent up to the Artin–Rees shift $c$".

> [!warning] 2. Believing $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n = 0$ unconditionally
> Krull's theorem is so clean for local rings that one is tempted to assert $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n = 0$ for any ideal in any Noetherian ring. It is **false** without $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(R)$. Take $R = k \times k$ and $\mathfrak{a} = (e)$ with $e = (1,0)$ idempotent: then $\mathfrak{a}^n = \mathfrak{a}$ for all $n$, so $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n = \mathfrak{a} = k \times 0 \neq 0$. What fails is the final Nakayama step: Artin–Rees still gives $\mathfrak{a}N = N$, but $1 + a$ (here $1 - e = (0,1)$) need not be a unit, so $N = 0$ does not follow. The operation becomes legal exactly when $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(R)$ (e.g. $R$ local, or $\mathfrak{a}$ proper in a domain) — the hypothesis that makes $1 + a$ invertible.

> [!warning] 3. Treating $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R)$ as the quotient $R/\mathfrak{a}$, or carrying the full equations into the tangent cone
> Two related traps. First, since the degree-zero piece of $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R)$ is $R/\mathfrak{a}$, one is tempted to identify the whole associated graded ring with $R/\mathfrak{a}$ — but that is only the *bottom layer*; for $R = k[x]$, $\mathfrak{a} = (x)$, $R/\mathfrak{a} = k$ while $\operatorname{gr}_{(x)}(R) = k[x]$ is infinite-dimensional. Second, when computing $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ for $R = k[\underline x]/I$, it is tempting to use the full generators of $I$; but the tangent cone is cut out by the **initial ideal** $\operatorname{in}(I)$ of lowest-degree forms, *not* $I$. For the cusp $y^2 - x^3$, the tangent cone is $y^2 = 0$ (a doubled line), the $-x^3$ discarded. The operation "use $I$" becomes legal only after replacing each equation by its initial form — and even then, the initial forms of a generating set may fail to generate $\operatorname{in}(I)$ (the Gröbner/standard-basis subtlety).

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any problem of this chapter is to name which *passage* you are using — filtration-to-graded, and which graded construction — because the two constructions serve opposite purposes and each has a characteristic route.

If the problem **asks you to prove a finiteness statement — a graded ring is Noetherian, a filtration is stable, a submodule's filtration is controlled** — you are using the Rees algebra, and the strategy is a fixed translation. Pass to the Rees algebra $R^* = \bigoplus \mathfrak{a}^n$, which is Noetherian whenever $R$ is (it is generated in degree one by generators of $\mathfrak{a}$, so [[Thm - A Graded Ring is Noetherian iff Finitely Generated in Degree One|the graded criterion]] / [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]] applies — this is [[Ex - The Rees algebra is Noetherian]]). Then use the dictionary "$(M_n)$ stable $\iff$ Rees module $M^*$ finitely generated over $R^*$". For stability of an induced filtration on a submodule $N$, the chain is: $M^*$ finitely generated (from the given stable filtration) $\Rightarrow$ Noetherian over $R^*$ $\Rightarrow$ its submodule $N^* = \bigoplus(N \cap M_n)$ finitely generated $\Rightarrow$ $(N \cap M_n)$ stable. That *is* [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]], and the uniform stabilization bound $c$ comes for free because a finitely generated graded module is generated in finitely many degrees. The recurring trap is to hunt for $c$ by hand; the Rees algebra exists precisely to manufacture it.

If the problem **asks you to show a stable / deep submodule vanishes** — typically $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M = 0$ — you are running the [[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull intersection]] two-stroke. Set $N = \bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$ and notice $N \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^n M$ for all $n$, so the induced filtration on $N$ is constant: $N \cap \mathfrak{a}^n M = N$. Apply Artin–Rees: the induced filtration is stable, so $N \cap \mathfrak{a}^{n+1}M = \mathfrak{a}(N \cap \mathfrak{a}^n M)$ for large $n$, which collapses to the single equation $N = \mathfrak{a}N$. Then apply [[Thm - Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama]] — legal exactly when $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(R)$ (e.g. $R$ local) — to force $N = 0$. The whole of [[Ex - Krull intersection for a Noetherian local ring]] is this, and the discipline is to check the Jacobson-radical hypothesis, because the theorem is false without it.

If the problem **asks you to compute a tangent cone or associated graded ring** — to identify $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ — you are using the associated graded construction, and the route is through *initial forms*. For $R = k[\underline x]/I$ localized at the origin, $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R) = k[\underline x]/\operatorname{in}(I)$, where $\operatorname{in}(I)$ is generated by the lowest-degree parts of elements of $I$. When the ring is already graded (a polynomial ring), the $\mathfrak{m}$-adic filtration *splits* and each layer $\mathfrak{m}^n/\mathfrak{m}^{n+1}$ lifts canonically to the degree-$n$ forms, making $\operatorname{gr}$ trivial to read off ([[Ex - The associated graded ring of a polynomial ring]]). The diagnostic payoff is geometric: $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ is a polynomial ring iff the point is smooth, and its reducibility/nonreducedness classifies the singularity (node = two lines, cusp = doubled line).

A fourth family **concerns Hilbert functions and topologies**. For Hilbert functions, count $\dim_k(\operatorname{gr}_n)$ combinatorially and package as a Poincaré series $\prod_i \frac{1}{1 - t^{w_i}}$; a genuine Hilbert *polynomial* exists iff the ring is generated in degree one, and weighted gradings give only quasi-polynomials ([[Ex - A nonstandard grading and its Hilbert function]]). For topologies, *never* chase open sets: show the two filtrations are equivalent (bounded mutual shift) and invoke [[Thm - Stable Filtrations Induce the Same Topology|that stable filtrations induce the same topology]]; the subspace-equals-adic theorem ([[Ex - The Artin-Rees lemma and the subspace topology]]) is this plus Artin–Rees.

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "what does this filtration become when I make it graded, and is the result finite?"** Stack the levels and ask if the Rees module is finitely generated (stability, Artin–Rees); collapse to the layers and ask what graded ring you get (tangent cone, Hilbert function). Decide which graded object the question wants, and the right tool — Rees algebra for finiteness, associated graded for geometry — is determined. The two are linked by $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = R^*/\mathfrak{a}R^*$, so when one is hard, quotient or lift to the other.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|The Artin–Rees Lemma]]**: over a Noetherian ring, a stable filtration induces a stable filtration on every submodule. This is the most reused fact because it is the only general tool that *controls how the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic filtration interacts with submodules*, and that control is the technical input to Krull intersection, separatedness and exactness of completion, and the comparison of subspace and adic topologies. Its standard use is the substitution into a Nakayama argument: whenever you have a submodule that is "too deep" and want to conclude it is fixed by $\mathfrak{a}$, Artin–Rees provides the one equation $N = \mathfrak{a}N$. Reach for it whenever a submodule, a quotient, or a completion of a submodule must be compared with the ambient module's filtration.

- **The Rees-algebra translation "$(M_n)$ stable $\iff M^*$ finitely generated"**: the dictionary between filtered $R$-modules and graded $R^*$-modules. Its reusability is that it converts *every* stability question into a finite-generation question over the Noetherian Rees algebra, where the standard Noetherian facts (submodules finitely generated, chains stabilize) apply. This is the engine under Artin–Rees itself, and the cleanest way to see why a uniform stabilization bound exists. Reach for it whenever "stable" appears and you want to deploy Noetherian-ness.

- **[[Thm - A Graded Ring is Noetherian iff Finitely Generated in Degree One|The graded Noetherian criterion]]**: a graded ring is Noetherian iff it is finitely generated in (positive, often degree-one) degrees over a Noetherian base. Its reusability is that it certifies, in one stroke, the Noetherian-ness of every graded ring in the chapter — the Rees algebra, the associated graded ring, the homogeneous coordinate ring of a projective variety. The degree-one case is doubly useful: it *is* the existence of a projective embedding. Use it whenever a graded ring's Noetherian-ness or a projective variety's finite-type-ness is at stake.

- **[[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|The Krull intersection theorem]]**: $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{m}^n M = 0$ in a Noetherian local ring. Its reusability is as the *separatedness* statement that makes the $\mathfrak{a}$-adic topology Hausdorff and the completion injective — the licence for the entire "complete, solve, descend" technique. The base equation it provides, $\mathfrak{a}N = N \Rightarrow N = 0$ under $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(R)$, is reused directly to prove modules vanish. Reach for it whenever you must know that no information is lost in completing, or that an element has finite order of vanishing.

- **The identity $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = R^*/\mathfrak{a}R^*$, and "$\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ is the tangent cone"**: the structural bridge between the two constructions and the geometric meaning of one of them. Its reusability is twofold: algebraically, Noetherian-ness and finiteness pass from $R^*$ to $\operatorname{gr}$ via this quotient; geometrically, $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ computes the tangent cone, detecting smoothness ($\operatorname{gr}$ a polynomial ring) and classifying singularities. Use it whenever you need to transfer a finiteness fact from the Rees algebra to the associated graded, or to read geometry off the layers $\mathfrak{m}^n/\mathfrak{m}^{n+1}$.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — the associated graded ring is the tangent cone, the Rees algebra is the blowup.** To a local ring $(R, \mathfrak{m})$ — the algebra of a point on a variety $\operatorname{Spec} R$ — the associated graded ring $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R) = \bigoplus_n \mathfrak{m}^n/\mathfrak{m}^{n+1}$ attaches the **tangent cone**: the cone of limiting secant directions through the point, the best conical approximation to the variety there. Its degree-one piece $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ is the **cotangent space**, so the linear part of the cone is the tangent space; the higher layers record curvature. A smooth point has $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R) \cong k[t_1, \dots, t_d]$ — the cone is a linear space — while a node has the union of its two branch directions $(y-x)(y+x)$ and a cusp a doubled line $y^2$. The Rees algebra $R^* = \bigoplus_n \mathfrak{a}^n$ attaches instead the **blowup** $\operatorname{Proj} R^*$ of $\operatorname{Spec} R$ along $V(\mathfrak{a})$ — the universal way to make $\mathfrak{a}$ locally principal, replacing the subvariety by the projectivization of its normal directions; and $\operatorname{Proj} \operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ is the **exceptional divisor**, the $\mathbb{P}$ of tangent directions glued in to replace the point. These are the basic surgeries of birational geometry and the engine of Hironaka's resolution of singularities. The chapter is the algebra underneath all of it: the identity $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = R^*/\mathfrak{a}R^*$ is "the exceptional divisor is the fibre of the blowup over the centre".

2. **Completions and $p$-adic analysis — Krull intersection is the separatedness that makes completion faithful.** The $\mathfrak{a}$-adic filtration topologizes $M$ with the $\mathfrak{a}^n M$ as neighbourhoods of $0$, and completing gives $\hat M = \varprojlim M/\mathfrak{a}^n M$ — the algebraic incarnation of Taylor expansion and of the $p$-adic numbers. The kernel of the completion map $M \to \hat M$ is exactly $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$, so [[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull intersection]] is the statement that completion is *injective*: no nonzero element is "infinitely small", and the formal Taylor series of an algebraic function determines it (sharply unlike the smooth category, where $e^{-1/x^2}$ vanishes to infinite order). That all stable filtrations induce the same topology ([[Thm - Stable Filtrations Induce the Same Topology]]) makes "the completion" well-defined; that [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]] makes the subspace and intrinsic filtrations on a submodule equivalent makes completion *exact*. Together these are why $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \hat{\mathbb{Z}}_p$ and $k[x]_{(x)} \hookrightarrow k[[x]]$ are faithful — the foundation of the prior chapter, [[Commutative Algebra X — Completions and Limits]], and of all $p$-adic and formal geometry.

3. **Dimension theory — the Hilbert function of the associated graded ring computes Krull dimension.** A finitely generated graded module $\operatorname{gr}(M)$ over a Noetherian graded ring generated in degree one has each piece finite-dimensional over $A_0$, so its Hilbert function $n \mapsto \dim_k(\mathfrak{m}^n M/\mathfrak{m}^{n+1}M)$ is defined and (Hilbert–Serre) eventually polynomial; the degree of that **[[Def - The Hilbert Function and Hilbert Polynomial|Hilbert polynomial]]** is one less than the [[Def - Krull Dimension and Height|Krull dimension]] of $R$, and its leading coefficient encodes the multiplicity. The chapter supplies the two prerequisites: that $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ is Noetherian (so the Hilbert function exists) and that $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{m}^n = 0$ (so $\operatorname{gr}$ loses no element and $\dim R = \dim \operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$). This is why **dimension theory** (Commutative Algebra XII) is built on top of the associated graded ring — the tangent cone's dimension is the variety's dimension at the point.

4. **Intersection theory — deformation to the normal cone.** The Rees algebra and associated graded ring fit into one family via the **extended Rees algebra** $\bigoplus_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} \mathfrak{a}^n t^{-n} \subseteq R[t, t^{-1}]$ (with $\mathfrak{a}^n = R$ for $n \leq 0$), which is flat over the affine line $\operatorname{Spec} k[t]$ with general fibre $\operatorname{Spec} R$ and special fibre at $t = 0$ the normal cone $\operatorname{Spec} \operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R)$. This **deformation to the normal cone** is the device by which Fulton and MacPherson make the intersection product rigorous: to intersect two cycles, deform one into the normal cone of the other, where the intersection becomes linear-algebraic. The associated graded ring is thereby the foundation of the Chow ring and modern intersection theory, and the single algebraic input is that $\mathfrak{a}^n$ filters $R$ multiplicatively.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: this chapter is the dictionary between the analytic word "stable" and the algebraic word "finitely generated", mediated by the Rees algebra.** Everything here is one idea applied twice. A filtration is an analytic object — a notion of smallness, a topology, an order of vanishing — and analysis is hard to run Noetherian machinery on. The Rees algebra $R^* = \bigoplus \mathfrak{a}^n$ stacks the filtration into a graded ring and translates "stable" into "the Rees module is finitely generated", at which point the Noetherian property of $R^*$ does all the work. [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]] is then the single observation "submodules of Noetherian modules are finitely generated", read through the translation; [[Thm - The Krull Intersection Theorem|Krull intersection]] is Artin–Rees plus Nakayama; the equivalence of stable filtrations is the translation read in reverse. The slogan to carry is **stack the levels to prove finiteness, collapse to the layers to extract geometry** — the Rees algebra and the associated graded ring are the two halves of the same conversion, joined by $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R) = R^*/\mathfrak{a}R^*$.

**The true name of the associated graded ring: it is the linearization of $R$ along $\mathfrak{a}$, and over a maximal ideal it is the tangent cone.** The [[Def - The Associated Graded Ring and the Rees Algebra|definition]] presents $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{a}}(R)$ as a direct sum of quotients, which is opaque. Its operational true name is "the simplest graded ring recording, layer by layer, how $R$ sits relative to $\mathfrak{a}$, over the simpler base $R/\mathfrak{a}$". When $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{m}$ is maximal, the base is a field and $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ is the coordinate ring of the **tangent cone** — and it is *computed from the initial forms* of the defining equations, not the equations themselves. This reorganises every computation: to find $\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)$ you do not carry the ideal $I$, you carry its initial ideal $\operatorname{in}(I)$ of lowest-degree parts. The cusp $y^2 - x^3 \rightsquigarrow y^2$ (a doubled line) and the node $y^2 - x^2 - x^3 \rightsquigarrow y^2 - x^2$ (two lines) show that the discarded higher-degree terms are exactly what the tangent cone forgets — and that the cone's shape (linear, reducible, nonreduced) *diagnoses* whether the point is smooth, a multiple branch, or a tangential singularity.

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "show a deep submodule is zero" means "Artin–Rees to get $N = \mathfrak{a}N$, then Nakayama to get $N = 0$".** When a submodule is defined as an infinite intersection $\bigcap_n \mathfrak{a}^n M$, or as a stable submodule, or as the kernel of a completion, the reaction should be automatic: it sits inside every filtration level, so $N \cap \mathfrak{a}^n M = N$; [[Thm - The Artin-Rees Lemma|Artin–Rees]] makes the induced filtration stable, collapsing to the single equation $N = \mathfrak{a}N$; and [[Thm - Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama]] — legal exactly when $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(R)$ — forces $N = 0$. The complementary reflex is the hypothesis check: the *necessity* of $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \operatorname{Jac}(R)$ is tested by manufacturing a ring where $1 + a$ fails to be a unit (an idempotent ideal $\mathfrak{a} = (e)$ has $\mathfrak{a}^n = \mathfrak{a}$ for all $n$). These two reflexes — *collapse-then-Nakayama*, and *test the Jacobson-radical hypothesis with an idempotent* — between them solve every vanishing problem in the chapter, and both are pure consequences of the interplay between Artin–Rees and Nakayama.

**An inheritance observation: every finiteness in this chapter is inherited from one ring being Noetherian — the Rees algebra.** Ask "where does the stabilization bound $c$ in Artin–Rees come from?", and the honest answer is: from $R^*$ being Noetherian, so that a finitely generated $R^*$-module is generated in finitely many degrees, the largest of which is $c$. The uniform bound over infinitely many degrees is not conjured by hand — it is *inherited* from the Noetherian property of the single ring $R^*$, which is in turn inherited from $R$ via [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]] (the Rees algebra is generated in degree one over $R$). Trace any hard finiteness theorem of the chapter — Artin–Rees, Krull intersection, the existence of Hilbert polynomials — back through the translations, and it bottoms out at "$R^*$ is Noetherian". That is the chapter's deepest reusable lesson: when a theorem reduces to "this auxiliary graded ring is Noetherian", that reduction is the theorem's real content, and the auxiliary ring is its engine.
