# Notation Registry

**Standing convention.** Throughout these commutative algebra notes, *ring* means a commutative ring with a multiplicative identity $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$. *Module* means a unital module. This chapter is about a single finiteness hypothesis, the **chain condition**, imposed at the level of *modules* rather than rings. The slogan, stated again in Motivation, is: **a module is Noetherian when its submodules cannot grow forever, Artinian when they cannot shrink forever, and these two halves of "finiteness" are genuinely independent.** Everything about Noetherian and Artinian *rings* you already know (from [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Rings IV]]) is the special case $M = R$.

- $R, A, B$ — commutative rings with $1$; $k$ — a field
- $M, N, L$ — [[Def - Module|R-modules]]; $N \subseteq M$ — $N$ is a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] of $M$
- $M/N$ — the [[Def - Quotient Module|quotient module]]; $\bigoplus_i M_i$ — the [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules|direct sum]]
- $R^{\oplus \ell}$ or $R^\ell$ — the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] of rank $\ell$, the direct sum of $\ell$ copies of $R$
- ACC / DCC — the **ascending** / **descending chain condition** on submodules
- $\Sigma$ — a non-empty set of submodules; a *maximal* (resp. *minimal*) element of $\Sigma$ is one not strictly contained in (resp. not strictly containing) any other member
- $0 \to N \xrightarrow{i} M \xrightarrow{p} L \to 0$ — a **short exact sequence**: $i$ injective, $p$ surjective, $\operatorname{im} i = \ker p$
- $\operatorname{im} f$, $\ker f$ — image and kernel of an $R$-linear map $f$
- $\ell(M)$ or $\ell_R(M)$ — the **length** of $M$: the common length of all composition series, $\infty$ if none exists
- A **simple** module is a non-zero module with no submodules other than $0$ and itself
- $\rho : R \to A$ — the **structural homomorphism** making the ring $A$ an **$R$-algebra**; one writes $rx := \rho(r)x$
- $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ — the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial algebra]] in $n$ variables over $R$
- $\langle S \rangle$ — the submodule, or subalgebra, generated by a set $S$ (the meaning is fixed by context)
- $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12] = \{\tfrac{a}{2^m} : a, m \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ — the dyadic rationals; $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]/\mathbb{Z}$ — the dyadic rationals modulo $1$

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **a "finiteness" hypothesis good enough to make algebra work is not "finitely many elements" but "no infinite chain of submodules", and once you say this for modules you can say it once and apply it everywhere — to ideals, to quotients, to direct sums, and to the polynomial rings that are the coordinate rings of geometry.** You met the ring version of this already: a [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian ring]] is one whose ideals satisfy the ascending chain condition, equivalently one in which every ideal is finitely generated. This chapter does two things. First it lifts that idea from ideals to arbitrary submodules of an arbitrary module — and, crucially, recognises that there is a *dual* condition, the descending chain condition, defining **Artinian** modules, which behaves completely differently. Second it identifies the two structures on which the whole later theory runs: the **short exact sequence**, which is the bookkeeping device for "$M$ is built from $N$ and $L = M/N$", and the **$R$-algebra**, which is a ring sitting over $R$ — the object [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]] is really about.

Why insist on chain conditions rather than plain finite generation? Because finite generation is not *inherited* well enough to build a theory on. A submodule of a finitely generated module need not be finitely generated — the standard witness is the polynomial ring $R = \mathbb{Z}[T_1, T_2, \dots]$ in infinitely many variables, which is generated as a module over itself by the single element $1$, yet whose submodule of constant-term-zero polynomials needs infinitely many generators $T_1, T_2, \dots$. Finiteness that does not survive passage to submodules is useless for induction. The chain condition fixes exactly this: a module is Noetherian if and only if *every* submodule is finitely generated, so "Noetherian" is the closure of "finitely generated" under taking submodules, and that closure is precisely what lets every later argument recurse. This is the backbone of the chapter, the chain of equivalences and implications that organises everything:

$$\text{ACC on submodules} \;\iff\; \text{every submodule finitely generated} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{$M$ finitely generated},$$

with the last arrow strictly one-directional, and with the Artinian (DCC) condition running in parallel but *not* equivalent to the Noetherian one.

The reason to package "$M$ is assembled from $N$ and $M/N$" as a short exact sequence $0 \to N \to M \to L \to 0$ is that almost every finiteness property is *two-out-of-three* along such a sequence: $M$ is Noetherian (or Artinian) if and only if both $N$ and $L$ are. This single lemma is the engine of the chapter. It instantly gives that finite direct sums of Noetherian modules are Noetherian, hence that $R^{\oplus \ell}$ is Noetherian whenever $R$ is, hence — since every finitely generated module is a quotient of some $R^{\oplus \ell}$ — that **every finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian**. The deeper statement, that *every finitely generated algebra over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian*, is Hilbert's basis theorem in its real, algebra-level form, and it is the gateway to algebraic geometry: a finitely generated algebra over a field is the coordinate ring of an affine variety, and Hilbert's theorem is what guarantees those coordinate rings are Noetherian, so that varieties are cut out by *finitely many* equations and have *finitely many* irreducible components.

The length story closes the loop between the two chain conditions. A **composition series** is a maximal chain of submodules $M = M_0 \supsetneq M_1 \supsetneq \cdots \supsetneq M_n = 0$ with simple quotients; the Jordan–Hölder phenomenon makes its length $n$ an invariant $\ell(M)$, and this length is **additive** on short exact sequences. The punchline is that **$\ell(M) < \infty$ if and only if $M$ is both Noetherian and Artinian** — finite length is exactly the conjunction of "cannot grow forever" and "cannot shrink forever". For a vector space this collapses to a single familiar number: a $k$-vector space is Noetherian, or Artinian, or of finite length, all if and only if it is finite-dimensional, and then $\ell = \dim$.

This chapter assumes you are fluent with [[Def - Module|modules]], [[Def - Submodule|submodules]], [[Def - Quotient Module|quotient modules]], [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finite generation]], [[Def - Free Module|free modules]], and the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|isomorphism theorems for modules]] from the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|Modules]] notes, and with [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian rings]], the equivalence [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals|ACC ⟺ every ideal finitely generated]], and [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem for rings]] from the [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8|Rings]] notes. The module-level material here is the natural sequel: it is those same ideas, said once for submodules so they can be reused for the rest of commutative algebra.

# Concept Map

## §2.1 Noetherian and Artinian Modules

- **[[Def - Noetherian and Artinian Module]]**
	- An $R$-module $M$ is **Noetherian** if every ascending chain of submodules $M_1 \subseteq M_2 \subseteq \cdots$ stabilises, equivalently if every non-empty set of submodules has a maximal element; it is **Artinian** if the same holds with "ascending"/"maximal" replaced by "descending"/"minimal". The two conditions are logically independent: $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is Noetherian but not Artinian (the chain $(2) \supsetneq (4) \supsetneq (8) \supsetneq \cdots$ never stabilises), while $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]/\mathbb{Z}$ is Artinian but not Noetherian. A ring is Noetherian/Artinian exactly when it is so as a module over itself, recovering the [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Rings IV definition]].

- **[[Thm - Noetherian iff Every Submodule is Finitely Generated]]**
	- $M$ is Noetherian if and only if every submodule of $M$ (including $M$ itself) is finitely generated. In particular every Noetherian module is finitely generated, but the converse is false: $\mathbb{Z}[T_1, T_2, \dots]$ is generated as a module over itself by $1$, yet its submodule of constant-term-zero polynomials is not finitely generated. This is the "true name" of Noetherian for module arguments — it is the form that lets proofs build a generator at a time and force termination by ACC.

- **[[Def - Exact Sequence and Short Exact Sequence]]**
	- A sequence $\cdots \to M_{i-1} \xrightarrow{f_i} M_i \xrightarrow{f_{i+1}} M_{i+1} \to \cdots$ of $R$-modules is **exact** if $\operatorname{im} f_i = \ker f_{i+1}$ at every spot. A **short exact sequence** is one of the form $0 \to N \xrightarrow{i} M \xrightarrow{p} L \to 0$; exactness forces $i$ injective, $p$ surjective, and $\operatorname{im} i = \ker p$, so $N \cong i(N)$ is a submodule and $L \cong M/i(N)$ is the corresponding quotient. The single notion "short exact sequence" packages "$M$ is an extension of $L$ by $N$".

- **[[Thm - Chain Conditions Pass Through Short Exact Sequences]]**
	- Given $0 \to N \to M \to L \to 0$, the module $M$ is Noetherian (resp. Artinian) if and only if *both* $N$ and $L$ are. This two-out-of-three principle is the engine of the chapter: it immediately gives that a finite direct sum $M_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus M_n$ of Noetherian (resp. Artinian) modules is Noetherian (resp. Artinian), via the sequence $0 \to M_1 \to M_1 \oplus M_2 \to M_2 \to 0$ applied repeatedly.

- **[[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules over a Noetherian Ring are Noetherian]]**
	- If $R$ is a Noetherian ring then every finitely generated $R$-module $M$ is a Noetherian module (and likewise for Artinian). The proof is two lines given the machinery: $M$ is a quotient of $R^{\oplus \ell}$, which is Noetherian by the direct-sum corollary, and quotients of Noetherian modules are Noetherian. Consequently a ring $R$ is Noetherian if and only if every submodule of every finitely generated $R$-module is finitely generated.

- **[[Ex - The dyadic rationals mod Z are Artinian but not Noetherian]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]/\mathbb{Z}$ is Artinian but not Noetherian as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, by classifying all its submodules as the cyclic groups $\tfrac{1}{2^n}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}$. The single canonical witness that the two chain conditions are independent: these submodules form one ascending chain with no top (no Noetherian) but admit no infinite descending chain (Artinian).

- **[[Ex - A vector space is Noetherian iff Artinian iff finite-dimensional]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove that for a $k$-vector space $V$, the conditions $\dim_k V < \infty$, $\ell(V) < \infty$, Noetherian, and Artinian are all equivalent. Shows that the entire chain-condition apparatus collapses to dimension over a field — the asymmetry between Noetherian and Artinian can only appear when the base is a genuine ring.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Spec is a Noetherian topological space *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> When $R$ is Noetherian, its prime spectrum **Spec $R$** is a *Noetherian topological space*: the descending chain condition on closed sets holds, because closed sets $V(I)$ correspond order-reversingly to radical ideals and ascending chains of ideals stabilise. The geometric payoff is that **every algebraic set has only finitely many irreducible components** — you cannot keep splitting a variety into smaller and smaller closed pieces forever. The ascending chain condition on ideals is, dictionary-translated, the *descending* chain condition on closed subsets. This is developed fully in [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

## §2.2 Algebras and Hilbert's Basis Theorem

- **[[Def - Composition Series and Length]]**
	- A **composition series** of $M$ is a chain $M = M_0 \supsetneq M_1 \supsetneq \cdots \supsetneq M_n = 0$ in which each quotient $M_i/M_{i+1}$ is **simple** (no submodules strictly between $M_{i+1}$ and $M_i$). The Jordan–Hölder theorem makes the length $n$ an invariant, the **length** $\ell(M)$, with $\ell(M) = \infty$ when no composition series exists. Length is finite if and only if $M$ is both Noetherian and Artinian, and it is additive: $\ell(M) = \ell(N) + \ell(L)$ for any short exact sequence $0 \to N \to M \to L \to 0$.

- **[[Def - Algebra over a Ring (R-algebra)]]**
	- An **$R$-algebra** is a ring $A$ together with a fixed ring homomorphism $\rho : R \to A$, the **structural map**; one writes $rx := \rho(r)x$. Every $R$-algebra is automatically an $R$-module (forget the multiplication on $A$), but it carries a *ring* structure $R$ maps into, not merely a module structure. Every ring is a $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra in exactly one way; a non-zero algebra over a field $k$ contains a copy of $k$ via the injective $\rho$. The distinction from a module is exactly the distinction between $\rho : R \to A$ landing in a ring and $\rho : R \to \operatorname{End}(M)$ landing in an endomorphism ring.

- **[[Def - Finitely Generated Algebra]]**
	- $A$ is a **finitely generated $R$-algebra** if there are $x_1, \dots, x_n \in A$ such that every element of $A$ is a polynomial $p(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ with coefficients in $R$ — equivalently $A$ is a quotient of a polynomial algebra $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$. This is strictly weaker than being a finitely generated *module*: a finitely generated module allows only $R$-linear combinations of generators, while a finitely generated algebra allows all polynomial expressions. The polynomial algebra $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ is finitely generated as a $k$-algebra but not as a $k$-module.

- **[[Ex - A finitely generated algebra need not be a finitely generated module]]** (⭐)
	- Prove that $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ is a finitely generated $k$-algebra but not a finitely generated $k$-module. The clean separating example for the algebra/module distinction: unbounded degree of monomials forces any spanning set as a module to be infinite, while $T_1, \dots, T_n$ generate as an algebra.

- **[[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem (Algebra Form)]]**
	- Every finitely generated algebra over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian. It suffices to prove $R[T]$ is Noetherian when $R$ is, then induct via $R[T_1, \dots, T_n] \cong R[T_1, \dots, T_{n-1}][T_n]$ and pass to quotients. The proof tracks leading coefficients: the ideals $\mathfrak{a}(i)$ of degree-$i$ leading coefficients form an ascending chain that stabilises by ACC in $R$, and finitely many witnessing polynomials generate the whole ideal. This is the deep finiteness theorem of the subject — far stronger than "finitely generated module", since polynomial rings are *not* finitely generated modules.

- **[[Thm - Length is Additive and Finite iff Noetherian and Artinian]]**
	- For any short exact sequence $0 \to N \to M \to L \to 0$ of finite-length modules, $\ell(M) = \ell(N) + \ell(L)$; and more generally an alternating sum of lengths along any finite exact sequence vanishes. Moreover $\ell(M) < \infty$ if and only if $M$ is both Noetherian and Artinian. Length is therefore the precise common refinement of the two chain conditions, and for a vector space it equals the dimension, so for $k$-vector spaces "Noetherian", "Artinian", "finite length", and "finite-dimensional" all coincide.

- **[[Ex - Every finitely generated ideal has a finite generating subset]]** (⭐)
	- Prove that if a finitely generated ideal $I$ is generated by a (possibly infinite) subset $S$, then some finite subset $S_0 \subseteq S$ already generates $I$. A pure finiteness argument: write each of the finitely many generators of $I$ as a combination of elements of $S$, collect the finitely many elements of $S$ used. (ES1 Q1a.)

- **[[Ex - Composition length is additive on exact sequences]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove that for any additive integer-valued invariant $\lambda$ (e.g. length) and any finite exact sequence $0 \to M_0 \to M_1 \to \cdots \to M_n \to 0$, the alternating sum $\sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i \lambda(M_i) = 0$. The telescoping argument that turns additivity-on-short-exact-sequences into the Euler-characteristic identity, by splitting the long sequence at each spot. (ES2 Q1c, ES1 Q12.)

- **[[Ex - A subalgebra of a Noetherian algebra need not be Noetherian]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Show that the subalgebra $k[T_1 T_2, T_1 T_2^2, T_1 T_2^3, \dots]$ of the Noetherian ring $k[T_1, T_2]$ is *not* Noetherian. The cautionary example showing Hilbert's basis theorem needs the algebra to be *finitely generated*: a subalgebra of a finitely generated algebra need not itself be finitely generated, and this one has an ideal requiring infinitely many generators. (ES1 Q8.)

- **[[Ex - Unboundedly many generators in k of X and Y]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove that the ideal $\mathfrak{a}_n = (X^n, X^{n-1}Y, \dots, Y^n)$ of $k[X, Y]$ requires at least $n+1$ generators, so finite generation gives no *uniform* bound on the number of generators. The degree-$n$ part of $k[X,Y]$ is $(n+1)$-dimensional and $\mathfrak{a}_n$ is generated in degree $n$; passing to $\mathfrak{a}_n/\mathfrak{a}_{n+1}$ counts the generators. (ES1 Q7.)

> [!tip] Unlocked: Coordinate ring of an affine variety *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> A finitely generated algebra over a field, $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I$, is exactly the **coordinate ring** of the affine algebraic set $V(I) \subseteq k^n$ — the ring of polynomial functions on the variety. Hilbert's basis theorem says this ring is Noetherian, which is the algebraic shadow of two geometric facts: every variety is cut out by *finitely many* polynomial equations (an infinite system is equivalent to a finite subsystem), and every variety decomposes into *finitely many* irreducible components. The dictionary "finitely generated $k$-algebra $\leftrightarrow$ affine variety" is the foundation of [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Grothendieck group and Euler characteristics *(from Homological Algebra)*
> Additivity of length on short exact sequences is the prototype of an **additive invariant**, and the vanishing of the alternating sum $\sum (-1)^i \ell(M_i)$ along a finite exact sequence is the prototype of an **Euler characteristic**. Any function on modules that is additive on short exact sequences factors through the **Grothendieck group** $K_0$, whose whole purpose is to be the universal receptacle for such invariants. Dimension of vector spaces, length of finite-length modules, and ranks of chain complexes are all instances; the alternating-sum identity is why Euler characteristics are homotopy invariants in the topological incarnation.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring goals of this chapter fall into five families. The first is **certifying a module or ring as Noetherian (or Artinian)** — the bread-and-butter conclusion, almost always reached not by inspecting chains directly but by exhibiting the object as a quotient or finite extension of something already known to satisfy the condition, via [[Thm - Chain Conditions Pass Through Short Exact Sequences|the short-exact-sequence lemma]] or [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem (Algebra Form)|Hilbert's basis theorem]]. The second is **refuting the condition** — showing a specific module is *not* Noetherian or *not* Artinian, which is done by exhibiting one non-stabilising chain (an infinite strictly-ascending chain of submodules kills Noetherian; an infinite strictly-descending one kills Artinian). The third is **establishing finite generation of every submodule**, the form of Noetherian that feeds inductions; this is exactly [[Thm - Noetherian iff Every Submodule is Finitely Generated|the equivalence]] and is the lever behind "choose a maximal counterexample" arguments. The fourth is **computing or bounding length** — finding $\ell(M)$, or proving an additivity/alternating-sum identity — which routes through [[Thm - Length is Additive and Finite iff Noetherian and Artinian|additivity of length]] and the Jordan–Hölder invariance. The fifth is **separating algebras from modules** — proving something is a finitely generated algebra but not a finitely generated module, or that a subalgebra of a Noetherian algebra fails to be Noetherian; these probe the gap between the two notions of "finitely generated". These are the natural targets because the chapter is fundamentally about *finiteness that is robust under construction*: every theorem says "this finiteness survives (or fails to survive) some operation — quotient, submodule, extension, direct sum, subalgebra".

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A short exact sequence is present, or can be built** — the moment a submodule $N \subseteq M$ appears, the sequence $0 \to N \to M \to M/N \to 0$ is available and the two-out-of-three principle applies; recognising this is the single most common move. **The base ring is Noetherian** — which unlocks that finitely generated modules over it are Noetherian, that polynomial extensions stay Noetherian, and that quotients and localisations stay Noetherian. **The module is finitely generated over a Noetherian ring** — combining the previous two, this is the standard hypothesis under which "Noetherian" becomes automatic. **A composition series exists, or finite length is given** — which makes length available as an additive integer-valued invariant and licenses induction on $\ell$. **The object is an algebra, generated by finitely many elements** — routing through the presentation $A \cong R[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I$ to Hilbert's theorem. The discipline is to match source to target along the chapter's spine: a submodule appearing routes through the exact-sequence lemma; a "prove Noetherian" target over a finitely generated algebra routes through Hilbert; a "refute Noetherian" target routes to building one explicit infinite chain; a length computation routes through additivity. The [[Commutative Algebra I — Chain Conditions#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every chain-condition problem is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

1. **Build the short exact sequence from a submodule.** Any submodule $N \subseteq M$ gives, for free, the sequence $0 \to N \xrightarrow{\subseteq} M \xrightarrow{\text{quot}} M/N \to 0$. This converts a statement about $M$ into linked statements about the smaller $N$ and the smaller $M/N$, on which induction or known results bite. *Trigger:* a submodule, kernel, image, or quotient is in play and you want to transfer a finiteness property. *Pattern:* "form $0 \to N \to M \to M/N \to 0$ and apply [[Thm - Chain Conditions Pass Through Short Exact Sequences|two-out-of-three]]".

2. **Decompose a direct sum into iterated short exact sequences.** A finite direct sum $M_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus M_n$ is reached by repeatedly applying $0 \to M_1 \to M_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus M_n \to M_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus M_n \to 0$, so a property holding for each summand and preserved by extensions holds for the sum. *Trigger:* a finite direct sum and a two-out-of-three property. *Pattern:* "peel off one summand at a time".

3. **Present a finitely generated module as a quotient of a free module.** If $M$ is generated by $m_1, \dots, m_k$ then $(r_1, \dots, r_k) \mapsto \sum r_i m_i$ is a surjection $R^{\oplus k} \twoheadrightarrow M$, so $M \cong R^{\oplus k}/K$ is a quotient of a free module. *Trigger:* a finitely generated module over a (Noetherian) ring. *Pattern:* "$M$ is a quotient of $R^{\oplus k}$, and $R^{\oplus k}$ is Noetherian, so $M$ is".

4. **Refute a chain condition with one explicit chain.** To prove $M$ is *not* Noetherian, exhibit a single strictly ascending chain of submodules that never stabilises; to prove $M$ is *not* Artinian, exhibit a strictly descending one. *Trigger:* a "is this Noetherian/Artinian?" question you suspect is negative. *Pattern:* "list $N_1 \subsetneq N_2 \subsetneq \cdots$ (or $\supsetneq$) and show each inclusion is strict".

5. **Use ACC to choose a maximal element.** When [[Thm - Noetherian iff Every Submodule is Finitely Generated|every submodule is finitely generated]] is in force, any non-empty family of submodules has a maximal member; "choose a maximal counterexample" then derives a contradiction by enlarging it. *Trigger:* an existence or finiteness claim provable by contradiction. *Pattern:* "let $N$ be maximal among submodules failing the property, then build a strictly larger one".

6. **Reduce an algebra to a polynomial-ring quotient.** A finitely generated $R$-algebra $A$ generated by $x_1, \dots, x_n$ is a quotient $R[T_1, \dots, T_n] \twoheadrightarrow A$, $T_i \mapsto x_i$, by the universal property of the polynomial algebra. *Trigger:* a finitely generated algebra and a need to invoke Noetherianity. *Pattern:* "$A \cong R[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I$, apply [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem (Algebra Form)|Hilbert]]".

7. **Track leading coefficients.** Inside $R[T]$, the leading coefficients of the degree-$i$ elements of an ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ form an ideal $\mathfrak{a}(i)$ of $R$, and $\mathfrak{a}(0) \subseteq \mathfrak{a}(1) \subseteq \cdots$ is ascending. *Trigger:* a polynomial-ring ideal and a Noetherian base. *Pattern:* "the leading-coefficient ideals ascend, hence stabilise; finitely many witnesses generate".

8. **Refine a chain to a composition series.** Insert submodules into a chain $M = M_0 \supsetneq \cdots \supsetneq M_n = 0$ until every quotient is simple; for a finite-length module this terminates at length $\ell(M)$ regardless of choices. *Trigger:* a length computation or a Jordan–Hölder argument. *Pattern:* "refine until quotients are simple; the length is an invariant".

9. **Add lengths across a short exact sequence.** For finite-length modules, $\ell(M) = \ell(N) + \ell(L)$ along $0 \to N \to M \to L \to 0$; iterate to get the alternating-sum identity $\sum (-1)^i \ell(M_i) = 0$ for a finite exact sequence. *Trigger:* a length identity or a count of composition factors. *Pattern:* "split into short exact pieces and add lengths".

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Concluding Noetherian implies Artinian (or conversely)
> Both conditions are chain conditions and look symmetric, so it is tempting to think one implies the other or that they are equivalent. They are *independent*. $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is **Noetherian but not Artinian**: every ideal $(n)$ is principal, so ACC holds, yet $(2) \supsetneq (4) \supsetneq (8) \supsetneq \cdots$ descends forever. The module $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]/\mathbb{Z}$ is **Artinian but not Noetherian**: its submodules are exactly $\tfrac{1}{2^n}\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z}$, which form a single *ascending* chain with no top (killing Noetherian) but whose only descending chains are finite (so DCC holds). The conditions coincide only under an extra hypothesis — exactly *finite length*, which is their conjunction, and for rings "Artinian $\iff$ Noetherian of Krull dimension zero". The repair: never infer one chain condition from the other; check finite length if you want both.

> [!warning] 2. Treating a finitely generated algebra as a finitely generated module
> A finitely generated $R$-algebra and a finitely generated $R$-module both say "$A$ is built from finitely many elements", so the two are easily conflated. They are different because a module allows only $R$-linear combinations $\sum r_i x_i$, while an algebra allows all polynomial combinations $p(x_1, \dots, x_n)$, which can have unbounded degree. The polynomial algebra $k[T]$ is generated as a $k$-algebra by the single element $T$, but as a $k$-module it needs the infinite basis $1, T, T^2, \dots$ — it is **not** a finitely generated $k$-module. The two notions agree only when the generators are *integral* over $R$ (each satisfies a monic polynomial), which is the subject of [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]]; "finite type plus integral equals finite". The repair: ask whether you may use products of the generators (algebra) or only their $R$-linear span (module).

> [!warning] 3. Assuming a submodule of a finitely generated module is finitely generated
> Finite generation feels like it should pass to submodules — for vector spaces it does, since every subspace of a finite-dimensional space is finite-dimensional. Over a general ring it fails. In $R = \mathbb{Z}[T_1, T_2, \dots]$, the module $R$ is generated by the single element $1$, yet its submodule of polynomials with zero constant term needs the infinitely many generators $T_1, T_2, \dots$ — any finite subset involves only finitely many variables and so misses some $T_j$. This failure is *exactly* the gap between "finitely generated" and "Noetherian": submodules of finitely generated modules are finitely generated precisely when the ring is [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian]]. The repair: only push finite generation to submodules over a Noetherian ring, where [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules over a Noetherian Ring are Noetherian|the relevant theorem]] licenses it.

> [!warning] 4. Reading "a $k$-algebra is a ring containing $k$"
> A non-zero $k$-algebra $A$ does contain a copy of $k$ (the structural map $\rho : k \to A$ is injective since $k$ is a field), so one is tempted to *define* a $k$-algebra as "a ring containing $k$". This loses the data that matters: a $k$-algebra is a ring *with a chosen* $\rho$, and the same ring can be a $k$-algebra in genuinely different ways. Make $\mathbb{C}$ a $\mathbb{C}$-algebra once by $\rho = \operatorname{id}$ and once by letting $\rho$ be complex conjugation; the identity map $\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ is then *not* a $\mathbb{C}$-algebra homomorphism, because it fails $\varphi \circ \rho_{\text{conj}} = \rho_{\text{id}}$. The repair: an algebra is the pair $(A, \rho)$, and algebra homomorphisms must respect $\rho$; forgetting $\rho$ forgets the notion of morphism.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any chain-condition problem is to name *which direction* the claim runs — are you proving a finiteness property holds, or that it fails? — because the two have opposite shapes.

If the problem **asks you to prove a module or ring is Noetherian (or Artinian)**, you almost never argue from chains directly. Instead you exhibit the object as the output of an operation that *preserves* the condition, applied to inputs already known to satisfy it. The three preserving operations are: passing to a submodule or quotient, taking a finite direct sum or extension, and (for rings) adjoining finitely many algebra generators. So the route is: recognise the object as a quotient of $R^{\oplus \ell}$ (then [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules over a Noetherian Ring are Noetherian|finite generation over a Noetherian ring]] finishes it), or as the middle of a short exact sequence whose ends are known Noetherian (then [[Thm - Chain Conditions Pass Through Short Exact Sequences|two-out-of-three]] finishes it), or as a finitely generated algebra over a Noetherian ring (then [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem (Algebra Form)|Hilbert]] finishes it). The recognition step is everything; once the object is presented in one of these three shapes, the theorem does the rest.

If the problem **asks you to prove the condition fails**, the entire task is to produce *one* explicit infinite chain. For Noetherian, build a strictly ascending chain of submodules $N_1 \subsetneq N_2 \subsetneq \cdots$ that never stabilises — the canonical recipe is "add one more generator each time" ($N_j = \langle T_1, \dots, T_j \rangle$). For Artinian, build a strictly descending chain $N_1 \supsetneq N_2 \supsetneq \cdots$ — the canonical recipe is "shrink by a factor each time" ($N_j = 2^j \mathbb{Z}$, or $\tfrac{1}{2^{n-j}}$-type pieces inside a divisible module). The art is choosing submodules whose strict inclusions are easy to verify; for ideals of polynomial rings, "involves the variable $T_{j+1}$" cleanly separates consecutive terms.

If the problem **asks about length**, you are computing or comparing an additive invariant, and the strategy is to break the module into short exact pieces and add. To find $\ell(M)$, build any composition series and count — Jordan–Hölder guarantees the count is independent of choices. To prove an identity, use [[Thm - Length is Additive and Finite iff Noetherian and Artinian|additivity]] on each short exact piece, and for a long exact sequence telescope the additivity into the alternating sum $\sum (-1)^i \ell(M_i) = 0$. The standing reduction is that finite length *is* the conjunction "Noetherian and Artinian", so a length argument is available exactly when both chain conditions hold; for vector spaces this is just finite-dimensionality and $\ell = \dim$.

If the problem **lives at the algebra/module boundary** — proving something is a finitely generated algebra but not a finitely generated module, or that a subalgebra of a Noetherian algebra is not Noetherian — the strategy is to separate the two notions by a *degree* or *generation* argument. A finitely generated algebra not finitely generated as a module is witnessed by unbounded degree (the powers $T, T^2, T^3, \dots$ are $k$-linearly independent). A subalgebra failing to be Noetherian is witnessed by an ideal needing infinitely many generators (the subalgebra $k[T_1 T_2, T_1 T_2^2, T_1 T_2^3, \dots]$ of $k[T_1, T_2]$ is the standard example — Hilbert's theorem applies to *finitely generated* algebras, and a subalgebra of one need not itself be finitely generated).

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "does this finiteness survive the construction in front of me?"** Quotient, submodule, extension, direct sum, polynomial extension, subalgebra, tensor — each operation either preserves a chain condition or does not, and the chapter is the catalogue of which do. Decide which operation built your object, recall whether it preserves the condition, and the proof writes itself: invoke the preservation theorem to prove the property, or exploit the one operation that breaks it (submodule for "finitely generated", subalgebra for "Noetherian algebra") to refute it.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Chain Conditions Pass Through Short Exact Sequences|The two-out-of-three lemma]]**: $M$ is Noetherian/Artinian $\iff$ both $N$ and $M/N$ are, along $0 \to N \to M \to L \to 0$. This is the single most reused fact in the chapter and the next several, because it converts *any* finiteness question about a module into questions about a sub and a quotient, and it is the proof of essentially every "X is Noetherian" result downstream. Its standard uses are: deducing direct sums of Noetherian modules are Noetherian, deducing quotients and submodules inherit the condition, and reducing a module to its composition factors. Reach for it the instant a submodule, kernel, image, or extension appears.

- **[[Thm - Noetherian iff Every Submodule is Finitely Generated|Noetherian ⟺ every submodule finitely generated]]**: this is the *working definition* of Noetherian for proofs. Its reusability is that it turns a hypothesis about chains into a constructive statement — "I may pick finitely many generators of any submodule" — and conversely turns "build a generator at a time and force termination" into a valid proof method via ACC. It underlies the maximal-counterexample technique and is the form of Noetherian used in primary decomposition, the Artin–Rees lemma, and dimension theory. Whenever you need *generators* rather than *chains*, this is the bridge.

- **[[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem (Algebra Form)|Hilbert's basis theorem (algebra form)]]**: a finitely generated algebra over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian. This is the workhorse that makes commutative algebra *applicable*, because the rings that actually arise — coordinate rings $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I$, finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-algebras, polynomial and power-series rings — are all finitely generated algebras over fields or $\mathbb{Z}$, hence Noetherian. Use it to certify Noetherianity of any concretely-presented ring, and remember it is *much* stronger than the module statement: it survives the unbounded degrees that break finite generation as a module.

- **[[Thm - Length is Additive and Finite iff Noetherian and Artinian|Additivity of length]]**: $\ell(M) = \ell(N) + \ell(L)$ on short exact sequences, and finite length $=$ Noetherian $+$ Artinian. Its reusability is as the prototype additive invariant: any integer-valued function additive on short exact sequences (rank, dimension, multiplicity, Euler characteristic) is governed by the same telescoping, giving $\sum (-1)^i \ell(M_i) = 0$ along finite exact sequences. Use it to count composition factors, to prove dimension formulas, and as the model whenever an invariant should be additive on extensions.

- **The presentation $M \cong R^{\oplus k}/K$**: every finitely generated module is a quotient of a free module. This is reused constantly as the *bridge between modules and the ring*: it transports any property that holds for $R$ (as a module over itself) and is preserved by finite direct sums and quotients down to all finitely generated modules. Noetherianity travels this way ([[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules over a Noetherian Ring are Noetherian|the relevant theorem]]); so do many later structural results. The trigger is "finitely generated module over a ring with a known module-property".

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — Noetherianity is why varieties are finite-equation, finite-component objects.** A finitely generated algebra over a field, $A = k[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I$, is the **coordinate ring** of the affine algebraic set $V(I) \subseteq k^n$: its elements are the polynomial functions on the variety, with two polynomials identified when they agree on $V(I)$. [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem (Algebra Form)|Hilbert's basis theorem]] makes $A$ Noetherian, and this has two precise geometric consequences. First, the ideal $I$ — even if you specify $V(I)$ by infinitely many equations — is finitely generated, so *every algebraic set is the zero locus of finitely many polynomials*, and an infinite system of polynomial equations is equivalent to a finite subsystem. Second, the ascending chain condition on ideals of $A$ becomes the descending chain condition on closed subsets of $\operatorname{Spec} A$ (closed sets reverse-correspond to radical ideals), which forces *every variety to have only finitely many irreducible components* — you cannot subdivide a closed set into strictly smaller closed pieces forever. The phrase "**Spec $R$** is a Noetherian topological space" is the topological face of "$R$ is a Noetherian ring", and it is the structural guarantee behind the entire finiteness of classical algebraic geometry.

2. **Homological algebra — additivity of length is the seed of the Grothendieck group and Euler characteristics.** A function $\lambda$ on modules that satisfies $\lambda(M) = \lambda(N) + \lambda(L)$ on every short exact sequence $0 \to N \to M \to L \to 0$ is called **additive**, and length is the basic example. The universal such function is the passage to the **Grothendieck group** $K_0$: one forms the free abelian group on isomorphism classes of modules and imposes exactly the relation $[M] = [N] + [L]$, so that every additive invariant factors through $K_0$ uniquely. The telescoped identity $\sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i \ell(M_i) = 0$ for a finite exact sequence is the algebraic prototype of an **Euler characteristic**: split the long sequence at each spot into short exact pieces, add lengths, and watch the boundary terms cancel in alternating fashion. In topology this same calculation, with $\ell$ replaced by the rank of a homology group, is why the Euler characteristic $\sum (-1)^i \dim H_i$ is computable from any chain complex and is a homotopy invariant. Length over a finite-length module category is the cleanest place to see the mechanism with no analysis in the way.

3. **Linear algebra — the chapter is "Noetherian-and-Artinian theory is the dimension theory of vector spaces, generalised".** Over a field $k$, all four notions collapse: a $k$-vector space is Noetherian, Artinian, of finite length, and finite-dimensional all simultaneously, and then $\ell(V) = \dim_k V$. The composition series of $V$ is any maximal flag $V = V_0 \supsetneq V_1 \supsetneq \cdots \supsetneq V_n = 0$ of subspaces with one-dimensional successive quotients, and Jordan–Hölder invariance of its length *is* the well-definedness of dimension. Reading the general theory back through this lens explains every phenomenon: ACC on submodules generalises "you cannot keep enlarging a subspace past the dimension", additivity of length generalises rank–nullity ($\dim V = \dim \ker + \dim \operatorname{im}$ is exactly $\ell$ additivity on $0 \to \ker \to V \to \operatorname{im} \to 0$), and the failure of the two chain conditions to coincide over $\mathbb{Z}$ is the failure that *cannot* happen over a field. The whole chapter is what survives, and what newly splits apart, when the base is a ring rather than a field.

4. **Group theory — composition series and Jordan–Hölder are the same theorem one category over.** The composition series of a module is the verbatim module analogue of the **composition series of a group**: a maximal chain of subgroups $G = G_0 \triangleright G_1 \triangleright \cdots \triangleright G_n = 1$ with each quotient $G_i/G_{i+1}$ *simple*, and the **Jordan–Hölder theorem** asserting the multiset of simple quotients is an invariant of $G$. The module version is gentler because every submodule is automatically "normal" (modules are abelian, so quotients always exist), which is why the chapter can state length cleanly without the normality bookkeeping groups require. The shared content is that *simple objects are the atoms and composition series count them with multiplicity*; the Grothendieck group of finite-length modules and the abelianised composition-factor count of finite groups are the same construction applied to the two categories. Recognising this tells you that "length" is to modules what "the simple-factor decomposition" is to finite groups — a complete additive bookkeeping of the simple constituents.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a chain condition is a finiteness hypothesis chosen to be inheritable.** Plain "finitely generated" is the naive notion of finiteness, and it is *not good enough*, for one reason — it does not survive passage to submodules. Every theorem in this chapter is, at bottom, the repair of that one defect and the exploration of its consequences. Noetherian is defined to be exactly the closure of finite generation under taking submodules ("every submodule is finitely generated"), and the whole reason it is the *right* hypothesis is that, being inheritable, it can drive inductions: you peel off a submodule, the submodule is still Noetherian, recurse. The short exact sequence is the formal device that makes "peel off a submodule" into an operation with a conservation law (two-out-of-three), and length is the numerical invariant that the conservation law produces. So the chapter is not a list of separate facts about chains; it is the single observation that *finiteness must be made inheritable before it is useful*, worked out into its module-theoretic, algebra-theoretic, and length-theoretic forms.

**The true name of Noetherian, for proof purposes, is "I may always pick finitely many generators, and a maximal element always exists".** The official definition (ascending chains stabilise) is the *recognition* form — it is what you check or refute. But the form you *use* inside a proof is [[Thm - Noetherian iff Every Submodule is Finitely Generated|"every submodule is finitely generated"]] together with its contrapositive twin "every non-empty family of submodules has a maximal element". The first lets you reach into any submodule and extract a finite generating set, which is what makes constructive arguments possible; the second licenses the maximal-counterexample method — to prove all submodules have a property, suppose not, take a maximal submodule lacking it, and enlarge it to a contradiction. When you return to a Noetherian argument after months, the reconstruction hinges on remembering that "Noetherian" should be *spent* as "choose a maximal one" or "grab finitely many generators", not as a statement about literal chains.

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "prove Noetherian" fires "exhibit as quotient / extension / finitely generated algebra"; "disprove Noetherian" fires "build one ascending chain".** These are opposite reflexes and confusing them wastes effort. To *prove* the condition, never inspect chains — recognise the object as built by a preserving operation: a quotient of $R^{\oplus \ell}$ (use [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules over a Noetherian Ring are Noetherian|finite generation over a Noetherian ring]]), a middle term of a short exact sequence with Noetherian ends (use [[Thm - Chain Conditions Pass Through Short Exact Sequences|two-out-of-three]]), or a finitely generated algebra over a Noetherian ring (use [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem (Algebra Form)|Hilbert]]). To *disprove* it, do inspect chains — produce a single strictly ascending chain that never stabilises, the canonical recipe being "add one more generator each step". The Artinian versions are the mirror image: disprove with one strictly *descending* chain. Carrying these two reflexes, paired with the recognition of *which operation built the object*, solves the large majority of the chapter's problems.

**Inheritance — where does the chain condition come from?** A Noetherian module's finiteness is never spontaneous; it is always inherited, ultimately from the base ring being Noetherian and from the rank being finite. Trace any "$M$ is Noetherian" backward and you reach: $M$ is a quotient of $R^{\oplus \ell}$ ($M$ finitely generated), $R^{\oplus \ell}$ is a finite direct sum of copies of $R$ (rank finite), and $R$ is a Noetherian module over itself (base Noetherian). The two finitenesses — finite rank of the free cover, and the chain condition of the base — combine through the exact-sequence lemma to give the chain condition of $M$. This is the same inheritance pattern as completeness of $L^p$ deriving from completeness of $\mathbb{R}$, or compactness diagonalising out of compactness of the line: a structural property of a complicated object is pulled back, through an explicit construction, to the same property of a simple object. When a chain-condition proof feels mysterious, ask where the finiteness is *coming from*, and the answer is always "the base ring, transported along $R^{\oplus \ell} \twoheadrightarrow M$".
