# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Let $A$ be an $R$-algebra via $\rho : R \to A$. We write $A = R x_1 + \cdots + R x_n$ to mean $A$ is generated *as an $R$-module* by $x_1, \dots, x_n$ (every element is an $R$-linear combination $\sum r_i x_i$), and $A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ to mean $A$ is generated *as an $R$-algebra* by $x_1, \dots, x_n$ (every element is a polynomial expression $\sum r_\alpha x_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots x_n^{\alpha_n}$, with finitely many terms but no bound on degree). The distinction between these two — module generation versus algebra generation — is the entire content of this page. We use "[[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]]" for the module notion. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]].

This is a compound page: it defines two interlocking notions — a **finite** $R$-algebra (finitely generated as a *module*) and a **finite-type** $R$-algebra (finitely generated as an *algebra*) — because they look almost identical, are constantly confused, and the precise gap between them is the heart of the chapter.

# Axiom Motivation

The goal is to make precise two different senses in which an algebra can be "small over its base", because they are routinely conflated and the difference is exactly integrality. When we say an $R$-algebra $A$ is "finitely generated", we must specify *as what* — as a module, or as an algebra — and the two answers are dramatically different. This page exists to keep them apart.

**The two ways to generate.** Generating $A$ *as an $R$-module* means: every element is an $R$-linear combination of finitely many fixed generators, $A = R x_1 + \cdots + R x_n$. You may add elements and multiply by scalars from $R$, but you may *not* multiply the generators together. Generating $A$ *as an $R$-algebra* means: every element is a *polynomial* in finitely many generators with $R$-coefficients, $A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. Now you *may* multiply generators, so all products and powers $x_i^k$ are available. The algebra-generated set is enormously larger from the same generators, because it includes all monomials of all degrees. **Module generation is linear; algebra generation is polynomial.** Naming the two notions separately is forced by the simple fact that they give different classes of algebras, and conflating them is a category error.

**Why the distinction is not pedantic — the polynomial ring.** The cleanest witness that the two notions differ is $A = k[T]$ over $R = k$. As an *algebra*, $A$ is generated by the single element $T$: every polynomial is a polynomial in $T$. So $A$ is finite-type with one generator. As a *module*, however, $A$ is *not* finitely generated: the powers $1, T, T^2, T^3, \dots$ are $k$-linearly independent, so no finite set of polynomials can $k$-span all of $k[T]$ — there is always a higher-degree polynomial outside the span. So **$k[T]$ is finite-type but not finite**, and the obstruction is precisely that $T$ has unboundedly large powers that cannot be reduced. This single example is the reason the two definitions must coexist: "finite-type" is a weak, ubiquitous condition (every variety's coordinate ring is finite-type), while "finite" is a strong, special condition (a finite branched cover).

**Why "finite" deserves the name and why it is the strong one.** A finite $R$-module is the algebraic analogue of a finite-dimensional vector space: it has a bounded spanning set, so all of linear algebra's finiteness consequences apply (Cayley–Hamilton, the determinant trick, Nakayama). Geometrically, finite over $R$ means the map $\operatorname{Spec} A \to \operatorname{Spec} R$ has *uniformly bounded fibres* — a finite branched cover — whereas finite-type only means $\operatorname{Spec} A$ is *cut out by finitely many equations in some affine space*, a far weaker constraint that allows fibres of growing or infinite size. The strength of "finite" is exactly the strength of "finite-dimensional": you can run determinant arguments. This is why the chapter's main theorems require *finite*, not merely *finite-type*, and why upgrading finite-type to finite is the central technical task.

**Why the gap between them is exactly integrality — the punchline that motivates everything.** Here is the fact that makes this page the conceptual hinge of the chapter. A finite-type algebra $A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is a *finite module* if and only if *every generator $x_i$ is integral over $R$*. The forward direction: if $A$ is a finite module, then each $x_i$ lies in a finite faithful module, so each is integral by the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]]. The reverse: if each $x_i$ satisfies a monic equation of degree $d_i$, then every power $x_i^k$ ($k \geq d_i$) reduces to lower powers, so the *bounded* monomials $x_1^{e_1} \cdots x_n^{e_n}$ with $e_i < d_i$ — finitely many — span $A$ as a module. So the missing ingredient that promotes finite-type to finite is *exactly* a monic equation on each generator: **finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral.** This is why the polynomial ring fails (its generator $T$ is *not* integral over $k$, satisfying no monic relation) and why an algebra generated by algebraic integers succeeds. The whole reason to define both notions is to be able to state this equivalence, which converts the hard, infinite-looking question "is $A$ a finite module?" into the local, checkable question "is each generator integral?".

# The Definition

Let $A$ be an $R$-algebra via $\rho : R \to A$.

**Finite algebra (finite as a module).** $A$ is **finite over $R$** (or a **finite $R$-algebra**) if $A$ is finitely generated as an $R$-*module*: there exist $x_1, \dots, x_n \in A$ with
$$A = R x_1 + R x_2 + \cdots + R x_n = \Big\{ \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^n r_i x_i : r_i \in R \Big\}.$$

**Finite-type algebra (finitely generated as an algebra).** $A$ is **finite-type over $R$** (or **finitely generated as an $R$-algebra**) if there exist $x_1, \dots, x_n \in A$ with
$$A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n] = \Big\{ \textstyle\sum_{\alpha} r_\alpha\, x_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots x_n^{\alpha_n} : r_\alpha \in R,\ \text{finite sum} \Big\},$$
equivalently $A$ is a quotient of a polynomial ring $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ by the surjection $T_i \mapsto x_i$.

**Relationship.** Every finite $R$-algebra is finite-type (a module-generating set is an algebra-generating set). The converse fails — $k[T]$ is finite-type but not finite — and the precise gap is integrality: a finite-type algebra is finite if and only if it is generated by elements [[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|integral]] over $R$ (see [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)]]).

# Categorical / Structural Definition

Both notions are *finiteness conditions on a forgetful functor*. Recall that an $R$-algebra is a ring $A$ together with a ring map $\rho : R \to A$, and that there are two ways to view $A$ over $R$: as an object of the category of $R$-*modules* (forgetting the multiplication on $A$ beyond the $R$-action), and as an object of the category of $R$-*algebras*.

- $A$ is **finite-type** iff the $R$-algebra $A$ is a quotient object of a *free $R$-algebra* (a polynomial ring) on finitely many generators: there is a surjection of $R$-algebras $R[T_1, \dots, T_n] \twoheadrightarrow A$. The polynomial ring $R[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ is the free commutative $R$-algebra on $n$ generators — characterised by the universal property that an $R$-algebra map out of it is the same as a choice of $n$ elements of the target.

- $A$ is **finite** iff the underlying $R$-*module* of $A$ is a quotient object of a *free $R$-module* on finitely many generators: there is a surjection of $R$-modules $R^n \twoheadrightarrow A$. The free module $R^n$ is the free object on $n$ generators in the category of $R$-modules.

So the two notions are the *same condition* — "finitely generated", i.e. a quotient of a free object on finitely many generators — applied in two different categories: $R$-algebras for finite-type, $R$-modules for finite. The functor relating them is the forgetful functor (algebras $\to$ modules), and "finite $\Rightarrow$ finite-type" is the statement that this functor *creates* surjections in a compatible way. The gap "finite-type but not finite" is exactly the failure of the forgetful functor to reflect finite generation, repaired by integrality.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

In **field theory** the two notions specialise and the gap is visible. A field extension $L/k$ is *finite-type* if $L = k(\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n)$ is generated as a field (finitely generated field extension), and *finite as a module* if $\dim_k L < \infty$ (finite extension). For a single generator: $k(\alpha)$ is finite over $k$ iff $\alpha$ is algebraic (then $k[\alpha] = k(\alpha)$ has dimension $\deg$ of the minimal polynomial), and finite-type-but-infinite iff $\alpha$ is transcendental (then $k(\alpha) \cong k(T)$ is infinite-dimensional). So "finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral" specialises to "finite extension $=$ finitely generated $+$ algebraic", the basic dichotomy algebraic/transcendental.

**True name:** the operational form of "finite over $R$" is **"has a bounded spanning set, so the determinant trick applies"** — it is the condition under which an $R$-linear endomorphism satisfies a monic polynomial. The operational form of "finite-type over $R$" is **"is a quotient of a polynomial ring", i.e. "is an affine variety's coordinate ring (cut out by equations)"**. Finite is the linear-algebra finiteness; finite-type is the cut-out-by-equations finiteness.

In **algebraic geometry**: finite-type over a field $=$ *affine variety* (a closed subset of some $\mathbb{A}^n$, defined by finitely many polynomial equations); finite over $R$ $=$ *finite morphism* to $\operatorname{Spec} R$ (a branched cover with bounded fibres). Every affine variety is finite-type; only the branched covers are finite. **Noether normalization** is the theorem that every finite-type domain over a field is *finite* over a polynomial subring — every variety is a finite branched cover of affine space — which is precisely the statement that finite-type can always be upgraded to finite after a good choice of subring.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is finite (hence finite-type) — $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ over $\mathbb{Z}$.** As a module, $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2] = \mathbb{Z} \cdot 1 + \mathbb{Z} \cdot \sqrt2$ is generated by $\{1, \sqrt2\}$: any power $(\sqrt2)^k$ reduces via $(\sqrt2)^2 = 2$ to one of $\pm 2^j$ or $\pm 2^j \sqrt2$. So it is a finite $\mathbb{Z}$-module of rank $2$, and therefore also finite-type. The reduction works because $\sqrt2$ is *integral* (monic $T^2 - 2$), truncating its powers.

**Is finite-type but NOT finite — $k[T]$ over $k$.** The polynomial ring is generated as an algebra by the single element $T$, so it is finite-type. But it is not a finite module: $1, T, T^2, \dots$ are $k$-linearly independent, so no finite set $k$-spans it. The element $T$ is *not* integral over $k$ — it satisfies no monic (indeed no non-zero) polynomial over $k$ forcing its powers to truncate. This is the defining separating example, witnessing finite-type $\not\Rightarrow$ finite.

**Is finite-type but NOT finite — $k[t, t^{-1}]$ over $k[t]$.** Generated as a $k[t]$-algebra by the single element $t^{-1}$, so finite-type. But not a finite $k[t]$-module: $t^{-1}, t^{-2}, \dots$ are independent over $k[t]$. Again the generator $t^{-1}$ is not integral over $k[t]$ (Example on [[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension]]). Geometrically the inclusion is an open immersion (delete the origin), not a finite cover.

**Is NOT even finite-type — $k[T_1, T_2, \dots]$ over $k$.** The polynomial ring in infinitely many variables is generated as an algebra only by an infinite set; no finite list of polynomials algebra-generates all variables. This is neither finite nor finite-type — the example to keep in mind so that "finite-type" does not feel automatic.

**Corollary — finite is transitive, and so is finite-type.** If $A$ is finite over $R$ and $B$ is finite over $A$, then $B$ is finite over $R$ (multiply generating sets: if $B = \sum A y_j$ and $A = \sum R x_i$ then $B = \sum R x_i y_j$); see [[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness]]. The same multiplication argument shows finite-type is transitive. This is the calibration check that the definitions compose.

**Corollary — finite-type $+$ integral generators $\Rightarrow$ finite.** If $A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ with each $x_i$ integral over $R$ (satisfying a monic of degree $d_i$), then the finitely many bounded monomials $\{x_1^{e_1} \cdots x_n^{e_n} : 0 \leq e_i < d_i\}$ span $A$ as an $R$-module, so $A$ is finite. This is the equivalence "finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral", proved in [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)]].

**Calibration check.** Decide for each of $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{R}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$, and $k[x, y]/(y^2 - x^3)$ over $k$ whether it is finite, finite-type, both, or neither. (Answers: $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$ is finite-type not finite — $\tfrac12$ is not integral, the powers $2^{-k}$ are independent; $\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}$ is finite of rank $2$; $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$ is neither — uncountable, not even finite-type; $k[x,y]/(y^2-x^3)$ is finite-type over $k$ but not finite.) If you can explain *why* $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$ is finite-type but not finite using the non-integrality of $\tfrac12$, you have understood the gap.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Noether normalization and the geometry of finite covers *(from Commutative Algebra / Algebraic Geometry)*
> The whole point of separating finite from finite-type is **Noether normalization**: every finite-type domain $A$ over a field $k$ is a *finite* module over a polynomial subring $k[y_1, \dots, y_d] \subseteq A$. Geometrically, every affine variety is a finite branched cover of affine space $\mathbb{A}^d$, and $d$ is its dimension. The proof works by replacing the algebra generators with new ones that become *integral* over a polynomial subring — i.e. by upgrading finite-type to finite. This is the gateway to the **Nullstellensatz** and to dimension theory; see [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

> [!tip] Module-finiteness is what makes Cayley–Hamilton and Nakayama available *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> A finite module is the setting where every $R$-linear endomorphism satisfies a monic polynomial ([[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|the determinant trick]]), and where [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama's lemma]] applies. Almost every structural theorem about integral extensions — the closure being a subring, transitivity, the field criterion — is ultimately a determinant-trick argument that *needs* the module to be finite, not merely the algebra finite-type. This is the practical reason the distinction is load-bearing rather than terminological.
