# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Let $A \subseteq B$ be a subring inclusion. We write $\overline A = \{b \in B : b \text{ integral over } A\}$ for the [[Def - Integral Closure and Normal Domain|integral closure]] of $A$ in $B$, and $A[x, y]$ for the $A$-subalgebra of $B$ generated by $x, y$. "Finite over $A$" means finitely generated as an $A$-[[Def - Finitely Generated Module|module]]. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (the integral closure is a subring).** Let $A \subseteq B$ be rings and let $\overline A = \{b \in B : b \text{ integral over } A\}$ be the set of elements of $B$ integral over $A$. Then $\overline A$ is an **$A$-subalgebra of $B$**: it contains $A$, and is closed under addition and multiplication. Concretely, if $x, y \in B$ are integral over $A$, then $x + y$, $x - y$, and $xy$ are integral over $A$.

> **Corollary (idempotence).** $\overline A$ is integrally closed in $B$: an element of $B$ integral over $\overline A$ is already integral over $A$, so $\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$.

---

# Motivation

This is the theorem that makes "the integral closure" a usable object rather than a curiosity. From the bare definition, $\overline A$ is just a *set* — the elements satisfying some monic equation — and there is no reason a set carved out by an existence-of-equation condition should be closed under the ring operations. Indeed, if $x$ satisfies one monic equation and $y$ another, you are handed two unrelated polynomials and asked to produce a *third* monic polynomial killing $x + y$. There is no formula. Trying to write one down leads to resultants and characteristic polynomials of large auxiliary matrices, and for $x + y$ the degree can balloon to the product of the two degrees. The naive approach drowns.

The theorem says: never mind the equations, $\overline A$ *is* a ring. This is what lets you speak of "the ring of algebraic integers", "the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of a number field", "the integral closure of a coordinate ring". Every one of these is the integral closure of a base in a bigger ring, and every one of them is *used as a ring* — you add and multiply algebraic integers freely. The fact that you may do so is precisely this theorem, and historically it was a genuine surprise: that the sum of two algebraic integers is again an algebraic integer is not at all obvious if you only know the definition.

The escape from the equation-juggling is the chapter's master idea: **switch from the monic equation to the finite module.** By the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]], "$x$ integral" means "$A[x]$ is a finite module", and finite modules compose. Put $x$ and $y$ into the single finite module $A[x, y]$; then $x + y$ and $xy$, being elements of a finite faithful module stabilised by themselves, are *automatically* integral — no equation needed, because the criterion produces one for free. The proof is three lines once you have the criterion and transitivity in hand. The lesson is general: when a property is defined by "there exists a witness" but witnesses do not combine, re-characterise the property by a *structural* condition that does combine. Here the structure is module-finiteness.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition is "$x, y \in B$ are integral over $A$", but the theorem is invoked in disguise whenever you need a *combination* of integral things to be integral.

The first disguised source is **a list of algebraic integers to be combined**. The property $B$ is "$\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m$ are each algebraic integers (integral over $\mathbb{Z}$)", and you want a polynomial expression in them to be integral. The bridge is that all $\alpha_i$ lie in the finite module $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m]$, so any $\mathbb{Z}$-polynomial in them is integral. Nonobvious because you would otherwise need to construct the combined equation. *Example problem:* show the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of a number field is closed under the field operations (Example Sheet 1 Q15(b)).

The second disguised source is **a generating set known to be integral**. The property $B$ is "$A = A_0[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ where each $x_i$ is integral over $A_0$". Then *every* element of $A$ is integral over $A_0$ (it is a polynomial in integral elements), so the whole algebra is an integral extension. The bridge is closure under $+, \times$ applied repeatedly. Nonobvious because it integralises an entire algebra from its generators. *Example problem:* a finite-type algebra generated by integral elements is an integral extension.

The third disguised source is **an element integral over the closure**. The property $B$ is "$z \in B$ is integral over $\overline A$" — its monic equation has coefficients in $\overline A$, not $A$. The corollary (idempotence) bridges this to "$z$ integral over $A$", because integrality is transitive and $\overline A$ is integral over $A$. Nonobvious because the coefficients are themselves only integral, yet $z$ collapses to integrality over the base. *Example problem:* proving the normalization is normal — closing once suffices.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$\overline A$ is a subring (and idempotently closed)".

Combine "$\overline A$ is a subring" with **the fraction field**. For a domain $A$, $\overline A \subseteq \operatorname{Frac}(A)$ is a subring between $A$ and its fraction field — the integral closure / normalization. The further result $E$ is that *every* domain has a canonical normalization, a normal domain $\overline A$ with the same fraction field, on which the going-down and Dedekind theory run. Nonobvious because "the set of integral fractions" being a ring is what gives normalization its ring structure.

Combine "$\overline A$ is a subring" with **idempotence**. Since $\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$, the normalization is *already normal* — you never iterate. The further result $E$ is that normalization is a well-defined idempotent operation $A \mapsto \overline A$ (a *closure operator*), which is what lets "the normal model of a variety" be unambiguous. Nonobvious because closure operations must be checked idempotent, and here idempotence is transitivity of integrality.

Combine "$\overline A$ is a subring" with **localization/quotient stability**. Because integral extensions survive localization and quotient, and $\overline A$ is a subring, one gets that integral closure commutes with localization ($\overline{S^{-1}A} = S^{-1}\overline A$) and that normality is a [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local property]]. The further result $E$ is "check normality prime by prime" (Example Sheet 3 Q7). Nonobvious because it converts a global ring property into a local one.

# Why Is It True

The whole proof is a refusal to look at the equations. **Two integral elements live together in one finite module, and a finite module integralises everything inside it.** That is the entire idea.

Concretely: if $x$ is integral over $A$, then $A[x]$ is a finite $A$-module — say spanned by $1, x, \dots, x^{p-1}$. If $y$ is integral over $A$, it is integral over the bigger ring $A[x]$ as well, so $A[x][y] = A[x, y]$ is a finite $A[x]$-module, spanned by $1, y, \dots, y^{q-1}$. Now stack: a finite module over a finite module is finite ([[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness|transitivity of finiteness]]), so $A[x, y]$ is a finite $A$-module, spanned by the products $x^i y^j$ ($i < p$, $j < q$). At this point the equations are irrelevant. The elements $x + y$, $x - y$, $xy$ all lie in $A[x, y]$, which is a finite $A$-module faithful over itself (it contains $1$) and stabilised by each of them (multiplication by an element of a ring keeps you in the ring). By the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]] (condition (4)), *every* element of $A[x, y]$ is integral over $A$. In particular $x + y$, $x - y$, $xy$ are.

**The one-line mechanism: $x$ and $y$ both live in the finite module $A[x,y]$, and the determinant trick hands a monic equation to every element of a finite module — so the sum and product inherit integrality without anyone ever exhibiting their equations.**

Idempotence is the same idea once more. If $z$ is integral over $\overline A$, its monic equation has finitely many coefficients $a_1, \dots, a_n \in \overline A$, each integral over $A$. So $A' = A[a_1, \dots, a_n]$ is a finite $A$-module (generated by integral elements), $z$ is integral over $A'$ (the equation lives in $A'[T]$), so $A'[z]$ is finite over $A'$, hence finite over $A$ by transitivity — and $z$, living in a finite $A$-module, is integral over $A$. The coefficients being "only" integral over $A$ does not matter, because finiteness transits through them. This is why closing once closes completely.

---

# What Makes This Hard

The conceptual hurdle is *resisting the urge to construct the monic equation for $x + y$* — there is no usable formula, and the proof's whole point is to avoid one by passing to the finite module $A[x, y]$. People get stuck trying to multiply or resultant the two given equations together. The non-obvious step is recognising that "$y$ integral over $A$" implies "$y$ integral over the larger ring $A[x]$" (trivially, the same equation works over a bigger coefficient ring), which is what lets you build the *tower* $A \subseteq A[x] \subseteq A[x, y]$ and apply transitivity of finiteness. The common error in the idempotence half is to think the coefficients being merely integral (not in $A$) blocks the argument; the fix is that they generate a *finite* $A$-algebra, so finiteness still transits.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:**
To show $x + y$, $xy$ integral, build the finite module $A[x, y]$ by stacking $A \subseteq A[x] \subseteq A[x, y]$ — each step finite because the new generator is integral — and apply the module-finite criterion to the whole module, which integralises all its elements at once. For idempotence, collect the integral-over-$\overline A$ coefficients into a finite $A$-algebra and transit finiteness.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **$A[x, y]$ is a finite $A$-module.** Show the tower $A \subseteq A[x] \subseteq A[x, y]$ has finite steps.
   - *Hint:* $x$ integral $\Rightarrow A[x]$ finite over $A$; $y$ integral over $A$ hence over $A[x]$ $\Rightarrow A[x, y]$ finite over $A[x]$; multiply generating sets ([[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness|transitivity]]).
   - *Why needed:* It produces the single finite module containing both $x$ and $y$.

2. **Every element of $A[x, y]$ is integral over $A$.** Apply condition (4) of the criterion.
   - *Hint:* $A[x, y]$ is faithful over itself ($1$ lies in it) and is stabilised by any of its elements; the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]] integralises it.
   - *Why needed:* It delivers integrality of $x + y$, $x - y$, $xy$ without their equations.

3. **Idempotence: integral over $\overline A$ $\Rightarrow$ integral over $A$.** Transit finiteness through the coefficient ring.
   - *Hint:* The finitely many coefficients $a_i \in \overline A$ generate a finite $A$-algebra $A'$; $z$ integral over $A'$ then finite-over-finite gives $z$ in a finite $A$-module.
   - *Why needed:* It shows $\overline A$ is integrally closed, so normalization is idempotent.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: $A[x, y]$ is finite over $A$ when $x, y$ are integral
> **Statement:** If $x, y \in B$ are integral over $A$, then $A[x, y]$ is a finite $A$-module.
>
> **Hint:** Stack the tower $A \subseteq A[x] \subseteq A[x, y]$; each step is finite, and finite-over-finite is finite.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the single finite module that contains $x + y$, $xy$ and everything built from $x, y$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Since $x$ is integral over $A$, the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]] gives that $A[x]$ is a finite $A$-module, say $A[x] = A \cdot 1 + A x + \cdots + A x^{p-1}$. Since $y$ is integral over $A$, it satisfies a monic polynomial over $A$, which is *a fortiori* a monic polynomial over the larger ring $A[x] \supseteq A$; so $y$ is integral over $A[x]$, and by the criterion $A[x][y] = A[x, y]$ is a finite $A[x]$-module, say $A[x, y] = A[x] \cdot 1 + A[x] y + \cdots + A[x] y^{q-1}$. By [[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness|transitivity of finiteness]] (finite-over-finite is finite), $A[x, y]$ is a finite $A$-module, spanned by the $pq$ products $\{x^i y^j : 0 \leq i < p,\ 0 \leq j < q\}$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Elements of a finite stable subring are integral
> **Statement:** If $M \subseteq B$ is a subring, finite as an $A$-module and containing $A$, then every element of $M$ is integral over $A$.
>
> **Hint:** Any $z \in M$ stabilises $M$ (multiplication keeps you in the ring), and $M$ is faithful (it contains $1$); apply condition (4) of the criterion.
>
> **Why needed:** It converts "lives in a finite module" into "is integral", finishing the closure-under-operations claim.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $z \in M$. Then $z M \subseteq M$ because $M$ is a ring, so $M$ is a faithful $A[z]$-module: it is an $A$-submodule of $B$, finite over $A$, with $1_B \in M$ (as $A \subseteq M$), and for $0 \neq p \in A[z] \subseteq M$ we have $p \cdot 1_B = p \neq 0$. By condition (4) of the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]], $z$ is integral over $A$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Idempotence via finite coefficient algebra
> **Statement:** If $z \in B$ is integral over $\overline A$, then $z$ is integral over $A$.
>
> **Hint:** The coefficients of $z$'s equation are finitely many elements of $\overline A$; the $A$-algebra they generate is finite, and finiteness transits.
>
> **Why needed:** It proves $\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$, so the normalization is already normal.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $z^n + a_1 z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n = 0$ with $a_i \in \overline A$ — each $a_i$ integral over $A$. By Lemma 1 (iterated) the algebra $A' = A[a_1, \dots, a_n]$ is a finite $A$-module. The equation shows $z$ is integral over $A'$, so $A'[z]$ is a finite $A'$-module by the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|criterion]]. By [[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness|transitivity of finiteness]], $A'[z]$ is a finite $A$-module. It is a subring of $B$ containing $A$ and $z$, so by Lemma 2, $z$ is integral over $A$. Hence $z \in \overline A$, proving $\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $A \subseteq B$ and $\overline A = \{b \in B : b \text{ integral over } A\}$.
>
> ---
> **$A \subseteq \overline A$.** Each $a \in A$ satisfies the monic $T - a$, so $a$ is integral over $A$. Thus $\overline A$ contains $A$ (in particular $0, 1 \in \overline A$).
>
> ---
> **Closed under $+, -, \times$.** Let $x, y \in \overline A$. By Lemma 1, $M = A[x, y]$ is a finite $A$-module; it is a subring of $B$ containing $A$. The elements $x + y$, $x - y$, $xy$ all lie in $M$. By Lemma 2, every element of $M$ is integral over $A$, so $x + y, x - y, xy \in \overline A$.
>
> Therefore $\overline A$ is a subring of $B$ containing $A$ — an $A$-subalgebra. $\blacksquare$
>
> ---
> **Corollary (idempotence).** By Lemma 3, any $z \in B$ integral over $\overline A$ is integral over $A$, i.e. $z \in \overline A$. Hence $\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$: the integral closure $\overline A$ is integrally closed in $B$. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**The ring of integers of a number field.** For a number field $K$, the set $\mathcal{O}_K$ of algebraic integers in $K$ is, by this theorem, a *ring* — sums and products of algebraic integers are algebraic integers (Example Sheet 1 Q15(b)). The application is nonobvious because the standard "construct the combined minimal polynomial" approach requires resultants or the tensor-eigenvalue trick $A \otimes I + I \otimes B$, whereas the module argument gives the closure-under-operations instantly. This is the foundational fact of algebraic number theory.

**Symmetric functions of conjugate roots.** If $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer with conjugates $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$, any symmetric polynomial in the $\alpha_i$ with $\mathbb{Z}$-coefficients is an algebraic integer — because the $\alpha_i$ all live in a single finite $\mathbb{Z}$-module (a splitting ring) and the subring is closed. The application is nonobvious because it explains why the coefficients of the minimal polynomial (elementary symmetric functions) are integers, closing a loop in the definition of algebraic integers.

**Closure of constructible / period rings.** In transcendence theory and the theory of periods, one repeatedly needs that a ring of "nice" numbers is closed under arithmetic; the integral-closure-is-a-subring pattern is the template, applied to the appropriate base. The application is nonobvious because it abstracts "the nice numbers form a ring" away from any particular construction.

**Invariant theory — the ring of invariants is integral over a polynomial subring.** When a finite group $G$ acts on $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, the invariant ring $k[x]^G$ has $k[x]$ integral over it (each $x_i$ satisfies $\prod_{g}(T - g \cdot x_i)$, a monic with invariant coefficients), so $k[x]^G$ contains a finitely generated subalgebra over which everything is integral — the seed of Noether's finiteness theorem. The application is nonobvious because the integral closure structure is what bounds the invariants.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|The module-finite criterion]]** — the tool that makes this proof short. The criterion's condition (4) — any element of a faithful finite module is integral — is applied to the module $A[x, y]$ to integralise $x + y$ and $xy$ at once. Without re-characterising integral as module-finite, there is no clean route from two monic equations to a third.

- **[[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness|Transitivity]]** — used twice: once to build $A[x, y]$ as a finite $A$-module by stacking finite steps, once in the idempotence proof to transit finiteness through the coefficient algebra $A'$. This theorem and transitivity are mutually reinforcing; together they make integral closure a genuine closure operator.

- **[[Def - Integral Closure and Normal Domain|Integral closure and normal domain]]** — this theorem is what gives that definition its ring structure. "The integral closure $\overline A$" is only a meaningful subring because of this result; idempotence is what makes "the normalization is normal" true and normalization an idempotent operation.

- **[[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed|A UFD is integrally closed]]** — the complementary direction: this theorem says $\overline A$ is *a* ring between $A$ and $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$; the UFD theorem identifies *when* that ring is $A$ itself (no enlargement). Together they compute closures by the sandwich $A \subseteq \overline A \subseteq A'$ with $A'$ a known UFD.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Normalization as a functor *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Because $\overline A$ is a subring and idempotent, the assignment $A \mapsto \overline A$ is a **closure operator**, and on varieties it becomes the **normalization functor** $X \mapsto \tilde X$ — the universal finite birational map from a normal variety. Idempotence is exactly "the normalization is normal", which is what makes $\tilde X$ unique. Sums-and-products-are-integral is what makes the normalization's coordinate ring a *ring*, not just a set of functions.

> [!tip] The ring of integers and ideal factorization *(from Algebraic Number Theory)*
> This theorem is what allows $\mathcal{O}_K = \overline{\mathbb{Z}}^{\,K}$ to be treated as a ring — the basic object on which unique factorization of ideals (the Dedekind theory) is built. The failure of the naive $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ to be closed under taking integral elements is why one must pass to the full closure; see [[Commutative Algebra XIII — Dedekind Domains and DVRs]].
