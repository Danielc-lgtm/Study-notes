# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. We work with a tower $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$ of subrings. "Finite over $A$" means finitely generated as an $A$-[[Def - Finitely Generated Module|module]]; "[[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|integral over A]]" means every element satisfies a monic polynomial over $A$. For a finite generating set we write $C = A y_1 + \cdots + A y_n$ (module generators). The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (transitivity of finiteness and integrality).** Let $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$ be rings.
>
> 1. **(Finiteness.)** If $C$ is [[Def - Finite and Finite-Type Algebra|finite]] over $B$ and $B$ is finite over $A$, then $C$ is finite over $A$.
> 2. **(Integrality.)** If $C$ is [[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|integral]] over $B$ and $B$ is integral over $A$, then $C$ is integral over $A$.

> **Remark.** Part 1 is the multiplicativity of module-generating sets; part 2 is reduced to part 1 by collecting the finitely many coefficients of an integral equation into a finite intermediate algebra. The same statements with "finite-type" in place of "finite" also hold, by the identical product-of-generators argument.

---

# Motivation

Integrality and finiteness should *compose*, and this theorem says they do. The need is constant. You build a big extension in stages — adjoin one algebraic integer, then another; pass from $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathcal{O}_K$ to $\mathcal{O}_L$ for $K \subseteq L$; stack a finite cover on top of a finite cover — and at each stage you know the *step* is finite or integral. The question is always whether the *composite* is. Without transitivity, every multi-stage construction would have to be re-analysed from the base, and the whole edifice of integral closures, normalizations, and towers of number fields would have no glue.

The finiteness half is the intuitive one, and it is genuine linear algebra. If $C$ is spanned over $B$ by $n$ elements and $B$ is spanned over $A$ by $\ell$ elements, then writing each $B$-coefficient in terms of the $A$-generators shows $C$ is spanned over $A$ by the $n\ell$ *products*. This is the exact analogue of the tower law for vector spaces, $[\,C : A\,] = [\,C : B\,]\,[\,B : A\,]$, and the proof is the same substitution. Finiteness multiplies through a tower.

The integrality half is subtler and is the real content, because integrality is *not* obviously transitive: an integral equation for $c \in C$ has coefficients in $B$, and those coefficients are only *integral* over $A$, not in $A$. You cannot just "plug in". The resolution is the chapter's recurring trick — **an integral equation has only finitely many coefficients**, so collect them into a small intermediate ring $A' = A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$, which (being generated by integral elements) is *finite* over $A$. Now $c$ is integral over $A'$, so $A'[c]$ is finite over $A'$, hence finite over $A$ by the finiteness half, and $c$ — living in a finite $A$-module — is integral over $A$ by the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]]. So integrality transits by *passing through finiteness*: this is why the two halves of the theorem belong together, and why the finiteness half is proved first. The deep point is that finiteness, not integrality, is the composable notion, and integrality borrows composability from it via the finite-coefficient device.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition is a tower with finite/integral steps, but it appears in disguise whenever a property is *known stepwise*.

The first disguised source is **a chain of adjunctions**. The property $B$ is "$C = A[\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m]$ with each $\alpha_i$ integral over $A[\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_{i-1}]$". Each step is integral, so by induction the whole tower is integral over $A$. The bridge is part 2 applied repeatedly. Nonobvious because each $\alpha_i$ is integral only over the *previous* ring, not over $A$ directly. *Example problem:* a repeated radical extension $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2) \subseteq \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt2, \sqrt3)$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ at the level of integers.

The second disguised source is **a finite cover of a finite cover**. The property $B$ is "$C$ finite over $B$, $B$ finite over $A$", as in a composite branched covering of varieties. Part 1 gives $C$ finite over $A$. Nonobvious in that the *rank* multiplies (the composite cover's degree is the product). *Example problem:* the composition of two finite morphisms of schemes is finite.

The third disguised source is **integrality over an integrally-closed-but-larger ring**. The property $B$ is "$c$ integral over $\overline A$", where $\overline A$ is the integral closure of $A$ — itself integral over $A$. Part 2 then collapses this to "$c$ integral over $A$", which is exactly the *idempotence* $\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$. Nonobvious because the intermediate ring $\overline A$ can be much bigger than $A$. *Example problem:* the normalization is already normal ([[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|idempotence corollary]]).

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "the composite tower is finite / integral".

Combine "integrality transits" with **the closure being a subring**. Transitivity is what makes $\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$, so $\overline A$ is integrally closed; combined with [[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|closure-is-a-subring]] this makes $A \mapsto \overline A$ a genuine *closure operator*. The further result $E$ is the well-definedness of normalization. Nonobvious because closure operators must be idempotent, and idempotence *is* transitivity.

Combine "finiteness transits" with **Noether normalization**. Once a finite-type algebra is finite over a polynomial subring (Noether normalization) and you have a finite cover of it, the composite is finite over the polynomial ring — so dimension and finiteness statements propagate up towers. The further result $E$ is preservation of dimension and the going-up theorem's finiteness inputs. Nonobvious because it lets you reduce any finite tower to a finite extension of affine space.

Combine "finiteness transits" with **module-theoretic finiteness theorems**. A finite module over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian; transitivity lets you propagate Noetherian-ness and finite-generation up a tower of finite extensions. The further result $E$ is that integral closures of Noetherian domains (under good hypotheses) stay finite — the finiteness of integral closure theorem. Nonobvious because finiteness of the closure is *not* automatic and relies on transitivity plus separability/excellence.

# Why Is It True

**Finiteness multiplies; integrality borrows that multiplicativity by hiding inside a finite module.** Two sentences, one per part.

Part 1 is substitution. Write $C = B y_1 + \cdots + B y_n$ and $B = A z_1 + \cdots + A z_\ell$. Take any $c \in C$. Then $c = \sum_i b_i y_i$ with $b_i \in B$, and each $b_i = \sum_j a_{ij} z_j$ with $a_{ij} \in A$. Substitute:
$$c = \sum_{i, j} a_{ij}\, z_j y_i.$$
So $c$ is an $A$-combination of the $n\ell$ products $z_j y_i$. Hence those products $A$-span $C$, and $C$ is finite over $A$. This is the vector-space tower law $[C:A] = [C:B][B:A]$ with "dimension" replaced by "module rank", and the proof is the identical double substitution.

Part 2 reduces to part 1. Take $c \in C$. It is integral over $B$, so $c^n + b_1 c^{n-1} + \cdots + b_n = 0$ for some $b_1, \dots, b_n \in B$ — *finitely many*. Each $b_i$ is integral over $A$ (since $B$ is integral over $A$). Form $A' = A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$. Because $A'$ is generated over $A$ by finitely many integral elements, it is a *finite $A$-module* ([[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]] / "finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral"). The equation for $c$ has all its coefficients in $A'$, so $c$ is integral over $A'$, whence $A'[c]$ is finite over $A'$. By part 1 (finite over finite), $A'[c]$ is finite over $A$. Now $c \in A'[c]$, a finite faithful $A$-module stabilised by $c$, so $c$ is integral over $A$ by the criterion.

**The one-line mechanism: an integral equation for $c$ uses only finitely many coefficients from $B$; the subring they generate is a finite $A$-module, so $c$ lands in a finite $A$-module — and a finite module always makes its elements integral.** The whole subtlety — that the coefficients are "only" integral over $A$ — is dissolved by the observation that finitely many integral elements still generate a finite module.

---

# What Makes This Hard

The trap in part 2 is to attempt to substitute the integral equations of the coefficients $b_i$ directly into the equation for $c$, producing an unwieldy mess of nested radicals with no monic structure. The non-obvious move is the *finite-coefficient device*: notice the equation has only finitely many coefficients, bundle them into $A' = A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$, and observe this is a finite $A$-module — then route everything through finiteness, which *does* transit. The most common error is forgetting that $A'$ is finite (not merely finite-type) over $A$ — it is finite precisely because its generators are integral, and this is the one place the module-finite criterion is invoked on the coefficients rather than on $c$.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:**
Prove finiteness by double substitution (the tower law for module ranks). Reduce integrality to finiteness: an integral equation for $c$ has finitely many $B$-coefficients, each integral over $A$; the algebra they generate is finite over $A$, $c$ is integral over it, and finite-over-finite plus the module-finite criterion makes $c$ integral over $A$.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Finiteness transits.** Show $C = \sum_{i,j} A\, z_j y_i$ from $C = \sum_i B y_i$, $B = \sum_j A z_j$.
   - *Hint:* Substitute the $A$-expansion of each $B$-coefficient into the $B$-expansion of a general $c \in C$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the composable notion; integrality will be routed through it.

2. **The finite-coefficient device.** From $c$ integral over $B$, build a finite $A$-algebra $A'$ containing the coefficients.
   - *Hint:* The equation $c^n + b_1 c^{n-1} + \cdots + b_n = 0$ has finitely many $b_i \in B$, each integral over $A$; let $A' = A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$, finite over $A$ because generated by integral elements.
   - *Why needed:* It localises the problem to a finite intermediate ring over which $c$ is integral.

3. **Collapse to integrality over $A$.** Use finite-over-finite and the criterion.
   - *Hint:* $c$ integral over $A'$ $\Rightarrow A'[c]$ finite over $A'$ $\Rightarrow$ (part 1) finite over $A$ $\Rightarrow$ ([[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|criterion]]) $c$ integral over $A$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the conclusion: integrality transits.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Finiteness is multiplicative through a tower
> **Statement:** If $C = B y_1 + \cdots + B y_n$ and $B = A z_1 + \cdots + A z_\ell$, then $C = \sum_{i,j} A\, z_j y_i$, so $C$ is finite over $A$.
>
> **Hint:** Expand a general $c \in C$ first over $B$, then expand each $B$-coefficient over $A$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is part 1, and the engine to which part 2 is reduced.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $c \in C$. Since $C = \sum_i B y_i$, write $c = \sum_{i=1}^n b_i y_i$ with $b_i \in B$. Since $B = \sum_j A z_j$, write each $b_i = \sum_{j=1}^\ell a_{ij} z_j$ with $a_{ij} \in A$. Substituting,
> > $$c = \sum_{i=1}^n \Big( \sum_{j=1}^\ell a_{ij} z_j \Big) y_i = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^\ell a_{ij}\, (z_j y_i).$$
> > Thus $c$ is an $A$-linear combination of the finite set $\{z_j y_i : 1 \leq i \leq n,\ 1 \leq j \leq \ell\}$. As $c$ was arbitrary, these $n\ell$ elements generate $C$ as an $A$-module, so $C$ is finite over $A$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: A finite algebra over $A$ generated by integral elements
> **Statement:** If $b_1, \dots, b_n$ are integral over $A$, then $A' = A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$ is a finite $A$-module.
>
> **Hint:** Each $b_i$ integral $\Rightarrow A[b_1, \dots, b_i]$ finite over $A[b_1, \dots, b_{i-1}]$; stack with Lemma 1.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the finite-coefficient device: it makes the intermediate ring of coefficients finite over $A$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Induct on $n$. For $n = 1$: $b_1$ integral over $A$ gives $A[b_1]$ finite over $A$ by the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]]. Inductively, $A_{n-1} := A[b_1, \dots, b_{n-1}]$ is finite over $A$. Since $b_n$ is integral over $A$, it is integral over the larger ring $A_{n-1}$, so $A_{n-1}[b_n] = A'$ is finite over $A_{n-1}$ by the criterion. By Lemma 1, $A'$ is finite over $A$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Integrality over a finite intermediate ring collapses to the base
> **Statement:** If $A'$ is finite over $A$ and $c$ is integral over $A'$, then $c$ is integral over $A$.
>
> **Hint:** $A'[c]$ is finite over $A'$, hence finite over $A$ (Lemma 1); apply the criterion to $c \in A'[c]$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the final collapse, turning "integral over the coefficient ring" into "integral over $A$".
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Since $c$ is integral over $A'$, the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|criterion]] gives $A'[c]$ finite over $A'$. By Lemma 1, $A'[c]$ is finite over $A$. Now $A'[c]$ is a subring of the ambient ring containing $A$ and $c$, finite over $A$, hence a faithful finite $A$-module stabilised by $c$ ($1 \in A'[c]$, and $c \cdot A'[c] \subseteq A'[c]$). By condition (4) of the criterion, $c$ is integral over $A$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$.
>
> ---
> **Part 1 (finiteness).** Suppose $C$ is finite over $B$ and $B$ finite over $A$. By Lemma 1, with $C = \sum_i B y_i$ and $B = \sum_j A z_j$, the products $z_j y_i$ generate $C$ over $A$, so $C$ is finite over $A$.
>
> ---
> **Part 2 (integrality).** Suppose $C$ is integral over $B$ and $B$ integral over $A$. Let $c \in C$; we show $c$ is integral over $A$. Since $c$ is integral over $B$, there are $b_1, \dots, b_n \in B$ with
> $$c^n + b_1 c^{n-1} + \cdots + b_n = 0.$$
> Each $b_i \in B$ is integral over $A$ (as $B$ is integral over $A$). By Lemma 2, $A' = A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$ is finite over $A$. The displayed equation has coefficients in $A'$, so $c$ is integral over $A'$. By Lemma 3, $c$ is integral over $A$.
>
> Since $c \in C$ was arbitrary, $C$ is integral over $A$. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Tower law for field extensions and degrees.** Specialising part 1 to fields recovers the multiplicativity of degrees $[C : A] = [C : B][B : A]$ — the products of basis elements form a basis. The application is nonobvious because the *same* substitution that proves transitivity of module-finiteness proves the field tower law; integrality transitivity is its non-field generalisation, used to show a tower of algebraic extensions is algebraic.

**Towers of number fields and rings of integers.** For $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq K \subseteq L$, the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_L$ is integral over $\mathcal{O}_K$ which is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$, so $\mathcal{O}_L$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ by part 2 — confirming $\mathcal{O}_L = \overline{\mathbb{Z}}^{\,L}$ is computed in one step regardless of the intermediate field. The application is nonobvious because it shows the ring of integers does not depend on the tower used to build $L$.

**Composite finite morphisms in geometry.** The composition of two finite morphisms of varieties is finite (part 1), and the composition of two integral ring extensions is integral (part 2) — so finite/integral is a *property of morphisms stable under composition*, a prerequisite for these forming a sensible class of maps (needed to define finite morphisms of schemes and to compose branched covers). The application is nonobvious because stability under composition is exactly what the theorem guarantees.

**Idempotence of integral closure (closing twice does nothing).** That $\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$ — central to defining normalization — is precisely part 2 applied to $A \subseteq \overline A \subseteq \overline{\overline A}$: integral-over-integral is integral. The application is nonobvious because it reframes a statement about closures as a statement about a three-step tower.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|The module-finite criterion]]** — invoked three times in part 2: to make the coefficient algebra $A'$ finite, to make $A'[c]$ finite over $A'$, and to conclude $c$ integral from finiteness. The criterion is the dictionary that lets integrality borrow composability from finiteness; transitivity is essentially "finiteness composes, and integrality is finiteness".

- **[[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|The integral closure is a subring (idempotence)]]** — transitivity is exactly what proves idempotence $\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$, since "integral over $\overline A$" is "integral over a ring integral over $A$". The two theorems are used together to establish that integral closure is a *closure operator*.

- **[[Def - Finite and Finite-Type Algebra|Finite and finite-type]]** — part 1 is the multiplicativity of module-generating sets; the analogous statement for algebra-generating sets ("finite-type is transitive") has the identical proof, and the interplay of the two is what governs Noether normalization. The two transitivities — of finite and of finite-type — are the bookkeeping rules of the whole theory.

- **Tower law / multiplicativity of rank** — over a field this is the degree formula $[C:A]=[C:B][B:A]$; over a ring it is finiteness transitivity. The bridge is that "module rank" generalises "vector-space dimension", and the substitution proof is unchanged. This connects integral extensions to the linear algebra of field extensions.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Stability of finite morphisms under composition *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Part 1 is the algebra behind "a composition of finite morphisms is finite" and part 2 behind "a composition of integral ring maps is integral" — the closure of the class of finite/integral morphisms under composition. This stability is a defining requirement for finite morphisms of schemes and underlies the construction of towers of branched covers and the behaviour of dimension under composites; see [[Commutative Algebra VIII — Going Up and Going Down]].

> [!tip] Finiteness of the integral closure *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> Transitivity of finiteness is one input to the deep theorem that the integral closure of a Noetherian domain in a finite separable extension of its fraction field is again a *finite* module — which is why rings of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ are finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-modules (have an integral basis). The composability of finiteness is what lets this be assembled stagewise; the full result needs separability or the excellence hypothesis.
