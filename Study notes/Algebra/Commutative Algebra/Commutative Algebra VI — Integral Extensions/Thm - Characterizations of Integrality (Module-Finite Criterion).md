# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Let $R \subseteq A$ be a subring (or $A$ an $R$-algebra) and $x \in A$. We write $R[x] = \{f(x) : f \in R[T]\}$ for the [[Def - Subring|subring]] generated by $R$ and $x$. An $R$-module $M \subseteq A$ is **faithful as an $R[x]$-module** if for every $0 \neq p \in R[x]$ there is $m \in M$ with $pm \neq 0$ (the structure map $R[x] \to \operatorname{End}_R M$ is injective); when $1_A \in M$ this is automatic, since $p \cdot 1_A = p \neq 0$. "$M$ is finite over $R$" means $M$ is a [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] $R$-module. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (module-finite criterion for integrality).** Let $R \subseteq A$ be rings and $x \in A$. The following are equivalent:
>
> 1. $x$ is [[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|integral over R]] (there is a monic $f \in R[T]$ with $f(x) = 0$).
> 2. $R[x]$ is [[Def - Finite and Finite-Type Algebra|finite]] as an $R$-module.
> 3. $R[x]$ is contained in a subring $C$ of $A$ that is finite as an $R$-module.
> 4. There exists a faithful $R[x]$-submodule $M \subseteq A$ that is finite as an $R$-module (i.e. $M$ is an $R$-submodule, $xM \subseteq M$, and $M$ is faithful over $R[x]$).

> **Corollary (finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral).** A finite-type $R$-algebra $A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is finite as an $R$-module if and only if each $x_i$ is integral over $R$.

---

# Motivation

This theorem is the technical soul of the whole chapter, and its job is to translate. On one side sits the *arithmetic* definition of integrality — "there is a monic equation" — which is exactly what you want for arithmetic intuition but almost useless for *doing* anything, because in practice you never have the monic equation in hand and cannot easily produce one for, say, $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$. On the other side sits a *module-theoretic* condition — "$R[x]$ is a finite module" — which is robust, manipulable, and inherits every good closure property of finitely generated modules. The theorem says these are the same. That equivalence is what makes every later result possible.

The reason you want the module form is that finiteness is *structural* where the monic equation is *individual*. A monic equation is a fact about one element $x$; a finite module is a fact about a whole space, and spaces are closed under sums, products, towers, quotients and localization. Once "integral" becomes "lives in a finite module", the statement "sums and products of integral elements are integral" becomes "sums and products of elements of a finite module are still in a finite module" — nearly a triviality. The hard, equation-juggling problems of the naive definition dissolve into easy module bookkeeping. This is the same move as recognising that "compact" is better used as "every sequence has a convergent subsequence" than as the open-cover definition: the official definition states the concept, the operational characterisation lets you use it.

The bridge between the two sides is the **[[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]]**, the Cayley–Hamilton theorem for finitely generated modules. It says a linear endomorphism of a finite module satisfies a monic polynomial — its characteristic polynomial — and applied to "multiplication by $x$" it manufactures the integral equation out of thin finiteness. So the equivalence is not a coincidence: finiteness *forces* a monic equation by linear algebra, and a monic equation *forces* finiteness by truncating powers. The theorem packages this two-way street, and condition (4) — the existence of *any* faithful finite module stabilised by $x$ — is the most flexible entry point, the one used to prove the integral closure is a subring.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition the later theory needs is (1), "$x$ is integral", but it is most often *established* through one of the module conditions, so the disguised sources are situations that hand you a finite module.

The first disguised source is **a finite extension already in hand**. The property $B$ is "$A$ is a finite $R$-module" (e.g. $A$ is a finite field extension of $k$, or $A = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2]$ is rank-$2$ over $\mathbb{Z}$). Then condition (3)/(4) is met by taking $C = M = A$, so *every* element of $A$ is integral over $R$ — a finite extension is automatically integral. The bridge $B \Rightarrow A$ (here "finite module" $\Rightarrow$ "integral") is condition (3)$\Rightarrow$(1), and it is nonobvious because finiteness of the *whole* algebra instantly integralises *each* element. *Example problem:* show every element of a degree-$n$ field extension $L/k$ is algebraic over $k$ — it lives in the finite module $L$.

The second disguised source is **a generator stabilising a known finite module**. The property $B$ is "$xM \subseteq M$ for some faithful finite $M$" — for instance $x$ a matrix acting on $R^n$, or $x$ multiplication on a finite module appearing in a diagram. By condition (4) this forces $x$ integral, with the monic equation being (a version of) the characteristic polynomial. The nonobviousness is that you need not exhibit the equation; *invariance of a finite module is enough*. *Example problem:* an $R$-algebra endomorphism preserving a finite faithful module is integral over $R$.

The third disguised source is **integrality over an intermediate ring plus finiteness of that ring**. The property $B$ is "$x$ integral over $R'$ where $R'$ is finite over $R$". Then $R'[x]$ is finite over $R'$ (condition (2) there), and finite-over-finite is finite ([[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness|transitivity]]), so $R[x] \subseteq R'[x]$ sits in a finite $R$-module, giving (3)$\Rightarrow$(1): $x$ is integral over $R$. The nonobviousness: integrality *transits* through finite intermediate rings, which is the engine of the transitivity theorem. *Example problem:* $c$ integral over $B$ in a tower $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$ is integral over $A$ once you collect the finitely many coefficients into a finite $A$-algebra.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$x$ integral / $R[x]$ finite". The amplifications combine this across operations.

Combine "$x, y$ integral" with **the bilinear structure of $A[x, y]$**. Each of $R[x]$, $R[x][y]$ is finite over the previous, so $A[x, y]$ is a finite $R$-module by transitivity. Then *every* element of $A[x, y]$ — including $x + y$, $xy$, $x - y$ — is integral by condition (4). The further result $E$ is [[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|the integral closure is a subring]], obtained with *no* equation for the combinations. This is nonobvious because there is no formula combining the monic equations of $x$ and $y$.

Combine "$A$ finite-type $+$ generators integral" with **the bounded-monomial spanning set**. If $A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ with each $x_i$ integral of degree $d_i$, the monomials $x_1^{e_1} \cdots x_n^{e_n}$ ($e_i < d_i$) span $A$ as a module — finitely many. The further result $E$ is the corollary **finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral**, the identity that upgrades algebras to modules and powers Noether normalization. Nonobvious because each generator's monic equation contributes a *bound*, and the bounds multiply to a finite spanning set.

Combine "$x$ integral over $R$" with **a quotient or localization**. Reducing the monic equation mod an ideal, or dividing it by $s^n$, keeps it monic; so integrality passes to $A/\mathfrak q$ over $R/(\mathfrak q \cap R)$ and to $S^{-1}A$ over $S^{-1}R$. The further result $E$ is that integral extensions are stable under quotient and localization ([[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|the basis of the field criterion]] and of locality of normality). Nonobvious because the *same* equation, mechanically transformed, certifies integrality in the new ring.

# Why Is It True

Strip away the four conditions and there is one idea: **a monic equation of degree $n$ is precisely a recipe for rewriting $x^n$ — and hence every higher power — in terms of $1, x, \dots, x^{n-1}$, which is precisely what it means for those $n$ powers to span $R[x]$ as a module.** The arithmetic statement and the module statement are two readings of the same sentence.

Go forward, (1)$\Rightarrow$(2). The monic relation $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n = 0$ rearranges to $x^n = -(r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n)$. So $x^n$ is an $R$-combination of lower powers; multiply by $x$ and substitute repeatedly to see $x^{n+1}, x^{n+2}, \dots$ are too. Hence $R[x] = R + Rx + \cdots + Rx^{n-1}$, a finite module. The monic-ness is essential: a non-monic relation $r_0 x^n + \cdots = 0$ with $r_0$ a non-unit gives $r_0 x^n = -(\cdots)$, which expresses $r_0 x^n$ but *not* $x^n$ in lower powers — the powers do not truncate, and $R[x]$ stays infinite. (This is exactly why $\tfrac12$ and $t^{-1}$ fail.)

Go backward, (4)$\Rightarrow$(1) — the determinant trick. Suppose $x$ stabilises a faithful finite module $M = Rm_1 + \cdots + Rm_n$, so $xm_i = \sum_j a_{ij} m_j$ for a matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ over $R$. In matrix language, $(x I - A)$ annihilates the column vector $(m_1, \dots, m_n)^\top$. Multiply by the adjugate $\operatorname{adj}(x I - A)$: by Cramer's identity $\operatorname{adj}(xI - A)(xI - A) = \det(xI - A) \cdot I$, so $\det(x I - A) \cdot m_i = 0$ for every $i$. Thus the element $p = \det(x I - A) \in R[x]$ kills all of $M$; faithfulness forces $p = 0$. But $\det(xI - A) = x^n + (\text{lower})$ is *monic* of degree $n$ in $x$ — it is the characteristic polynomial. So $x$ satisfies a monic equation: $x$ is integral.

**The whole theorem in one sentence: a monic equation is the arithmetic face of a finite spanning set, and the determinant trick is the linear algebra that turns one into the other — a finite module's "multiply by $x$" operator satisfies its own characteristic polynomial, and that polynomial is monic.**

The chain closes trivially: (2)$\Rightarrow$(3) take $C = R[x]$; (3)$\Rightarrow$(4) take $M = C$, which is faithful because $1 \in C$; (4)$\Rightarrow$(1) is the determinant trick. So all four are equivalent, with the only real content in the forward truncation and the backward determinant.

---

# What Makes This Hard

The non-obvious step is the determinant trick in (4)$\Rightarrow$(1): one must think to write "multiplication by $x$" as a *matrix* over $R$ and then apply the adjugate identity to extract a monic polynomial — the leap from "module is finite" to "here is an explicit monic equation" is where people stall. The most common error is to mishandle faithfulness: the determinant $p = \det(xI - A)$ annihilates $M$, but to conclude $p = 0$ (the actual integral equation, not merely "$p$ kills $M$") you *need* $M$ faithful over $R[x]$, and forgetting this gives a vacuous conclusion. The second common error is to believe a non-monic equation also gives finiteness — it does not, and seeing exactly why ($x^n$ versus $r_0 x^n$) is the crux of the forward direction.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:**
Prove (1)$\Rightarrow$(2) by truncating powers using the monic relation. Close (2)$\Rightarrow$(3)$\Rightarrow$(4) by taking the obvious modules ($R[x]$ itself, which is faithful because it contains $1$). Prove (4)$\Rightarrow$(1) by the determinant trick: express "multiply by $x$" as a matrix $A$ over $R$ on a finite generating set, apply the adjugate to get $\det(xI - A) \cdot M = 0$, and use faithfulness to conclude $\det(xI - A) = 0$ — a monic equation.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **(1)$\Rightarrow$(2): a monic equation makes $R[x]$ finite.** Show $R[x] = R + Rx + \cdots + Rx^{n-1}$.
   - *Hint:* Rearrange $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots = 0$ to express $x^n$ in lower powers; induct to handle $x^{n+k}$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the forward truncation, the easy half, and shows where monic-ness is used.

2. **(2)$\Rightarrow$(3)$\Rightarrow$(4): produce a faithful finite module.** Take $C = R[x]$, then $M = C$.
   - *Hint:* $R[x]$ contains $1_A$, so it is faithful over itself ($p \cdot 1 = p$), and $xR[x] \subseteq R[x]$.
   - *Why needed:* It funnels the strong-looking condition (1) into the flexible condition (4) used in proofs of later theorems.

3. **(4)$\Rightarrow$(1): the determinant trick.** From $xM \subseteq M$ with $M = \sum R m_i$ finite and faithful, extract a monic equation.
   - *Hint:* Write $xm_i = \sum_j a_{ij} m_j$; then $(xI - A)\mathbf m = 0$; multiply by $\operatorname{adj}(xI - A)$ to get $\det(xI - A) m_i = 0$; faithfulness gives $\det(xI - A) = 0$, which is monic in $x$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the entire content of the reverse direction and the source of the manufactured monic equation.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: A monic relation truncates the powers of $x$
> **Statement:** If $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n = 0$ with $r_i \in R$, then $R[x] = R + Rx + \cdots + Rx^{n-1}$, a finite $R$-module.
>
> **Hint:** Express $x^n$ as an $R$-combination of $1, x, \dots, x^{n-1}$ and induct upward on the exponent.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the forward direction (1)$\Rightarrow$(2) and pinpoints why the leading coefficient must be $1$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $N = R + Rx + \cdots + Rx^{n-1} \subseteq R[x]$. Clearly $N \subseteq R[x]$. For the reverse, it suffices to show $x^k \in N$ for all $k \geq 0$, since $R[x]$ is $R$-spanned by the powers of $x$. For $k < n$ this is immediate. For $k = n$: the relation gives $x^n = -(r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n) \in N$. Inductively, suppose $x^{n}, \dots, x^{n+j-1} \in N$. Then $x^{n+j} = x \cdot x^{n+j-1}$, and $x^{n+j-1} \in N$ is an $R$-combination of $1, x, \dots, x^{n-1}$; multiplying by $x$ gives an $R$-combination of $x, \dots, x^n$, and $x^n \in N$ by the base case, so $x^{n+j} \in N$. Hence every power lies in $N$, so $R[x] = N$, generated by the $n$ elements $1, x, \dots, x^{n-1}$. (The step "$x^n \in N$" is exactly where monic-ness is used: a non-monic relation $r_0 x^n + \cdots = 0$ would only give $r_0 x^n \in N$, not $x^n \in N$, when $r_0$ is not a unit.)

> [!note]- Lemma 2: $R[x]$ is a faithful finite module over itself
> **Statement:** If $R[x]$ is finite over $R$, then $M = R[x]$ is a faithful $R[x]$-module, finite over $R$, with $xM \subseteq M$.
>
> **Hint:** $R[x]$ contains $1_A$; multiplication by a non-zero $p \in R[x]$ sends $1_A$ to $p \neq 0$.
>
> **Why needed:** It bridges (2)$\Rightarrow$(4), supplying the faithful finite module the determinant trick consumes, and is the form used to prove later theorems (where one supplies a *different* faithful module).
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > $M = R[x]$ is an $R$-submodule of $A$, finite over $R$ by hypothesis, and $x M = x R[x] \subseteq R[x] = M$ since $R[x]$ is a ring. Faithfulness over $R[x]$: take $0 \neq p \in R[x]$. Then $p \cdot 1_A = p \neq 0$, and $1_A \in R[x] = M$, so $p$ does not annihilate $M$. Hence the structure map $R[x] \to \operatorname{End}_R M$ is injective, i.e. $M$ is faithful.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The determinant trick produces a monic equation
> **Statement:** If $M \subseteq A$ is a faithful $R[x]$-module, finite over $R$, with $xM \subseteq M$, then $x$ satisfies a monic polynomial over $R$.
>
> **Hint:** Write multiplication by $x$ as a matrix on a finite generating set; apply the adjugate identity; use faithfulness to kill the determinant.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the reverse direction (4)$\Rightarrow$(1) and the entire source of the manufactured integral equation.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $M = R m_1 + \cdots + R m_n$. Since $xM \subseteq M$, write $x m_i = \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} m_j$ with $a_{ij} \in R$; let $A = (a_{ij}) \in M_n(R)$. In matrix form, with $\mathbf m = (m_1, \dots, m_n)^\top$,
> > $$(x I_n - A)\, \mathbf m = \mathbf 0.$$
> > Multiply on the left by the adjugate matrix $\operatorname{adj}(xI_n - A)$ (entries in $R[x]$). The adjugate identity $\operatorname{adj}(B) \, B = \det(B)\, I_n$ gives $\det(xI_n - A)\, \mathbf m = \mathbf 0$, i.e. $\det(xI_n - A)\, m_i = 0$ for every $i$. Set $p := \det(x I_n - A) \in R[x]$. Then $p \, m_i = 0$ for all $i$, so $p$ annihilates all of $M = \sum R m_i$. By faithfulness of $M$ over $R[x]$, $p = 0$. Finally, expanding the determinant, $\det(xI_n - A) = x^n + c_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + c_n$ with $c_i \in R$ (it is monic of degree $n$, the characteristic polynomial of $A$). Hence $x^n + c_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + c_n = 0$: $x$ is integral over $R$. This is the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|Cayley–Hamilton/determinant trick]] specialised to $\mathfrak a = R$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R \subseteq A$ and $x \in A$. We prove (1)$\Rightarrow$(2)$\Rightarrow$(3)$\Rightarrow$(4)$\Rightarrow$(1).
>
> ---
> **(1)$\Rightarrow$(2).** By Lemma 1, a monic equation $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n = 0$ gives $R[x] = R + Rx + \cdots + Rx^{n-1}$, finite over $R$.
>
> ---
> **(2)$\Rightarrow$(3).** Take $C = R[x]$: it is a subring of $A$ containing $R[x]$, finite over $R$ by hypothesis.
>
> ---
> **(3)$\Rightarrow$(4).** Let $C \supseteq R[x]$ be a subring finite over $R$. Take $M = C$. It is an $R$-submodule, finite over $R$; $x M = xC \subseteq C = M$ since $C$ is a ring containing $x$; and $M$ is faithful over $R[x]$ because $1_A \in C = M$, so for $0 \neq p \in R[x]$, $p \cdot 1_A = p \neq 0$. (When $C = R[x]$ this is Lemma 2.)
>
> ---
> **(4)$\Rightarrow$(1).** This is Lemma 3: a faithful finite $R[x]$-module $M$ with $xM \subseteq M$ forces $\det(xI - A) = 0$ for the matrix $A$ of multiplication-by-$x$, a monic equation for $x$.
>
> Hence all four conditions are equivalent. $\blacksquare$
>
> ---
> **Corollary (finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral).** Let $A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$.
>
> *($\Leftarrow$)* Suppose each $x_i$ is integral of degree $d_i$. By Lemma 1 each power $x_i^{k}$ ($k \geq d_i$) is an $R$-combination of lower powers of $x_i$. Hence every monomial $x_1^{e_1} \cdots x_n^{e_n}$ reduces to one with $e_i < d_i$, and the finitely many such bounded monomials $R$-span $A$. So $A$ is finite over $R$.
>
> *($\Rightarrow$)* If $A$ is finite over $R$, then each $x_i \in A$ lies in the faithful finite module $A$ (faithful since $1 \in A$), with $x_i A \subseteq A$; by (4)$\Rightarrow$(1) each $x_i$ is integral over $R$. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Matrices and the Cayley–Hamilton theorem proper.** Over a field $k$, an $n \times n$ matrix $T$ acts on the finite module $k^n$, which it stabilises; condition (4) then says $T$ is "integral over $k$", and the manufactured monic equation is *exactly the characteristic polynomial* — so this theorem *contains* the classical Cayley–Hamilton theorem as the case $R = k$, $M = k^n$. Recognising the determinant trick as Cayley–Hamilton-for-modules is the nonobvious unification, and it shows why "integral $=$ satisfies its characteristic polynomial".

**Algebraic numbers as eigenvalues, the companion-matrix trick.** To show $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$ is an algebraic integer (Example Sheet 1 Q15(b)), realise it as multiplication on the rank-$4$ $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt2, \sqrt3]$; condition (4) gives a monic degree-$4$ equation with no need to compute it by hand. The application is nonobvious because the *finite module*, not a clever algebraic identity, is what produces the equation — and the matrix's characteristic polynomial is the minimal polynomial of the algebraic integer.

**Lie theory and integral weights.** In the representation theory of semisimple Lie algebras, "integral" weights are those on which a certain lattice acts finitely; the same finite-module-forces-integrality logic underlies why weight lattices are discrete. The application is nonobvious because the word "integral" there is secretly the same module-finiteness condition, transported to a weight space.

**Dynamical systems and algebraic stability.** An algebraic integer of absolute value $1$ all of whose conjugates also have absolute value $1$ is a root of unity (Kronecker); the proof uses that the powers of the number lie in a *fixed* finite $\mathbb{Z}$-module (its ring of integers), so only finitely many conjugate-tuples occur, forcing periodicity. The application is nonobvious because "the orbit stays in a finite module" is condition (4) doing dynamical work.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|Cayley–Hamilton for modules / the determinant trick]]** — this theorem *is* the determinant trick wearing the costume of integrality. The trick says a finite-module endomorphism $\phi$ with $\phi(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$ satisfies a monic polynomial with coefficients in $\mathfrak a$; taking $\phi$ to be "multiply by $x$" and $\mathfrak a = R$ gives exactly condition (4)$\Rightarrow$(1). The very same lemma with $\mathfrak a$ a proper ideal gives [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama's lemma]] and the §8 integrality-over-an-ideal theory — so this page and Nakayama are two specialisations of one linear-algebra fact.

- **[[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|The integral closure is a subring]]** — the immediate payoff. Because integrality is module-finiteness, two integral elements $x, y$ both live in the finite module $R[x, y]$, and condition (4) integralises *every* element of that module, including $x \pm y$ and $xy$. Without this criterion, closure under $+, \times$ would require combining monic equations, which has no general formula.

- **[[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness|Transitivity]]** — finiteness of modules is multiplicative through a tower, so condition (2) composes: $c$ integral over $B$ and $B$ integral over $A$ gives $c$ integral over $A$ by collecting the finitely many coefficients of $c$'s equation into a finite $A$-algebra. The criterion is what lets integrality "transit" by reducing it to finiteness.

- **The corollary finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral** — the form used in [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz|Noether normalization]], where one upgrades a finite-type algebra to a finite module by arranging that its generators become integral over a polynomial subring. This corollary is the precise statement of what normalization buys.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Noether normalization *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> The corollary "finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral" is the lever behind **Noether normalization**: every finite-type domain over a field is a *finite module* over a polynomial subring, because one can choose coordinates making all generators integral. This is the structural backbone of the **Nullstellensatz** and of dimension theory; see [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

> [!tip] Finite morphisms and proper maps *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Condition (2) — $R[x]$ a finite module — is the local model of a **finite morphism** of schemes: over each point the fibre algebra is finite-dimensional, so fibres are finite. The determinant trick is the algebra behind "finite morphisms are proper", and the manufactured monic equation is what forbids points from escaping to infinity. This is the dividing line between finite covers and open immersions.
