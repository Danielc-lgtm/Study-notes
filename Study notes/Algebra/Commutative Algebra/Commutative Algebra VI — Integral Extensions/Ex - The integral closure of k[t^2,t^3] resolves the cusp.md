# Problem Statement

Let $k$ be a field and $A = k[t^2, t^3] \subseteq k[t]$ the subring generated by $t^2$ and $t^3$ — the coordinate ring of the **cuspidal cubic** $y^2 = x^3$ under $x \mapsto t^2$, $y \mapsto t^3$. Prove that:

1. $\operatorname{Frac}(A) = k(t)$, so $t \in \operatorname{Frac}(A)$;
2. $t$ is integral over $A$ but $t \notin A$, so $A$ is **not** integrally closed (not normal);
3. the integral closure is $\overline A = k[t]$.

Interpret the result as the **normalization** resolving the cusp singularity.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are integral elements, the integral closure, the field of fractions, and normality.

![[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension#The Definition]]

![[Def - Integral Closure and Normal Domain#The Definition]]

$A = k[t^2, t^3]$ consists of the $k$-polynomials in $t$ with **no linear term**: spans of $1, t^2, t^3, t^4, \dots$ — every power of $t$ except $t^1$. The field of fractions is $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$, the smallest field containing $A$.

![[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed#Statement]]

![[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring#Statement]]

$k[t]$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] (Euclidean, via polynomial degree), hence a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|UFD]], hence normal.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is the *geometric normalization* problem — computing the integral closure of a singular curve's coordinate ring and recognising it as the smooth model. It is the algebraic-geometry showcase of the chapter and the prototype for "normalization resolves a cusp". It exercises operation 5 (the sandwich) and operation 1 (exhibit a monic equation) from the [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions#Legal Operations|topic page]].

**Assumption pattern.** The leverable structure: $A$ is "$k[t]$ with the linear term deleted", so the only element missing to reach $k[t]$ is $t$ itself — and $t = t^3 / t^2 \in \operatorname{Frac}(A)$ is exactly a ratio of generators. The trigger is recognising that the "missing" element $t$ satisfies a monic equation because $t^2 \in A$, namely $T^2 - t^2 = 0$.

**Theorem routing.** Three moves mirroring [[Ex - Z[sqrt 5] is not integrally closed]]. (i) $\operatorname{Frac}(A) = k(t)$: since $t = t^3/t^2 \in \operatorname{Frac}(A)$, the fraction field contains $t$ and $k$, hence all of $k(t)$; the reverse is clear. (ii) $t$ integral over $A$ via the monic $T^2 - t^2$ (with $t^2 \in A$), but $t \notin A$ (it is the deleted linear term) — proving non-normality. (iii) $\overline A = k[t]$ by the sandwich: $k[t] = A[t]$ is integral over $A$ (so $\subseteq \overline A$), and $k[t]$ is a PID hence normal (so caps $\overline A$ from above by [[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed|UFD ⇒ integrally closed]]).

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious recognition is that $t = t^3/t^2$ is a *ratio of two elements of $A$* lying in the fraction field, and that it satisfies the monic $T^2 - t^2$ precisely because its square $t^2$ is back in $A$. The geometric reading — that adjoining $t$ unfolds the cusp into the smooth line, with the map $t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$ being the normalization — is the payoff that makes this more than a computation.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Prove integrality by exhibiting a monic equation (operation 1).** $t$ satisfies $T^2 - t^2 = 0$ with $t^2 \in A$.

2. **Compute a closure by the sandwich $A \subseteq A' \subseteq \operatorname{Frac}(A)$ (operation 5).** Take $A' = k[t]$; show it integral over $A$ and already normal.

3. **Cap a closure with a normal (UFD) ring (operation 4).** $k[t]$ is a PID, hence a UFD, hence integrally closed, so $\overline A \subseteq k[t]$.

4. **Membership test by degree/coefficient structure.** $t \notin A = k[t^2, t^3]$ because $A$ has no linear term.

# Solution

The plan: identify $A$ as "$k[t]$ minus the linear term", so the missing element $t = t^3/t^2$ lies in the fraction field and satisfies the monic $T^2 - t^2$ — proving non-normality at once. Then run the sandwich to show the closure is exactly $k[t]$, the smooth line, and read off the geometry: adjoining $t$ resolves the cusp.

**Step 1: $\operatorname{Frac}(A) = k(t)$.**

Since $t = t^3 / t^2$ with $t^2, t^3 \in A$, the fraction field of $A$ contains $t$, hence equals $k(t)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> As a $k$-vector space, $A = k[t^2, t^3]$ is spanned by all monomials $t^i$ with $i$ a non-negative integer expressible as $2a + 3b$ ($a, b \geq 0$) — that is, $i \in \{0, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots\}$, every non-negative integer *except* $1$. So $A = k \oplus k t^2 \oplus k t^3 \oplus k t^4 \oplus \cdots$, missing only the linear term $t$.
>
> Now $t^2 \in A$ and $t^3 \in A$ with $t^2 \neq 0$, so the ratio $t = t^3 / t^2$ lies in $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$. Hence $\operatorname{Frac}(A) \supseteq k(t)$ (it contains $k$ and $t$, so all rational functions in $t$). Conversely $A \subseteq k[t]$, so $\operatorname{Frac}(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Frac}(k[t]) = k(t)$. Therefore $\operatorname{Frac}(A) = k(t)$.

**Step 2: $t$ is integral over $A$ but $t \notin A$.**

$t$ satisfies the monic $T^2 - t^2 = 0$ (coefficient $t^2 \in A$), yet $t \notin A$ since $A$ has no linear term. So $A$ is not normal.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Integral:* $t$ is a root of $f(T) = T^2 - t^2 \in A[T]$, which is [[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|monic]] with constant term $-t^2 \in A$. Indeed $f(t) = t^2 - t^2 = 0$. So $t$ is integral over $A$.
>
> *Not in $A$:* From Step 1, $A = k \oplus kt^2 \oplus kt^3 \oplus \cdots$ contains no nonzero multiple of $t^1$. If $t \in A$, then $t$ would be a $k$-combination of $\{1, t^2, t^3, \dots\}$; but $1, t, t^2, \dots$ are linearly independent over $k$ in $k[t]$, so the coefficient of $t$ would have to be both $1$ and $0$ — contradiction. Hence $t \notin A$.
>
> Therefore $t \in \operatorname{Frac}(A) \setminus A$ is integral over $A$: by definition $A$ is **not** [[Def - Integral Closure and Normal Domain|integrally closed]] in $\operatorname{Frac}(A) = k(t)$.

**Step 3: $\overline A = k[t]$.**

$k[t] = A[t]$ is integral over $A$ (so $\subseteq \overline A$) and is itself normal (so $\supseteq \overline A$).

> [!note]- Derivation
> *$k[t] \subseteq \overline A$.* First, $A[t] = k[t]$: $A$ contains $t^2, t^3$, and adjoining $t$ recovers all powers of $t$ (in particular the missing $t^1$), so $A[t] = k[t]$. The generator $t$ is integral over $A$ (Step 2), so by [[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|the integral closure being a subring]], every element of $A[t] = k[t]$ is integral over $A$. Hence $k[t] \subseteq \overline A$.
>
> *$\overline A \subseteq k[t]$.* The ring $k[t]$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]] (it is Euclidean for the degree function), hence a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|UFD]], hence integrally closed in $\operatorname{Frac}(k[t]) = k(t)$ by [[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed|"a UFD is integrally closed"]]. Now $\overline A$ consists of elements of $k(t)$ integral over $A$; such an element is *a fortiori* integral over the larger ring $k[t] \supseteq A$, hence lies in the integrally closed ring $k[t]$. So $\overline A \subseteq k[t]$.
>
> Combining, $\overline A = k[t]$.

**Step 4: Geometric interpretation — normalization resolves the cusp.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> The inclusion $A = k[t^2, t^3] \hookrightarrow k[t] = \overline A$ corresponds to a map of varieties $\mathbb{A}^1 = \operatorname{Spec} k[t] \to \operatorname{Spec} A = \{y^2 = x^3\}$, namely $t \mapsto (x, y) = (t^2, t^3)$. This is the **normalization** of the cusp:
> - it is a *finite* (hence proper, finite-fibre) map, because $k[t]$ is integral over $A$;
> - it is *birational* — an isomorphism on fraction fields ($\operatorname{Frac} = k(t)$ on both sides) and an isomorphism away from the singular point $t = 0$;
> - it *separates* nothing here (the cusp is unibranch) but *unfolds* the singularity: the cusp's coordinate ring was missing the function $t = y/x$, and the normalization adjoins exactly it, replacing the cusped curve by the smooth affine line $\mathbb{A}^1$.
>
> Non-normality of $A$ is the algebraic signature of the cusp singularity; the integral closure $k[t]$ is the smooth model. This is the universal pattern: **normalization $=$ integral closure $=$ resolution of singularities in codimension one** (for curves, full resolution).

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $A = k[t^2, t^3]$ is not normal; its integral closure is $k[t]$, the normalization of the cusp $y^2 = x^3$.
>
> As a $k$-space, $A = k \oplus kt^2 \oplus kt^3 \oplus \cdots$, all powers of $t$ but $t^1$. Then $t = t^3/t^2 \in \operatorname{Frac}(A)$, so $\operatorname{Frac}(A) = k(t)$. The element $t$ satisfies the monic $T^2 - t^2 = 0$ over $A$ ($t^2 \in A$), so $t$ is integral over $A$; but $t \notin A$ (no linear term, and $1, t, t^2, \dots$ are $k$-independent). Hence $A$ is not integrally closed.
>
> Closure: $A[t] = k[t]$ with $t$ integral over $A$, so $k[t] \subseteq \overline A$ by [[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|closure-is-a-subring]]. Conversely $k[t]$ is a PID, hence a UFD, hence integrally closed ([[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed]]); any element of $k(t)$ integral over $A$ is integral over $k[t]$, hence in $k[t]$, so $\overline A \subseteq k[t]$. Thus $\overline A = k[t]$.
>
> Geometrically, $\operatorname{Spec} k[t] \to \operatorname{Spec} A$, $t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$, is the finite birational normalization unfolding the cusp into the smooth line $\mathbb{A}^1$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Non-normality always points to a singularity, and the integral closure is the smooth model.** The cusp is the cleanest example of the chapter's central geometric dictionary: a coordinate ring that fails to be integrally closed is exactly the coordinate ring of a *singular* variety, and the integral closure is the coordinate ring of its *normalization* — for curves, the smooth model. The missing integral element ($t = y/x$ here) is the function the singular curve "should have had": it is regular on the smooth curve but not on the singular one, where $x$ vanishes to higher order than $y$. When you compute an integral closure and find it strictly larger, you have found and resolved a singularity. The trigger "subring generated by some but not all monomials" (like $k[t^2, t^3]$, $k[t^3, t^4, t^5]$, numerical-semigroup rings) should immediately suggest "non-normal, with the missing monomials in the closure".

**The sandwich method computes every normalization the same way: build from below, cap with a UFD from above.** Exactly as in [[Ex - Z[sqrt 5] is not integrally closed]], the closure is pinned by two inequalities. From below, $A[\text{integral elements}] \subseteq \overline A$ by [[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|closure-is-a-subring]] — here adjoining the single integral element $t$ already gives $k[t]$. From above, if the candidate is a UFD (here $k[t]$, a PID), it is integrally closed, so it *caps* the closure: $\overline A \subseteq k[t]$. The two coincide. The reliability of this method comes from [[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed|"a UFD is integrally closed"]] being the universal capping certificate — whenever you can present a plausible closure as a polynomial ring, PID, or other UFD, you have proved the closure is no bigger. Master this two-sided squeeze and you can compute any normalization that has a UFD answer.

**"Ratio of generators that satisfies a monic" is the recognition pattern for a missing integral element.** The element that breaks normality here, $t = t^3/t^2$, is a *ratio of two elements of $A$* that happens to satisfy a monic equation over $A$ — because its square $t^2$ lands back in $A$. This is the general shape of a missing integral element: look for fractions $p/q$ (with $p, q \in A$) whose powers re-enter $A$, giving a monic relation. For $k[t^2, t^3]$ the fraction $t^3/t^2$ squares to $t^2 \in A$; for $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$ the element $\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$ satisfies a quadratic with integer coefficients. The diagnostic — "is there a fraction of ring elements whose minimal monic relation has coefficients back in the ring?" — is how you *find* the elements to adjoin when computing a normalization, rather than guessing blindly. Geometrically, such a fraction is a rational function that is secretly regular on the normalization.
