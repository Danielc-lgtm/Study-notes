# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Let $R \subseteq A$ be a subring, or more generally let $A$ be an $R$-algebra with structure map $\rho : R \to A$; we treat $A$ as both an $R$-algebra and an $R$-module. For $x \in A$ we write $R[x] = \{f(x) : f \in R[T]\}$ for the [[Def - Subring|subring]] of $A$ generated by $R$ and $x$ — the image of the evaluation map $R[T] \to A$, $T \mapsto x$. A polynomial $f = T^n + r_1 T^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n \in R[T]$ is **monic** if its leading coefficient is $1$; an **equation of integral dependence** for $x$ is a monic relation $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n = 0$ with $r_i \in R$. We write $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ for the field of fractions of a domain. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]].

This is a compound page: it defines two interlocking notions — an element being **integral over $R$**, and the algebra $A$ being an **integral extension** of $R$ — because the second is the global form of the first and neither is fully usable without the other.

# Axiom Motivation

The goal is to find the right notion of "whole number" inside an arbitrary ring extension. You already own the prototype: inside $\mathbb{Q}$, the subring $\mathbb{Z}$ consists of the "integers", and the [[Def - Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial|algebraic integers]] inside $\mathbb{C}$ are the roots of *monic* integer polynomials. The question this page answers is: *given a subring $R \subseteq A$, which elements of $A$ deserve to be called "integral over $R$"?* — and the answer should generalise "algebraic integer" while specialising, when $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and $A = \mathbb{C}$, back to it.

**Why "algebraic" is too weak.** The first guess is: $x$ should be *algebraic* over $R$, meaning it satisfies *some* non-zero polynomial $f \in R[T]$. But this guess is hopeless as a notion of "integer", and the reason is instructive. Consider $R = \mathbb{Z}$, $A = \mathbb{Q}$. *Every* rational $\tfrac ab$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Z}$ — it is the root of $bT - a$. So "algebraic over $\mathbb{Z}$" would declare *all* of $\mathbb{Q}$ to be integers, which is absurd: $\tfrac12$ is not a whole number. The notion of algebraicity simply does not see the difference between $\tfrac12$ and $7$, because both satisfy *a* polynomial relation. Whatever "integral" means, it must be strictly stronger than algebraic, and it must reject $\tfrac12$ while keeping $7$.

**Why monic is exactly the right strengthening.** Look at what separates the two relations. The whole number $7$ satisfies the *monic* relation $T - 7 = 0$; the fraction $\tfrac12$ satisfies only $2T - 1 = 0$, whose leading coefficient is $2 \neq 1$. The difference is the *leading coefficient*, and the leading coefficient is precisely the "denominator you would have to divide by". To turn $2T - 1 = 0$ into a relation with leading coefficient $1$, you would divide by $2$ — and dividing by $2$ is illegal in $\mathbb{Z}$, because $2$ is not a unit. So **demanding a monic equation is demanding that no denominator be hidden in the leading coefficient.** This is exactly the constraint that distinguishes integers from fractions, and it generalises perfectly: define $x$ to be **integral over $R$** if it satisfies a *monic* polynomial over $R$. Now $\tfrac12$ fails (no monic integer polynomial has $\tfrac12$ as a root — by the rational root theorem, a monic integer polynomial's rational roots are integers) and $7$ succeeds, as desired. The definition was not invented; it was forced by the demand "be a notion of integer, not a notion of fraction".

**Why the leading coefficient $1$ and not some other unit.** One might worry that fixing the leading coefficient to be exactly $1$, rather than allowing any unit, is arbitrary. It is not, and it is harmless. If $u x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots = 0$ with $u$ a *unit* of $R$, multiply through by $u^{-1}$ to get a genuinely monic relation; so "leading coefficient a unit" is the same as "monic". The content of integrality is therefore entirely about whether the leading coefficient *can be made* $1$ — equivalently, whether it is a unit — and over a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]], where every non-zero element is a unit, *every* algebraic element is integral. This is the single most important boundary case: **over a field, integral and algebraic coincide**, because the only obstruction (a non-unit leading coefficient) cannot occur. Integrality only becomes a strictly stronger condition than algebraicity when $R$ has non-units to obstruct the monic-ing.

**Why the global notion (integral extension) is worth naming separately.** Having defined when a *single element* is integral, we package the condition "*every* element of $A$ is integral over $R$" as "$A$ is an integral extension of $R$". This is worth a name because it is exactly the hypothesis under which the geometry becomes tame: an integral extension corresponds to a **finite map** of spaces, with finite fibres and no escape to infinity, and it is the condition that makes the going-up theorem, the Nullstellensatz, and dimension theory run. A single integral element is a local statement about one function; an integral *extension* is a global statement about a *map*, and the chapter's main theorems are about extensions, not elements. The forward-reference test for the definition is the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]]: with the monic definition, "$x$ integral" is equivalent to "$R[x]$ is a finite $R$-module", and *that* equivalence — the technical soul of the subject — would simply be false for "algebraic" (a non-monic relation does not truncate the powers of $x$, so $R[x]$ need not be finite). The monic definition is the unique one that makes integrality and module-finiteness the same condition.

# The Definition

Let $R \subseteq A$ be commutative rings (or let $A$ be an $R$-algebra via $\rho : R \to A$, with $R$ identified with its image).

**Integral element.** An element $x \in A$ is **integral over $R$** (or **$R$-integral**) if there is a **monic** polynomial $f \in R[T]$ with $f(x) = 0$; that is, there exist $n \geq 1$ and $r_1, \dots, r_n \in R$ with
$$x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_{n-1} x + r_n = 0.$$
Such a relation is called an **equation of integral dependence** for $x$ over $R$.

**Comparison with algebraicity.** Recall $x$ is **algebraic over $R$** if there is a *non-zero* (not necessarily monic) $f \in R[T]$ with $f(x) = 0$. Every integral element is algebraic; the converse holds when $R$ is a field but fails in general.

**Integral extension.** The $R$-algebra $A$ is **integral over $R$** if *every* element of $A$ is integral over $R$. When $R \subseteq A$ are rings and $A$ is integral over $R$, the inclusion $R \subseteq A$ is called an **integral extension**.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The cleanest compression is the proportion **integral : algebraic :: $\mathbb{Z}$ : $\mathbb{Q}$**. Algebraicity is the field-of-fractions-level notion (it allows you to divide by leading coefficients, i.e. to use denominators), and integrality is its denominator-free shadow. Just as $\mathbb{Z}$ is the subring of $\mathbb{Q}$ where no denominators are permitted, the integral elements form the subring of the algebraic elements where the leading coefficient is not allowed to be a denominator. This is literally the definition of the [[Def - Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial|algebraic integers]]: they are the elements of $\mathbb{C}$ integral over $\mathbb{Z}$, and "integral over $\mathbb{Z}$" is "satisfies a monic integer polynomial".

**True name:** $x$ is integral over $R$ **iff $R[x]$ is a finite $R$-module** — iff the powers $1, x, x^2, \dots$ span a finitely generated $R$-module. This operational characterisation (the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]]) is what you actually use: a monic equation of degree $n$ truncates the powers of $x$ so that $1, x, \dots, x^{n-1}$ already span $R[x]$, and conversely finiteness produces a monic equation by the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]]. Integrality is *finiteness in disguise*.

In **algebraic geometry** the definition compresses to: a ring map is integral iff the corresponding map of spaces has **finite fibres and is proper**. The monic equation of degree $n$ for a coordinate $x$ bounds the number of preimages of a point by $n$ (the roots of the specialised equation), and monic-ness forbids roots "at infinity". The boundary case "over a field integral $=$ algebraic" is the statement that for a map to a point (a field), finite fibres and finite-dimensionality of the fibre algebra are the same. In **field theory** the definition specialises: over a field $k$, $x$ integral over $k$ means $x$ algebraic over $k$, so the integral closure of $k$ in an extension $L$ is the *algebraic closure of $k$ inside $L$*, and $k[\alpha] = k(\alpha)$ for algebraic $\alpha$.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — $\sqrt2$ over $\mathbb{Z}$.** The element $\sqrt2 \in \mathbb{R}$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$: it satisfies the monic $T^2 - 2 = 0$. More generally $\sqrt{d}$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ for any integer $d$ (monic $T^2 - d$), and $\zeta = e^{2\pi i/n}$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ (monic $T^n - 1$). These are the basic algebraic integers, and the monic equation is visible by inspection.

**Is an instance — $\alpha = \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$ over $\mathbb{Z}$.** Although $\alpha$ is a "fraction" in appearance, it is integral: compute $\alpha^2 = \tfrac{1 + 2\sqrt5 + 5}4 = \tfrac{6 + 2\sqrt5}4 = \tfrac{3 + \sqrt5}2 = \alpha + 1$, so $\alpha$ satisfies the monic $T^2 - T - 1 = 0$. This is the golden ratio's defining equation, and it shows that "looks like a fraction" is *not* the same as "is not integral" — the denominator $2$ is absorbed into a monic relation. (It is exactly this $\alpha$, not $\sqrt5$, that generates the full ring of integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$.)

**Is NOT an instance — $\tfrac12$ over $\mathbb{Z}$.** The rational $\tfrac12$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{Z}$ (root of $2T - 1$) but *not* integral. A monic integer polynomial $T^n + c_{n-1}T^{n-1} + \cdots + c_0$ with $\tfrac12$ as a root would, multiplied by $2^n$, give $1 + 2 c_{n-1} + \cdots = 0$ reduced mod $2$ to $1 \equiv 0$, impossible. Equivalently, by [[Thm - Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers|a rational algebraic integer is an integer]], the only rationals integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ are the integers themselves, and $\tfrac12 \notin \mathbb{Z}$. This is the defining non-example: integrality strictly refines algebraicity.

**Is NOT an instance — $t^{-1}$ over $k[t]$.** Inside $A = k[t, t^{-1}] = \operatorname{Frac}$-subring, the element $t^{-1}$ is algebraic over $R = k[t]$ (root of $t \cdot T - 1$) but not integral. A monic relation $(t^{-1})^n + r_1(t) (t^{-1})^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n(t) = 0$ multiplied by $t^n$ gives $1 + t r_1(t) + \cdots + t^n r_n(t) = 0$, but the left side has constant term $1 \neq 0$ (every other term is divisible by $t$), a contradiction. Geometrically, $t^{-1}$ being integral would make $k[t] \hookrightarrow k[t, t^{-1}]$ a finite map, but it is an open immersion deleting the origin. This is the example that shows *inverting* an element produces a non-integral extension.

**Corollary — over a field, integral $=$ algebraic.** If $R = k$ is a field and $x$ satisfies any non-zero $f = a_0 T^n + \cdots \in k[T]$ with $a_0 \neq 0$, divide by $a_0$ to get a monic relation. So every algebraic element over a field is integral. This is the calibration check that the leading coefficient is the *only* obstruction.

**Corollary — a unit times monic is still integral.** If $u x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots = 0$ with $u \in R^\times$, then multiplying by $u^{-1}$ gives a monic relation, so $x$ is integral. "Leading coefficient a unit" $=$ "monic". This is why integrality is insensitive to scaling the equation by units, and why it collapses to algebraicity over a field.

**Calibration check.** Verify that $\sqrt[3]7$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ (find the monic equation) but $\tfrac13$ is not. Confirm that over $\mathbb{Q}$, *every* element of any finite field extension is integral (because $\mathbb{Q}$ is a field). Explain in one sentence why "$x$ algebraic over $R$" allows you to divide out the leading coefficient while "$x$ integral over $R$" does not — and why this is the same as the distinction between $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Z}$. If you can produce the monic equation for $\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$ and the contradiction ruling out $\tfrac12$ from memory, you have understood the definition.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The ring of integers of a number field *(from Algebraic Number Theory)*
> For a finite extension $K/\mathbb{Q}$, the **ring of integers** $\mathcal{O}_K$ is the set of elements of $K$ integral over $\mathbb{Z}$. The whole subject of algebraic number theory is the study of $\mathcal{O}_K$ — its unique factorization of ideals, its units (Dirichlet), its class group. That $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a *ring* is the theorem [[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|"sums and products of integral elements are integral"]]; that the naive $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ may be too small (as for $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)$, where $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2] \supsetneq \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$) is why one must take the full integral closure.

> [!tip] Finite morphisms and branched covers *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> An integral, finite-type ring map is a **finite morphism** of schemes — the algebraic model of a branched covering of varieties. The degree of the covering is the rank of the module, and the branch points are where fibres degenerate. Noether normalization (next chapter) exhibits *every* affine variety as a finite branched cover of affine space, and integral extensions are the tool that makes "finite cover" precise.
