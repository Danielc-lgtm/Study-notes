# Notation Registry

**Standing convention.** Throughout these commutative algebra notes, *ring* means a commutative ring with a multiplicative identity $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$. *Module* means a unital module. This chapter studies how two functors — $M \otimes_R (-)$ and $\operatorname{Hom}_R(-,-)$ — interact with **[[Def - Exact Sequence and Short Exact Sequence|exact sequences]]**. The single organising slogan, stated again in Motivation, is **a module is flat when tensoring with it never destroys an injection, and tensoring with it is the algebra of a continuously varying family.** Everything is about which arrows survive which functor.

- $R, A, B, S$ — commutative rings with $1$
- $M, N, N', P, Q, L$ — $R$-modules; $f, g$ — [[Def - Module Homomorphism|R-linear maps]]
- $M \otimes_R N$ — the [[Def - Tensor Product of Modules|tensor product]]; we drop the subscript and write $M \otimes N$ when $R$ is fixed
- $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f : M \otimes N \to M \otimes N'$ — the map induced on tensor products by $f : N \to N'$
- $A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C$ — a sequence; **exact at $B$** means $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$
- $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$ — a **short exact sequence**: $f$ injective, $g$ surjective, $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$
- $T_M = M \otimes_R (-)$ — the **tensor-with-$M$ functor**, sending $N \mapsto M \otimes N$ and $f \mapsto \operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ (covariant)
- $\operatorname{Hom}_R(Q, P)$ — the $R$-module of $R$-linear maps $Q \to P$, with $(rf)(x) = r\,f(x)$
- $\operatorname{Hom}_R(Q, -)$ — covariant $\operatorname{Hom}$ functor; $f_* (\varphi) = f \circ \varphi$
- $\operatorname{Hom}_R(-, P)$ — **contravariant** $\operatorname{Hom}$ functor; $f^*(\varphi) = \varphi \circ f$ (reverses arrows)
- $\operatorname{coker} f = B / \operatorname{im} f$ — the **cokernel** of $f : A \to B$
- $M$ **flat** — $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ is injective whenever $f$ is injective
- $M$ **projective** — every map onto a quotient lifts; equivalently $M$ is a direct summand of a [[Def - Free Module|free module]]
- $M$ **torsion-free** — $rm = 0$ with $r$ a non-zero-divisor forces $m = 0$
- $R^{\oplus I}$ — the free $R$-module on an index set $I$; $\mu_r : R \to R$ — multiplication by $r$
- $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ — the [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] of an integral domain $R$

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **tensoring with a module is a machine that can break injections, and a *flat* module is one for which this machine is safe.** You met the tensor product in the previous chapter as the universal home for bilinear maps and as the engine of [[Def - Restriction and Extension of Scalars|extension of scalars]]. The natural next question is the one any functor must answer: does it respect the structure that organises all of module theory — the [[Def - Exact Sequence and Short Exact Sequence|exact sequences]] that encode injections, surjections, kernels and quotients? The answer comes in two halves and they are not symmetric, and that asymmetry is the whole subject.

The first half is good news. If $A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$ is exact — meaning $g$ is onto and $\operatorname{im} f = \ker g$ — then tensoring with any fixed $M$ keeps it exact: $M \otimes A \to M \otimes B \to M \otimes C \to 0$ is still exact. Tensoring is **right exact**. The trailing $\to 0$, the surjectivity, is preserved; quotients survive tensoring intact, which is why $M \otimes (R/I) \cong M/IM$ and why $S^{-1}M \cong S^{-1}R \otimes M$. The second half is the bad news, and it is the reason this chapter exists. The *leading* part — injectivity — can fail. Tensor the injection $\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\,\times 2\,} \mathbb{Z}$ with $\mathbb{Z}/2$ and you get $\mathbb{Z}/2 \xrightarrow{\,\times 2 = 0\,} \mathbb{Z}/2$, the zero map, which is no longer injective. An honest embedding became a non-embedding the instant we tensored. So tensoring is right exact but not, in general, *left* exact, and a single example destroys any hope that it always is.

A module $M$ for which this never happens — for which $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ is injective whenever $f$ is — is called **flat**. Flatness is precisely the missing left-exactness: a flat $M$ upgrades $T_M$ from a right-exact functor to a fully exact one, so tensoring with a flat module preserves *every* exact sequence, injections and all. The chapter's first job is to make this definition usable, and the key technical result is that flatness has a much friendlier face: $M$ is flat if and only if it is flat *for finitely generated modules*, which reduces every flatness check to a finite computation. The chapter's structural backbone is a strict descending chain of conditions, each implying the next and none reversing:

$$\textbf{free} \;\Longrightarrow\; \textbf{projective} \;\Longrightarrow\; \textbf{flat} \;\Longrightarrow\; \textbf{torsion-free}.$$

Free modules are flat for the cheapest possible reason — tensoring with $R^{\oplus I}$ is just taking $I$ copies, and a direct sum of injections is an injection. Projective modules — the direct summands of free modules — are flat because a summand inherits flatness. Flat modules are torsion-free because a torsion element $r m_0 = 0$ (with $r$ a non-zero-divisor) is exactly a witness that tensoring the injection $\mu_r : R \to R$ with $M$ kills something. Each arrow is a genuine theorem, and each *failure* of the reverse arrow is a genuine example: $\mathbb{Q}$ is flat but not projective over $\mathbb{Z}$; a summand of $\mathbb{Z}/6$ is projective but not free; the maximal ideal $(X,Y) \trianglelefteq k[X,Y]$ is torsion-free but not flat. Mapping this chain, with a witness at every link, is the spine of the chapter.

Running parallel is the second functor, $\operatorname{Hom}$. Where tensoring is right exact, $\operatorname{Hom}$ is **left exact** — it preserves the *leading* injectivity and loses the *trailing* surjectivity, the exact mirror image. The two are not independent facts: they are forced on each other by the **tensor–Hom adjunction** $\operatorname{Hom}(M \otimes N, L) \cong \operatorname{Hom}(N, \operatorname{Hom}(M, L))$, the precise statement that $T_M$ and $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ are adjoint, with the general principle (a left adjoint is right exact, a right adjoint is left exact) explaining both exactness directions at once. From left-exactness of $\operatorname{Hom}$ one extracts **projective** modules — those $M$ for which $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ is moreover *right* exact, hence fully exact — and the chapter closes by proving the four faces of projectivity equivalent: lifting, exactness of $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$, every short exact sequence onto $M$ splitting, and being a direct summand of a free module.

The reason all of this matters beyond bookkeeping is geometric, and it is worth stating now because it motivates the name. A homomorphism $A \to B$ makes $B$ a family of rings (geometrically, a family of spaces) parametrised by $A$; the family is **flat** exactly when $B$ is flat as an $A$-module, and flatness is the precise algebraic condition that the fibres of the family vary *continuously*, with no fibre suddenly jumping in dimension as the parameter moves. This is why a quotient by a **monic** polynomial — a family of finitely many points whose count never drops — is flat, while $k[X,Y]/(XY)$, two lines crossing, is not flat over $k[X]$: at the origin the fibre jumps. Flatness is the technical heart of deformation theory and the construction of moduli, and projectivity is its partner: a finitely generated projective module is a **vector bundle** (locally free), and the chain free $\Rightarrow$ projective $\Rightarrow$ flat $\Rightarrow$ torsion-free reads, geometrically, trivial bundle $\Rightarrow$ vector bundle $\Rightarrow$ flat family $\Rightarrow$ no torsion.

This chapter assumes fluency with the [[Def - Tensor Product of Modules|tensor product of modules]] and [[Thm - Tensoring is Right Exact|its basic isomorphisms]], with [[Def - Restriction and Extension of Scalars|extension of scalars]] (the previous chapter, [[Commutative Algebra II — Tensor Products]]), with [[Def - Exact Sequence and Short Exact Sequence|exact sequences]] (from [[Commutative Algebra I — Chain Conditions]]), and with [[Def - Module|modules]], [[Def - Submodule|submodules]], [[Def - Quotient Module|quotients]], [[Def - Free Module|free modules]] and [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphisms]] from the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|Modules]] notes. The [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] from the [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings]] notes appears in the flat-not-projective example.

# Concept Map

## §3.4 Flatness and the Exactness of Tensor

- **[[Thm - Tensoring is Right Exact]]**
	- For any $R$-module $M$, the functor $T_M = M \otimes_R (-)$ is **right exact**: applying it to an exact $A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$ yields an exact $M \otimes A \to M \otimes B \to M \otimes C \to 0$. The trailing surjectivity (the $\to 0$) is essential and is preserved; the leading injectivity need not be. The cleanest corollary is $M \otimes (P/Q) \cong (M \otimes P)/\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{id}_M \otimes \iota)$, giving $M \otimes (R/I) \cong M/IM$, and for algebras $S \otimes_R (T/I) \cong (S \otimes_R T)/I^e$. The proof routes through the tensor–Hom adjunction and the left-exactness of $\operatorname{Hom}$, turning a statement about $\otimes$ into one about $\operatorname{Hom}$.

- **[[Def - The Hom Functor and Left Exactness]]**
	- $\operatorname{Hom}_R(Q,P)$ is the $R$-module of $R$-linear maps $Q \to P$ under pointwise addition and $(rf)(x) = r\,f(x)$. It gives two functors: the **covariant** $\operatorname{Hom}_R(Q,-)$, with $f_*(\varphi) = f \circ \varphi$, and the **contravariant** $\operatorname{Hom}_R(-,P)$, with $f^*(\varphi) = \varphi \circ f$, which reverses arrows. Both are **left exact**: they preserve the leading injectivity of an exact sequence but in general lose the trailing surjectivity — the exact mirror image of tensoring's right-exactness.

- **[[Thm - Hom is Left Exact]]**
	- If $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C$ is exact then so is $0 \to \operatorname{Hom}(Q,A) \to \operatorname{Hom}(Q,B) \to \operatorname{Hom}(Q,C)$; and if $A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$ is exact then so is $0 \to \operatorname{Hom}(C,P) \to \operatorname{Hom}(B,P) \to \operatorname{Hom}(A,P)$ (the contravariant functor turns the surjection on the right into an injection on the left). A converse holds — the exactness of $\operatorname{Hom}(-,P)$ for *all* $P$ forces the original sequence to be exact — which is the lemma that powers the right-exactness of tensor.

- **[[Def - Flat Module]]**
	- $M$ is **flat** if $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ is injective whenever $f : N \to N'$ is injective — that is, tensoring with $M$ preserves injections, upgrading the always-right-exact $T_M$ to a fully exact functor. Free modules are flat; $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is not flat over $\mathbb{Z}$ (witnessed by $\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2}\mathbb{Z}$); flatness depends on the base ring, since $\mathbb{Z}/2$ *is* flat over itself. The standard failure is torsion: $R/I$ for a proper non-zero ideal of a domain is never flat.

- **[[Thm - Characterization of Flat Modules]]**
	- For an $R$-module $M$, the following are equivalent: (1) $T_M$ preserves all exact sequences; (2) $T_M$ preserves short exact sequences; (3) $M$ is flat (preserves injections $0 \to N' \to N$); (4) $M$ is flat *for finitely generated modules*. The equivalence $(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)$ rests on splicing any exact sequence from short ones; $(4)\Rightarrow(3)$ is the workhorse, reducing every flatness question to finitely generated submodules via the fact that any tensor relation already holds in a finitely generated piece. There is a further criterion (Atiyah–Macdonald): $M$ is flat iff $I \otimes_R M \to IM$ is injective for every finitely generated ideal $I$, iff $\operatorname{Tor}_1^R(R/I, M) = 0$.

- **[[Thm - Extension of Scalars Preserves Flatness]]**
	- If $f : R \to S$ is a ring homomorphism and $M$ is a flat $R$-module, then $S \otimes_R M$ is a flat $S$-module. The proof is pure diagram chasing through the associativity isomorphism $(S \otimes_R M) \otimes_S N \cong M \otimes_R N$: an $S$-injection $N \to N'$, viewed on the bottom row as an $R$-tensor, stays injective by flatness of $M$, and the vertical isomorphisms transport injectivity to the top. Localization $S^{-1}R$ being flat is the prototype.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Flat families and flat morphisms *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> A ring map $A \to B$ presents $B$ as a **family of fibres** over the points of $\operatorname{Spec} A$; the family is a **flat family** exactly when $B$ is flat as an $A$-module. Flatness is the algebraic encoding of "the fibres vary continuously" — no fibre suddenly jumps in dimension or in length as the base point moves. This is the technical heart of **deformation theory** and of every **moduli** construction: one allows only flat families precisely so that the geometry cannot tear. The non-flat $k[X,Y]/(XY)$ over $k[X]$ is the canonical picture of a family that *does* tear — two crossing lines whose fibre over the origin jumps from one point to a whole line.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Tor and homological algebra *(from Homological Algebra)*
> The failure of $T_M$ to be left exact is measured by a sequence of derived functors $\operatorname{Tor}_n^R(M, -)$, with $\operatorname{Tor}_0 = M \otimes (-)$ and $M$ flat exactly when all higher $\operatorname{Tor}$ vanish. The single criterion $\operatorname{Tor}_1^R(R/I, M) = 0$ for all finitely generated $I$ detects flatness, and the long exact sequence of $\operatorname{Tor}$ is the systematic bookkeeping of "how badly injectivity is broken." This is the gateway from a caveat about tensoring into the whole edifice of homological algebra.

- **[[Ex - A monic-polynomial quotient is a flat algebra]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For any ring $A$ and monic $f \in A[T]$, the algebra $B = A[T]/(f)$ is a **free** $A$-module with basis $1, T, \dots, T^{d-1}$ ($d = \deg f$), hence flat. Geometrically a finite flat family of $d$ points whose count never drops. By contrast $k[X,Y]/(XY)$ is not flat over $k[X]$, the fibre jumping at the origin — the prototype of a non-flat family.

- **[[Ex - The maximal ideal (X,Y) is torsion-free but not flat]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- The maximal ideal $\mathfrak m = (X,Y) \trianglelefteq k[X,Y]$ is torsion-free (a submodule of the domain $R$), but not flat: tensoring the inclusion $\mathfrak m \hookrightarrow R$ with $\mathfrak m$ fails to be injective — the Koszul relation $Y \otimes X - X \otimes Y$ is a non-zero element of $\mathfrak m \otimes \mathfrak m$ killed by the multiplication map. Separates torsion-free from flat, completing the four-step tower with a witness at every gap.

- **[[Ex - Free implies projective implies flat implies torsion-free]]** (⭐⭐)
	- The structural backbone: each implication proved cleanly (free is a summand of itself hence projective; projective is a summand of free hence flat; flat is torsion-free because a torsion element witnesses a broken injection $\mu_r$), and the chain is the spine the rest of the chapter hangs failures on. Read here as the flatness tower; revisited in §3.5 once projectivity is in hand.

## §3.5 Projectivity, Hom, and the Tower of Modules

- **[[Def - Projective Module]]**
	- $M$ is **projective** if every $R$-linear map $M \to N/N'$ to a quotient *lifts* through the quotient map $\pi : N \to N/N'$, i.e. factors as $M \xrightarrow{g} N \xrightarrow{\pi} N/N'$ (the lift $g$ need not be unique — this is a lifting property, not a universal property). Equivalently, $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ is exact, or every short exact sequence onto $M$ splits, or $M$ is a direct summand of a free module. Free modules are projective; over $\mathbb{Z}/6$ the summand $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is projective but not free.

- **[[Thm - Projective iff Direct Summand of a Free Module]]**
	- For an $R$-module $M$ the following are equivalent: (i) $M$ is projective; (ii) $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ is an exact functor; (iii) every short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to M \to 0$ splits; (iv) $M$ is a direct summand of a free module, $M \oplus N \cong R^{\oplus I}$. The pivot is $(iii)\Leftrightarrow(iv)$: present $M$ as a quotient of a free module $F \twoheadrightarrow M$, then $M$ projective $\Leftrightarrow$ this sequence splits $\Leftrightarrow$ $M$ is a summand of $F$. From (iv) one gets at once that **projective $\Rightarrow$ flat**, since a summand of a flat (indeed free) module is flat.

- **[[Ex - The splitting lemma]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For a short exact sequence $0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$, the conditions "splits", "$g$ has a right inverse $s$ with $gs = \operatorname{id}_C$", and "$f$ has a left inverse $r$ with $rf = \operatorname{id}_A$" are equivalent, and each forces $B \cong A \oplus C$ compatibly with $f, g$. The retraction/section is the data that builds the splitting isomorphism, and a projective quotient $C$ hands you the section for free.

- **[[Ex - Q is a flat but not projective Z-module]]** (⭐⭐)
	- $\mathbb{Q}$ is flat over $\mathbb{Z}$ (it is a localization, or directly: every tensor in $\mathbb{Q} \otimes V$ is pure), but not projective — a hypothetical splitting of a free cover would make $\mathbb{Q}$ a summand of a free abelian group, impossible since $\mathbb{Q}$ is divisible and free abelian groups have no non-trivial divisible elements. The first separation of flat from projective.

- **[[Ex - A projective module that is not free]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Over $R = \mathbb{Z}/6 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/3$, the module $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is a direct summand of the free module $R$, hence projective, but is not free (every non-zero free $R$-module has at least $6$ elements). The idempotent $e = (1,0)$ is the engine: $Re$ is projective for any idempotent $e$. Separates projective from free.

- **[[Ex - A short exact sequence that does not split though B is the direct sum]]** (⭐⭐)
	- The sequence $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$ does not split (no section, as $\mathbb{Z}$ has no $2$-torsion). More subtly, one can have $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ non-split yet $B \cong A \oplus C$ abstractly — the isomorphism exists but not *compatibly* with the maps. A caution that "splits" is about the maps, not the abstract isomorphism type of $B$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Vector bundles and Serre–Swan *(from Algebraic Geometry / Topology)*
> A finitely generated **projective** module is exactly a **vector bundle** in algebraic guise: it is *locally free* — free after localizing at each prime — yet may be globally twisted, just as a vector bundle is locally trivial but globally non-trivial. The **Serre–Swan** correspondence makes this precise: finitely generated projective modules over the ring of functions on a space are the same data as vector bundles over the space, with [[Def - Free Module|free]] modules corresponding to *trivial* bundles. The projective-not-free phenomenon (the $\mathbb{Z}/6$ example, or the non-principal ideal $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$) is the algebraic seed of every non-trivial bundle. Reading the chapter's tower geometrically: **trivial bundle $\Rightarrow$ vector bundle $\Rightarrow$ flat family $\Rightarrow$ no torsion**.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Adjoint functors and (co)continuity *(from Category Theory)*
> The isomorphism $\operatorname{Hom}(M \otimes N, L) \cong \operatorname{Hom}(N, \operatorname{Hom}(M, L))$ says $T_M$ and $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ form an **adjoint pair**, with $T_M$ the left adjoint. The general theorem — a left adjoint preserves colimits (is right exact) and a right adjoint preserves limits (is left exact) — explains in one stroke *both* the right-exactness of tensor and the left-exactness of $\operatorname{Hom}$, and the isomorphism $M \otimes (A \oplus B) \cong (M \otimes A) \oplus (M \otimes B)$ as a left adjoint commuting with the coproduct. This is the categorical why behind the chapter's two exactness facts.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring goals of this chapter cluster into five types. The first is **proving (or refuting) flatness of a specific module** — showing $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ stays injective, or exhibiting a single injection it breaks. The standard route to *prove* flatness is to recognise $M$ as free, projective, a localization, or an extension of scalars of a flat module; the standard route to *refute* it is to find torsion, since [[Thm - Characterization of Flat Modules|flat ⇒ torsion-free]] contrapositively kills flatness the instant a non-zero-divisor annihilates a non-zero element. The second is **placing a module on the tower free $\Rightarrow$ projective $\Rightarrow$ flat $\Rightarrow$ torsion-free** — deciding exactly which conditions a given module satisfies, which is the same as deciding which of the standard separating examples it resembles. The third, and the chapter's signature, is **deciding whether a short exact sequence splits** — producing a section $s$ or retraction $r$, or proving none exists; this is where projectivity of the quotient is the clean sufficient condition via [[Ex - The splitting lemma|the splitting lemma]]. The fourth is **establishing an exactness property of a functor** — that $T_M$ or $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ preserves a given class of sequences — which is settled by the characterization theorems. The fifth is **identifying a tensor or Hom module concretely** — computing $M \otimes (R/I) = M/IM$, $\operatorname{Hom}(R^n, P) = P^n$, or $S \otimes_R (T/I) = (S \otimes T)/I^e$ — using right-exactness of tensor and left-exactness of Hom as calculational tools. These are the natural targets because the chapter is fundamentally about *which arrows a functor preserves*: every theorem says "this functor keeps that property of a map."

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A module is named free or a free module is in sight** — and freeness is the cheapest entry to the whole tower, since a free module is flat, projective, and torsion-free at once; recognising freeness (a monic-quotient $A[T]/(f)$, a vector space, $S \otimes_R R^n$) ends most problems immediately. **An injection is to be tensored** — the trigger for a flatness question; the move is to ask whether $M$ is flat, and if its flatness is in doubt, to test the specific injection $\mu_r : R \to R$ or $I \hookrightarrow R$. **A short exact sequence is given with a projective (or free) right-hand term** — which licenses splitting and the decomposition $B \cong A \oplus C$ via [[Thm - Projective iff Direct Summand of a Free Module|the projectivity characterization]]. **A non-zero-divisor annihilates something, or torsion is present** — the immediate certificate of *non*-flatness. **A ring map $R \to S$ and a flat $R$-module** — routing through [[Thm - Extension of Scalars Preserves Flatness|extension of scalars preserves flatness]] to get a flat $S$-module, localization being the prototype. The discipline is to match source to target along the tower: a "prove flat" target with a "free/projective/localization" source is a one-liner; a "prove not flat" target wants the torsion source; a "does it split" target wants a projective right term. The [[Commutative Algebra III — Flatness and Exactness#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every flatness or exactness problem is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

1. **Tensor an exact sequence and keep the right end.** Because [[Thm - Tensoring is Right Exact|Tₘ is right exact]], you may apply $M \otimes (-)$ to any exact $A \to B \to C \to 0$ and the result $M \otimes A \to M \otimes B \to M \otimes C \to 0$ is still exact — surjectivity and the cokernel survive, only injectivity is at risk. *Trigger:* a surjection, quotient, or cokernel appears and you want to tensor. *Pattern:* "apply $M \otimes -$ to $Q \to P \to P/Q \to 0$ to get $M \otimes (P/Q) = (M \otimes P)/\operatorname{im}$."

2. **Compute $M \otimes (R/I)$ as $M/IM$.** The single most reused special case of right-exactness: tensoring with a cyclic quotient $R/I$ kills exactly the submodule $IM$. *Trigger:* a tensor product with a quotient ring or a residue field. *Pattern:* "$M \otimes_R (R/I) \cong M/IM$, and at a maximal ideal $M \otimes \kappa(\mathfrak m) = M/\mathfrak m M$ is the fibre."

3. **Test flatness on the injection $\mu_r : R \to R$.** To detect *non*-flatness, tensor the multiplication-by-$r$ injection (valid whenever $r$ is a non-zero-divisor) with $M$; $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes \mu_r$ is "multiply by $r$ on $M$", and it fails to be injective exactly when $M$ has $r$-torsion. *Trigger:* you suspect a module is not flat. *Pattern:* "find a non-zero-divisor $r$ and a non-zero $m$ with $rm = 0$ — that single torsion element breaks flatness."

4. **Reduce flatness to finitely generated modules.** By [[Thm - Characterization of Flat Modules|the characterization]], $M$ is flat as soon as $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ is injective for all injections $f$ between *finitely generated* modules; any tensor relation $\sum m_i \otimes n_i = 0$ already holds inside a finitely generated submodule. *Trigger:* a flatness proof seems to need control of infinitely generated modules. *Pattern:* "pull the finitely many $n_i$ and the relation into a finitely generated $M_0 \otimes N_0$, decide it there."

5. **Recognise freeness, then read off the whole tower.** A free module is flat, projective, and torsion-free simultaneously; the fastest flatness proof is "this is free." *Trigger:* a module with an evident basis — $A[T]/(f)$ for monic $f$ (basis $1, T, \dots, T^{d-1}$), a vector space, $S \otimes_R R^n$, a localization of a free module. *Pattern:* "exhibit a basis; freeness hands you flat + projective + torsion-free for free."

6. **Split a short exact sequence whose right term is projective.** If $0 \to A \to B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$ has $C$ [[Def - Projective Module|projective]], lift $\operatorname{id}_C$ through $g$ to get a section $s$, hence $B \cong A \oplus C$ by [[Ex - The splitting lemma|the splitting lemma]]. *Trigger:* "does this sequence split / is $B$ a direct sum?" with a free or projective quotient. *Pattern:* "$C$ projective $\Rightarrow$ lift $\operatorname{id}_C$ $\Rightarrow$ section $\Rightarrow$ splitting."

7. **Present a module as a quotient of a free module.** Every module is $F/K$ for some free $F$; pairing this with projectivity, $M$ is projective iff the presentation $0 \to K \to F \to M \to 0$ splits, iff $M$ is a summand of $F$. *Trigger:* you must decide projectivity, or extract a complement. *Pattern:* "choose generators, map $R^{(\text{gens})} \twoheadrightarrow M$; projectivity is the splitting of this surjection."

8. **Convert a tensor question into a Hom question via adjunction.** The isomorphism $\operatorname{Hom}(M \otimes N, L) \cong \operatorname{Hom}(N, \operatorname{Hom}(M, L))$ moves a problem about $\otimes$ to one about $\operatorname{Hom}$, where [[Thm - Hom is Left Exact|left-exactness]] is available. *Trigger:* proving an exactness property of tensor. *Pattern:* "apply $\operatorname{Hom}(-, P)$ then $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$, use that the result is exact for all $P$, then [[Thm - Hom is Left Exact|the converse lemma]]."

9. **Push flatness through extension of scalars.** A flat $R$-module $M$ gives a flat $S$-module $S \otimes_R M$ along any $R \to S$, by [[Thm - Extension of Scalars Preserves Flatness|extension of scalars preserves flatness]]. *Trigger:* a base change of a flat module. *Pattern:* "chase the associativity isomorphism $(S \otimes_R M) \otimes_S N \cong M \otimes_R N$ to transport injectivity."

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming tensoring preserves injectivity
> Because tensoring preserves surjections and quotients so cleanly, it is tempting to believe it preserves injections too — to treat $M \otimes (-)$ as if it were exact for every $M$. It is not. The injection $\mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z}$ tensored with $\mathbb{Z}/2$ becomes $\mathbb{Z}/2 \xrightarrow{\times 2 = 0} \mathbb{Z}/2$, the zero map, which is not injective: an embedding was destroyed. The operation becomes legal **exactly when $M$ is [[Def - Flat Module|flat]]** — that is the *definition* of flat. The lesson: before tensoring an injection and asserting the result is injective, you owe a flatness check, and "the original map was injective" is never enough on its own.

> [!warning] 2. Concluding $B \cong A \oplus C$ means the sequence splits
> A short exact sequence $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ in which $B$ happens to be isomorphic to $A \oplus C$ *as a module* looks like it must split — but "splits" is a statement about the *maps*, not the abstract isomorphism type of $B$. There exist non-split sequences with $B \cong A \oplus C$ abstractly: the isomorphism exists but cannot be chosen to commute with the given $f$ and $g$ (see [[Ex - A short exact sequence that does not split though B is the direct sum]]). Splitting becomes equivalent to "$B \cong A \oplus C$" only when the isomorphism is required **compatibly with $f$ and $g$** — i.e. when there is a genuine section or retraction. Checking the abstract module $B$ is not the same as producing the splitting data.

> [!warning] 3. Believing torsion-free implies flat
> Since [[Thm - Characterization of Flat Modules|flat modules are torsion-free]], one is tempted to run the implication backwards and treat "no torsion" as a flatness certificate. It is not, except over very special rings. The maximal ideal $\mathfrak m = (X,Y) \trianglelefteq k[X,Y]$ is torsion-free — it sits inside the domain $R$ — yet **not flat**: tensoring $\mathfrak m \hookrightarrow R$ with $\mathfrak m$ kills the non-zero element $Y \otimes X - X \otimes Y$ (see [[Ex - The maximal ideal (X,Y) is torsion-free but not flat]]). The implication reverses **only over a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]]** (more generally a Prüfer/Dedekind domain), where torsion-free, flat, and "no $\operatorname{Tor}_1$" coincide. Over a two-dimensional ring like $k[X,Y]$ the gap is real, and it is exactly where the geometry of singular families lives.

> [!warning] 4. Treating "projective" as "free"
> Projective modules behave so much like free ones — they are summands of free modules, $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ is exact, lifting always works — that it is tempting to assume they *are* free. Over a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]] or a local ring this is even true. But in general it fails: over $\mathbb{Z}/6 \cong \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/3$ the summand $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is projective but not free (it has $2$ elements; every non-zero free $\mathbb{Z}/6$-module has at least $6$), and the ideal $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5}) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is projective but not free (see [[Ex - A projective module that is not free]]). Projective equals free **only with extra hypotheses** (PID, local ring, or finitely generated over a polynomial ring by the Quillen–Suslin theorem); the gap between them is precisely the room in which non-trivial vector bundles live.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any problem from this chapter is to identify which of three questions you are really being asked, because each routes to a different tool.

If the problem **asks whether a module is flat**, the controlling principle is the tower free $\Rightarrow$ projective $\Rightarrow$ flat $\Rightarrow$ torsion-free, read in whichever direction settles the question fastest. To *prove* flatness, try to climb: is the module free (does it have an obvious basis, like $A[T]/(f)$ for monic $f$, or $S \otimes_R R^n$)? Is it projective (a direct summand of a free module)? Is it a localization or an extension of scalars of something flat, via [[Thm - Extension of Scalars Preserves Flatness|extension of scalars preserves flatness]]? Any of these ends the problem. To *refute* flatness, descend: the contrapositive of [[Thm - Characterization of Flat Modules|flat ⇒ torsion-free]] says that finding a single torsion element — a non-zero $m$ killed by a non-zero-divisor $r$ — kills flatness outright. So the reflex is "look for torsion first": if there is torsion, you are done, and if there is none, you must work harder (torsion-free does not imply flat), tensoring a specific injection like $\mathfrak m \hookrightarrow R$ with the module and hunting for an element of the kernel. The genuinely hard flatness problems are exactly the torsion-free non-flat ones, where the witness is a subtle tensor relation (the Koszul element $Y \otimes X - X \otimes Y$ for $(X,Y)$), and the [[Thm - Characterization of Flat Modules|finitely generated criterion]] is what makes the search finite.

If the problem **asks whether a short exact sequence splits**, the question is whether you can produce a section $s : C \to B$ with $gs = \operatorname{id}_C$ or a retraction $r : B \to A$ with $rf = \operatorname{id}_A$; by [[Ex - The splitting lemma|the splitting lemma]] either one suffices and either one yields $B \cong A \oplus C$ compatibly. The single most useful sufficient condition is **the right-hand term $C$ is projective**: then $\operatorname{id}_C$ lifts through the surjection $g$, manufacturing the section for free, which is the content of [[Thm - Projective iff Direct Summand of a Free Module|the projectivity characterization]]. So the recognition step is to inspect $C$: free or projective means split. To prove a sequence does *not* split, the cleanest argument is usually an invariant a direct sum would have to share — divisibility ($\mathbb{Q}$ is divisible but no non-trivial element of a free abelian group is), torsion structure, or cardinality — that the middle term lacks compatibly. And keep firmly in mind the trap: $B \cong A \oplus C$ abstractly does *not* mean the sequence splits.

If the problem **asks for an exactness property of a functor** — does $T_M$ or $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ preserve this sequence — the two anchor facts are that tensoring is right exact and $\operatorname{Hom}$ is left exact, and the strategy is to position the unknown against them. Tensoring upgrades to fully exact exactly when $M$ is flat ([[Def - Flat Module]]); $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ upgrades to fully exact exactly when $M$ is projective ([[Def - Projective Module]]). So "is $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ exact?" *is* "is $M$ projective?", and "is $M \otimes (-)$ exact?" *is* "is $M$ flat?" — the exactness questions and the structural questions are the same question. The bridge between the two functors is the adjunction $\operatorname{Hom}(M \otimes N, L) \cong \operatorname{Hom}(N, \operatorname{Hom}(M, L))$, which is the standard device for converting a tensor-exactness claim into a Hom-exactness claim where left-exactness and [[Thm - Hom is Left Exact|its converse lemma]] do the work.

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "which arrows does this functor preserve, and what extra condition would make it preserve more?"** Tensoring preserves surjections always and injections only when the module is flat; $\operatorname{Hom}$ preserves injections always and surjections only when the module is projective. Splitting is the special case where a surjection has a one-sided inverse, and projectivity is exactly the condition that makes such inverses appear on demand. Decide which arrow is in danger — the injection at the front or the surjection at the back — and the right tool is determined.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Tensoring is Right Exact|Right-exactness of tensor]]**: $M \otimes A \to M \otimes B \to M \otimes C \to 0$ stays exact, giving $M \otimes (R/I) \cong M/IM$. This is the most reused fact because it is the calculational backbone of every tensor computation — it turns tensoring with a quotient into a quotient, and is what makes $S^{-1}M = S^{-1}R \otimes M$, the fibre $M/\mathfrak m M$, and the base change $S \otimes_R (T/I) = (S\otimes T)/I^e$ all routine. Reach for it whenever a tensor product meets a quotient or a cokernel; the surjection at the back always survives, so you may freely tensor and read off the quotient.

- **[[Thm - Characterization of Flat Modules|The flat characterization, especially the finitely generated criterion]]**: $M$ flat $\iff$ flat for finitely generated modules $\iff$ preserves short exact sequences. Its reusability is that it reduces every flatness question to a finite computation — any tensor relation already lives in a finitely generated submodule — and it is the tool that lets one *prove* flatness rather than merely fail to refute it. The companion criterion $I \otimes M \to IM$ injective for finitely generated $I$ (equivalently $\operatorname{Tor}_1(R/I, M) = 0$) is the form used in practice and the entry to homological algebra. Whenever flatness is the target and the module is not obviously free, this is the first move.

- **[[Thm - Projective iff Direct Summand of a Free Module|Projective ⇔ direct summand of a free module]]**: the four-way equivalence (lifting, $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ exact, every SES onto $M$ splits, summand of free). This is the workhorse of §3.5 because it converts the abstract lifting property into the concrete "summand of free", from which **projective $\Rightarrow$ flat** is immediate (a summand of free is flat). Use it to certify projectivity by exhibiting a complement, to deduce flatness, or to split a sequence whose quotient is projective. It is the hinge between the Hom-side (exactness, splitting) and the tensor-side (flatness) of the chapter.

- **The tower free $\Rightarrow$ projective $\Rightarrow$ flat $\Rightarrow$ torsion-free**, with a separating example at each gap. Its reusability is as a *classification reflex*: given any module, the question "where does it sit on the tower?" organises both proofs (climb to prove flatness) and refutations (find the torsion, or the non-free summand). The four canonical witnesses — $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$-flavoured failures, $\mathbb{Z}/6$'s summand, $(X,Y) \trianglelefteq k[X,Y]$, monic quotients — are the library every concrete problem reduces to. Knowing the tower with witnesses is knowing the chapter.

- **[[Ex - The splitting lemma|The splitting lemma]]**: section $\iff$ retraction $\iff$ split $\iff$ $B \cong A \oplus C$ (compatibly). Its reusability is that it converts "is this a direct sum?" into "does one map have a one-sided inverse?", a far more tractable question, and it is the precise interface where projectivity feeds in (a projective quotient hands you the section). Reach for it whenever a short exact sequence appears and you want to break the middle term apart — but remember the compatibility clause, which is what separates genuine splitting from a coincidental abstract isomorphism.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — flatness is the continuity of a family of fibres.** A ring homomorphism $A \to B$ presents $\operatorname{Spec} B$ as a family of spaces fibred over $\operatorname{Spec} A$: the fibre over a point $\mathfrak p$ is the geometric object attached to $B \otimes_A \kappa(\mathfrak p)$. The family is called **flat** precisely when $B$ is flat as an $A$-module, and the content of that condition is geometric — flatness forbids the fibres from jumping discontinuously, so that the dimension, and in good cases the length, of the fibre is locally constant as the parameter varies. A quotient by a monic polynomial $A[T]/(f)$ is the model flat family: it is *free* over $A$ of rank $\deg f$, so every fibre is $\deg f$ points (counted with multiplicity) and the count never drops — a finite flat family of points. The model *non*-flat family is $k[X,Y]/(XY)$ over $k[X]$: two lines crossing at the origin, whose fibre over $X = c \neq 0$ is a single point but whose fibre over $X = 0$ is the whole $Y$-line, a discontinuous jump that flatness exists to forbid. Because flatness is exactly "no tearing", it is the technical hypothesis under which deformations behave and moduli spaces can be built; this chapter is the affine-algebraic foundation of deformation theory.

2. **Topology and bundles — finitely generated projective modules are vector bundles (Serre–Swan).** A [[Def - Free Module|free module]] of rank $n$ is the algebra of a *trivial* rank-$n$ bundle, the product of the base with $k^n$. A finitely generated **projective** module is the algebra of a possibly *non-trivial* vector bundle: by [[Thm - Projective iff Direct Summand of a Free Module|the summand characterization]] it is a direct summand of a free module $M \oplus N \cong R^n$, which is exactly the data of a sub-bundle of a trivial bundle — and every vector bundle arises this way, since a bundle over a compact space embeds in a trivial one with a complementary bundle. The **Serre–Swan theorem** turns this analogy into an equivalence: vector bundles over a (compact Hausdorff, or affine algebraic) space correspond precisely to finitely generated projective modules over its ring of functions, trivial bundles to free modules. The existence of projective-but-not-free modules — the $\mathbb{Z}/6$ summand, or the ideal class group element $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ — is therefore the algebraic shadow of the existence of non-trivial bundles, and "locally free but not free" is the module-theoretic statement of "locally trivial but globally twisted".

3. **Homological algebra — the failure of left-exactness is measured by $\operatorname{Tor}$, and flat means $\operatorname{Tor}$ vanishes.** The whole subject is born from the observation that $M \otimes (-)$ keeps the right end of an exact sequence but can break the left end. Homological algebra makes that defect into an invariant: the **derived functors** $\operatorname{Tor}_n^R(M, -)$, with $\operatorname{Tor}_0 = M \otimes (-)$, fit into a long exact sequence that records exactly how much injectivity was lost, and $M$ is [[Def - Flat Module|flat]] if and only if $\operatorname{Tor}_n^R(M, -) = 0$ for all $n \geq 1$ — equivalently $\operatorname{Tor}_1^R(R/I, M) = 0$ for every finitely generated ideal $I$. The companion functor $\operatorname{Hom}$, being [[Thm - Hom is Left Exact|left exact]], is derived the other way into $\operatorname{Ext}_n^R$, and projectivity is the vanishing of $\operatorname{Ext}^n(M, -)$. So the two structural notions of the chapter, flat and projective, are precisely the "acyclic" objects for the two derived theories, and the caveat that opened the chapter — tensoring is only right exact — is the doorway into all of homological algebra.

4. **Number theory and Dedekind domains — over a PID the tower collapses, and the ideal class group measures how far projective is from free.** Over a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] the four conditions of the tower partly merge: finitely generated torsion-free, flat, and projective all coincide, and a finitely generated projective module is even *free* (the structure theorem). The interesting failures appear one step up, over **Dedekind domains** like rings of integers $\mathcal O_K$: there finitely generated projective modules are sums of ideals, every ideal is projective, but a non-principal ideal is projective and *not* free. The set of projective-rank-one modules up to free modules is exactly the **ideal class group**, the central invariant of algebraic number theory measuring the failure of unique factorization. So the chapter's projective-not-free gap, made quantitative, *is* the class group: $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5}) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is a non-free projective module representing the non-trivial class that makes $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ fail to be a UFD.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a functor is judged by which end of an exact sequence it preserves, and flat/projective are the conditions that buy back the missing end.** An exact sequence has a front (an injection) and a back (a surjection). Tensoring, $M \otimes (-)$, always keeps the *back* and may drop the *front* — it is right exact. $\operatorname{Hom}(M, -)$ always keeps the *front* and may drop the *back* — it is left exact. These are mirror images, and they are forced to be mirror images by the tensor–Hom adjunction, under which $T_M$ is a left adjoint (hence preserves colimits, the right-exact end) and $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ is a right adjoint (hence preserves limits, the left-exact end). Everything in the chapter is then organised by a single question: *what does it cost to preserve the other end too?* Making tensoring preserve the front costs exactly **flatness**. Making $\operatorname{Hom}(M,-)$ preserve the back costs exactly **projectivity**. The two definitions are not separate ideas to memorise; they are the two answers to one question — "what makes this half-exact functor fully exact?" — for the two fundamental functors of module theory.

**The true name of flatness: tensoring with $M$ cannot manufacture a relation that was not already there.** The official definition — $\operatorname{id}_M \otimes f$ injective whenever $f$ is — is correct but not the form you reason with. The operational true name is **"$M$ is flat when, for any submodule inclusion $N' \hookrightarrow N$, the natural map $M \otimes N' \to M \otimes N$ is again an inclusion: no two distinct tensors in $M \otimes N'$ become equal after the inclusion."** Tensoring can identify elements (that is what the relations $rm \otimes n = m \otimes rn$ do), and flatness is the promise that it identifies only what it is *forced* to, never collapsing a genuine distinction that lived in a submodule. This is why torsion kills flatness so directly: a torsion relation $rm_0 = 0$ is precisely a distinction in $R$ (namely $r \neq 0$) that tensoring with $M$ collapses (it sends $m_0 \otimes r \mapsto rm_0 \otimes 1 = 0$). And it is why localization is flat: passing to fractions never creates a linear relation among elements that did not already hold. Carry the slogan **flat = no new relations, torsion = a relation tensoring wrongly creates**, and most flatness arguments write themselves.

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "is it flat?" should immediately split into "is it free/projective/a localization?" (to prove yes) and "is there torsion?" (to prove no).** When flatness is the target, the tower free $\Rightarrow$ projective $\Rightarrow$ flat $\Rightarrow$ torsion-free fires two reflexes at once. Upward: look for a basis, a complement in a free module, or a presentation as $S \otimes_R(\text{flat})$ — any of these climbs above flat and ends the problem. Downward: look for a non-zero element annihilated by a non-zero-divisor — any such torsion element drops below flat and ends the problem the other way. The only residual difficulty is the thin band between flat and torsion-free, the torsion-free-but-not-flat modules, and the reaction *there* is to invoke the [[Thm - Characterization of Flat Modules|finitely generated criterion]] and tensor the inclusion of a finitely generated ideal, hunting for a Koszul-type relation in the kernel. The complementary pattern, for splitting questions, is "does the right-hand term look projective?" — because a projective quotient hands you the section for free, and freeness or projectivity of $C$ is the single recognition that converts "is this a direct sum?" from hard to immediate.
