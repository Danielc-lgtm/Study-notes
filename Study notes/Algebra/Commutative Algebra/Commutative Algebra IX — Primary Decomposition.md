# Notation Registry

**Standing convention.** Throughout these commutative algebra notes, *ring* means a commutative ring with a multiplicative identity $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$. This chapter studies how to factor a single ideal $I$ into more tractable pieces — its **primary decomposition** $I = \mathfrak{q}_1 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{q}_n$ — and how much of that factorisation is forced by $I$ alone. The governing slogan, stated again in Motivation, is: **primary decomposition is the algebraic factorisation that, geometrically, breaks a vanishing locus into its irreducible pieces while remembering the embedded, non-reduced structure that the naked variety forgets.** The single new closure operation is the **colon (ideal quotient)** $(I : x)$, which is the workhorse for extracting the primes attached to $I$.

- $R, A$ — commutative rings with $1$
- $I, J, \mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b}$ — ideals of $R$
- $\mathfrak{p}, \mathfrak{q}$ — usually a prime and a primary ideal respectively; $\mathfrak{m}, \mathfrak{n}$ — maximal ideals
- $\sqrt{I} = \{ r \in R : r^n \in I \text{ for some } n \geq 1\}$ — the **radical** of $I$; $\operatorname{nil} R = \sqrt{(0)}$ — the **nilradical**
- $I$ **primary** — $R/I \neq 0$ and every zero-divisor of $R/I$ is nilpotent
- $\mathfrak{q}$ **$\mathfrak{p}$-primary** — $\mathfrak{q}$ is primary with $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}} = \mathfrak{p}$ (a prime)
- $I = \mathfrak{q}_1 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{q}_n$ — a **primary decomposition**; **minimal** when the $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_i}$ are distinct and no $\mathfrak{q}_i$ contains the intersection of the others
- $\operatorname{Ass}(I) = \{\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_1}, \dots, \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_n}\}$ — the **associated primes** of $I$ (well-defined by the First Uniqueness Theorem)
- **isolated** (or **minimal**) prime of $I$ — a minimal element of $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$; **embedded** prime — an associated prime that is not isolated
- $(I : x) = \{ r \in R : rx \in I \}$ — the **colon ideal** (ideal quotient) of $I$ by $x$; more generally $(I : J) = \{r : rJ \subseteq I\}$
- $\operatorname{Spec} R$ — the set of prime ideals of $R$ (the **prime spectrum**); $\operatorname{mSpec} R$ — the maximal ideals
- $V(I) = \{\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec} R : I \subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}$ — the closed set cut out by $I$; over a field, $V(I) \subseteq \mathbb{A}^n$ is the vanishing locus
- $R_{\mathfrak{p}} = (R \setminus \mathfrak{p})^{-1}R$ — the **localization** of $R$ at $\mathfrak{p}$; $I^{ec}$ — extension-then-contraction of $I$ along $R \to R_{\mathfrak{p}}$

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **primary decomposition is the attempt to do for an arbitrary ideal what prime factorisation does for an integer — write it as an intersection of "prime-power-like" pieces — and the surprise is that this works in every [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian ring]], but only up to a precisely identifiable ambiguity.** You already know the prototype. In $\mathbb{Z}$, the ideal $(90)$ factors as $(90) = (2) \cap (3^2) \cap (5)$, an intersection of powers of prime ideals, and this intersection is just the prime factorisation $90 = 2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$ rewritten in the language of ideals (intersection of ideals is the ideal version of "least common multiple", and coprime ideals make intersection equal to product). The pieces $(2), (3^2), (5)$ are not all prime — $(3^2)$ is not — but each is *primary*: a ramped-up version of prime that is to a prime power what "prime" is to a "prime number". The goal of this chapter is to extend "$n = p_1^{a_1} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$" from $\mathbb{Z}$ to every ideal of every Noetherian ring.

What goes wrong, and why is the answer subtle? The first issue is that the pieces $\mathfrak{q}_i$ need not be powers of primes — the right generalisation of "prime power" is the strictly weaker notion of a **primary ideal**, an ideal $\mathfrak{q}$ whose quotient $R/\mathfrak{q}$ has the property that *every zero-divisor is nilpotent*. (In $R/\mathfrak{q}$, a non-unit might fail to be a genuine non-zero-divisor, but if it is a zero-divisor it must be "infinitesimally small", i.e. some power vanishes.) The radical $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$ of a primary ideal is automatically prime, and we say $\mathfrak{q}$ is $\mathfrak{p}$-primary; this $\mathfrak{p}$ is the "prime $\mathfrak{q}$ belongs to". The second, deeper issue is **uniqueness**. The integer factorisation of $90$ is unique. A primary decomposition is *not* unique as a list of ideals — the same $I$ can have genuinely different minimal decompositions. What *is* unique is the set of attached primes $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_i}$, the **associated primes** $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$; and among those, the *minimal* ones (the **isolated primes**) pin down their primary components uniquely, while the non-minimal ones (the **embedded primes**) leave their components free to wobble. This is the entire content of the two Uniqueness Theorems, and it is why one says primary decomposition is "unique where it matters and ambiguous where it does not".

The hierarchy of ideal-types that organises the whole chapter is:
$$\text{prime} \;\subsetneq\; \text{primary} \;\supsetneq\; \text{prime powers}, \qquad \text{prime} \;=\; \text{radical} \;\cap\; \text{primary}, \qquad \mathfrak{q} \text{ primary} \;\Rightarrow\; \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}} \text{ prime}.$$
Read it carefully: an ideal is prime exactly when it is both radical and primary; primary is *weaker* than prime but *incomparable* with "prime power" (there are primary ideals that are not prime powers, and prime powers that are not primary); and taking the radical of a primary ideal always lands on a prime. These containments are the source of every example and counterexample in the chapter.

Why does any of this deserve a place in a geometry-facing course? Because primary decomposition is the algebraic skeleton of one of the most important operations in **algebraic geometry**: decomposing a geometric figure into its irreducible components. When $R = k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$, the ideal $I$ cuts out a vanishing locus $V(I)$, and the minimal primes of $I$ are exactly the **irreducible components** of $V(I)$ — the maximal irreducible pieces the figure breaks into. The radical $\sqrt{I} = \bigcap \text{(minimal primes)}$ is the ideal of the figure as a *set*; passing from $I$ to $\sqrt{I}$ is the act of forgetting everything except the underlying point-set. But $I$ itself remembers more than the point-set: the embedded primes record **embedded components** — lower-dimensional pieces sitting *inside* a bigger component, invisible to the naked variety but carrying genuine "non-reduced" or "multiplicity" information that only the full theory of **schemes** makes precise. The canonical picture, which we will compute three times, is $I = (X^2, XY) \subseteq k[X,Y]$: as a set $V(I)$ is just the line $X = 0$, one irreducible component with prime $(X)$; but $I$ also carries an embedded prime $(X,Y)$ — a "fat point" stuck at the origin — that the line alone cannot see. Primary decomposition is exactly the bookkeeping that separates the line from the fat point.

This chapter assumes you are fluent with [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime and maximal ideals]] and the [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients|domain/field-quotient dictionary]] from the [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings]] notes; with [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian rings]] and the ascending chain condition from [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8|Rings IV]]; with the [[Def - Radical of an Ideal and the Nilradical|radical of an ideal]], the [[Def - The Prime Spectrum (Spec)|prime spectrum]], and [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|localization]] from the earlier commutative-algebra chapters. The module-theoretic cousin of this material — primary decomposition of submodules and the associated primes of a module — appears as the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|Primary Decomposition Theorem]] in [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4|Modules II]]; this chapter does the *ideal* version, which is the case of the zero submodule of $R/I$, and the two are worth reading side by side.

# Concept Map

## §10.1 Primary Ideals and Decomposition

- **[[Def - Primary Ideal]]**
	- An ideal $\mathfrak{q} \subsetneq R$ is **primary** if every zero-divisor of $R/\mathfrak{q}$ is nilpotent — equivalently, $xy \in \mathfrak{q}$ forces $x \in \mathfrak{q}$ or $y^n \in \mathfrak{q}$ for some $n \geq 1$. This is "prime with nilpotents allowed": prime says $xy \in \mathfrak{q} \Rightarrow x \in \mathfrak{q}$ or $y \in \mathfrak{q}$, primary relaxes the second clause to $y \in \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$. The radical $\mathfrak{p} = \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$ is then automatically prime — the smallest prime containing $\mathfrak{q}$ — and one calls $\mathfrak{q}$ **$\mathfrak{p}$-primary**. In $\mathbb{Z}$ the primary ideals are exactly $(0)$ and the prime powers $(p^n)$; so $(9)$ is primary (not radical) but $(6)$ is radical and not primary.

- **[[Def - Irreducible Ideal]]**
	- An ideal $I \subsetneq R$ is **irreducible** if it is not the intersection of two strictly larger ideals: $I = J_1 \cap J_2$ forces $I = J_1$ or $I = J_2$. This is the lattice-theoretic notion of "indecomposable" applied to the lattice of ideals, and it is the *engine* behind existence of primary decomposition — every ideal in a Noetherian ring is a finite intersection of irreducibles (a Noetherian-induction argument), and every irreducible ideal turns out to be primary. Irreducible is strictly stronger than primary: $(X^2, XY, Y^2) = (X,Y)^2$ is $(X,Y)$-primary but reducible, equal to $(X^2, Y) \cap (X, Y^2)$.

- **[[Thm - Irreducible Ideals are Primary]]**
	- In any ring, an irreducible ideal is primary; this is the bridge that converts the easy *existence of irreducible decompositions* into the *existence of primary decompositions*. The proof passes to $R/I$, where irreducibility says $(0)$ is irreducible, and studies the ascending chain of annihilators $\operatorname{Ann}(y) \subseteq \operatorname{Ann}(y^2) \subseteq \cdots$; the Noetherian (or, in the general statement, the irreducibility-plus-stabilisation) hypothesis makes the chain stabilise, and a short manipulation forces a zero-divisor to be nilpotent. The converse fails: $(X,Y)^2$ is primary but not irreducible.

- **[[Thm - Primary Decomposition Exists in a Noetherian Ring (Lasker-Noether)]]**
	- In a [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian ring]], *every* ideal $I$ admits a primary decomposition $I = \mathfrak{q}_1 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{q}_n$, and hence a minimal one (distinct radicals, no redundant component). The proof is two clean steps: every ideal is a finite intersection of irreducible ideals (Noetherian induction on the poset of ideals that fail this), and every irreducible ideal is primary. Minimality is then achieved by grouping components with equal radical — using that a finite intersection of $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals is again $\mathfrak{p}$-primary — and deleting redundant terms. This is the Lasker–Noether theorem; existence can fail in non-Noetherian rings (the ring $C[0,1]$ of continuous functions has $(0)$ with no primary decomposition).

> [!tip] Unlocked: Irreducible components of an algebraic set *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Specialise to $R = k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ with $k$ algebraically closed. The minimal primes of $I$ are the prime ideals $\mathfrak{p}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{p}_t$, and the vanishing locus decomposes as $V(I) = V(\mathfrak{p}_1) \cup \cdots \cup V(\mathfrak{p}_t)$, where each $V(\mathfrak{p}_i)$ is **irreducible** — it cannot be written as a union of two smaller closed sets, precisely because $\mathfrak{p}_i$ is prime. These $V(\mathfrak{p}_i)$ are the **irreducible components** of $V(I)$, and primary decomposition is the algebra that produces them. The existence half — Lasker–Noether — is the statement that *every algebraic set has finitely many irreducible components*, the foundational finiteness theorem of the subject. The full Nullstellensatz dictionary linking radical ideals to varieties is developed in [[Commutative Algebra VII — Noether Normalization and the Nullstellensatz]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Embedded points and non-reduced scheme structure *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The primary components $\mathfrak{q}_i$ whose radicals are *not* minimal carry information no variety can see. For $I = (X^2, XY)$, the locus $V(I)$ is just the line $X = 0$, yet $I$ has an extra associated prime $(X,Y)$ — a point sitting *embedded* inside the line. Passing to $R/I$ as a **scheme** rather than a variety, this records a "thickening" or **embedded point** at the origin: the origin appears with extra, non-reduced infinitesimal structure (here, a copy of the dual numbers $k[Y]/(Y^2)$ transverse to the line). The systematic theory of this thickening is the theory of **schemes**, where $\operatorname{Spec}(R/I)$ remembers nilpotents and primary decomposition becomes the decomposition of a scheme into its components with multiplicities.

- **[[Ex - A primary decomposition in k[X,Y] with an embedded prime]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute two distinct minimal primary decompositions of $I = (X^2, XY) \subseteq k[X,Y]$: $I = (X) \cap (X^2, XY, Y^2)$ and $I = (X) \cap (X^2, Y)$. Verify each component is primary, identify the radicals $(X)$ and $(X,Y)$, and exhibit the non-uniqueness of the $(X,Y)$-primary component. This is the canonical worked example of the whole chapter.

- **[[Ex - Powers of a maximal ideal are primary]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove that if $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}} = \mathfrak{m}$ is *maximal* then $\mathfrak{q}$ is primary (hence $\mathfrak{m}$-primary), and deduce that every power $\mathfrak{m}^n$ of a maximal ideal is $\mathfrak{m}$-primary. The key is that $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$ maximal forces $R/\mathfrak{q}$ to be a local ring with nilpotent maximal ideal, so every non-unit is nilpotent. This is the cleanest large supply of primary ideals.

- **[[Ex - A primary ideal need not be a prime power]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show $\mathfrak{q} = (X, Y^2) \subseteq k[X,Y]$ is primary but is *not* a power of any prime ideal, and (the dual pathology) that $\mathfrak{p}^2$ for $\mathfrak{p} = (\bar X, \bar Z) \subseteq k[X,Y,Z]/(XY - Z^2)$ is *not* primary even though $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime. Together these sever the naive identification "primary = prime power" in both directions.

## §10.2 Uniqueness and Associated Primes

- **[[Def - Associated and Minimal Primes]]**
	- Given a minimal primary decomposition $I = \mathfrak{q}_1 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{q}_n$ with $\mathfrak{p}_i = \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_i}$, the set $\operatorname{Ass}(I) = \{\mathfrak{p}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{p}_n\}$ is the set of **associated primes** of $I$; the First Uniqueness Theorem makes it independent of the decomposition. The minimal members are the **isolated** (or **minimal**) primes — exactly the primes minimal over $I$, equivalently the minimal primes of $R/I$ — and the non-minimal members are the **embedded** primes. A **minimal prime over $I$** is a prime $\mathfrak{p} \supseteq I$ with no prime strictly between $I$ and $\mathfrak{p}$; these are the primes "the radical $\sqrt I$ sees", while embedded primes are extra structure $I$ carries beyond its radical.

- **[[Thm - Uniqueness of the Associated Primes (First Uniqueness Theorem)]]**
	- For any ideal $I$ with a minimal primary decomposition $I = \bigcap \mathfrak{q}_i$, $\mathfrak{p}_i = \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_i}$, the set of radicals is recovered intrinsically as $\{\mathfrak{p}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{p}_n\} = \{\sqrt{(I : x)} : x \in R\} \cap \operatorname{Spec} R$. Hence $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$ does not depend on the chosen decomposition — it is an invariant of $I$. The proof computes $\sqrt{(I : x)}$ by intersecting the colon through the decomposition: $(I : x) = \bigcap (\mathfrak{q}_i : x)$, and $\sqrt{(\mathfrak{q}_i : x)}$ is $\mathfrak{p}_i$ when $x \notin \mathfrak{q}_i$ and $R$ otherwise. The colon ideal is the probe that detects exactly which primes are attached.

- **[[Thm - Minimal Primes are Finite in a Noetherian Ring]]**
	- A radical ideal in a Noetherian ring is a finite intersection of prime ideals, and consequently every ideal in a Noetherian ring has only finitely many minimal primes (and $\operatorname{Spec} R$ has finitely many irreducible components). The proof is a model Noetherian-induction: the set of radical ideals that are *not* finite intersections of primes, if nonempty, has a maximal element $I$, which cannot be prime, so $\sqrt{(I+(x))} \cap \sqrt{(I+(y))}$ properly exceeds $I$ on both sides for a suitable $xy \in I$ — contradicting maximality. This is the finiteness that makes "irreducible components" a finite list.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The reduced subscheme and the radical *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The radical $\sqrt{I} = \mathfrak{p}_1 \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{p}_t$ — the intersection of just the *isolated* primes — is the ideal of $V(I)$ as a set, and $R/\sqrt{I}$ is the **reduced** ring (no nilpotents). Geometrically this is the **reduced subscheme** $V(I)_{\mathrm{red}}$: you keep the point-set and erase all infinitesimal thickening. The map $I \mapsto \sqrt{I}$ throws away every embedded prime and every multiplicity, retaining only the irreducible components as honest varieties. That a radical ideal in a Noetherian ring is a *finite* intersection of primes is exactly "a variety has finitely many irreducible components", and the resulting bijection between radical ideals and finite incomparable families of primes is the algebraic backbone of the variety–ideal correspondence.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Associated primes and the support of a module *(from Algebraic Geometry / Homological Algebra)*
> Reading $I$ as the annihilator of $R/I$, the associated primes $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$ become the **associated primes of the module** $R/I$ — the primes $\mathfrak{p}$ for which $\mathfrak{p} = \operatorname{Ann}(m)$ for some $m$, equivalently for which $R/\mathfrak{p}$ embeds in $R/I$. These are precisely the points where the module "has embedded structure", and they control the zero-divisors of $R/I$ (their union is the set of zero-divisors). The isolated ones are the generic points of the **support** $\operatorname{Supp}(R/I) = V(I)$, while the embedded ones are extra associated points. This module-level reading, developed in [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4|Modules II]] and downstream in homological algebra, is the modern home of the theory: primary decomposition of ideals is the special case of the zero submodule.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The goals of this chapter cluster into five recurring types. The first is **certifying an ideal is (or is not) primary** — showing every zero-divisor of $R/\mathfrak{q}$ is nilpotent, or producing a witness $xy \in \mathfrak{q}$ with $x \notin \mathfrak{q}$, $y \notin \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$ that breaks primariness. The standard levers are "$\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$ maximal $\Rightarrow$ $\mathfrak{q}$ primary" and the quotient criterion. The second is **computing a primary decomposition** — writing a given $I$ as an explicit intersection $\bigcap \mathfrak{q}_i$, then verifying each $\mathfrak{q}_i$ is primary and the intersection is correct; the chapter's running example $(X^2, XY)$ is the model. The third, the chapter's signature, is **extracting the associated primes** $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$, and sorting them into isolated and embedded — done by computing colon ideals $\sqrt{(I:x)}$ and applying the First Uniqueness Theorem. The fourth is **finiteness and decomposition into components** — proving $V(I)$ has finitely many irreducible components, that a radical ideal is a finite intersection of incomparable primes, and identifying those primes as the minimal primes of $R/I$. The fifth is **distinguishing $I$ from $\sqrt{I}$** — quantifying the extra structure $I$ carries beyond its underlying set, which is exactly the embedded primes and the multiplicities in the isolated components. These are the natural targets because the chapter is fundamentally a *factorisation-and-invariants* theory: every theorem either produces a factorisation or isolates the part of it that is canonical.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **The ring is Noetherian** — this is the master hypothesis, and it is used in exactly one way: to run an *induction on the poset of ideals* (take a maximal counterexample, derive a contradiction), which is how both "every ideal is a finite intersection of irreducibles" and "a radical ideal is a finite intersection of primes" are proved. **An ideal is given as a primary, or as a primary decomposition** — then the move is to take radicals (landing on the associated primes) and to probe with colon ideals. **A radical is maximal** — instantly upgrading a candidate to a primary ideal, the cheapest route to primariness. **A prime $\mathfrak{p}$ is named** — switching on the localization $R_{\mathfrak{p}}$, in which the isolated component $\mathfrak{q}_i = I^{ec}$ becomes a contraction and hence canonical. **A geometric figure is presented as $V(I)$** — routing through "minimal primes $=$ irreducible components" and "$\sqrt I$ $=$ the reduced structure". The discipline is to match source to target along the chapter's spine: a "compute $\operatorname{Ass}$" target routes through colon ideals and the First Uniqueness Theorem; a "components are finite" target routes through Noetherian induction; a "is this primary" target routes through the radical-maximal shortcut or the quotient criterion. The [[Commutative Algebra IX — Primary Decomposition#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every primary-decomposition problem is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

1. **Pass to the quotient $R/\mathfrak{q}$ and read off the definition.** "$\mathfrak{q}$ primary" *means* "in $R/\mathfrak{q}$, every zero-divisor is nilpotent", and "$\mathfrak{q}$ prime" means "in $R/\mathfrak{q}$, the only zero-divisor is $0$". So any question about a single ideal becomes a question about zero-divisors and nilpotents in a quotient ring, where you can compute. *Trigger:* you must decide whether $\mathfrak{q}$ is primary/prime/radical. *Pattern:* "compute $R/\mathfrak{q}$ explicitly, then inspect its zero-divisors".

2. **Take radicals to find the attached prime.** For a [[Def - Primary Ideal|primary ideal]] $\mathfrak{q}$, $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$ is the unique prime it belongs to, and $\sqrt{\bigcap \mathfrak{q}_i} = \bigcap \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}_i}$ lets you read the radical of a whole decomposition. Use $\sqrt{I + J} = \sqrt{\sqrt I + \sqrt J}$ and $\sqrt{I^n} = \sqrt I$ to simplify radicals of explicit ideals. *Trigger:* you have a primary ideal or a decomposition and want its prime(s). *Pattern:* "the associated primes are the radicals of the components".

3. **Probe with a colon ideal $(I : x)$.** The colon $(I : x) = \{r : rx \in I\}$ is the central computational tool: it distributes over intersections, $(I : x) = \bigcap (\mathfrak{q}_i : x)$, and its radical $\sqrt{(I:x)}$ is an intersection of exactly those $\mathfrak{p}_i$ with $x \notin \mathfrak{q}_i$. Choosing $x$ in all components but one isolates a single associated prime. *Trigger:* you want to extract $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$ or test which primes are attached. *Pattern:* "compute $\sqrt{(I:x)}$ for cleverly chosen $x$ to peel off one prime at a time".

4. **Upgrade to primary via a maximal radical.** If $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}} = \mathfrak{m}$ is [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|maximal]], then $\mathfrak{q}$ is automatically $\mathfrak{m}$-primary — no zero-divisor check needed. In particular every power $\mathfrak{m}^n$ of a maximal ideal is $\mathfrak{m}$-primary. *Trigger:* you suspect an ideal is primary and its radical is a maximal ideal. *Pattern:* "$\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$ maximal $\Rightarrow$ $\mathfrak{q}$ primary, instantly".

5. **Group same-radical components to reach a minimal decomposition.** A finite intersection of $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals (same $\mathfrak{p}$) is again $\mathfrak{p}$-primary, so you may merge any components sharing a radical, then delete any component that already contains the intersection of the rest. This is how an arbitrary primary decomposition is refined to a *minimal* one. *Trigger:* you have a decomposition with repeated radicals or redundant terms. *Pattern:* "merge equal radicals, drop redundant components".

6. **Decompose into irreducibles by Noetherian induction.** To prove *existence* of a decomposition, consider the set of ideals lacking the desired form; if nonempty it has a maximal element by the [[Def - Noetherian Ring|ascending chain condition]], which (being non-irreducible) splits as $J_1 \cap J_2$ with both strictly larger, hence both decomposable, hence so is their intersection — contradiction. *Trigger:* "prove every ideal is a finite intersection of [irreducibles / primes]". *Pattern:* "take a maximal counterexample; split it; derive a contradiction".

7. **Localize to canonicalise an isolated component.** For an isolated (minimal) prime $\mathfrak{p}_i$, the corresponding primary component is $\mathfrak{q}_i = I^{ec}$ along $R \to R_{\mathfrak{p}_i}$ — the contraction of the extension — which depends only on $I$, not on the decomposition. This is the proof that isolated components are unique. *Trigger:* "show this component is canonical" or "compute the $\mathfrak{p}$-primary part". *Pattern:* "$\mathfrak{q}_i$ is the contraction of $I R_{\mathfrak{p}_i}$".

8. **Use that $\bigcap \mathfrak{a}_i \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$ forces some $\mathfrak{a}_i \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$ (prime avoidance, dual form).** When a prime contains an intersection of ideals, it contains one of them; when it *equals* the intersection, it equals one of them. This is the workhorse for "minimal primes of $\bigcap \mathfrak{q}_i$ are the minimal $\mathfrak{p}_i$". *Trigger:* a prime sits over an intersection. *Pattern:* "$\bigcap \mathfrak{a}_i \subseteq \mathfrak{p} \Rightarrow \mathfrak{a}_j \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$ for some $j$".

9. **Translate to geometry via $V(-)$ and $I(-)$.** Over $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ ($k$ algebraically closed), $V(I) = V(\sqrt I) = \bigcup V(\mathfrak{p}_i)$ with $\mathfrak{p}_i$ the minimal primes, and $I(V(I)) = \sqrt I$. So set-theoretic questions about the figure are radical questions, and component questions are minimal-prime questions. *Trigger:* the problem is about a variety or its components. *Pattern:* "components $\leftrightarrow$ minimal primes; the set $\leftrightarrow$ the radical".

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming primary means "a power of a prime"
> The integer case is seductive: in $\mathbb{Z}$, primary ideals are exactly the prime powers $(p^n)$, so one expects "primary $=$ prime power" in general. Both inclusions fail. The ideal $\mathfrak{q} = (X, Y^2) \subseteq k[X,Y]$ is $(X,Y)$-primary (its quotient is $k[Y]/(Y^2)$, where every zero-divisor is a multiple of $\bar Y$, hence nilpotent) yet is *not* a power of any prime — the only candidate prime is $\mathfrak{m} = (X,Y)$, but $\mathfrak{m}^2 \subsetneq \mathfrak{q} \subsetneq \mathfrak{m}$, so $\mathfrak{q}$ sits strictly between consecutive powers. Conversely a prime power can fail to be primary: in $k[X,Y,Z]/(XY - Z^2)$ the prime $\mathfrak{p} = (\bar X, \bar Z)$ has $\mathfrak{p}^2$ *not* primary, because $\bar X \bar Y = \bar Z^2 \in \mathfrak{p}^2$ exhibits $\bar Y$ as a zero-divisor of $R/\mathfrak{p}^2$ that is not nilpotent. The repair: "primary $=$ prime power" is correct exactly in a **principal ideal domain** (more generally when the ring is locally a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]], i.e. a Dedekind domain), and in general the correct statement is only "primary $\Rightarrow$ radical is prime".

> [!warning] 2. Believing the primary decomposition is unique
> Because integer factorisation is unique and the field-of-fractions case is so rigid, one expects $I = \bigcap \mathfrak{q}_i$ to be a unique list. It is not. The same ideal $(X^2, XY)$ has $(X) \cap (X^2, XY, Y^2)$ and $(X) \cap (X^2, Y)$ as *distinct* minimal primary decompositions — the $(X,Y)$-primary component is genuinely different in the two. What *is* unique is the set of associated primes (First Uniqueness Theorem) and the *isolated* components (those over minimal primes). The repair: uniqueness holds for $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$ always, and for the embedded components *never* in general — the embedded component over a non-minimal prime can be perturbed by adding high powers of the embedded prime's elements. Treat "the primary decomposition" as ill-defined; speak of *a* minimal decomposition, and of *the* associated primes.

> [!warning] 3. Reading the radical $\sqrt I$ and concluding you know $I$
> Since $\sqrt I = \bigcap (\text{minimal primes})$ records the irreducible components, it is tempting to think $\sqrt I$ — equivalently the variety $V(I)$ — determines $I$. It does not: $I$ and $\sqrt I$ have the same minimal primes and the same vanishing locus, but $I$ may carry embedded primes and multiplicities that $\sqrt I$ erases. For $I = (X^2, XY)$, $\sqrt I = (X)$ is just the line, losing the embedded point $(X,Y)$ and the doubling along $X$. The repair: $\sqrt I$ determines $I$ exactly when $I$ is *radical* (then the decomposition is the unique intersection of its isolated primes, with no embedded primes and no multiplicity) — equivalently when $R/I$ is reduced. Passing $I \mapsto \sqrt I$ is precisely the loss of all scheme-theoretic, non-reduced information.

> [!warning] 4. Assuming every ideal in every ring has a primary decomposition
> The Lasker–Noether theorem is so clean that one forgets its hypothesis. Existence of a primary decomposition is *not* automatic — it is a theorem about **Noetherian** rings. In the ring $C[0,1]$ of continuous real functions on $[0,1]$, the zero ideal has infinitely many minimal primes (one can separate points arbitrarily finely), so it cannot be a finite intersection of primary ideals; $(0)$ has no primary decomposition there. The repair: assume the ring is Noetherian, or assume outright that the particular $I$ has *some* primary decomposition (the uniqueness theorems need only that hypothesis, not Noetherianity). Having a primary decomposition is a property of $I$, and it is finiteness of the minimal-prime set that it secretly demands.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any primary-decomposition problem is to identify which of the chapter's three questions is being asked, because each has a characteristic route.

If the problem **asks whether a given ideal is primary** (or prime, or radical), the controlling move is to *pass to the quotient and inspect its zero-divisors and nilpotents*. By definition $\mathfrak{q}$ is primary exactly when every zero-divisor of $R/\mathfrak{q}$ is nilpotent, prime when the only zero-divisor is $0$, radical when the only nilpotent is $0$ — so compute $R/\mathfrak{q}$ as concretely as possible and read these off. Two shortcuts cut most of the work. First, if $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$ is *maximal*, $\mathfrak{q}$ is automatically primary (operation 4) — this disposes of every power of a maximal ideal in one line. Second, to prove an ideal is *not* primary, you do not inspect all zero-divisors; you exhibit a single witness $xy \in \mathfrak{q}$ with $x \notin \mathfrak{q}$ and $y \notin \sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$. The running counterexample $\mathfrak{p}^2$ in $k[X,Y,Z]/(XY-Z^2)$ is found exactly this way: the relation $\bar X \bar Y = \bar Z^2$ hands you the witness for free. The recurring trap is to conflate "primary" with "prime power"; resist it and compute the quotient.

If the problem **asks for the primary decomposition itself**, the strategy is *guess the components, then verify*. There is no purely mechanical algorithm at this level, but the geometry guides the guess: the minimal primes are the irreducible components of $V(I)$, so write those down first (they will be the isolated components, up to multiplicity), then look for the embedded primes by asking where the figure "doubles up" or has lower-dimensional thickening. For $(X^2, XY)$, the locus is the line $(X)$ (isolated), and the generators' common factor $X$ vanishing to order two along the $Y$-axis signals an embedded $(X,Y)$. Having guessed $I = \bigcap \mathfrak{q}_i$, verify primariness of each $\mathfrak{q}_i$ via the quotient criterion, verify the intersection equals $I$ (usually a short generator chase, as in the $(X^2,XY)$ computation), then refine to minimal by merging equal radicals and dropping redundancies (operation 5). Existence in the abstract — that *some* decomposition exists — is never in doubt over a Noetherian ring; that is the Lasker–Noether guarantee.

If the problem **asks for the associated primes**, or to sort them into isolated and embedded, you are in the domain of the First Uniqueness Theorem, and the strategy is a fixed computation with *colon ideals*. The associated primes are $\{\sqrt{(I:x)} : x \in R, \sqrt{(I:x)} \text{ prime}\}$, so the technique is to choose $x$ that lies in all primary components but one — then $(I:x)$ collapses to (a $\mathfrak{p}_i$-primary ideal whose radical is) the single surviving prime $\mathfrak{p}_i$. Concretely: compute $(I : x) = \bigcap (\mathfrak{q}_i : x)$, recall that $(\mathfrak{q}_i : x) = R$ when $x \in \mathfrak{q}_i$ and is $\mathfrak{p}_i$-primary when $x \notin \mathfrak{q}_i$ (operation 3), and take radicals. To separate isolated from embedded, find the minimal elements of $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$ under inclusion — those are the isolated primes, equal to the minimal primes of $R/I$; the rest are embedded. The isolated *components* are then pinned down by localizing at the isolated prime (operation 7), while the embedded components are left genuinely ambiguous.

A fourth family **concerns finiteness and geometry** — proving $V(I)$ has finitely many components, that a radical ideal is a finite intersection of primes, or identifying the components concretely. These route through Noetherian induction (operation 6) for the finiteness, and through "$\bigcap \mathfrak{a}_i \subseteq \mathfrak{p} \Rightarrow$ some $\mathfrak{a}_i \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$" (operation 8) to show the minimal primes are exactly the components and are incomparable. The translation dictionary $V(I) = V(\sqrt I) = \bigcup V(\mathfrak{p}_i)$ (operation 9) is what makes the algebraic finiteness a geometric statement.

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "how much more does the ideal $I$ remember than its variety $V(I)$ does?"** The variety remembers only $\sqrt I$, i.e. the isolated primes as a set. The ideal $I$ remembers, in addition, the multiplicities along each isolated component (encoded in the isolated primary component) and the embedded subvarieties (the embedded primes). Decide whether the problem is about the figure (work with $\sqrt I$ and minimal primes) or about the ideal's finer structure (work with the full decomposition and the colon ideals), and the right tool is determined.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Primary Decomposition Exists in a Noetherian Ring (Lasker-Noether)]]**: every ideal of a Noetherian ring is a finite intersection of primary ideals. This is the most reused fact because it *licenses* the entire theory — it guarantees that the objects the uniqueness theorems analyse actually exist, so one may always speak of "a minimal primary decomposition of $I$". Its standard uses are existential (asserting components exist before manipulating them) and structural (reducing a question about a general ideal to questions about its primary pieces). Reach for it whenever you want to argue "decompose $I$ and treat each component", and remember its two-step engine — finite intersection of irreducibles, then irreducible-is-primary — which is itself reusable as a proof template.

- **[[Thm - Uniqueness of the Associated Primes (First Uniqueness Theorem)]]**: $\operatorname{Ass}(I) = \{\sqrt{(I:x)} : x \in R\} \cap \operatorname{Spec} R$ is an invariant of $I$. Its reusability is that it converts a question about a *non-canonical* object (the decomposition) into a question about a *canonical* one (the associated primes), computable purely from $I$ via colon ideals. Use it to define $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$ unambiguously, to detect embedded primes (an associated prime that is not minimal), and to characterise the zero-divisors of $R/I$ (their union is exactly $\bigcup \operatorname{Ass}(I)$). It is the bridge from "I computed *a* decomposition" to "here is what every decomposition agrees on".

- **The colon ideal $(I : x)$ and the identity $(I : x) = \bigcap (\mathfrak{q}_i : x)$**: the single most-used computation in the chapter. Its reusability is mechanical — it turns the search for associated primes into a finite radical computation, and it is the same tool that proves a $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideal stays $\mathfrak{p}$-primary under colon. The trigger is any appearance of $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$, any "which primes are attached", and (in module language) any annihilator computation. Combined with "$(\mathfrak{q} : x)$ is $\mathfrak{p}$-primary for $x \notin \mathfrak{q}$", it is the entire proof of the First Uniqueness Theorem.

- **"$\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}}$ maximal $\Rightarrow$ $\mathfrak{q}$ primary"**: the cheapest source of primary ideals. Its reusability is that it removes the zero-divisor check entirely whenever the radical happens to be a maximal ideal — which covers all powers $\mathfrak{m}^n$ of maximal ideals, the most common primary ideals in practice (local rings, points of varieties, $\mathbb{Z}/(p^n)$). Use it to populate decompositions with primary components for free, and to recognise that the primary ideals supported at a *closed point* are exactly the $\mathfrak{m}$-primary ones.

- **"$\sqrt I = \bigcap (\text{minimal primes})$, finitely many in a Noetherian ring"**: the radical-as-intersection-of-isolated-primes identity. Its reusability is geometric and structural at once: it identifies the variety's irreducible components, it certifies their finiteness, and it pins down exactly which information survives the passage $I \mapsto \sqrt I$. Reach for it whenever the problem is about the *set* $V(I)$ rather than the ideal, and remember its companion — the bijection between radical ideals and finite incomparable families of primes — which is the algebraic form of the variety–ideal correspondence.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — primary decomposition is the decomposition of a figure into irreducible components plus its embedded structure.** To an ideal $I \subseteq k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ one attaches the vanishing locus $V(I) = \{a : f(a) = 0 \ \forall f \in I\}$, and the minimal primes $\mathfrak{p}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{p}_t$ of $I$ correspond to the **irreducible components** $V(\mathfrak{p}_i)$ — the maximal pieces of $V(I)$ that cannot be split into two proper closed subsets, irreducibility of the component being literally primality of $\mathfrak{p}_i$. The radical $\sqrt I = \bigcap \mathfrak{p}_i$ is the ideal of the figure as a point-set, so $V(I)$ "sees" only $\sqrt I$. But the full ideal $I$ records two further pieces of data the figure cannot: the *multiplicity* with which $V(I)$ runs along each component (encoded in how the isolated primary component $\mathfrak{q}_i$ thickens $\mathfrak{p}_i$), and the **embedded components** — lower-dimensional subvarieties $V(\mathfrak{p})$ for embedded primes $\mathfrak{p}$, glued inside a bigger component. The example to hold is $I = (X^2, XY) = (X) \cap (X,Y)^2$: the figure is the line $X = 0$, a single irreducible component over $(X)$, but $I$ carries the embedded prime $(X,Y)$ — a "fat point" at the origin where the line is thickened transversally. Primary decomposition is the algebra that separates the line from the fat point, and it is why **schemes**, which remember the full ring $R/I$ rather than just the set $V(I)$, are the correct ambient category: a scheme records the embedded points and the multiplicities, a variety does not.

2. **Number theory and Dedekind domains — primary decomposition generalises prime factorisation, and recovers it exactly when there is no embedded or non-reduced structure.** In $\mathbb{Z}$, $(90) = (2) \cap (3^2) \cap (5)$ is the prime factorisation $90 = 2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5$ written as an intersection of $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals, the components $(p^a)$ being exactly the prime powers. This is clean precisely because $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]]: there are no embedded primes (every nonzero prime is maximal, so all associated primes are isolated), and the primary ideals *are* the prime powers. The same holds in any Dedekind domain — the ring of integers of a number field, or the coordinate ring of a smooth curve — where every nonzero ideal factors uniquely as a product of prime powers, and primary decomposition collapses to that unique factorisation. The general theory is what you need the moment the ring has dimension $\geq 2$ or a singular point: then primary decomposition is the *only* available factorisation, and its non-uniqueness is the price of working beyond Dedekind domains. This connection is developed fully in [[Commutative Algebra XIII — Dedekind Domains and DVRs]].

3. **Module theory — the ideal version is the special case of the zero submodule, and associated primes live most naturally at the module level.** Reading $I$ as $\operatorname{Ann}(R/I)$, the associated primes $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$ become the **associated primes of the module** $M = R/I$: the primes $\mathfrak{p}$ such that $\mathfrak{p} = \operatorname{Ann}(m)$ for some $m \in M$, equivalently such that $R/\mathfrak{p}$ embeds in $M$. The [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition theorem for modules]] in [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4|Modules II]] decomposes a submodule $N \subseteq M$ as an intersection of primary submodules, and the ideal theory of this chapter is exactly the case $N = (0) \subseteq R/I$. The module framing is more flexible and is the one homological algebra uses: it makes the union of associated primes equal the set of zero-divisors, identifies the isolated associated primes as the generic points of the support $\operatorname{Supp}(M) = V(\operatorname{Ann} M)$, and is the setting in which depth and the Auslander–Buchsbaum machinery are stated. The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID, where the elementary divisors are exactly the $\mathfrak{p}$-primary pieces, is the one-dimensional shadow of this whole picture.

4. **Lattice theory — irreducible decomposition is a general phenomenon, and primary decomposition is its arithmetically-refined form.** Stripped of ring structure, the statement "every element is a finite meet of meet-irreducible elements" is a fact about the lattice of ideals satisfying the ascending chain condition, proved by the same maximal-counterexample induction. What ring theory adds is the identification "meet-irreducible $\Rightarrow$ primary" ([[Thm - Irreducible Ideals are Primary]]), which upgrades a purely order-theoretic decomposition into one whose pieces carry arithmetic meaning (a prime and a multiplicity). The non-uniqueness of primary decomposition has a lattice-theoretic shadow too: meet-irreducible decompositions are not unique in a general lattice, and the associated primes are the invariant that survives — the analogue of the well-defined "join-irreducible content" in a distributive lattice. This is why the existence half of Lasker–Noether is so robust (it needs only the chain condition) while the uniqueness half is delicate (it needs the prime structure).

# Insights

**The unifying frame: an ideal is a variety plus bookkeeping, and primary decomposition is the act of separating the two.** Every result in this chapter is one facet of a single idea — that an ideal $I$ contains strictly more information than the set $V(I)$ it cuts out, and the surplus is organised by the primes attached to $I$. The variety is recovered from the *isolated* primes (the minimal primes, the irreducible components); the multiplicities are recovered from the isolated primary *components* (how much each component is thickened along itself); and the embedded subvarieties are recovered from the *embedded* primes. Read in this light, the two uniqueness theorems are saying exactly which parts of the bookkeeping are forced by $I$: the *list* of attached primes is forced (First Uniqueness), the multiplicities along isolated components are forced (Second Uniqueness), but the embedded components are free. The slogan to carry is that **$\sqrt I$ is the photograph and $I$ is the X-ray** — the photograph shows the figure, the X-ray shows the figure together with the internal density and the hidden inclusions, and primary decomposition is the development process that turns the X-ray into a labelled diagram.

**The true name of a primary ideal: it is a prime, blurred by a single direction of nilpotence.** The official definition — "every zero-divisor of $R/\mathfrak{q}$ is nilpotent" — is correct but unilluminating. The operational true name is: **a $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideal is one whose quotient $R/\mathfrak{q}$ looks like a domain $R/\mathfrak{p}$ that has been infinitesimally thickened — its reduced part is the domain $R/\mathfrak{p}$, and everything non-reduced is nilpotent.** This is why $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}} = \mathfrak{p}$ is forced to be prime (the reduced quotient is a domain), and why $\mathfrak{m}^n$ is the cleanest example (its quotient is a local ring whose maximal ideal is nilpotent, so it is a single point thickened to order $n$). Knowing this true name tells you instantly how to test primariness — compute the reduced quotient and check that everything you quotiented away is nilpotent — and it explains the failure mode in the counterexamples: $\mathfrak{p}^2$ fails to be primary in $k[X,Y,Z]/(XY-Z^2)$ precisely because the singularity of the cone introduces a *second* direction of zero-divisors ($\bar Y$) that is not nilpotent, so the quotient is not "one prime thickened" but two primes touching.

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "find the associated primes" means "compute $\sqrt{(I:x)}$ for $x$ chosen to kill all components but one".** When the target is $\operatorname{Ass}(I)$, the colon ideal should fire automatically, because the First Uniqueness Theorem guarantees $\operatorname{Ass}(I) = \{\sqrt{(I:x)}\} \cap \operatorname{Spec} R$, and the colon distributes through any decomposition as $(I:x) = \bigcap (\mathfrak{q}_i : x)$. The reaction is to *pick $x$ inside every primary component except the $i$-th* — then $(\mathfrak{q}_j : x) = R$ for $j \neq i$ and only $\mathfrak{p}_i$ survives in the radical. The complementary pattern is the existence argument: when the goal is "every ideal is a finite intersection of [irreducibles / primes]", the reaction is "Noetherian induction — take a maximal counterexample and split it". These two reflexes — *colon-ideal probing* for the invariants and *maximal-counterexample induction* for the existence — between them solve the large majority of the chapter's problems, and both are pure consequences of the two structural pillars: the prime-detecting power of the colon, and the ascending chain condition.

**Inheritance — where does the embedded prime come from?** An embedded prime is never an accident of bad bookkeeping; it is forced by the geometry of the generators. The reflex worth installing is to ask, of any non-radical ideal, *which generator relation produces the embedded structure*. For $(X^2, XY)$, the embedded prime $(X,Y)$ arises because both generators share the factor $X$ and the "leftover" $(X, Y)$ is where they fail to cut out a clean line — the origin is where the two generators' common factor forces extra vanishing. More generally, embedded primes appear at the *singular locus* and at *intersections of components*, exactly the places where the naive set-theoretic figure hides infinitesimal data. Tracking the embedded prime back to its generating relation is the geometric content of the algebra, and it is what makes primary decomposition a computational tool for finding singularities and multiplicities rather than a mere existence theorem.
