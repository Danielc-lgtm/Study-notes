# Problem Statement

Let $\varphi : A\to B$ be a [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]], with [[Def - Extension and Contraction of Ideals|contraction]] $\mathfrak{b}^c = \varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ and [[Def - Extension and Contraction of Ideals|extension]] $\mathfrak{a}^e = (\varphi(\mathfrak{a}))$. Prove (Example Sheet 1, Q3):

1. $\mathfrak{b}^c$ is an ideal of $A$, but $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$ need not be an ideal of $B$ (give an example).
2. The round-trip inequalities $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{ec}$ and $\mathfrak{b}\supseteq\mathfrak{b}^{ce}$, and the stabilisations $\mathfrak{a}^e = \mathfrak{a}^{ece}$, $\mathfrak{b}^c = \mathfrak{b}^{cec}$.
3. **Characterisation:** $\mathfrak{a}$ is contracted $\iff\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$; $\mathfrak{b}$ is extended $\iff\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{b}^{ce}$.
4. **The bijection:** $e$ and $c$ are mutually inverse, inclusion-preserving bijections
$$\{\text{contracted ideals of } A\}\;\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\;\{\text{extended ideals of } B\}.$$

The key abstract fact is the **Galois-connection (adjunction) identity**: $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\iff\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}^c$, from which both round-trip inequalities and the bijection follow formally.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *structural-identities* problem: establish the formal machinery of a Galois connection between two ideal lattices. Per the [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization#Sources and Targets|topic sources]], tracking ideals through extension and contraction is a recurring task, and the payoff is the bijection that, for the localization map, becomes the prime correspondence.

**Assumption pattern.** No special hypotheses on $\varphi$ — the entire result is formal, holding for *any* ring map. The recognisable structure is that $(-)^e$ and $(-)^c$ are order-preserving maps satisfying the adjunction $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\iff\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}^c$; once that single identity is established, everything else is abstract-nonsense bookkeeping about Galois connections.

**Theorem routing.** Establish the adjunction identity from the definitions; deduce the unit $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{ec}$ (set $\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{a}^e$) and counit $\mathfrak{b}^{ce}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$ (set $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{b}^c$); deduce stabilisation $\mathfrak{a}^e = \mathfrak{a}^{ece}$ by applying $e$ to the unit and the counit to $\mathfrak{a}^e$; deduce the contracted/extended characterisations as fixed points; assemble the bijection. The example for part 1 is $\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}$.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious move is to *prove the single adjunction identity first and derive everything formally*, rather than verifying each of the four parts by separate ideal manipulations. The adjunction $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\iff\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}^c$ is the master fact; the unit, counit, stabilisation, fixed-point characterisation, and bijection are *automatic consequences* of any Galois connection. The decision to recognise the abstract structure turns a four-part computation into one identity plus formal deductions.

# Solution

Prove the adjunction $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\iff\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}^c$ once, then read off the unit, counit, stabilisations, fixed-point characterisations, and bijection as formal consequences. The non-ideal image example is $2\mathbb{Z}$ under $\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}$.

**Step 1: $\mathfrak{b}^c$ is an ideal; $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$ need not be (part 1).**

Preimages of ideals are ideals; images need not be, witnessed by $\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *$\mathfrak{b}^c = \varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ is an ideal:* if $x, y\in\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ then $\varphi(x-y) = \varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\in\mathfrak{b}$, and for $a\in A$, $\varphi(ax) = \varphi(a)\varphi(x)\in\mathfrak{b}$ (as $\mathfrak{b}$ absorbs multiplication by $\varphi(a)\in B$). So $\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ is closed under subtraction and under multiplication by $A$ — an ideal.
>
> *$\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$ need not be an ideal:* take $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathfrak{a} = 2\mathbb{Z}$. Then $\varphi(2\mathbb{Z}) = \{2n : n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$, the even integers. This is not an ideal of $\mathbb{Q}$: it is not closed under multiplication by $\tfrac13\in\mathbb{Q}$, since $\tfrac13\cdot 2 = \tfrac23\notin\{2n\}$ (indeed $\tfrac23\notin\mathbb{Z}$). So the *image* fails to be an ideal, which is exactly why the extension $\mathfrak{a}^e$ is *defined* as the ideal *generated* by $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$.

**Step 2: The adjunction identity (master fact).**

$\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\iff\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}^c$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$. For $a\in\mathfrak{a}$, $\varphi(a)\in\varphi(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq(\varphi(\mathfrak{a})) = \mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$, so $a\in\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b}) = \mathfrak{b}^c$. Hence $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}^c$.
>
> ($\Leftarrow$) Suppose $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}^c = \varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$. Then $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$, and since $\mathfrak{b}$ is an ideal, the *generated* ideal $(\varphi(\mathfrak{a})) = \mathfrak{a}^e$ is also $\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$ (the generated ideal is the smallest ideal containing $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$, and $\mathfrak{b}$ is one such). Hence $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$.
>
> This is the [[Def - Extension and Contraction of Ideals|Galois connection]]: $e$ is left adjoint to $c$.

**Step 3: Unit, counit, and stabilisation (part 2).**

$\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{ec}$, $\mathfrak{b}^{ce}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$, and $\mathfrak{a}^e = \mathfrak{a}^{ece}$, $\mathfrak{b}^c = \mathfrak{b}^{cec}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Unit:* in the adjunction set $\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{a}^e$. The trivial $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^e$ gives, via the adjunction, $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq(\mathfrak{a}^e)^c = \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$.
>
> *Counit:* set $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{b}^c$. The trivial $\mathfrak{b}^c\subseteq\mathfrak{b}^c$ gives $(\mathfrak{b}^c)^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$, i.e. $\mathfrak{b}^{ce}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$.
>
> *Stabilisation $\mathfrak{a}^e = \mathfrak{a}^{ece}$:* applying the order-preserving $e$ to the unit $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{ec}$ gives $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq(\mathfrak{a}^{ec})^e = \mathfrak{a}^{ece}$. Conversely, the counit with $\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{a}^e$ gives $(\mathfrak{a}^e)^{ce} = \mathfrak{a}^{ece}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^e$. So $\mathfrak{a}^e = \mathfrak{a}^{ece}$. The dual $\mathfrak{b}^c = \mathfrak{b}^{cec}$ follows symmetrically.

**Step 4: Contracted/extended characterisation (part 3).**

$\mathfrak{a}$ contracted $\iff\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$; $\mathfrak{b}$ extended $\iff\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{b}^{ce}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> ($\Leftarrow$) If $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{a}^{ec} = (\mathfrak{a}^e)^c$, then $\mathfrak{a}$ is the contraction of the ideal $\mathfrak{a}^e$, hence contracted.
>
> ($\Rightarrow$) If $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{b}^c$ for some $\mathfrak{b}$, then $\mathfrak{a}^{ec} = \mathfrak{b}^{cec} = \mathfrak{b}^c$ (stabilisation, Step 3) $= \mathfrak{a}$. So $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$.
>
> The extended characterisation is dual: $\mathfrak{b}$ extended $\iff\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{a}^e$ for some $\mathfrak{a}$ $\iff\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{b}^{ce}$ (using $\mathfrak{b}^{ce} = \mathfrak{a}^{ece} = \mathfrak{a}^e = \mathfrak{b}$).

**Step 5: The bijection (part 4).**

$e$ and $c$ restrict to inverse bijections between contracted and extended ideals.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $\mathcal{C} = \{$contracted ideals of $A\} = \{\mathfrak{a} : \mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{a}^{ec}\}$ and $\mathcal{E} = \{$extended ideals of $B\} = \{\mathfrak{b} : \mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{b}^{ce}\}$.
>
> *$e$ maps $\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{E}$:* for $\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{C}$, $\mathfrak{a}^e$ is extended by definition, so $\mathfrak{a}^e\in\mathcal{E}$.
>
> *$c$ maps $\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{C}$:* for $\mathfrak{b}\in\mathcal{E}$, $\mathfrak{b}^c$ is contracted, so $\mathfrak{b}^c\in\mathcal{C}$.
>
> *Mutually inverse:* for $\mathfrak{a}\in\mathcal{C}$, $(\mathfrak{a}^e)^c = \mathfrak{a}^{ec} = \mathfrak{a}$; for $\mathfrak{b}\in\mathcal{E}$, $(\mathfrak{b}^c)^e = \mathfrak{b}^{ce} = \mathfrak{b}$. Both $e$ and $c$ preserve inclusions (immediate from the definitions). Hence $e : \mathcal{C}\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathcal{E}$ is an inclusion-preserving bijection with inverse $c$.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **(1)** $\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ is an ideal (closed under subtraction and $A$-multiplication, as $\varphi$ is a homomorphism and $\mathfrak{b}$ an ideal). For $\mathbb{Z}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{Q}$, $\varphi(2\mathbb{Z}) = 2\mathbb{Z}$ is not closed under multiplication by $\tfrac13$, so not an ideal of $\mathbb{Q}$.
>
> **Adjunction.** $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\iff\varphi(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\iff\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b}) = \mathfrak{b}^c$ (using that $\mathfrak{a}^e$ is the smallest ideal containing $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$, and $\mathfrak{b}$ is an ideal).
>
> **(2)** Unit $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{ec}$ (adjunction with $\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{a}^e$); counit $\mathfrak{b}^{ce}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$ (with $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{b}^c$). Then $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{ece}$ (apply $e$ to unit) and $\mathfrak{a}^{ece}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^e$ (counit at $\mathfrak{a}^e$), so $\mathfrak{a}^e = \mathfrak{a}^{ece}$; dually $\mathfrak{b}^c = \mathfrak{b}^{cec}$.
>
> **(3)** $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{b}^c\Rightarrow\mathfrak{a}^{ec} = \mathfrak{b}^{cec} = \mathfrak{b}^c = \mathfrak{a}$; conversely $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$ exhibits $\mathfrak{a}$ as a contraction. Dually for extended.
>
> **(4)** $e, c$ restrict to inverse inclusion-preserving bijections $\{$contracted$\}\leftrightarrow\{$extended$\}$, since $\mathfrak{a}^{ec} = \mathfrak{a}$ on contracted and $\mathfrak{b}^{ce} = \mathfrak{b}$ on extended. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**A single adjunction identity generates the entire theory — recognise the Galois connection and stop computing.** The deep efficiency of this exercise is that all four parts collapse to one master fact, $\mathfrak{a}^e\subseteq\mathfrak{b}\iff\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}^c$. The unit, counit, stabilisation-after-one-step, fixed-point characterisations, and the bijection are *formal consequences of any Galois connection*, with no further ideal theory. The transferable trigger: whenever you meet a pair of order-preserving maps going opposite ways between two posets, *test for the adjunction inequality* — if it holds, you instantly get two closure operators, two one-sided inclusions, and a bijection between fixed-point sets, for free. This same skeleton governs the fundamental theorem of Galois theory (subgroups $\leftrightarrow$ subfields), topological closure, and the syntax–semantics duality in logic; learning to spot it converts pages of computation into one line plus citations.

**The asymmetry — contraction total and clean, extension needing a generation step — is the source of every one-sided inequality.** The reason the round trips are inequalities ($\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{ec}$, $\mathfrak{b}^{ce}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}$) rather than equalities is precisely that extension and contraction are *not* inverse in general: contraction is an honest preimage (always an ideal, total), while extension must *generate* an ideal from a set that is usually not one. The example $2\mathbb{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{Q}\mapsto\mathbb{Z}$ shows the loss concretely — extending $2\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$ (a unit ideal, since $2$ inverts) and contracting back recovers all of $\mathbb{Z}$, not $2\mathbb{Z}$. The fixed points of the round trips — the contracted and extended ideals — are exactly the ideals the map tracks *faithfully*, and they are what the bijection pairs up. The diagnostic: when an ideal fails $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$, the map has "blurred" it, and the amount of blur measures the failure of $\varphi$ to reflect $\mathfrak{a}$.

**For the localization map this bijection upgrades to the prime correspondence — the geometric payoff.** This exercise is the abstract engine; its most important instance is the [[Def - Multiplicative Set and Localization|localization map]] $\iota : R\to S^{-1}R$, where *every* ideal of $S^{-1}R$ turns out to be extended ($\mathfrak{b}^{ce} = \mathfrak{b}$ always), so the contracted–extended bijection becomes a bijection between certain ideals of $R$ and *all* ideals of $S^{-1}R$. Restricting to primes gives the [[Thm - Prime Ideals of a Localization|prime-correspondence theorem]] $\{\mathfrak{p} : \mathfrak{p}\cap S = \varnothing\}\leftrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}(S^{-1}R)$, the geometric statement that localization carves a clean piece out of the spectrum. So the formal Galois-connection bookkeeping here is what makes the entire geometry of localization possible — the bijection is general, but its faithfulness for localization (all ideals extended) is what makes $S^{-1}R$ such a transparent operation on $\operatorname{Spec}$.
