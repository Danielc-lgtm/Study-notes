# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$, and a [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]] sends $1 \mapsto 1$. Fix a ring homomorphism $\varphi : A \to B$. For an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $\mathfrak{b} \trianglelefteq B$ we write $\mathfrak{b}^c = \varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ for its **contraction** to $A$, and for an ideal $\mathfrak{a} \trianglelefteq A$ we write $\mathfrak{a}^e = (\varphi(\mathfrak{a}))$ — the ideal of $B$ *generated by* the image $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$ — for its **extension** to $B$. We use $\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{a}_1, \mathfrak{a}_2$ for ideals of $A$ and $\mathfrak{b}, \mathfrak{b}_1, \mathfrak{b}_2$ for ideals of $B$, and $\mathfrak{p}, \mathfrak{q}$ for [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime ideals]]. When $\varphi$ is the localization map $\iota : R \to S^{-1}R$, $r \mapsto \tfrac{r}{1}$, the two operations get the explicit forms recorded below; in that case we write $\mathfrak{a}^e = S^{-1}\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{b}^c = \{r \in R : \tfrac{r}{1} \in \mathfrak{b}\}$. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization]].

This is a compound page: it defines two interlocking operations — **extension** $(-)^e$ and **contraction** $(-)^c$ — together with the derived notions of **contracted** and **extended** ideals, because the two maps only become useful as an interlocking pair (they are an adjoint Galois connection, and the closure operators $(-)^{ec}$ and $(-)^{ce}$ are what make a clean bijection possible).

# Axiom Motivation

A ring homomorphism $\varphi : A \to B$ is a way of moving elements from $A$ into $B$. The question this page answers is: how do you move *ideals*? There are two natural directions — push an ideal of $A$ forward into $B$, or pull an ideal of $B$ back into $A$ — and the entire subtlety is that the two directions are *not* symmetric. Getting the definitions right is a matter of asking, for each direction, "what is the smallest piece of structure that survives the transport?"

**Why contraction is the *preimage*, and why it is automatically an ideal.** Pulling back is the easy direction. Given $\mathfrak{b} \trianglelefteq B$, the only sensible candidate for "$\mathfrak{b}$ viewed in $A$" is the set of elements of $A$ that land inside $\mathfrak{b}$, namely $\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$. This is automatically an ideal of $A$, and the reason is structural: $\varphi^{-1}$ of an ideal is an ideal for the same reason that the preimage of a subgroup is a subgroup. If $x, y \in \varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ then $\varphi(x), \varphi(y) \in \mathfrak{b}$, so $\varphi(x - y) = \varphi(x) - \varphi(y) \in \mathfrak{b}$; and for any $a \in A$, $\varphi(ax) = \varphi(a)\varphi(x) \in \mathfrak{b}$ because $\mathfrak{b}$ absorbs multiplication by $\varphi(a) \in B$. The absorption property of $\mathfrak{b}$ in $B$ transfers, through $\varphi$, to absorption of $\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ in $A$. Nothing can go wrong: contraction is total, well behaved, and preserves primality (see below).

**Why extension must be *generated by* the image, not the image itself.** Pushing forward is the direction where the naive definition fails, and watching it fail tells you the right definition. The naive candidate for "$\mathfrak{a}$ viewed in $B$" is the image $\varphi(\mathfrak{a}) = \{\varphi(a) : a \in \mathfrak{a}\}$. But this set is usually *not an ideal of $B$*. The defect is that an ideal of $B$ must absorb multiplication by *all* of $B$, whereas $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$ only knows how to absorb multiplication by elements coming from $A$. Concretely, for the inclusion $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$, the image of the ideal $2\mathbb{Z}$ is the set $\{2n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ of even integers, and multiplying $2 \in \varphi(2\mathbb{Z})$ by $\tfrac{1}{3} \in \mathbb{Q}$ gives $\tfrac{2}{3}$, which is not even an integer, let alone an even one. The image is not closed under multiplication by $\mathbb{Q}$. The repair is forced: take the *smallest ideal of $B$ containing the image*, which is the ideal generated by $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$, written $(\varphi(\mathfrak{a}))$ and consisting of all finite sums $\sum_i b_i \varphi(a_i)$ with $b_i \in B$, $a_i \in \mathfrak{a}$. This is the unique minimal way to make the image into an honest ideal of $B$. If you tried any *smaller* set you would not get an ideal; any *larger* one would forget where the ideal came from. So extension is "generate the smallest ideal you can from the pushed-forward elements", and the asymmetry with contraction — one is a clean preimage, the other needs a generation step — is the whole reason the two operations behave so differently.

**Why one always has $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$ and $\mathfrak{b} \supseteq \mathfrak{b}^{ce}$, never equality in general.** The two operations form what is called a *Galois connection*: extending is left adjoint to contracting, in the sense that $\mathfrak{a}^e \subseteq \mathfrak{b} \iff \mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{b}^c$. From any adjunction two one-sided inclusions fall out for free. Start with $\mathfrak{a} \trianglelefteq A$: every element of $\mathfrak{a}$ maps into $\mathfrak{a}^e$, so $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{a}^e) = \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$. Start with $\mathfrak{b} \trianglelefteq B$: the contraction $\mathfrak{b}^c$ consists of elements mapping into $\mathfrak{b}$, so generating an ideal from their images cannot escape $\mathfrak{b}$, giving $\mathfrak{b}^{ce} \subseteq \mathfrak{b}$. These inclusions are generically strict, and *that strictness is the point*: it measures how much information is lost in the round trip. For $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$, the ideal $2\mathbb{Z}$ extends to $(2) = \mathbb{Q}$ (since $2$ is a unit in $\mathbb{Q}$), which contracts back to all of $\mathbb{Z}$ — so $2\mathbb{Z} \subsetneq (2\mathbb{Z})^{ec} = \mathbb{Z}$. The ideals on which the round trip *is* the identity are exactly the ones the theory can faithfully track, and they get names.

**Why the fixed points are exactly the contracted and extended ideals.** An ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of $A$ is called **contracted** if it is the contraction of *some* ideal of $B$, and **extended** ideals of $B$ are defined dually. The clean fact — and the reason these definitions are worth isolating — is that $\mathfrak{a}$ is contracted if and only if $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$, and $\mathfrak{b}$ is extended if and only if $\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{b}^{ce}$. This is the standard feature of a Galois connection: the "closed" objects on each side are precisely the fixed points of the two round-trip closure operators, and $e$ and $c$ restrict to a bijection between them. This bijection is what makes localization tractable: for the localization map, *every* ideal of $S^{-1}R$ turns out to be extended, so the ideals of $S^{-1}R$ are governed entirely by the contracted ideals of $R$ — and among primes this becomes the [[Thm - Prime Ideals of a Localization|prime-correspondence theorem]].

# The Definition

Let $\varphi : A \to B$ be a [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]] of commutative rings.

## Contraction

For an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $\mathfrak{b} \trianglelefteq B$, the **contraction** of $\mathfrak{b}$ is
$$\mathfrak{b}^c := \varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b}) = \{a \in A : \varphi(a) \in \mathfrak{b}\}.$$
This is always an ideal of $A$. Moreover, **the contraction of a prime ideal is prime**: if $\mathfrak{q} \trianglelefteq B$ is prime then $\mathfrak{q}^c$ is prime, because $A/\mathfrak{q}^c$ embeds in the [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] $B/\mathfrak{q}$ via $\varphi$ (the composite $A \xrightarrow{\varphi} B \to B/\mathfrak{q}$ has kernel exactly $\mathfrak{q}^c$).

## Extension

For an ideal $\mathfrak{a} \trianglelefteq A$, the **extension** of $\mathfrak{a}$ is the ideal of $B$ generated by the image $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$:
$$\mathfrak{a}^e := (\varphi(\mathfrak{a})) = \left\{ \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^n b_i\,\varphi(a_i) : n \geq 0,\ b_i \in B,\ a_i \in \mathfrak{a} \right\}.$$
The image $\varphi(\mathfrak{a})$ by itself is usually *not* an ideal of $B$, which is why the generation step is required. The extension of a prime need not be prime.

## Contracted and extended ideals; the round-trip inequalities

An ideal $\mathfrak{a} \trianglelefteq A$ is **contracted** if $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{b}^c$ for some $\mathfrak{b} \trianglelefteq B$. An ideal $\mathfrak{b} \trianglelefteq B$ is **extended** if $\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{a}^e$ for some $\mathfrak{a} \trianglelefteq A$. For all $\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b}$,
$$\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^{ec}, \qquad \mathfrak{b} \supseteq \mathfrak{b}^{ce}, \qquad \mathfrak{a}^e = \mathfrak{a}^{ece}, \qquad \mathfrak{b}^c = \mathfrak{b}^{cec}.$$
Consequently $\mathfrak{a}$ is contracted $\iff \mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$, and $\mathfrak{b}$ is extended $\iff \mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{b}^{ce}$, and the maps $e, c$ restrict to a mutually inverse bijection
$$\{\text{contracted ideals of } A\} \;\overset{\sim}{\longleftrightarrow}\; \{\text{extended ideals of } B\}, \qquad \mathfrak{a} \mapsto \mathfrak{a}^e,\quad \mathfrak{b}^c \mapsfrom \mathfrak{b}.$$

## Behaviour under the operations (Example Sheet 1, Q3)

Extension respects sums and products, and contraction respects intersections and radicals:
$$(\mathfrak{a}_1 + \mathfrak{a}_2)^e = \mathfrak{a}_1^e + \mathfrak{a}_2^e, \quad (\mathfrak{a}_1\mathfrak{a}_2)^e = \mathfrak{a}_1^e \mathfrak{a}_2^e, \quad (\mathfrak{b}_1 \cap \mathfrak{b}_2)^c = \mathfrak{b}_1^c \cap \mathfrak{b}_2^c, \quad (\sqrt{\mathfrak{b}})^c = \sqrt{\mathfrak{b}^c}.$$

## Explicit formulas for the localization map

For $\varphi = \iota : R \to S^{-1}R$, $r \mapsto \tfrac{r}{1}$ (with $S \subseteq R$ a [[Def - Multiplicative Set and Localization|multiplicative subset]]):
$$\mathfrak{a}^e = S^{-1}\mathfrak{a} = \left\{ \tfrac{a}{s} : a \in \mathfrak{a},\ s \in S \right\}, \qquad \mathfrak{b}^c = \left\{ r \in R : \tfrac{r}{1} \in \mathfrak{b} \right\},$$
$$\mathfrak{a}^{ec} = \bigcup_{s \in S}(\mathfrak{a} : s) = \{ r \in R : rs \in \mathfrak{a} \text{ for some } s \in S\}, \qquad \mathfrak{b}^{ce} = \mathfrak{b}.$$
In this case **every** ideal of $S^{-1}R$ is extended (the last equality), and $\mathfrak{a}^e = S^{-1}R$ if and only if $\mathfrak{a} \cap S \neq \varnothing$.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

Extension and contraction are an **adjoint pair** between two posets. View the set of ideals of $A$ as a poset $\mathcal{I}(A)$ ordered by inclusion, and similarly $\mathcal{I}(B)$. Then $(-)^e : \mathcal{I}(A) \to \mathcal{I}(B)$ and $(-)^c : \mathcal{I}(B) \to \mathcal{I}(A)$ are order-preserving, and they satisfy the **Galois connection** (adjunction) identity
$$\mathfrak{a}^e \subseteq \mathfrak{b} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{b}^c.$$
Read as an adjunction, $(-)^e$ is the left adjoint and $(-)^c$ the right adjoint. The two one-sided inequalities $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$ (the unit of the adjunction) and $\mathfrak{b}^{ce} \subseteq \mathfrak{b}$ (the counit) are *formal consequences* of any adjunction, not facts about ideals at all — this is why they hold for *every* ring map. The composites $(-)^{ec}$ on $\mathcal{I}(A)$ and $(-)^{ce}$ on $\mathcal{I}(B)$ are **closure operators** (idempotent, increasing/decreasing, order-preserving), and a standard lemma about Galois connections says the two operators restrict to an isomorphism between their fixed-point sets — exactly the bijection between contracted and extended ideals. Spelling this out is the cleanest way to see *why* the round trip stabilises after one step ($\mathfrak{a}^e = \mathfrak{a}^{ece}$): closure operators idempotently land in the closed set.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The cleanest compression: **contraction is the easy, total, structure-preserving direction (it is just a preimage and it preserves primes); extension is the hard direction that needs a generation step and generically loses information.** Every awkwardness in the theory of how ideals move under ring maps traces back to this single asymmetry.

**True name:** the operationally useful name for the contracted/extended dichotomy is "**the round-trip-stable ideals**". An ideal is contracted exactly when extending-then-contracting returns it ($\mathfrak{a}^{ec} = \mathfrak{a}$); these are the ideals about which the map $\varphi$ remembers everything. For the localization map this set is enormous on the $S^{-1}R$ side — *all* ideals are extended — which is why localization is such a faithful operation on ideals.

This is the same phenomenon as **pullback and pushforward** elsewhere in mathematics. In topology, the preimage of an open set is open (the easy, total operation) while the image of an open set need not be open (the hard one); contraction is the algebraic preimage. In geometry, a regular map $f : X \to Y$ of affine varieties corresponds to a ring map $\varphi : A(Y) \to A(X)$ in the *opposite* direction, and then contraction of ideals is what computes the **image of a subvariety** while extension computes the **preimage** — the arrows flip because the algebra–geometry dictionary is contravariant. The Galois-connection structure is literally the same one that governs closure operators in topology and the fundamental theorem of Galois theory.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — a unit makes the extension collapse to the whole ring.** Under $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$, take $\mathfrak{a} = 2\mathbb{Z}$. Then $2$ becomes the unit $2 \in \mathbb{Q}^\times$, so $\mathfrak{a}^e = (2) = \mathbb{Q}$. Contracting back, $\mathfrak{a}^{ec} = \varphi^{-1}(\mathbb{Q}) = \mathbb{Z}$. So $2\mathbb{Z} \subsetneq (2\mathbb{Z})^{ec} = \mathbb{Z}$: this is the basic example of strict $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$, and it shows $2\mathbb{Z}$ is *not* a contracted ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ along this map.

**Is an instance — the prime correspondence in $\mathbb{Z}$.** Under $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ (localize at $(p)$), the prime ideal $(p)$ extends to $(p)\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, which contracts back to $(p)$: it is contracted. But for a different prime $q \neq p$, the ideal $(q)$ extends to all of $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ (since $q$ is now a unit), so $(q)$ is *not* contracted along this map. Exactly the primes disjoint from $S = \mathbb{Z}\setminus(p)$ survive — here only $(0)$ and $(p)$.

**Is an instance — image of an ideal is genuinely not an ideal.** Under $\varphi : k \hookrightarrow k[X]$, the ideal $\mathfrak{a} = k$ (the whole field, the unit ideal of $k$) has image $k \subseteq k[X]$, which is *not* an ideal of $k[X]$ — it is not closed under multiplication by $X$. The extension $\mathfrak{a}^e = (k) = k[X]$ is the whole ring. This is the smallest example where the generation step does real work.

**Is NOT an instance — the extension of a prime need not be prime.** Contraction preserves primes, but extension does not. Under $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}[i]$, the prime $(5) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$ extends to $(5)\mathbb{Z}[i] = (2+i)(2-i)$, which is *not* prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — the prime $5$ splits. So "$\mathfrak{p}$ prime $\Rightarrow \mathfrak{p}^e$ prime" is false in general; only the *contraction* direction is safe for primes.

**Corollary — for the localization map, contraction is injective on primes that survive.** Because every ideal of $S^{-1}R$ is extended ($\mathfrak{b}^{ce} = \mathfrak{b}$) and survivor primes are contracted ($\mathfrak{p}^{ec} = \mathfrak{p}$ when $\mathfrak{p} \cap S = \varnothing$), the pair $(e, c)$ is a bijection on primes — this is the content of [[Thm - Prime Ideals of a Localization]].

**Calibration check.** Verify directly that $\varphi^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ absorbs multiplication by $A$, using only that $\mathfrak{b}$ absorbs multiplication by $B$ and $\varphi$ is a homomorphism. Confirm the Galois-connection identity $\mathfrak{a}^e \subseteq \mathfrak{b} \iff \mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{b}^c$ from the definitions, and deduce both round-trip inequalities $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$ and $\mathfrak{b}^{ce} \subseteq \mathfrak{b}$ as one-line consequences. Finally, check that for $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ every nonzero ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ extends to $\mathbb{Q}$ and hence is not contracted, so the only contracted ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $(0)$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The induced map on spectra *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Contraction is the formula for the continuous map $\operatorname{Spec}(\varphi) : \operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$, $\mathfrak{q} \mapsto \mathfrak{q}^c$, that a ring map $\varphi : A \to B$ induces on prime spectra — the algebraic incarnation of a **morphism of affine schemes** running the other way. That contraction preserves primes is exactly what makes this map well defined; that it is continuous for the Zariski topology is the statement $(\mathfrak{a}^e)$-vanishing $= \varphi^{-1}$ of $\mathfrak{a}$-vanishing. The full functor $\operatorname{Spec} : \mathbf{Ring}^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Top}$ is built on this construction.

> [!tip] Pullback and pushforward of ideal sheaves *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Under the variety–ring dictionary a regular map $f : X \to Y$ reverses to $\varphi : A(Y) \to A(X)$, and then extension computes the **inverse image** $f^{-1}(Z)$ of a subvariety while contraction computes the (closure of the) **image** $\overline{f(W)}$. The strictness $\mathfrak{a} \subsetneq \mathfrak{a}^{ec}$ is the algebraic shadow of the fact that the closure of an image can be strictly larger than the image itself — the source of the theory of **dominant** and **closed** morphisms.
