# Problem Statement

Let $m, n\geq 1$ be integers. Compute the tensor product of cyclic groups
$$\mathbb{Z}/m\ \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\ \mathbb{Z}/n$$
as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. Show that
$$\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/n\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n),$$
and in particular that the tensor product is $0$ whenever $\gcd(m,n) = 1$ (for instance $\mathbb{Z}/2\otimes\mathbb{Z}/3 = 0$). Give two proofs: one by directly manipulating the bilinearity relations, and one by the quotient isomorphism $R/I\otimes_R R/J\cong R/(I+J)$.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the tensor product of modules, its defining computational rules, and the quotient isomorphism.

![[Def - Tensor Product of Modules#The Definition]]

The defining rules used below are $r(a\otimes b) = (ra)\otimes b = a\otimes(rb)$ for $r\in\mathbb{Z}$, and $0\otimes b = a\otimes 0 = 0$. Pure tensors $a\otimes b$ generate $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n$, even as an abelian group.

For the second proof, regard $\mathbb{Z}/m = \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}/n = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as $\mathbb{Z}/I$ and $\mathbb{Z}/J$ for the [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] $I = m\mathbb{Z}$, $J = n\mathbb{Z}$, and recall the quotient isomorphism:

> The cyclic case of the [[Thm - Standard Isomorphisms of Tensor Products|standard isomorphisms]]: for ideals $I, J\trianglelefteq R$, $\;R/I\otimes_R R/J\cong R/(I+J)\;$ via $(r_1+I)\otimes(r_2+J)\mapsto r_1 r_2 + (I+J)$.

The arithmetic fact closing the second proof is $m\mathbb{Z} + n\mathbb{Z} = \gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$ — Bézout's identity in $\mathbb{Z}$: the ideal generated by $m$ and $n$ is generated by their greatest common divisor.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is an *identify-a-tensor-product-concretely* problem of the most basic kind, and it is the first computation everyone does to feel the two characteristic phenomena of tensor products: *collapse* (a tensor can vanish though neither factor does) and *the base ring controlling identifications*. As the [[Commutative Algebra II — Tensor Products#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic strategy]] records, "deciding whether a tensor vanishes" splits into an inside computation (slide scalars to collapse) and the quotient route (recognise the factors as $R/I$, $R/J$).

**Assumption pattern.** The recognisable trigger is *both factors are cyclic*, i.e. quotients $\mathbb{Z}/I$, $\mathbb{Z}/J$. A cyclic module is generated by a single element, so $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n$ is generated by the *single* pure tensor $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ (every $\bar a\otimes\bar b = ab(\bar1\otimes\bar1)$). The whole tensor product is therefore a cyclic group, and the only question is its order — which is exactly what the relations, or the quotient isomorphism, pin down.

**Theorem routing.** Two routes converge. The direct route slides scalars: $\gcd(m,n)\cdot(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = 0$ because $\gcd(m,n) = um + vn$ (Bézout), and $m(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = \bar m\otimes\bar1 = 0$, $n(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = \bar1\otimes\bar n = 0$. The structural route applies the [[Thm - Standard Isomorphisms of Tensor Products|quotient isomorphism]] $R/I\otimes R/J\cong R/(I+J)$ with $I+J = m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z} = \gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$, reading off $\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$ immediately.

**Key decision point.** The one non-obvious move is in the direct route: showing the order of $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ is *exactly* $\gcd(m,n)$, not just *at most* it. The "at most" half is easy (slide $m$ or $n$ to kill the tensor, and Bézout shows $\gcd$ kills it). The "exactly" half — that $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ is *not* killed by anything smaller — requires producing a *surviving bilinear map*: the multiplication $\mathbb{Z}/m\times\mathbb{Z}/n\to\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$, $(\bar a,\bar b)\mapsto\overline{ab}$, which is well-defined precisely because $\gcd(m,n)\mid m$ and $\gcd(m,n)\mid n$. This is the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Modules|nonzero-certificate technique]]: the relations give an upper bound on the order; one bilinear map gives the matching lower bound.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Commutative Algebra II — Tensor Products#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Slide scalars across the $\otimes$ (operation 1).** Move $m$ or $n$ from one factor to the other so it annihilates: $\bar m\otimes\bar1 = m(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = \bar1\otimes\overline{m} $ collapses because $\bar m = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/m$.

2. **Use that pure tensors of generators generate (operation 6).** Since $\bar1$ generates $\mathbb{Z}/m$ and $\bar1$ generates $\mathbb{Z}/n$, the single tensor $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ generates $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n$, so the group is cyclic.

3. **Certify a tensor nonzero with one bilinear map (operation 3).** The multiplication map into $\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$ shows $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ has order $\geq\gcd(m,n)$, matching the upper bound.

4. **Push $\otimes$ through a quotient (operation 5).** In the second proof, apply $R/I\otimes R/J\cong R/(I+J)$ directly with $I = m\mathbb{Z}$, $J = n\mathbb{Z}$.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Both factors are cyclic — generated by $\bar 1$. So $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n$ is generated by the single pure tensor $\bar1\otimes\bar1$. The whole problem is: what is the *order* of $\bar1\otimes\bar1$?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Slide scalars. $m(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = \bar m\otimes\bar1 = \bar0\otimes\bar1 = 0$, and $n(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = \bar1\otimes\bar n = 0$. So the order of $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ divides both $m$ and $n$, hence divides $\gcd(m,n)$. By Bézout, $\gcd(m,n) = um+vn$, so $\gcd(m,n)(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = 0$ too. The order divides $\gcd(m,n)$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> To show the order is *exactly* $\gcd(m,n)$ (not smaller), build a bilinear map that does not collapse. Try $b : \mathbb{Z}/m\times\mathbb{Z}/n\to\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$, $b(\bar a,\bar b) = \overline{ab}$. Check it is well-defined (uses $\gcd(m,n)\mid m$ and $\gcd(m,n)\mid n$) and bilinear; then $b(\bar1,\bar1) = \bar1$ has order $\gcd(m,n)$, so $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ does too.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For the slick proof, recognise $\mathbb{Z}/m = \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ and apply $R/I\otimes_R R/J\cong R/(I+J)$ with $I = m\mathbb{Z}$, $J = n\mathbb{Z}$. Since $m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z} = \gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$, the answer is $\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$ in one line.

# Solution

The proof breaks into establishing that $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n$ is cyclic generated by $\bar1\otimes\bar1$, bounding the order of that generator above by $\gcd(m,n)$ via scalar-sliding, and matching it below by a surviving bilinear map. Step 1 reduces to the order of one element; Step 2 gives "order divides $\gcd$"; Step 3 gives "order is at least $\gcd$"; Step 4 is the one-line structural proof.

**Step 1: The tensor product is cyclic, generated by $\bar1\otimes\bar1$.**

Every element of $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n$ is an integer multiple of $\bar1\otimes\bar1$, so the group is cyclic.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Pure tensors $\bar a\otimes\bar b$ generate $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/n$ as an abelian group. Using scalar-sliding,
> $$\bar a\otimes\bar b = a(\bar1\otimes\bar b) = a\,b\,(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = (ab)(\bar1\otimes\bar1),$$
> where $a, b$ are integer representatives. So every pure tensor — hence every element — is an integer multiple of $g_0 := \bar1\otimes\bar1$. Therefore $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n = \langle g_0\rangle$ is cyclic, and the group is determined by the order of $g_0$.

**Step 2: The order of $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ divides $\gcd(m,n)$.**

Both $m\cdot g_0 = 0$ and $n\cdot g_0 = 0$, and by Bézout $\gcd(m,n)\cdot g_0 = 0$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Slide scalars onto the factor where they vanish:
> $$m\cdot g_0 = m(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = \bar m\otimes\bar1 = \bar0\otimes\bar1 = 0\quad(\text{since }\bar m = 0\text{ in }\mathbb{Z}/m),$$
> $$n\cdot g_0 = n(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = \bar1\otimes\bar n = \bar1\otimes\bar0 = 0\quad(\text{since }\bar n = 0\text{ in }\mathbb{Z}/n).$$
> So $\operatorname{ord}(g_0)$ divides both $m$ and $n$, hence divides $\gcd(m,n)$. Independently, by Bézout there are integers $u, v$ with $\gcd(m,n) = um + vn$, so $\gcd(m,n)\cdot g_0 = u(m g_0) + v(n g_0) = 0$ — confirming $\operatorname{ord}(g_0)\mid\gcd(m,n)$. Hence $|\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n|\leq\gcd(m,n)$.

**Step 3: The order of $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ is at least $\gcd(m,n)$, via a surviving bilinear map.**

The multiplication map into $\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$ is bilinear and sends $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ to a generator, forcing $\operatorname{ord}(g_0)\geq\gcd(m,n)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Write $g = \gcd(m,n)$. Define $b : \mathbb{Z}/m\times\mathbb{Z}/n\to\mathbb{Z}/g$ by $b(\bar a,\bar b) = \overline{ab}$ (the bar on the right is mod $g$).
>
> *Well-defined.* If $\bar a = \bar a'$ in $\mathbb{Z}/m$, then $m\mid(a-a')$, and since $g\mid m$ also $g\mid(a-a')$, so $\overline{ab} = \overline{a'b}$ in $\mathbb{Z}/g$; symmetrically in the second slot. So $b$ does not depend on representatives.
>
> *Bilinear.* $b(\bar a+\bar a',\bar b) = \overline{(a+a')b} = \overline{ab}+\overline{a'b} = b(\bar a,\bar b)+b(\bar a',\bar b)$; similarly in the second slot; and $b(r\bar a,\bar b) = \overline{rab} = r\,b(\bar a,\bar b) = b(\bar a, r\bar b)$ for $r\in\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> By the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Modules|universal property]], $b$ induces a homomorphism $h : \mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n\to\mathbb{Z}/g$ with $h(g_0) = b(\bar1,\bar1) = \bar1$, a generator of $\mathbb{Z}/g$ of order $g$. A homomorphism cannot increase order, so $\operatorname{ord}(g_0)\geq\operatorname{ord}(h(g_0)) = g$. Combined with Step 2, $\operatorname{ord}(g_0) = g$, so $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n\cong\mathbb{Z}/g = \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$. (In fact $h$ is an isomorphism: it is onto, and both groups have order $g$.)

**Step 4: The structural one-liner via the quotient isomorphism.**

$\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n\cong\mathbb{Z}/(m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Write $\mathbb{Z}/m = \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}/n = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. The cyclic case of the [[Thm - Standard Isomorphisms of Tensor Products|standard isomorphisms]] gives
> $$\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/(m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z}),$$
> with $(\bar1)\otimes(\bar1)\mapsto\bar1$. By Bézout, $m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z} = \gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$ (the ideal generated by $m$ and $n$ is principal, generated by their gcd). Hence $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n\cong\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$. In particular, if $\gcd(m,n) = 1$ then $m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}$ and the tensor product is $\mathbb{Z}/\mathbb{Z} = 0$; e.g. $\mathbb{Z}/2\otimes\mathbb{Z}/3 = 0$.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/n\cong\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$.
>
> Write $g = \gcd(m,n)$, $g_0 = \bar1\otimes\bar1$.
>
> *Cyclic.* Pure tensors generate, and $\bar a\otimes\bar b = (ab)g_0$, so $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n = \langle g_0\rangle$ is cyclic.
>
> *Order divides $g$.* $m g_0 = \bar m\otimes\bar1 = 0$ and $n g_0 = \bar1\otimes\bar n = 0$, so $\operatorname{ord}(g_0)\mid\gcd(m,n) = g$.
>
> *Order is $g$.* The map $b(\bar a,\bar b) = \overline{ab}\in\mathbb{Z}/g$ is well-defined (as $g\mid m$ and $g\mid n$) and bilinear, so induces $h : \mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n\to\mathbb{Z}/g$ with $h(g_0) = \bar1$, of order $g$. Hence $\operatorname{ord}(g_0)\geq g$, so $\operatorname{ord}(g_0) = g$ and $h$ is an isomorphism.
>
> Therefore $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n\cong\mathbb{Z}/g = \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$; in particular it is $0$ when $\gcd(m,n) = 1$.
>
> *Alternative (structural).* $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}/(m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$ by the quotient isomorphism $R/I\otimes R/J\cong R/(I+J)$ and Bézout $m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z} = \gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Tensor of cyclic = quotient by the sum of the ideals, and the sum of ideals computes the gcd.** The cleanest lesson is the dictionary $\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n = \mathbb{Z}/(m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z})$: tensoring two cyclic groups *adds the relation ideals*, and in $\mathbb{Z}$ the sum of ideals $m\mathbb{Z}+n\mathbb{Z}$ is the gcd ideal $\gcd(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$. This is worth contrasting sharply with the *product* $\mathbb{Z}/m\times\mathbb{Z}/n$, whose kernel from $\mathbb{Z}$ is $m\mathbb{Z}\cap n\mathbb{Z} = \operatorname{lcm}(m,n)\mathbb{Z}$ — so the *same two cyclic groups* give $\gcd$ under $\otimes$ and $\operatorname{lcm}$ under $\times$. The trigger for spaced recall: whenever you see a tensor of two quotient rings, reach for $R/I\otimes R/J\cong R/(I+J)$ and then *compute the ideal $I+J$*; in a PID this is "gcd of the generators", in a polynomial ring it is "union the relations". The whole subtlety of $\otimes$-versus-$\times$ is the subtlety of $I+J$ versus $I\cap J$.

**Collapse is real and is detected by sliding the killer scalar across.** The most counterintuitive fact for a newcomer is that $\mathbb{Z}/2\otimes\mathbb{Z}/3 = 0$ with neither factor zero — a genuine collapse. The mechanism to internalise: a scalar that annihilates one factor can be *slid onto that factor* from the other side, killing the whole pure tensor. Concretely, $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ in $\mathbb{Z}/2\otimes\mathbb{Z}/3$ satisfies $3(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = \bar1\otimes\bar3 = 0$ and $2(\bar1\otimes\bar1) = \bar2\otimes\bar1 = 0$, so $\gcd(2,3) = 1$ kills it. The transferable diagnostic: to bound the order (or annihilator) of a tensor from above, look for a scalar killing *either* factor and slide it across; coprimality of the two "killer" scalars forces total collapse. This is the upper-bound half of every tensor-order computation.

**Upper bound from the relations, lower bound from one bilinear map — the two-sided method for tensor orders.** The relations only ever give "the order divides $\gcd$" — an *upper* bound — because sliding scalars can only shrink things. To prove the tensor is as large as claimed (order *exactly* $\gcd$, not smaller), you must step outside and exhibit a single bilinear map that does not collapse, here the multiplication into $\mathbb{Z}/\gcd(m,n)$. Its well-definedness is exactly the divisibility $\gcd\mid m$, $\gcd\mid n$, and the image of $\bar1\otimes\bar1$ has the right order, supplying the matching *lower* bound. The pattern recurs everywhere: *to prove a tensor (or a tensor element) is nonzero or has a given size, construct one incriminating bilinear map.* This is the universal-property nonzero-certificate, and it is the single most useful technique for the harder vanishing problems, including [[Ex - A pure tensor that is zero without either factor being zero]] where the same multiplication-style map distinguishes $2\otimes\bar1$ in two different tensor products.
