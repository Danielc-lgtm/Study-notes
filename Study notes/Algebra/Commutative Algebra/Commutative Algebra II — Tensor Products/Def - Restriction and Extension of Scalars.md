# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$; ring homomorphisms send $1\mapsto 1$. Fix a ring homomorphism $f : R\to S$ in the background — it is the *only* ingredient both constructions depend on. We write $M$ for an [[Def - Module|$S$-module]] and $N$ for an $R$-module; $\operatorname{End}(M)$ for the ring of endomorphisms of $M$ as an abelian group; and $S\otimes_R N$ (more generally $M\otimes_R N$) for the [[Def - Tensor Product of Modules|tensor product]], which we will endow with an $S$-module structure. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra II — Tensor Products]].

This is a compound page: it defines two opposite operations — **restriction of scalars** (an $S$-module becomes an $R$-module, *along $f$*) and **extension of scalars** (an $R$-module is pushed to an $S$-module, *by $\otimes_R S$*) — because they are a pair: extension is left adjoint to restriction, and one is rarely used without the other.

---

# Axiom Motivation

Modules live over rings, and a ring homomorphism $f : R\to S$ relates two rings. The natural question is: how does $f$ let us *move modules between $R$ and $S$*? There are exactly two directions, and they are forced by what data we already have. To turn an $S$-module into an $R$-module we have *more* scalars than we need ($S$ acts, but we only want $R$ to act) — we restrict. To turn an $R$-module into an $S$-module we have *fewer* scalars than we need ($R$ acts, but we want $S$ to act) — we must *adjoin* the missing scalars, and adjoining scalars is exactly what the tensor product does. The two constructions are the down-map and up-map of $f$.

**Why restriction is just composition, and needs no choices.** An $S$-module $M$ is a ring homomorphism $\rho : S\to\operatorname{End}(M)$ (the action). Given $f : R\to S$, the composite $\rho\circ f : R\to\operatorname{End}(M)$ is a ring homomorphism, hence an $R$-module structure on the *same underlying abelian group* $M$, with action $r\cdot m := f(r)m$. Nothing is constructed; we just forget down to the $R$-scalars we can reach through $f$. This is the *only* reasonable way to make $M$ an $R$-module compatibly with $f$: any $R$-action extending to the given $S$-action through $f$ must be $r\mapsto f(r)$. The price is that restriction can be lossy in size — $\mathbb{C}^n$, an $\mathbb{C}$-module, restricts along $\mathbb{R}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}$ to the $\mathbb{R}$-module $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$, doubling the dimension because real scalars see the real and imaginary parts separately. Restriction is a forgetful functor; it adds nothing and chooses nothing.

**Why extension must use the tensor product, and why the action is $s(m\otimes n) = (sm)\otimes n$.** Going up is harder: an $R$-module $N$ has only $R$ acting, and we want $S$ to act. We cannot just "declare" an $S$-action — there is no canonical way to multiply $n\in N$ by $s\in S$. The universal solution is to *freely adjoin* the $S$-scalars: form $S\otimes_R N$, where $S$ is regarded as an $R$-module via $f$. An element is a sum $\sum s_i\otimes n_i$, and $S$ acts on the *left factor*: $s\cdot(s'\otimes n) = (ss')\otimes n$. This is forced because $S\otimes_R N$ is generated by pure tensors and the action must be $S$-linear and agree with multiplication in $S$ on the $S$-factor. (More generally, when $M$ is already an $S$-module, the $R$-tensor $M\otimes_R N$ carries the $S$-action $s(m\otimes n) = (sm)\otimes n$, well-defined by the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Modules|universal property]]: for fixed $s$, the map $(m,n)\mapsto(sm)\otimes n$ is $R$-bilinear, hence induces an endomorphism $h_s$, and $s\mapsto h_s$ is a ring homomorphism $S\to\operatorname{End}(M\otimes_R N)$.) If we tried to put the action on the *right* factor $N$, it would not be defined — $N$ has no $S$-action — so the left factor is the only home for the new scalars.

**Why this is the right notion: it acts on maps, not just objects.** A construction that only moved objects would be weak. Extension of scalars also moves *morphisms*: an $R$-linear $g : N\to N'$ extends to the $S$-linear $\operatorname{id}_M\otimes g : M\otimes_R N\to M\otimes_R N'$. The check that $\operatorname{id}_M\otimes g$ is $S$-linear (not merely $R$-linear) is exactly $(sm)\otimes g(n) = s((m)\otimes g(n))$, which holds by the action's definition. This functoriality is what makes extension of scalars a genuine *base change*: it transports an entire diagram of $R$-modules to a diagram of $S$-modules. Without it, "complexify a real linear map" would have no meaning; with it, the matrix of $T : \mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ is unchanged (reinterpreted with complex entries) by $\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{C}}\otimes T$.

**Why the two are adjoint, and what that buys.** Extension and restriction are not independent: extension is the [[Thm - Extension of Scalars and the Adjunction|left adjoint]] of restriction,
$$\operatorname{Hom}_S(S\otimes_R N,\ M)\ \cong\ \operatorname{Hom}_R(N,\ M),$$
naturally, where on the right $M$ is restricted to an $R$-module. In words: an $S$-linear map out of the extension $S\otimes_R N$ is the same as an $R$-linear map out of $N$. This adjunction is the structural reason every extension computation reduces to an $R$-linear one, and it is the algebraic skeleton of the pullback–pushforward adjunction $f^*\dashv f_*$ in geometry. Were the two not adjoint, base change would not interact predictably with maps; the adjunction is what makes the whole apparatus coherent.

# The Definition

Fix a ring homomorphism $f : R\to S$.

## Restriction of scalars

Let $M$ be an $S$-module, with structure map $\rho : S\to\operatorname{End}(M)$. The **restriction of scalars** of $M$ along $f$ is the $R$-module with the *same* underlying abelian group $M$ and action
$$r\cdot m := f(r)\,m\qquad(r\in R,\ m\in M),$$
i.e. structure map $\rho\circ f : R\to\operatorname{End}(M)$. Every $S$-module thereby becomes an $R$-module, and every $S$-linear map is automatically $R$-linear.

## Extension of scalars

Let $N$ be an $R$-module and $M$ an $S$-module (regarded as an $R$-module by restriction). The **extension of scalars** is the [[Def - Tensor Product of Modules|tensor product]] $M\otimes_R N$, made an $S$-module by
$$s\cdot(m\otimes n) := (sm)\otimes n\qquad(s\in S,\ m\in M,\ n\in N),$$
extended $S$-linearly. This is well-defined: for fixed $s\in S$ the map $(m,n)\mapsto(sm)\otimes n$ is $R$-bilinear, so induces $h_s\in\operatorname{End}(M\otimes_R N)$ with $h_s(m\otimes n) = (sm)\otimes n$, and $s\mapsto h_s$ is a ring homomorphism $S\to\operatorname{End}(M\otimes_R N)$. The principal case is $M = S$: then $S\otimes_R N$ is the extension of $N$ to an $S$-module, with $s\cdot(s'\otimes n) = (ss')\otimes n$.

## Extension acts on maps

For an $R$-linear map $g : N\to N'$ and an $S$-module $M$, the map $\operatorname{id}_M\otimes g : M\otimes_R N\to M\otimes_R N'$ is $S$-linear:
$$(\operatorname{id}_M\otimes g)\big(s(m\otimes n)\big) = (sm)\otimes g(n) = s\,\big((\operatorname{id}_M\otimes g)(m\otimes n)\big).$$
Thus extension of scalars is a functor from $R$-modules to $S$-modules. If $T : R^n\to R^m$ has matrix $[T]$ in the standard bases, then $\operatorname{id}_S\otimes T : S^n\to S^m$ has the *same* matrix $[T]$, with entries reinterpreted in $S$.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

Restriction of scalars is the **forgetful functor** $f_* : S\text{-Mod}\to R\text{-Mod}$ induced by $f : R\to S$, and extension of scalars is its **left adjoint** $f^* = S\otimes_R(-) : R\text{-Mod}\to S\text{-Mod}$. The adjunction is the natural isomorphism
$$\operatorname{Hom}_S(S\otimes_R N,\ M)\ \cong\ \operatorname{Hom}_R(N,\ f_* M),$$
whose unit $N\to f_*(S\otimes_R N)$, $n\mapsto 1\otimes n$, and counit $S\otimes_R f_* M\to M$, $s\otimes m\mapsto sm$, encode the two constructions. Adjointness says extension is the universal way to turn an $R$-module into an $S$-module: $S\otimes_R N$ is the *free* $S$-module on $N$ relative to $f$. (Compare: the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^{(X)}$ is the left adjoint to the forgetful functor from modules to sets; extension of scalars is the same pattern with "set" replaced by "$R$-module".) Geometrically, $f$ corresponds to a map of spaces $\mathbf{Spec}\,S\to\mathbf{Spec}\,R$, restriction is **pushforward** $f_*$, and extension is **pullback** $f^*$; the adjunction is $f^*\dashv f_*$.

---

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The cleanest compression: **restriction forgets scalars down through $f$; extension freely adjoins scalars up through $\otimes_R S$. They are the pushforward and pullback of $f$, and they are adjoint.**

**True name:** extension of scalars is *base change* — "view this $R$-module over $S$". Operationally, $S\otimes_R(-)$ replaces the scalars: $S\otimes_R R^n = S^n$ (same basis), $S\otimes_R R/I = S/IS$ (reduce relations into $S$), $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}V = $ complexification. Restriction is *"view this $S$-module over the smaller ring $R$"*, generally enlarging the dimension.

In **algebraic geometry**, extension is pullback of a module / quasi-coherent sheaf along $\mathbf{Spec}\,S\to\mathbf{Spec}\,R$, and restriction is pushforward; specialising a family over $\mathbf{Spec}\,\mathbb{Z}$ to a prime $p$ is extension along $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{F}_p$, and viewing a real variety over $\mathbb{C}$ is extension along $\mathbb{R}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}$. In **representation theory**, restriction and induction of representations along a group homomorphism are exactly restriction and extension of scalars for the group algebras, and Frobenius reciprocity *is* this adjunction. In **number theory**, $\mathcal{O}_K\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q} = K$ and $\mathcal{O}_K\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}_p$ are base changes that localise and complete a ring of integers.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — complexification.** Along $\mathbb{R}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}$, extension sends the $\mathbb{R}$-module $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^n\cong\mathbb{C}^n$ (see [[Ex - Extension of scalars of a free module]]), and a real linear map $T : \mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ to $\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{C}}\otimes T$ with the *same* matrix over $\mathbb{C}$. Restriction sends $\mathbb{C}^n$ back to $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ (not $\mathbb{R}^n$): the two operations are not mutually inverse.

**Is an instance — reduction modulo $I$.** Along $R\to R/I$, extension sends an $R$-module $M$ to $(R/I)\otimes_R M\cong M/IM$ (see [[Ex - Tensoring with R over I gives M over IM]]); for $R = \mathbb{Z}$, $S = \mathbb{Z}/n$, $N = \mathbb{Z}^k$ this is $(\mathbb{Z}/n)\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}^k\cong(\mathbb{Z}/n)^k$. "Extend scalars to $\mathbb{Z}/n$" is "reduce the module mod $n$" — a perfectly valid extension even though it shrinks the module.

**Is an instance — the two-step factorisation.** For an $S$-module $M$ and $R$-module $N$, $M\otimes_R N\cong M\otimes_S(S\otimes_R N)$: a general extension factors as the simple extension $S\otimes_R N$ followed by an $S$-tensor (see [[Thm - Extension of Scalars and the Adjunction]]). E.g. $\mathbb{C}^n\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^\ell\cong\mathbb{C}^n\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^\ell)\cong\mathbb{C}^n\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}^\ell\cong\mathbb{C}^{n\ell}$.

**Is NOT mutually inverse — restrict-then-extend, extend-then-restrict.** Starting from the $\mathbb{C}$-module $\mathbb{C}^n$: restrict to $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$, then extend to $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^{2n}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2n}\neq\mathbb{C}^n$. Starting from the $\mathbb{R}$-module $\mathbb{R}^n$: extend to $\mathbb{C}^n$, then restrict to $\mathbb{R}^{2n}\neq\mathbb{R}^n$. Neither round trip is the identity; this is why they are *adjoint* rather than inverse.

**Corollary — extension preserves direct sums and free modules.** Because $\otimes$ distributes over [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules|direct sums]], $S\otimes_R\bigoplus_i N_i\cong\bigoplus_i(S\otimes_R N_i)$ as $S$-modules; in particular $S\otimes_R R^{(I)}\cong S^{(I)}$, with $S$-basis $\{1\otimes e_i\}$. Free modules extend to free modules of the same rank, with the same basis indices.

**Calibration check.** Verify that restriction of $\mathbb{C}^n$ along $\mathbb{R}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}$ is $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$, and that extension of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $\mathbb{C}^n$. Confirm the $S$-action $s(m\otimes n) = (sm)\otimes n$ is well-defined by checking $(m,n)\mapsto(sm)\otimes n$ is $R$-bilinear for fixed $s$. Show $\operatorname{id}_S\otimes T$ has the same matrix as $T$. Finally, write the adjunction $\operatorname{Hom}_S(S\otimes_R N, M)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_R(N, M)$ for $N = R$ and confirm it reduces to $M\cong M$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Pullback and pushforward of sheaves; base change *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Along a map of spaces $\mathbf{Spec}\,S\to\mathbf{Spec}\,R$, extension of scalars is the **pullback** $f^*$ of a quasi-coherent **sheaf** (the algebraic stand-in for a vector bundle) and restriction is the **pushforward** $f_*$. The adjunction $\operatorname{Hom}_S(S\otimes_R N, M)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_R(N, M)$ is the adjunction $f^*\dashv f_*$ that organises all of sheaf cohomology and base-change theory. Specialising a family to a fibre, or spreading a variety out over a larger field, is this single operation.

> [!tip] Induction and restriction of representations; Frobenius reciprocity *(from Representation Theory)*
> For a group homomorphism $H\to G$ and the induced map of group algebras $k[H]\to k[G]$, restriction of scalars is **restriction of representations** $\operatorname{Res}^G_H$ and extension of scalars is **induction** $\operatorname{Ind}_H^G = k[G]\otimes_{k[H]}(-)$. The adjunction $\operatorname{Hom}_G(\operatorname{Ind}V, W)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_H(V, \operatorname{Res}W)$ is **Frobenius reciprocity**, the cornerstone of character theory.

> [!tip] Flat base change and faithfully flat descent *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> When $f : R\to S$ is **flat**, extension of scalars $S\otimes_R(-)$ is exact, so base change preserves exact sequences, kernels, and finite limits — the property that makes localization and completion well-behaved. When $f$ is **faithfully flat**, descent theory reconstructs $R$-modules from their base changes plus gluing data, the algebraic foundation of the fpqc topology. Flatness is the subject of [[Commutative Algebra III — Flatness and Exactness]].
