# Notation Registry

**Standing convention.** Throughout these commutative algebra notes, *ring* means a commutative ring with a multiplicative identity $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$; *module* means a unital module. The single new construction of this chapter is the **tensor product** $M \otimes_R N$, the object that *linearises bilinearity*: it converts maps that are linear in each argument separately into ordinary linear maps out of one new module. When the base ring $R$ is clear we write $M \otimes N$ for $M \otimes_R N$. The whole chapter turns on one slogan, repeated in Motivation: **the tensor product is the universal receptacle for bilinear maps, and over algebras it is the fibre product of geometric spaces.** A second running theme is that *the base ring matters* — $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}^3$ is enormous while $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes_{\mathbb{C}} \mathbb{C}^3$ is small, because over the smaller ring fewer scalars may be moved across the $\otimes$.

- $R, S$ — commutative rings with $1$; $f : R \to S$ — a ring homomorphism (the background map for restriction/extension of scalars)
- $M, N, P, L$ — $R$-modules; $M', N'$ — further $R$-modules (targets of maps); $A, B, C$ — $R$-algebras
- $M \otimes_R N$ — the **tensor product** of $M$ and $N$ over $R$; an $R$-module
- $m \otimes n$ — a **pure (or simple) tensor**, the image of $(m,n)$; a general **tensor** is a finite sum $\sum_i m_i \otimes n_i$
- $i_{M \otimes N} : M \times N \to M \otimes N$, $(m,n) \mapsto m \otimes n$ — the canonical **bilinear map** into the tensor product
- $F = R^{\oplus(M \times N)}$ — the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] on the set $M \times N$, with basis $\{e_{m,n}\}$; $K \leq F$ — the submodule of bilinearity relations, so $M \otimes_R N = F/K$
- $\operatorname{Bil}_R(M \times N, L)$ — the set of $R$-**bilinear maps** $M \times N \to L$; $\operatorname{Hom}_R(M, N)$ — the $R$-module of $R$-linear maps
- $f \otimes g : M \otimes N \to M' \otimes N'$ — the tensor product of $R$-linear maps $f : M \to M'$, $g : N \to N'$, defined by $(f \otimes g)(m \otimes n) = f(m) \otimes g(n)$
- $[T] \otimes [S]$ or the **Kronecker product** — the matrix of $T \otimes S$ in the lexicographically ordered tensor basis
- $A \otimes_R B$ — the **tensor product of $R$-algebras**, an $R$-algebra under $(b \otimes c)(b' \otimes c') = bb' \otimes cc'$
- $i_A : A \to A \otimes_R B$, $a \mapsto a \otimes 1$, and $i_B : B \to A \otimes_R B$, $b \mapsto 1 \otimes b$ — the **structure maps** of the algebra tensor product
- $S \otimes_R N$ — **extension of scalars** of the $R$-module $N$ along $f : R \to S$; an $S$-module
- $\sqrt{I},\ I+J,\ IJ,\ I^e$ — radical, sum, product, and extension of ideals (used in the algebra computations)
- $\operatorname{rank}(t)$ — the **tensor rank** of $t \in M \otimes_R N$: the least number of pure tensors summing to $t$
- $\mathbb{Z}/m$ — the cyclic group/ring $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$; $\gcd(m,n)$ — greatest common divisor

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **the tensor product is the machine that turns a map which is linear in two arguments separately into a map which is linear in one argument total, and it is the algebraic operation underneath "multiplying spaces together".** You meet bilinearity constantly — the product $rs$ of two ring elements, the action $r \cdot m$ of a scalar on a vector, the pairing $\langle v, w\rangle$ of an inner product, the evaluation $(\varphi, v) \mapsto \varphi(v)$ of a functional on a vector, the multiplication of matrices. Each is a map $M \times N \to L$ that is [[Def - Module Homomorphism|linear]] in $m$ for fixed $n$ and linear in $n$ for fixed $m$, but *not* linear on the product $M \times N$ as a whole (it is quadratic: $(2m, 2n) \mapsto 4\,\square$, not $2\,\square$). The category of modules only understands *linear* maps, so bilinear maps are second-class citizens. The tensor product fixes this once and for all by building a single new module $M \otimes_R N$ and a single bilinear map $i : M \times N \to M \otimes_R N$ through which *every* bilinear map factors uniquely as an honest linear map. Bilinearity is thereby "compiled down" to linearity, and the entire theory of bilinear and multilinear algebra becomes ordinary module theory on a cleverly enlarged module.

The construction itself is brutally direct, and worth holding in mind even though you will almost never use it: take the gigantic [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $F$ on the *set* $M \times N$ — one basis vector $e_{m,n}$ for each pair — and then forcibly impose the bilinearity relations by quotienting out the submodule $K$ generated by $e_{m,n_1} + e_{m,n_2} - e_{m,n_1 + n_2}$, $e_{m_1,n} + e_{m_2,n} - e_{m_1+m_2,n}$, and $r e_{m,n} - e_{rm,n}$, $r e_{m,n} - e_{m,rn}$. The image of $e_{m,n}$ is written $m \otimes n$, and the four relation families become the four computational rules

$$m \otimes (n_1 + n_2) = m\otimes n_1 + m\otimes n_2, \quad (m_1+m_2)\otimes n = m_1\otimes n + m_2\otimes n, \quad r(m\otimes n) = (rm)\otimes n = m \otimes (rn).$$

These are the only laws you ever use; from them, $0 \otimes n = 0 = m \otimes 0$, and any tensor is a finite sum of pure tensors $\sum_i m_i \otimes n_i$. But the free-module presentation is a *model*, not the meaning. The meaning is the **universal property**, and the chapter's first lesson is to stop computing with fractions of bilinearity and start reasoning with the universal arrow.

There are two things that make tensor products genuinely subtle, and both deserve flagging up front. First, **not every tensor is pure**, and *deciding whether a tensor is zero is non-trivial* — you cannot read it off the symbols. The relation defining $M \otimes N$ can collapse things violently: $\mathbb{Z}/2 \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}} \mathbb{Z}/3 = 0$ even though neither factor is zero, because $a \otimes b = 3(a\otimes b) - 2(a \otimes b) = a \otimes 3b - \dots$ slides the scalars until everything dies. To *prove a tensor is zero* you push the relations; to *prove it is nonzero* you must exhibit a single bilinear map that does not kill it. Second, **the answer depends on the base ring $R$.** Tensoring $\mathbb{C}^2$ with $\mathbb{C}^3$ over $\mathbb{C}$ identifies $i(v\otimes w) = v \otimes iw$ and gives the small space $\mathbb{C}^6$; over $\mathbb{R}$ those identifications are illegal — only real scalars cross the $\otimes$ — and the result is the much larger $\mathbb{R}^{24}$. Shrinking the base ring makes fewer identifications and a bigger tensor product. These two facts — collapse and base-dependence — are where every error in the subject comes from.

The chapter has three movements, and they build the dictionary that makes tensor products the algebra of geometry. First, **tensor products of modules**: the universal property, the standard isomorphisms ($M\otimes N \cong N\otimes M$, associativity, distributivity over direct sums, $R \otimes M \cong M$), and functoriality — the rule $f \otimes g$ that makes $\otimes$ act on maps, not just modules, of which the **Kronecker product** of matrices is the concrete shadow. Second, **tensor products of algebras**: when $A$ and $B$ are rings over $R$, $A \otimes_R B$ is again a ring, and it is the **coproduct** of $R$-algebras — the universal algebra receiving maps from both. The headline computation is $R[X_1,\dots,X_n] \otimes_R R[T_1,\dots,T_r] \cong R[X_1,\dots,X_n,T_1,\dots,T_r]$: gluing variable sets is tensoring polynomial rings. Third, **restriction and extension of scalars**: given $f : R \to S$, an $S$-module is automatically an $R$-module (restriction), and an $R$-module $N$ can be *pushed forward* to the $S$-module $S \otimes_R N$ (extension) — the operation that turns a real vector space into a complex one, or a $\mathbb{Z}$-module into a $\mathbb{Z}/n$-module.

The reason this is the spine of algebraic geometry is the algebra–geometry dictionary applied to $\otimes$. A commutative ring is the ring of functions on a space $\mathbf{Spec}\,R$; a ring map $R \to A$ is a map of spaces $\mathbf{Spec}\,A \to \mathbf{Spec}\,R$. Under this translation the tensor product of algebras becomes the **fibre product of spaces**: $\mathbf{Spec}(A \otimes_R B) = \mathbf{Spec}\,A \times_{\mathbf{Spec}\,R} \mathbf{Spec}\,B$. Tensoring polynomial rings is taking the product of affine spaces; extension of scalars $S \otimes_R N$ is **base change** — pulling a geometric object back along $\mathbf{Spec}\,S \to \mathbf{Spec}\,R$, for instance viewing a real variety over the complex numbers. And the deceptively small example $\mathbb{C} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}$ — a tensor product of fields that is *not* a field — is the algebraic fingerprint of a variety that *becomes reducible after base change*: a single point over $\mathbb{R}$ that splits into two points over $\mathbb{C}$, the failure of geometric irreducibility.

This chapter assumes you are fluent with [[Def - Module|modules]], [[Def - Submodule|submodules]], [[Def - Quotient Module|quotient modules]], [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphisms]], [[Def - Free Module|free modules]] and [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules|direct sums]] from the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|Modules]] notes; with [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphisms]] and [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] from the [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings]] notes; and with the notion of an [[Def - Algebra over a Ring (R-algebra)|R-algebra]] from [[Commutative Algebra I — Chain Conditions]]. The vector-space special case — [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces|V⊗ₖW]] and its [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|universal property]] — is the model this chapter generalises from fields to arbitrary rings; if it is fresh, the generalisation is almost free. The next chapter, [[Commutative Algebra III — Flatness and Exactness]], asks the one question this one postpones: how does $M \otimes -$ interact with exact sequences? (Answer: it is right exact but not left exact, and the modules that fix this are the flat ones.)

# Concept Map

## §3.1 Tensor Products of Modules

- **[[Def - Bilinear and Multilinear Maps]]**
	- A map $f : M \times N \to L$ of $R$-modules is **$R$-bilinear** if $v \mapsto f(v, n_0)$ and $w \mapsto f(m_0, w)$ are $R$-linear for every fixed $m_0, n_0$ — linear in each slot separately. More generally $f : M_1 \times \cdots \times M_k \to L$ is **$k$-multilinear** if linear in each argument with the others held fixed. Bilinear maps are *not* linear on $M \times N$ (they are "degree-2"); the set $\operatorname{Bil}_R(M\times N, L)$ is itself an $R$-module. Examples: ring multiplication, scalar action, the determinant as a multilinear function of columns, the evaluation pairing $V^* \times V \to k$.

- **[[Def - Tensor Product of Modules]]**
	- $M \otimes_R N := F/K$, where $F = R^{\oplus(M\times N)}$ is the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] on the set $M \times N$ and $K$ is generated by the four bilinearity relations. The image of $e_{m,n}$ is the **pure tensor** $m \otimes n$; pure tensors generate $M\otimes N$ as an $R$-module (indeed as an abelian group, since $r(m\otimes n) = (rm)\otimes n$), but a general tensor $\sum_i m_i \otimes n_i$ need not be pure. The defining relations give $m\otimes(n_1+n_2) = m\otimes n_1 + m\otimes n_2$, $(m_1+m_2)\otimes n = m_1\otimes n + m_2 \otimes n$, $r(m\otimes n) = (rm)\otimes n = m\otimes(rn)$, and hence $0\otimes n = m\otimes 0 = 0$. The canonical bilinear map is $i_{M\otimes N}(m,n) = m\otimes n$.

- **[[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Modules]]**
	- For every $R$-module $L$ and $R$-bilinear $f : M\times N \to L$, there is a *unique* $R$-linear $h : M\otimes N \to L$ with $f = h \circ i_{M\otimes N}$, i.e. $h(m\otimes n) = f(m,n)$. Equivalently there is a natural bijection $\operatorname{Bil}_R(M\times N, L) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_R(M\otimes N, L)$ — the tensor product *represents the bilinear-maps functor*. The pair $(M\otimes N, i)$ is unique up to unique isomorphism (any other pair with the property is canonically isomorphic), so the universal property, not the fractions, is the true definition. A companion test: $\sum_i m_i \otimes n_i = 0$ in $M\otimes N$ if and only if $\sum_i f(m_i,n_i) = 0$ for *every* bilinear $f$ — the only honest way to certify a tensor nonzero.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The tensor algebra, symmetric and exterior powers *(from Multilinear Algebra)*
> Iterating $\otimes$ builds the **tensor algebra** $T(M) = \bigoplus_k M^{\otimes k}$, the universal associative algebra on $M$. Quotienting by the relations $m\otimes m' = m'\otimes m$ gives the **symmetric algebra** $\operatorname{Sym}(M)$ (which for a free module is a polynomial ring), and by $m \otimes m = 0$ gives the **exterior algebra** $\Lambda(M)$, whose top power $\Lambda^n$ defines the **determinant** basis-free. Each is the universal receptacle for the corresponding flavour of multilinear map; the tensor product is the unrestricted parent.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Differential forms and the cotangent bundle *(from Differential Geometry)*
> A **differential $k$-form** on a manifold is, pointwise, an alternating $k$-multilinear map on the tangent space — an element of $\Lambda^k T_p^* M$, built from the cotangent space by exactly the multilinear machinery this section installs. The fibrewise tensor and exterior products of the cotangent bundle are what make the algebra of forms, the wedge product, and the de Rham complex possible; bilinear/multilinear maps over $R$ are the algebraic seed of tensor fields over a ring of functions.

- **[[Thm - Standard Isomorphisms of Tensor Products]]**
	- The natural isomorphisms making $\otimes$ behave like a product: **commutativity** $M\otimes N \cong N\otimes M$ ($m\otimes n \mapsto n\otimes m$); **associativity** $(M\otimes N)\otimes P \cong M\otimes(N\otimes P) \cong M\otimes N\otimes P$; **distributivity** $\left(\bigoplus_i M_i\right)\otimes P \cong \bigoplus_i (M_i \otimes P)$; **identity** $R\otimes_R M \cong M$ ($r\otimes m \mapsto rm$); and **quotients** $(M/M')\otimes(N/N') \cong (M\otimes N)/L$ for the submodule $L$ generated by $M'\otimes N$ and $M\otimes N'$. Consequences: $R^m \otimes_R R^n \cong R^{mn}$ with basis $\{e_i \otimes f_j\}$, and $R/I \otimes_R R/J \cong R/(I+J)$ — the kernel of $R \to R/I\otimes R/J$ is $I+J$, to be contrasted with the kernel $I\cap J$ of $R \to R/I\times R/J$.

- **[[Thm - Functoriality of the Tensor Product]]**
	- $R$-linear maps $f : M\to M'$, $g : N\to N'$ induce a *unique* $R$-linear $f\otimes g : M\otimes N\to M'\otimes N'$ with $(f\otimes g)(m\otimes n) = f(m)\otimes g(n)$, and this respects composition: $(f\otimes g)\circ(h\otimes i) = (f\circ h)\otimes(g\circ i)$. So $M\otimes -$ and $-\otimes N$ are **functors**. If $f,g$ are isomorphisms (resp. surjective) so is $f\otimes g$; the concrete matrix incarnation is the **Kronecker product** $[T\otimes S]$, a block matrix $([T]_{\ell i}[S])_{\ell,i}$ of size $bd\times ac$. The catch — the seed of the next chapter — is that $f\otimes g$ need **not** preserve injectivity: $\times p : \mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$ tensored with $\mathbb{Z}/p$ is the zero map though $\times p$ is injective.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Right exactness and flat modules *(from Homological Algebra)*
> Functoriality says $M\otimes -$ is a functor; the failure of $f\otimes g$ to stay injective says it is *not exact*. The precise statement — $M\otimes -$ is **right exact** (preserves cokernels and surjections) but not left exact — and the identification of the modules $M$ for which it *is* exact as the **flat modules** is the whole content of [[Commutative Algebra III — Flatness and Exactness]]. The map $\times p \otimes \operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{Z}/p} = 0$ is the canonical witness that $\mathbb{Z}/p$ is not flat.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Kronecker product and quantum entanglement *(from Quantum Information)*
> The state space of a composite quantum system is the tensor product $\mathcal{H}_A \otimes \mathcal{H}_B$ of the component Hilbert spaces, and operators on it are Kronecker products $T\otimes S$. A **pure tensor** $|\psi\rangle = |\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle$ is an *unentangled* (product) state; a tensor that is not pure is **entangled**, and its [[Ex - The Kronecker product of matrices|tensor rank]] (Schmidt rank) measures the degree of entanglement. This section's "most tensors are not pure" is literally "most quantum states are entangled".

## §3.2 Tensor Products of Algebras

- **[[Def - Tensor Product of Algebras]]**
	- For $R$-algebras $B, C$, the $R$-module $B\otimes_R C$ becomes an $R$-algebra under $(b\otimes c)(b'\otimes c') = bb'\otimes cc'$, extended $R$-linearly; well-definedness needs the standard isomorphisms (the multiplication is assembled from $m_B \otimes m_C$ through $(B\otimes C)\otimes(B\otimes C)\cong(B\otimes B)\otimes(C\otimes C)$). The unit is $1\otimes 1$, the structure map is $r\mapsto r(1\otimes 1) = r\otimes 1 = 1\otimes r$ (note $r(1\otimes1)\neq r\otimes r$ in general), and $B\otimes C$ is simultaneously a $B$-algebra ($b\mapsto b\otimes 1$) and a $C$-algebra ($c\mapsto 1\otimes c$). Multiplicativity need only be checked on a generating set, by the upgrading lemma.

- **[[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Algebras]]**
	- $A\otimes_R B$ with $i_A(a) = a\otimes 1$, $i_B(b) = 1\otimes b$ is the **coproduct** in $R$-algebras: for any $R$-algebra $C$ and $R$-algebra maps $f_1 : A\to C$, $f_2 : B\to C$, there is a *unique* $R$-algebra map $h : A\otimes_R B\to C$ with $h\circ i_A = f_1$, $h\circ i_B = f_2$, namely $h(a\otimes b) = f_1(a)f_2(b)$. This characterises $A\otimes_R B$ up to unique isomorphism, and instantly gives $R[X_*]\otimes_R R[T_*] \cong R[X_*,T_*]$ (the right side is the coproduct of polynomial algebras), $A\otimes_R B\cong B\otimes_R A$, $A\otimes_R(B\times C)\cong(A\otimes_R B)\times(A\otimes_R C)$, and associativity. (In sets the coproduct is disjoint union, in groups the free product, in modules the direct sum.)

> [!tip] Unlocked: The fibre product of affine schemes *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Under the dictionary "ring $R$ $\leftrightarrow$ space $\mathbf{Spec}\,R$, ring map $\leftrightarrow$ map of spaces reversed", the algebra tensor product computes the **fibre product**: $\mathbf{Spec}(A\otimes_R B) = \mathbf{Spec}\,A \times_{\mathbf{Spec}\,R}\mathbf{Spec}\,B$. The coproduct universal property of $A\otimes_R B$ is precisely the universal property of a fibre product, with all arrows reversed. The computation $R[X_*]\otimes_R R[T_*]\cong R[X_*,T_*]$ is the statement that $\mathbb{A}^n\times\mathbb{A}^r = \mathbb{A}^{n+r}$ — the product of affine spaces — and $C[X,Y,Z]/(f,g)\otimes C[W,U]/(h)\cong C[X,Y,Z,W,U]/(f,g,h)$ is the product of the varieties they cut out. This is the categorical reason products of varieties exist and why their coordinate ring is the tensor product.

## §3.3 Restriction and Extension of Scalars

- **[[Def - Restriction and Extension of Scalars]]**
	- Fix a ring homomorphism $f : R\to S$. **Restriction**: any $S$-module $M$ becomes an $R$-module via $r\cdot m := f(r)m$ (compose the structure map $S\to\operatorname{End}M$ with $f$); e.g. $\mathbb{C}^n$ restricts to $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$. **Extension**: an $R$-module $N$ is pushed to the $S$-module $S\otimes_R N$ (more generally, for an $S$-module $M$ the $R$-tensor $M\otimes_R N$ is given an $S$-action $s(m\otimes n) = (sm)\otimes n$), well-defined by the universal property; e.g. $\mathbb{R}^n$ extends to $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^n\cong\mathbb{C}^n$. Extension acts on maps too: $\operatorname{id}_M\otimes g$ is $S$-linear, and the matrix of $\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{C}}\otimes T$ is the matrix of $T$ read with complex entries.

- **[[Thm - Extension of Scalars and the Adjunction]]**
	- The two-step formula $M\otimes_R N\cong M\otimes_S(S\otimes_R N)$ as $S$-modules ($m\otimes n\mapsto m\otimes(1\otimes n)$): a complicated extension of scalars factors as a *simple* one ($S\otimes_R N$) followed by an ordinary $S$-tensor. With it come the analogues of the standard isomorphisms over $S$, the base-change identity $S\otimes_R(N\otimes_R N')\cong(S\otimes_R N)\otimes_S(S\otimes_R N')$, and the algebra versions $A\otimes_R B\cong(A\otimes_R S)\otimes_S B$ and $S\otimes_R R[T_*]\cong S[T_*]$. Conceptually this is the **extension–restriction adjunction**: $\operatorname{Hom}_S(S\otimes_R N, M)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_R(N, M)$ — extension of scalars is left adjoint to restriction.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Base change and pullback of modules/sheaves *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Extension of scalars $S\otimes_R(-)$ is the algebra of **base change**: along a map of spaces $\mathbf{Spec}\,S\to\mathbf{Spec}\,R$ it *pulls back* a module (the algebraic model of a vector bundle or quasi-coherent **sheaf**) from the base to the total space. Restriction of scalars is the **pushforward**. The adjunction $\operatorname{Hom}_S(S\otimes_R N, M)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_R(N,M)$ is the pullback–pushforward adjunction $f^*\dashv f_*$ that runs through all of sheaf theory. Viewing a real variety over $\mathbb{C}$, or specialising a family over $\mathbf{Spec}\,\mathbb{Z}$ to a prime $p$ (extend along $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{F}_p$), are both this single operation.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring goals cluster into five types. The first is **identifying a tensor product concretely** — recognising $M\otimes_R N$ as a module or ring you already know ($R^{mn}$, $M/IM$, $S^n$, $\mathbb{Z}/\gcd$, $\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$, a polynomial ring in more variables). The universal property is the standard route: build a bilinear (or bi-algebra) map into the candidate and a map back, and check they are inverse — or show the candidate has the universal property directly and invoke uniqueness. The second is **deciding whether a tensor is zero or nonzero** — a genuinely two-sided problem: to prove zero, slide scalars using $r(m\otimes n) = (rm)\otimes n = m\otimes(rn)$ until the tensor collapses; to prove nonzero, manufacture *one* bilinear map $f$ with $\sum f(m_i,n_i)\neq 0$. The third is **establishing a standard isomorphism or its consequence** — commutativity, associativity, distributivity, $R/I\otimes R/J\cong R/(I+J)$ — almost always by the universal property, "produce both directions and check they compose to identities on pure tensors". The fourth is **computing an extension/base change** — what is the $S$-module $M\otimes_R N$, or the $S$-algebra $A\otimes_R B$? The lever is the two-step formula $M\otimes_R N\cong M\otimes_S(S\otimes_R N)$ that reduces to a simple extension. The fifth is **transferring a property along $f\otimes g$ or extension** — surjectivity, freeness, basis, eigenvalue, rank — using functoriality and that $1\otimes T$ keeps the matrix of $T$. These are the natural targets because the tensor product is fundamentally a *construction* tool: every theorem says "this combination of modules/algebras is again a recognisable object, with a predictable universal property".

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A bilinear map is in hand (or wanted)** — the universal property converts it to a linear map out of $M\otimes N$, or vice versa; recognising bilinearity is the entry ticket. **A module/algebra is presented as a quotient $R/I$, $M/N$, or $R[X]/(f)$** — then $\otimes$ commutes with the quotient ($R/I\otimes M\cong M/IM$, $A\otimes_R B\cong B[X]/(\bar f)$), which is how almost every concrete computation is done. **A generating set or basis is given** — pure tensors of generators generate the tensor product, and $\{e_i\otimes f_j\}$ is a basis when both factors are free, so freeness/dimension/rank transfer. **A ring homomorphism $f : R\to S$ lives in the background** — switching on restriction (an $S$-module is an $R$-module) and extension ($S\otimes_R N$), and the two-step factorisation. **A map is to be tensored** — functoriality gives $f\otimes g$, the Kronecker product makes it a matrix, and one watches whether the property survives (surjectivity yes, injectivity no). The discipline is to match source to target: a bilinear map routes through the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Modules|universal property]]; a quotient presentation routes through the [[Thm - Standard Isomorphisms of Tensor Products|quotient isomorphism]]; a background ring map routes through [[Thm - Extension of Scalars and the Adjunction|extension of scalars]]; an algebra-pair routes through the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Algebras|coproduct]]. The [[Commutative Algebra II — Tensor Products#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every tensor-product problem is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

1. **Slide scalars across the $\otimes$.** The single most-used rule: $r(m\otimes n) = (rm)\otimes n = m\otimes(rn)$ for $r\in R$. Use it to move a scalar from one side to the other until a tensor collapses ($a\otimes 3b = 3a\otimes b$, and if $3b = 0$ the tensor dies), or to a normal form. *Trigger:* you want to show a tensor is zero, or simplify a pure tensor. *Pattern:* "push the offending scalar onto the factor where it annihilates".

2. **Replace a bilinear map by a linear map out of $M\otimes N$ (and back).** By the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Modules|universal property]], an $R$-bilinear $f : M\times N\to L$ is the *same data* as an $R$-linear $h : M\otimes N\to L$ with $h(m\otimes n) = f(m,n)$. To define a map *out of* $M\otimes N$, never check well-definedness by hand — exhibit a bilinear map and let the property hand you $h$. *Trigger:* "define / construct a homomorphism from $M\otimes N$". *Pattern:* "the assignment on pure tensors is bilinear, so it extends uniquely".

3. **Certify a tensor nonzero with one bilinear map.** $\sum_i m_i\otimes n_i\neq 0$ as soon as some bilinear $f : M\times N\to L$ has $\sum_i f(m_i,n_i)\neq 0$. Manufacturing that single $f$ (often a projection or pairing) is the *only* way to prove non-vanishing. *Trigger:* you suspect a tensor is nonzero but cannot reduce it. *Pattern:* "build a coordinate/pairing map that survives the relations".

4. **Build an isomorphism by maps both ways on pure tensors.** To prove $T\otimes N\cong$ (candidate), define $R$-linear maps in both directions via the universal property, then check the composites are identities — and it suffices to check on pure tensors since they generate. *Trigger:* "show $X\otimes Y\cong Z$". *Pattern:* "$\varphi,\psi$ on pure tensors; $\psi\varphi = \operatorname{id}$, $\varphi\psi = \operatorname{id}$ on generators".

5. **Push $\otimes$ through a quotient.** $R/I\otimes_R M\cong M/IM$, and $(M/M')\otimes(N/N')\cong(M\otimes N)/L$, and for algebras $R[X]/(f)\otimes_R B\cong B[X]/(\bar f)$. This turns abstract tensors into concrete quotient modules/rings. *Trigger:* a factor is presented as a quotient. *Pattern:* "tensoring with $R/I$ = reducing mod $I$".

6. **Use that pure tensors of generators generate.** If $S$ generates $M$ and $T$ generates $N$, then $\{s\otimes t : s\in S, t\in T\}$ generates $M\otimes N$; if both are free with bases, this set is a basis. *Trigger:* you need a spanning/basis set, a dimension, or a rank. *Pattern:* "reduce any computation to the basis tensors $e_i\otimes f_j$".

7. **Tensor a map: apply $f\otimes g$ and track what survives.** $f\otimes g$ exists and is functorial; isomorphisms and surjections survive, injections need not. Its matrix is the [[Ex - The Kronecker product of matrices|Kronecker product]]. *Trigger:* a linear map appears with a tensor product, or you want to transfer a property. *Pattern:* "$f\otimes g$ on pure tensors is $f(m)\otimes g(n)$; check surjectivity (free), beware injectivity".

8. **Factor an extension of scalars in two steps.** $M\otimes_R N\cong M\otimes_S(S\otimes_R N)$: prepare $N$ by the simple extension $S\otimes_R N$, then tensor over $S$. *Trigger:* an $S$-module structure on an $R$-tensor is wanted, or $M$ is an $S$-module. *Pattern:* "extend $N$ to $S$ first, then work entirely over $S$".

9. **Invoke the coproduct universal property for algebras.** To build an $R$-algebra map *out of* $A\otimes_R B$, give $R$-algebra maps $A\to C$ and $B\to C$; the unique $h(a\otimes b) = f_1(a)f_2(b)$ is handed to you. To identify $A\otimes_R B$, show the candidate is the coproduct. *Trigger:* "construct a map from $A\otimes_R B$" or "identify it as a known algebra". *Pattern:* "two algebra maps in, one map out".

10. **Upgrade an $R$-linear map to an $R$-algebra map on generators.** If $f : A\to B$ is $R$-linear, $f(1)=1$, and $f$ is multiplicative on a *generating set* $S$ of $A$ as an $R$-module, then $f$ is an $R$-algebra homomorphism. *Trigger:* you have a linear isomorphism and want it to respect multiplication. *Pattern:* "check $1\mapsto 1$ and multiplicativity on monomials/generators only".

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming every tensor is a pure tensor
> Pure tensors $m\otimes n$ generate $M\otimes N$, so it is tempting to think a general element *is* one. It is not: in $R^2\otimes_R R^2$ the element $e_1\otimes e_1 + 2\,e_1\otimes e_2 + 3\,e_2\otimes e_1 + 4\,e_2\otimes e_2$ is **not** pure, because a pure tensor $(\alpha e_1+\beta e_2)\otimes(\gamma e_1+\delta e_2)$ has coefficient matrix $\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\gamma & \alpha\delta\\ \beta\gamma & \beta\delta\end{pmatrix}$ of *rank $\leq 1$*, while $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}$ has rank $2$. A tensor is pure exactly when its coefficient matrix has rank $\leq 1$; its **tensor rank** is the matrix rank. (And $3\,e_1\otimes e_1 + 4\,e_1\otimes e_2 + 6\,e_2\otimes e_1 + 8\,e_2\otimes e_2 = (e_1+2e_2)\otimes(3e_1+4e_2)$ *is* pure — rank-1 matrix — even though it does not look it.) The repair is to remember that purity is a rank-1 condition, not a default.

> [!warning] 2. Reading "$m\otimes n = 0$" without naming the tensor product
> The symbol $2\otimes\bar 1$ is meaningless until you say *which* tensor product. In $\mathbb{Z}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/2$ it is $0$, because $2\otimes\bar1 = 2(1\otimes\bar1) = 1\otimes\overline{2} = 1\otimes\bar 0 = 0$. In $(2\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Z}/2$ the *same symbol* is **nonzero**: the bilinear map $b(2x, \bar y) = \overline{xy}$ has $b(2,\bar1) = \bar1\neq 0$. The computation that worked in the first used $2\cdot(\text{something}) = 1\cdot(2\cdot\text{something})$, illegal inside $2\mathbb{Z}$ where you cannot factor out the inverted scalar. The repair: vanishing of a tensor is *not* inherited from a submodule and *not* a property of the symbols — it depends on the ambient $M, N, R$. ("Going the other way works": if $\sum m_i\otimes n_i = 0$ in $M'\otimes N'$ it is $0$ in $M\otimes N$.)

> [!warning] 3. Expecting $f\otimes g$ to preserve injectivity
> Functoriality preserves isomorphisms and surjections, so by analogy one expects injectivity to survive too. It does not. The map $\times p : \mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$ is injective, $\operatorname{id} : \mathbb{Z}/p\to\mathbb{Z}/p$ is injective, but $(\times p)\otimes\operatorname{id} : \mathbb{Z}\otimes\mathbb{Z}/p\to\mathbb{Z}\otimes\mathbb{Z}/p$ sends $a\otimes\bar b \mapsto pa\otimes\bar b = a\otimes\overline{pb} = a\otimes\bar 0 = 0$ — it is the **zero map** on $\mathbb{Z}\otimes\mathbb{Z}/p\cong\mathbb{Z}/p\neq 0$. The repair condition: $f\otimes g$ preserves injectivity exactly when the factor being tensored is **flat** (the defining property of flatness), and $\mathbb{Z}/p$ is not flat over $\mathbb{Z}$. This single failure is the entire reason [[Commutative Algebra III — Flatness and Exactness]] exists.

> [!warning] 4. Confusing $r(1\otimes 1)$ with $r\otimes r$ in $B\otimes_R C$
> The structure map of the algebra $B\otimes_R C$ sends $r\mapsto r(1\otimes 1)$, and by sliding scalars $r(1\otimes 1) = (r\cdot 1)\otimes 1 = r\otimes 1 = 1\otimes r$. It is tempting to write the image as $r\otimes r$, but that is wrong: $r\otimes r = r(1\otimes r) = r\cdot r(1\otimes 1) = r^2(1\otimes 1)\neq r(1\otimes 1)$ in general. The repair: a scalar enters $B\otimes_R C$ on *one* side only (either side, they agree); doubling it onto both sides squares it. This is exactly why $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}$ is two-dimensional over $\mathbb{C}$ and not one — the two copies of $\mathbb{C}$ are independent, not identified.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any tensor-product problem is to name the *role* the tensor product is playing, because there are only a few, and each has a characteristic route.

If the problem **asks you to construct or identify a tensor product** — build a map out of $M\otimes N$, or recognise $M\otimes N$ as a known object — abandon the free-module presentation and reach for the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Modules|universal property]]. A map out of $M\otimes N$ is the *same data* as a bilinear map out of $M\times N$; so to define $h : M\otimes N\to L$, write down the bilinear $f(m,n)$, observe it is linear in each slot, and the property hands you $h$ with $h(m\otimes n) = f(m,n)$ — no well-definedness check required. To prove $M\otimes N\cong Z$, either build maps both ways and check they compose to the identity on pure tensors, or show $Z$ carries the universal property and invoke uniqueness. The recurring trap is to try to verify a fraction-level formula by hand; the universal property exists precisely to spare you that.

If the problem **asks whether a tensor vanishes**, recognise that this is two different problems wearing one mask. To prove $\sum m_i\otimes n_i = 0$, you compute *inside* the tensor product, sliding scalars $r(m\otimes n) = (rm)\otimes n = m\otimes(rn)$ until everything collapses to $0$ — this is how $\mathbb{Z}/2\otimes\mathbb{Z}/3 = 0$ and $2\otimes\bar1 = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}\otimes\mathbb{Z}/2$. To prove $\sum m_i\otimes n_i\neq 0$, you must step *outside* and exhibit a single bilinear map $f$ with $\sum f(m_i,n_i)\neq 0$ — by the universal-property test, that is necessary and sufficient. The art is choosing $f$: a coordinate projection, a determinant, an evaluation pairing. When in doubt about which way the answer goes, try to collapse first; if the relations refuse to finish the job, that resistance often points to the surviving bilinear map.

If the problem **involves a quotient or a presentation by relations**, push the tensor product through it. $R/I\otimes_R M\cong M/IM$ converts "tensor with the residue ring" into "reduce the module mod $I$"; $R[X]/(f)\otimes_R B\cong B[X]/(\bar f)$ converts a tensor of algebras into a single quotient ring, and is how $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}[X]/(X^2+1)\cong\mathbb{C}[X]/(X-i)(X+i)\cong\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$ is computed. Recognising a factor as a quotient is the recognition step, and the quotient isomorphism is almost always the fastest path to a concrete answer.

If the problem **has a background ring homomorphism $f : R\to S$** — anything about complexifying, reducing mod $p$, base change, or an $S$-module structure on an $R$-tensor — you are doing restriction/extension of scalars, and the controlling fact is the two-step factorisation $M\otimes_R N\cong M\otimes_S(S\otimes_R N)$. Prepare the $R$-module $N$ by the *simple* extension $S\otimes_R N$ (which for free modules just replaces $R^n$ by $S^n$, keeping the same basis and the same matrices), then work entirely over $S$. The same factorisation in algebra form, $A\otimes_R B\cong(A\otimes_R S)\otimes_S B$, breaks a complicated algebra extension into a simple one followed by an $S$-tensor.

For **algebra problems**, the coproduct universal property is the analogue of the module one: a map out of $A\otimes_R B$ is a pair of algebra maps into the common target, and the upgrading lemma lets you check multiplicativity on generators only. This is why $R[X_*]\otimes_R R[T_*]\cong R[X_*,T_*]$: the polynomial ring on the union of variables receives maps from both polynomial rings and is universal among such — it is literally the coproduct.

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "what is the universal home for this flavour of multilinear data, and what does it look like concretely?"** Bilinear data over modules lives in $M\otimes_R N$; pairs of algebra maps live in $A\otimes_R B$; a module pushed along $R\to S$ lives in $S\otimes_R N$. Decide which universal property governs the data, write the arrows, and the concrete identification follows from the standard isomorphisms and the quotient rule.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product of Modules|The universal property]]**: every bilinear map factors uniquely through $M\otimes N$, i.e. $\operatorname{Bil}_R(M\times N, L)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_R(M\otimes N, L)$. This is the most reused fact because it converts *all* questions about maps out of a tensor product into questions about bilinear maps, which are concrete and checkable slot-by-slot. Its standard uses are constructing homomorphisms (write the bilinear formula, get the linear extension free), proving two constructions agree (both have the property, hence are uniquely isomorphic), and certifying non-vanishing (a surviving bilinear map). **Typical use:** reach for it whenever a tensor product appears as the *source* of a map, or whenever well-definedness on pure tensors would otherwise need checking.

- **[[Thm - Standard Isomorphisms of Tensor Products|The standard isomorphisms]]**: $\otimes$ is commutative, associative, distributes over $\bigoplus$, has unit $R$, and commutes with quotients. Their reusability is that they let you *rearrange and compute* tensor products like an ordinary product — $R^m\otimes R^n\cong R^{mn}$, $R/I\otimes R/J\cong R/(I+J)$, $\bigoplus$-distribution turning any free tensor into a sum of copies of $R$. **Typical use:** the quotient isomorphism $R/I\otimes M\cong M/IM$ is the single most common concrete computation in the subject; distributivity reduces free-module tensors to dimension counts.

- **[[Thm - Functoriality of the Tensor Product|Functoriality and the Kronecker product]]**: $f\otimes g$ exists, composes correctly, and preserves isomorphisms and surjections. Its reusability is that it lets $\otimes$ act on *maps and diagrams*, not just objects, so properties transfer along it; the Kronecker product is its matrix shadow, with eigenvalues $\lambda\mu$ of $A\otimes B$ and $\lambda+\mu$ of $A\otimes I + I\otimes B$. **Typical use:** transferring surjectivity/basis/rank across a tensor; computing the spectrum of a composite system; and — by its *failure* to preserve injectivity — defining flatness in the next chapter.

- **[[Thm - Extension of Scalars and the Adjunction|Extension of scalars and the two-step formula]]**: $M\otimes_R N\cong M\otimes_S(S\otimes_R N)$, with extension left adjoint to restriction. Its reusability is as a *reduction strategy*: it breaks any base change into a simple extension plus an $S$-tensor, and the adjunction $\operatorname{Hom}_S(S\otimes_R N, M)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_R(N, M)$ converts $S$-linear questions to $R$-linear ones. **Typical use:** complexification, reduction mod $p$, and any computation of the $S$-module structure on an $R$-tensor; the workhorse $(R/I)\otimes M\cong M/IM$ is its simplest instance.

- **The coproduct property of $A\otimes_R B$ (algebras)**: $A\otimes_R B$ is universal among $R$-algebras receiving maps from $A$ and $B$. Its reusability is that it makes $A\otimes_R B$ the canonical "join" of two algebras over a common base, computed concretely as a polynomial-ring/quotient gluing. **Typical use:** $R[X_*]\otimes R[T_*]\cong R[X_*,T_*]$ (product of affine spaces), $R[X]/(f)\otimes_R B\cong B[X]/(\bar f)$ (base change of a hypersurface), and the geometric fibre product $\mathbf{Spec}(A\otimes_R B) = \mathbf{Spec}\,A\times_{\mathbf{Spec}\,R}\mathbf{Spec}\,B$.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — the tensor product of algebras is the fibre product of spaces, and extension of scalars is base change.** To a ring $R$ one attaches the space $\mathbf{Spec}\,R$, and a ring map $R\to A$ becomes a map of spaces $\mathbf{Spec}\,A\to\mathbf{Spec}\,R$ pointing the *other* way. Under this reversal the coproduct $A\otimes_R B$ of algebras becomes the **fibre product** $\mathbf{Spec}\,A\times_{\mathbf{Spec}\,R}\mathbf{Spec}\,B$ — the universal space mapping to both $\mathbf{Spec}\,A$ and $\mathbf{Spec}\,B$ compatibly over $\mathbf{Spec}\,R$. Concretely $R[X_*]\otimes_R R[T_*]\cong R[X_*,T_*]$ says $\mathbb{A}^n\times\mathbb{A}^r = \mathbb{A}^{n+r}$, and $C[X,Y,Z]/(f,g)\otimes C[W,U]/(h)\cong C[X,Y,Z,W,U]/(f,g,h)$ says the product of the variety $V(f,g)\subseteq\mathbb{A}^3$ with $V(h)\subseteq\mathbb{A}^2$ is $V(f,g,h)\subseteq\mathbb{A}^5$. Extension of scalars $S\otimes_R N$ is **base change**: pulling a module (a vector bundle / quasi-coherent sheaf) back along $\mathbf{Spec}\,S\to\mathbf{Spec}\,R$. So this chapter is the affine-local algebra of products and base change for all of scheme theory.

2. **Field theory and Galois theory — $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$ is geometric reducibility, and $A\otimes_R B\cong B[X]/(\bar f)$ is how a polynomial factors after base change.** When $A = R[X]/(f)$ is a simple extension, $A\otimes_R B\cong B[X]/(\bar f)$, where $\bar f$ is $f$ read with coefficients in $B$; the tensor product is a *field* exactly when $f$ stays irreducible over $B$, and *splits into a product* when $f$ factors. Tensoring $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)$ with $\mathbb{C}$ factors $X^2+1 = (X-i)(X+i)$ over $\mathbb{C}$, giving $\mathbb{C}[X]/((X-i)(X+i))\cong\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$ by the Chinese Remainder Theorem — two copies, two idempotents $\tfrac{1\pm i\otimes \cdot}{2}$, hence not a field. Geometrically $\mathbf{Spec}\,\mathbb{C}$ is one point over $\mathbb{R}$ but $\mathbf{Spec}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C})$ is *two* points over $\mathbb{C}$: the point fails to be **geometrically irreducible**, and the number of points it splits into counts the Galois-conjugate embeddings. The whole theory of separable/étale algebras is "$L\otimes_K\bar K$ is a product of copies of $\bar K$".

3. **Quantum mechanics and information — composite systems are tensor products, and non-pure tensors are entanglement.** The state space of a quantum system built from parts $A$ and $B$ is the tensor product $\mathcal{H}_A\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\mathcal{H}_B$ of the component Hilbert spaces; observables and time-evolution operators on the composite are Kronecker products $T\otimes S$, with eigenvalues $\lambda\mu$ exactly as in this chapter's functoriality. A state that *is* a pure tensor $|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle$ is an **unentangled** product state — the two subsystems are independent. A state that is not a pure tensor is **entangled**, and its [[Ex - The Kronecker product of matrices|tensor rank]] (the Schmidt rank) quantifies how entangled it is. The chapter's structural fact "most tensors are not pure, and purity is a rank-1 condition" is, word for word, "most quantum states are entangled, and the Schmidt rank measures it"; the impossibility of writing certain tensors as products is the mathematical content of Bell-type nonlocality.

4. **Homological algebra — functoriality plus the failure of injectivity defines $\operatorname{Tor}$ and flatness.** This chapter establishes that $M\otimes -$ is a functor and that it can *destroy injectivity* ($\times p\otimes\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{Z}/p} = 0$). The precise repair is the next chapter's content: $M\otimes -$ is **right exact** (it preserves cokernels and surjections, by the quotient and distributivity isomorphisms) but not left exact, and the discrepancy is measured by the derived functors $\operatorname{Tor}^R_i(M, -)$, with $\operatorname{Tor}_0 = M\otimes -$. A module $M$ is **flat** precisely when $M\otimes -$ *is* exact, equivalently all higher $\operatorname{Tor}$ vanish; $\mathbb{Z}/p$ is the standard non-flat module, witnessed by the very map above. Localization (Chapter IV) is the prototype flat extension, and extension of scalars along a flat map preserves exactness — the homological backbone of base change.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: the tensor product is a compiler from bilinear to linear, and "universal" means "loses no information and adds no relations beyond the forced ones".** Every construction in this chapter is one instance of representing a functor — finding a single object that *embodies* a flavour of map. $M\otimes_R N$ embodies bilinear maps out of $M\times N$; $A\otimes_R B$ embodies pairs of algebra maps; $S\otimes_R N$ embodies $S$-linear maps that restrict to a given $R$-linear one. The free-module presentation $F/K$ makes the "loses no information, adds no relations" precise: $F$ is the most permissive thing (a basis for *every* pair), and $K$ imposes *exactly* the bilinearity relations and nothing more, so a bilinear map is the same as a linear map that already respects $K$. This is why proofs never compute with the fractions — the relations are the whole content, and the universal property packages them. The slogan to carry is that **$\otimes$ is the left adjoint that turns the bilinear into the linear, just as the free module is the left adjoint that turns a set into a module and localization is the left adjoint that turns elements into units.** Each is a universal solution to "freely add the structure I want while forcing the relations I demand".

**The true name of the tensor product: it is not "formal sums $\sum m_i\otimes n_i$" but "the receptacle through which all bilinear maps factor".** The fraction-level description is for hands-on computation — collapsing $\mathbb{Z}/2\otimes\mathbb{Z}/3$, finding a basis, reducing mod $I$. But the operational characterisation, the one you actually reach for when proving anything structural, is the universal property: *to map out of $M\otimes N$, give a bilinear map; to identify $M\otimes N$, match the universal property; to certify a tensor nonzero, find a surviving bilinear map.* This reorganises the two hardest skills. **Proving a tensor is zero** is an *inside* computation (slide scalars until collapse); **proving it is nonzero** is an *outside* construction (exhibit one bilinear map that does not kill it). They feel like the same question but require opposite manoeuvres, and recognising which you are in is the single highest-leverage move in the subject. The symmetry to internalise: zero is certified by the relations, nonzero is certified by a map that respects the relations yet survives.

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "tensor with $R/I$" means "reduce mod $I$", and "tensor a free module" means "keep the basis, change the scalars".** When you see $R/I\otimes_R M$, the reaction is instant: this is $M/IM$, the module with its $I$-multiples killed — so $\mathbb{Z}/n\otimes\mathbb{Z}^k = (\mathbb{Z}/n)^k$, and $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{R}^n = \mathbb{C}^n$. When you see $S\otimes_R R^n$ for a free module, the reaction is "$S^n$, with the *same* basis $1\otimes e_i$ and the *same* matrices reinterpreted over $S$" — complexifying a real linear map does not change its matrix, only the field the entries live in. The complementary pattern is the **purity test**: when asked whether a tensor in a free module is pure, write its coefficient matrix and check the rank — pure means rank $\leq 1$, and the tensor rank *is* the matrix rank. These three reflexes — *tensor with a residue ring = reduce*, *tensor a free module = rebase*, *purity = rank one* — between them dispatch the large majority of concrete computations, and all three are pure consequences of distributivity plus the quotient isomorphism. The deepest of them, and the bridge to everything downstream, is the fourth reflex: when $f\otimes g$ *fails* to be injective, the word that should fire is **flat** — the property that fixes the failure, and the gateway to homological algebra.
