# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Let $A$ be a [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian]] ring, $\mathfrak{p}, \mathfrak{q} \in \operatorname{Spec} A$ primes, $\mathfrak{a} = (x_1, \dots, x_r)$ an ideal generated by $r$ elements. We write $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p}$ for the [[Def - Krull Dimension and Height|height]] of $\mathfrak{p}$ (the length of the longest chain of primes ending at $\mathfrak{p}$), and recall $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = \dim A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ where $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is the [[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field|localization]]. A **minimal prime of $\mathfrak{a}$** is a [[Def - Associated and Minimal Primes|minimal element]] of $\{\mathfrak{p} \in \operatorname{Spec} A : \mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{p}\}$. An ideal $\mathfrak{q}$ in a local ring $(A, \mathfrak{m})$ is **$\mathfrak{m}$-primary** if $\sqrt{\mathfrak{q}} = \mathfrak{m}$. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra XII — Dimension Theory]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Krull's height theorem).** Let $A$ be a Noetherian ring and $\mathfrak{a} = (x_1, \dots, x_r)$ an ideal generated by $r$ elements. Then every minimal prime $\mathfrak{p}$ of $\mathfrak{a}$ satisfies
> $$\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} \leq r.$$

> **Corollary (Krull's principal ideal theorem, the hypersurface case).** If $x \in A$ is a single element, every minimal prime of $(x)$ has height $\leq 1$. If moreover $x$ is a non-zero-divisor and a non-unit, every minimal prime of $(x)$ has height *exactly* $1$.

> **Converse (existence of a height-$r$ ideal).** Conversely, if $\mathfrak{a}$ is a proper ideal with $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} = r$, there exist $a_1, \dots, a_r \in \mathfrak{a}$ with $\operatorname{ht}(a_1, \dots, a_i) = i$ for each $0 \leq i \leq r$; in particular $\mathfrak{a}$ contains an $r$-generated ideal of height $r$, so the bound $\operatorname{ht} \leq (\text{number of generators})$ is sharp.

The principal-ideal case ($r = 1$) is the geometric statement that *a single equation cuts dimension by exactly one*: a hypersurface in an irreducible $n$-dimensional space has every component of dimension $n - 1$.

# Motivation

Linear algebra has a clean rule: one linear equation on $k^n$ cuts out a hyperplane of dimension $n - 1$, and $r$ equations cut out a subspace of dimension at least $n - r$. Krull's height theorem is the statement that this *survives the passage to nonlinear equations and general Noetherian rings* — but only as a one-sided bound, and the subtlety of why it is one-sided is the whole content. You cannot hope for equality in general: in $k[X, Y]$ the ideal $(XY)$ is principal yet its zero set is the union of two lines, each of dimension $1$, while $(X^2, XY)$ has two generators but its radical is $(X)$, height $1$, so the height can be strictly below the number of generators. What is always true is the upper bound: $r$ generators force every minimal prime to have height at most $r$. Geometrically, **$r$ equations cannot cut codimension by more than $r$** — each equation removes at most one dimension.

Why is even the upper bound nontrivial? Because height is defined by chains of primes, an object with no obvious relation to "number of generators". A priori, an ideal cut out by a few equations could have a component buried arbitrarily deep in the prime lattice. Krull's theorem says no: the depth of any component is capped by the number of equations. This is one of the foundational finiteness results of the subject — it is what guarantees that the codimension of a variety is controlled by the number of defining equations, and it is the precise algebraic form of the dimension count that pervades intersection theory.

The theorem is, at bottom, a *re-reading of the dimension theorem*. Localize at the minimal prime $\mathfrak{p}$: then $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ becomes $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$-primary (its radical is the maximal ideal of the local ring), generated by the $r$ images of the $x_i$. The [[Thm - The Dimension Theorem for Noetherian Local Rings|dimension theorem]] says the dimension of a local ring equals the *minimal* number of generators of any primary ideal, so $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = \dim A_{\mathfrak{p}} = \delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}}) \leq r$. The entire force of Krull's theorem is packed into the inequality $\dim = \delta$, the constructive face of dimension; once you have that, the height bound is a one-line localization.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition is "$\mathfrak{p}$ is a minimal prime of an $r$-generated ideal in a Noetherian ring". Several situations are secretly this.

The first disguised source is **a variety defined by $r$ equations**, $V(f_1, \dots, f_r) \subseteq \mathbb{A}^n$. The property $B$ is "an algebraic set cut out by $r$ polynomials". Each irreducible component corresponds to a minimal prime of $(f_1, \dots, f_r)$, so by the theorem each has codimension $\leq r$, i.e. dimension $\geq n - r$. The non-obviousness: a *global* lower bound on the dimension of every component follows from a *local* height bound. *Example problem:* show every component of $V(f_1, \dots, f_r) \subseteq \mathbb{A}^n$ has dimension $\geq n - r$ — see [[Ex - Krull's principal ideal theorem and hypersurfaces]].

The second disguised source is **a non-zero-divisor in a domain**, $x \in A$ with $A$ a Noetherian domain. The property $B$ is "$x$ is a nonzero non-unit". Then $(x)$ is a proper ideal, $(0)$ is its unique prime of height $0$ but $(0) \not\supseteq (x)$, so every minimal prime of $(x)$ has height $\geq 1$; combined with the theorem, height exactly $1$. The non-obviousness: a single nonzero equation in a nice ring carves out *pure* codimension one — no embedded high-codimension pieces. *Example problem:* prime divisors in a UFD are principal, the first step toward [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization]].

The third disguised source is **a localization that turns an ideal $\mathfrak{m}$-primary**. The property $B$ is "$\mathfrak{p}$ is minimal over $\mathfrak{a}$, and I localize at $\mathfrak{p}$". Then $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$-primary because $\mathfrak{p}$ is the *only* prime of $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ containing it (minimality). The non-obviousness: minimality globally becomes "radical equals the maximal ideal" locally, the exact hypothesis the dimension theorem needs. *Example problem:* this is the proof of the theorem itself.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} \leq r$ for every minimal prime $\mathfrak{p}$ of an $r$-generated ideal".

Combine the height bound with **a domain lower bound** to pin codimension exactly. The additional input $D$ is "$A$ is a domain and the generators avoid $(0)$, so heights are $\geq 1$ where expected". Then for $r = 1$ one gets $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = 1$ exactly. The result $E$ is *pure codimension one* for hypersurfaces — no embedded components of higher codimension. Non-obvious because the theorem alone gives only $\leq$; the domain structure supplies $\geq$.

Combine the bound with **the converse** to characterize height. The additional input $D$ is "$\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} = r$ is known". Then the converse builds $r$ elements of $\mathfrak{a}$ generating a height-$r$ ideal, so $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} = \min\{$generators needed to reach height $r\}$. The result $E$ is that height equals "minimal number of equations to reach that codimension" — the defining property of a system of parameters globalized. Non-obvious because it inverts the inequality into an exact description.

Combine the bound with **dimension theory of the quotient** to get $\dim A/\mathfrak{a} \geq \dim A - r$. The additional input $D$ is "$A$ is a finitely generated domain over a field, so $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} + \dim A/\mathfrak{p} = \dim A$". Then $\dim A/\mathfrak{a} = \max_{\mathfrak{p}} \dim A/\mathfrak{p} \geq \dim A - r$. The result $E$ is the dimension-of-intersection bound. Non-obvious because it converts a height bound into a dimension bound via the catenary identity — see [[Ex - Height plus dimension of the quotient equals dimension]].

# Why Is It True

The theorem is true because it *is* the dimension theorem, viewed at a single prime. The whole proof is the observation that minimality of $\mathfrak{p}$ over $\mathfrak{a}$, after localizing, becomes exactly the primary condition the dimension theorem feeds on.

Here is the mechanism. Fix a minimal prime $\mathfrak{p}$ of $\mathfrak{a} = (x_1, \dots, x_r)$ and pass to the local ring $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$. Two things happen. First, $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = \dim A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ by definition of height — the primes below $\mathfrak{p}$ are precisely the primes of $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$. Second, and this is the key, the extended ideal $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ becomes $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$-primary. Why? Because $\mathfrak{p}$ is *minimal* over $\mathfrak{a}$, so $\mathfrak{p}$ is the only prime of $A$ containing $\mathfrak{a}$ and contained in $\mathfrak{p}$; after localizing, $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is the only prime containing $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, which means $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}} = \mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ — the radical is the maximal ideal, i.e. $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is primary to the maximal ideal.

Now invoke the [[Thm - The Dimension Theorem for Noetherian Local Rings|dimension theorem]]: in a Noetherian local ring, $\dim = \delta$, the minimal number of generators of *any* primary ideal. We have exhibited one primary ideal, $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, generated by the $r$ images $\tfrac{x_1}{1}, \dots, \tfrac{x_r}{1}$. So $\delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}}) \leq r$, hence
$$\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = \dim A_{\mathfrak{p}} = \delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}}) \leq r.$$
That is the entire proof. The depth of the content is upstream, in the equality $\dim = \delta$; Krull's theorem is the harvest.

**The one-line mechanism: localize at the minimal prime; minimality makes the $r$-generated ideal primary to the maximal ideal; the dimension theorem says the dimension is at most the number of generators of any such ideal, so the height is at most $r$.**

For the *converse*, run the construction inside the dimension theorem in reverse: given $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} = r$, build elements $a_1, \dots, a_r \in \mathfrak{a}$ one at a time by prime avoidance, at each step choosing $a_i$ outside the finitely many height-$(i-1)$ primes minimal over $(a_1, \dots, a_{i-1})$ *and* keeping inside $\mathfrak{a}$ — possible because $\mathfrak{a}$ is not contained in any of those lower primes (else $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} < r$). This forces $\operatorname{ht}(a_1, \dots, a_i) = i$ at each stage, the bound being achieved.

# What Makes This Hard

The difficulty is entirely upstream: Krull's height theorem is a two-line corollary of the dimension theorem, so all the genuine work — the analytic induction that killing an element drops the Hilbert–Samuel degree by one — lives in [[Thm - The Dimension Theorem for Noetherian Local Rings|that theorem]], not here. The one conceptual step that trips people is recognizing that *minimality of $\mathfrak{p}$ over $\mathfrak{a}$* is what makes $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ primary to the maximal ideal — without minimality there could be a prime strictly between $\mathfrak{a}$ and $\mathfrak{p}$ after localizing, breaking the primary condition. The most common error in the converse is to choose the $a_i$ outside the lower primes but forget the constraint that they must stay inside $\mathfrak{a}$, which requires checking $\mathfrak{a} \not\subseteq$ each lower prime — true precisely because $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} = r$.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** Localize at the minimal prime $\mathfrak{p}$. Minimality makes $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ primary to the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, generated by $r$ elements. The dimension theorem gives $\dim A_{\mathfrak{p}} = \delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}}) \leq r$, and $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = \dim A_{\mathfrak{p}}$.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Reduce to a local ring.** Replace $A$ by $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, $\mathfrak{a}$ by $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$; note $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = \dim A_{\mathfrak{p}}$.
   - *Hint:* Primes below $\mathfrak{p}$ = primes of $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ (prime correspondence for localizations).
   - *Why needed:* It is what makes the dimension theorem applicable — that theorem is local.

2. **Show $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is primary to the maximal ideal.** Prove $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}} = \mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$.
   - *Hint:* Minimality of $\mathfrak{p}$ over $\mathfrak{a}$ means $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is the only prime containing $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, and the radical is the intersection of such primes.
   - *Why needed:* "Primary to the maximal ideal, with $r$ generators" is exactly the input to $\dim = \delta$.

3. **Apply the dimension theorem.** Conclude $\dim A_{\mathfrak{p}} = \delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}}) \leq r$.
   - *Hint:* $\delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}})$ is the min generators of *any* primary ideal; we exhibited one with $r$ generators.
   - *Why needed:* It is the inequality, hence $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} \leq r$.

4. **(Converse.)** Given $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} = r$, build $a_1, \dots, a_r \in \mathfrak{a}$ with $\operatorname{ht}(a_1, \dots, a_i) = i$.
   - *Hint:* Prime avoidance inside $\mathfrak{a}$, dodging the finitely many height-$(i-1)$ minimal primes of $(a_1, \dots, a_{i-1})$; possible because $\mathfrak{a}$ avoids each.
   - *Why needed:* Shows the bound is sharp.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: A minimal prime over $\mathfrak{a}$ localizes to a primary ideal
> **Statement:** If $\mathfrak{p}$ is a minimal prime of an ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ in a Noetherian ring $A$, then $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$-primary; i.e. $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}} = \mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, the maximal ideal of $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$.
>
> **Hint:** The radical of an ideal is the intersection of the primes above it; the only prime of $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ above $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, by minimality.
>
> **Why needed:** It converts the global hypothesis "minimal prime" into the local hypothesis "primary to the maximal ideal", the input the dimension theorem requires.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > The primes of $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ correspond bijectively to primes $\mathfrak{n} \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$ of $A$, via $\mathfrak{n} \mapsto \mathfrak{n} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$. A prime $\mathfrak{n} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ contains $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ iff $\mathfrak{n} \supseteq \mathfrak{a}$ (contract: $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq (\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}})^c \subseteq (\mathfrak{n} A_{\mathfrak{p}})^c = \mathfrak{n}$). So such $\mathfrak{n}$ satisfies $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{n} \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$; by minimality of $\mathfrak{p}$ over $\mathfrak{a}$, the only possibility is $\mathfrak{n} = \mathfrak{p}$. Hence $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is the unique prime of $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ containing $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$. Since $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}}$ equals the intersection of all primes containing $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, we get $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}} = \mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$. Thus $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is primary to the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The height bound from $\dim = \delta$
> **Statement:** $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = \dim A_{\mathfrak{p}} = \delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}}) \leq r$.
>
> **Hint:** $\delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}})$ is the minimal number of generators of any ideal primary to the maximal ideal; $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is one such, with $r$ generators.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the theorem itself, given Lemma 1.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > By definition $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p}$ is the length of the longest chain of primes below $\mathfrak{p}$, which equals $\dim A_{\mathfrak{p}}$. By the [[Thm - The Dimension Theorem for Noetherian Local Rings|dimension theorem]] applied to the Noetherian local ring $(A_{\mathfrak{p}}, \mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}})$, $\dim A_{\mathfrak{p}} = \delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}})$, the least number of generators of an ideal primary to $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$. By Lemma 1, $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is such an ideal, generated by the $r$ elements $\tfrac{x_1}{1}, \dots, \tfrac{x_r}{1}$. Hence $\delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}}) \leq r$, so $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} \leq r$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The converse — building a height-$r$ ideal inside $\mathfrak{a}$
> **Statement:** If $\mathfrak{a}$ is a proper ideal of a Noetherian ring with $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} = r$, there exist $a_1, \dots, a_r \in \mathfrak{a}$ with $\operatorname{ht}(a_1, \dots, a_i) = i$ for $0 \leq i \leq r$.
>
> **Hint:** Inductively choose $a_i \in \mathfrak{a}$ outside the finitely many height-$(i-1)$ minimal primes of $(a_1, \dots, a_{i-1})$; $\mathfrak{a}$ avoids each such prime because $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} = r > i - 1$.
>
> **Why needed:** It shows the bound $\operatorname{ht} \leq$ (generators) is achieved, completing the characterization of height.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Build $a_1, \dots, a_r$ with $\operatorname{ht}(a_1, \dots, a_i) \geq i$ (the reverse inequality $\leq i$ is automatic by the theorem). Base: $\operatorname{ht}(0) = 0$. Suppose $(a_1, \dots, a_{i-1})$ is built with $\operatorname{ht} = i - 1$, where $i - 1 < r$. Let $\mathfrak{p}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{p}_t$ be the (finitely many, as $A$ is Noetherian) minimal primes of $(a_1, \dots, a_{i-1})$ of height exactly $i - 1$. Since $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} = r > i - 1 = \operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p}_j$, we cannot have $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{p}_j$ (else $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a} \leq i - 1$). So $\mathfrak{a} \not\subseteq \mathfrak{p}_j$ for each $j$, and by **prime avoidance** $\mathfrak{a} \not\subseteq \bigcup_j \mathfrak{p}_j$. Pick $a_i \in \mathfrak{a} \setminus \bigcup_j \mathfrak{p}_j$. Any prime $\mathfrak{p} \supseteq (a_1, \dots, a_i)$ contains $(a_1, \dots, a_{i-1})$ and is not among the $\mathfrak{p}_j$ (it contains $a_i$), so $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} \geq i$; thus $\operatorname{ht}(a_1, \dots, a_i) \geq i$. By the theorem it is exactly $i$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> **Step 0 — reduce to the local ring.** Let $\mathfrak{p}$ be a minimal prime of $\mathfrak{a} = (x_1, \dots, x_r)$ in the Noetherian ring $A$. Localize at $\mathfrak{p}$: the primes of $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ are exactly the primes $\mathfrak{n} \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$ of $A$, so $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = \dim A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, and $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is Noetherian local with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$.
>
> **Step 1 — the localized ideal is primary to the maximal ideal.** By **Lemma 1**, since $\mathfrak{p}$ is minimal over $\mathfrak{a}$, the ideal $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ has radical $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$, i.e. it is primary to the maximal ideal of $A_{\mathfrak{p}}$. It is generated by the $r$ elements $\tfrac{x_1}{1}, \dots, \tfrac{x_r}{1}$.
>
> **Step 2 — apply the dimension theorem.** By **Lemma 2**, the [[Thm - The Dimension Theorem for Noetherian Local Rings|dimension theorem]] gives $\dim A_{\mathfrak{p}} = \delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}})$, the minimal number of generators of an ideal primary to $\mathfrak{p} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$. Since $\mathfrak{a} A_{\mathfrak{p}}$ is such an ideal with $r$ generators, $\delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}}) \leq r$. Therefore
> $$\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = \dim A_{\mathfrak{p}} = \delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}}) \leq r.$$
>
> **Principal-ideal corollary.** Take $r = 1$, $\mathfrak{a} = (x)$: every minimal prime of $(x)$ has height $\leq 1$. If $x$ is a non-zero-divisor and a non-unit, then $(x)$ is proper (non-unit) and no minimal prime of $A$ contains $x$ (a minimal prime consists of zero-divisors, so cannot contain the non-zero-divisor $x$); hence any minimal prime $\mathfrak{p}$ of $(x)$ strictly contains a minimal prime of $A$, giving $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} \geq 1$, so $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = 1$ exactly.
>
> **Converse.** This is **Lemma 3**. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Codimension of complete intersections (algebraic geometry).** A variety $X = V(f_1, \dots, f_r) \subseteq \mathbb{A}^n$ is a *complete intersection* when every component has codimension exactly $r$ — the maximum allowed by Krull. The theorem guarantees codimension $\leq r$ always, and equality is the special, well-behaved case. The application is non-obvious because the theorem gives only the inequality; recognizing when equality holds (e.g. for a regular sequence) is the gateway to the theory of complete intersections and their Koszul resolutions.

**Prime divisors in factorial rings (number theory).** In a Noetherian UFD, a height-one prime is principal, generated by an irreducible element — the algebraic content of "codimension-one cycles are divisors of functions". The proof runs: a height-one prime $\mathfrak{p}$ contains an irreducible $p$ (factor any nonzero element), and $(p) \subseteq \mathfrak{p}$ with $(p)$ prime of height $1$ (principal-ideal theorem) forces $(p) = \mathfrak{p}$. The application is non-obvious because it links unique factorization of *elements* to the structure of height-one *primes*, the seed of divisor theory in Dedekind domains.

**The expected dimension of intersections (intersection theory).** When two subvarieties $Y, Z \subseteq X$ of an $n$-dimensional smooth variety meet, the expected dimension of $Y \cap Z$ is $\dim Y + \dim Z - n$, and Krull's theorem (applied locally, cutting $Z$ by its $\operatorname{codim} Z$ equations) guarantees every component of $Y \cap Z$ has *at least* this dimension. The application is non-obvious because it converts a global intersection-dimension estimate into iterated single-equation height bounds, the foundation of the moving lemma and the intersection product.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - The Dimension Theorem for Noetherian Local Rings|The dimension theorem]]** — the source of all the content. Krull's height theorem is precisely the inequality $\dim A_{\mathfrak{p}} = \delta(A_{\mathfrak{p}}) \leq (\text{generators})$, localized at a minimal prime. Everything hard — the Hilbert-function growth argument, Artin–Rees — is in the dimension theorem; Krull's theorem is its most-used corollary.

- **[[Def - System of Parameters|System of parameters]]** — the converse direction. The construction of $a_1, \dots, a_r \in \mathfrak{a}$ with $\operatorname{ht}(a_1, \dots, a_i) = i$ is exactly the construction of a partial system of parameters; for $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{m}$ in a local ring it produces a full system of parameters, the witness that $\dim = \delta$.

- **[[Thm - Dimension of a Polynomial Ring|Dimension of a polynomial ring]]** — a key application. To prove $\dim A[T] = 1 + \dim A$ for Noetherian $A$, one uses Krull's height theorem (and its converse) to control how chains of primes in $A[T]$ contract to $A$. The height bound is what prevents the dimension from jumping by more than one when adjoining a variable.

- **[[Def - Krull Dimension and Height|Height as codimension]]** — the geometric reading. For a finitely generated domain over a field, $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = \dim A - \dim A/\mathfrak{p}$, so Krull's theorem says $r$ equations cut codimension at most $r$, i.e. dimension at least $\dim A - r$. This is the algebraic form of the linear-algebra fact that $r$ linear conditions cut dimension by at most $r$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Hypersurfaces, divisors, and the Picard group *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The principal-ideal theorem is the statement that **a hypersurface has pure codimension one**: $V(f) \subseteq X$ on an irreducible variety has every component of codimension exactly one. This makes the height-one primes the **prime divisors**, and the free abelian group they generate the group of **Weil divisors**; principal divisors $\operatorname{div}(f)$ are the images of rational functions, and the quotient is the **divisor class group**, which for a smooth variety is the **Picard group** of line bundles. Krull's theorem is thus the foundation of the entire theory of divisors and line bundles — it is what guarantees "codimension one" is the right notion for $\operatorname{div}(f)$.

> [!tip] Complete intersections and the unmixedness theorem *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> When an $r$-generated ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ achieves the maximal height $r$ on *every* associated prime — not just minimal ones — it is a **complete intersection**, and Macaulay's **unmixedness theorem** says that in a Cohen–Macaulay ring this is automatic: an ideal generated by $r = \operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{a}$ elements has *no embedded primes*, all associated primes of height exactly $r$. Krull's theorem gives the height bound that makes "the generators are a regular sequence" detectable, and the converse provides the system of parameters whose regularity defines the Cohen–Macaulay condition.

> [!tip] The codimension-dimension formula and catenary rings *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Krull's theorem provides the inequality half of the **dimension formula** $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} + \dim A/\mathfrak{p} = \dim A$, valid for finitely generated domains over a field (see [[Ex - Height plus dimension of the quotient equals dimension]]). The full equality requires the ring to be **catenary** — all maximal chains between two primes have equal length — which holds for finitely generated algebras over a field but *fails* in pathological Noetherian rings. The theorem is the codimension-controlling half that, combined with Noether normalization, yields the equality and hence well-defined codimension throughout algebraic geometry.
