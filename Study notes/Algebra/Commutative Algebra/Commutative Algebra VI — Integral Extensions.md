# Notation Registry

**Standing convention.** Throughout these commutative algebra notes, *ring* means a commutative ring with a multiplicative identity $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$. The single new relation of this chapter is **integrality**: an element $x$ of a ring $A$ is *integral over a subring $R$* if it satisfies a **monic** polynomial equation with coefficients in $R$. Replacing "polynomial" by "monic polynomial" — a one-word change from the notion of *algebraic* — is the entire subject, and it has a sharp geometric meaning, stated again in Motivation: **an integral extension of rings is a finite map of spaces, and the integral closure of a domain is its normalization.** When $R \subseteq A$ are rings we freely regard $A$ as an $R$-algebra and as an $R$-module; when we say "$A$ is *finite* over $R$" we always mean finite as a *module* (finitely generated as an $R$-module), never merely finitely generated as an algebra — the difference between the two is the crux of the chapter.

- $R, A, B, C$ — commutative rings with $1$; usually $R \subseteq A$ or $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$
- $R[x]$ — the **$R$-subalgebra generated by $x$**: the smallest subring of $A$ containing $R$ and $x$, equal to $\{f(x) : f \in R[T]\}$
- $R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ — the $R$-subalgebra generated by $x_1, \dots, x_n$
- $x$ **integral over $R$** ($R$-integral) — there is a monic $f \in R[T]$ with $f(x) = 0$; the equation $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n = 0$ is an **equation of integral dependence**
- $x$ **algebraic over $R$** ($R$-algebraic) — there is a *non-zero* (not necessarily monic) $f \in R[T]$ with $f(x) = 0$
- $\overline{A}$ or $\overline{A}^{\,B}$ — the **integral closure of $A$ in $B$**: the set of $b \in B$ integral over $A$; for a domain, $\overline{A}$ alone means the integral closure in $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$
- "$A$ is **integrally closed in $B$**" — $\overline{A}^{\,B} = A$; "$A$ is **integrally closed**" (or **normal**, for a domain) — $A$ is integrally closed in $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$
- "$A$ is **integral over $R$**" — every element of $A$ is $R$-integral; "$A$ is **finite over $R$**" — $A$ is a finitely generated $R$-module
- "$A$ is a **finite-type** $R$-algebra" / "finitely generated as an $R$-algebra" — $A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ for finitely many $x_i$
- $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$ — the field of fractions of a domain $A$; $A^\times$ — the group of units of $A$
- $M$ a **faithful** $R[x]$-module — for every $0 \neq p \in R[x]$ there is $m \in M$ with $pm \neq 0$ (equivalently, the structure map $R[x] \to \operatorname{End} M$ is injective)
- $\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b}, \mathfrak{q}$ — ideals; $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}$ — the radical of $\mathfrak{a}$; $\mathfrak{a}^e = \mathfrak{a}\overline{A}$ — the extension of $\mathfrak{a}$ to $\overline{A}$
- $x$ **integral over an ideal $\mathfrak{a}$** ($\mathfrak{a}$-integral) — there is a monic $f = T^n + a_1 T^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n \in A[T]$ with *every* $a_i \in \mathfrak{a}$ and $f(x) = 0$
- $S^{-1}A$, $A_{\mathfrak p}$ — the localization of $A$ at a multiplicative set $S$, at a prime $\mathfrak p$ (from [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization]])

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **integrality is algebraicity with the leading coefficient forced to be $1$, and that one restriction turns a wild, infinite-dimensional extension into a tame, finite, geometrically proper map.** You have met the contrast already, perhaps without naming it. Every rational number $\tfrac ab$ is *algebraic* over $\mathbb{Z}$ — it satisfies $bT - a = 0$ — yet only the integers themselves are *integral* over $\mathbb{Z}$, because clearing the leading coefficient $b$ is exactly the move that integrality forbids. The polynomial $bT - a$ is algebraic but not monic; the monic equation $T - n = 0$ is the only kind $\tfrac ab$ can satisfy, and it needs $\tfrac ab = n \in \mathbb{Z}$. So already the simplest case carries the moral: **demanding a monic relation is demanding that denominators cannot appear.** This is why integrality is the correct notion of "algebraic integer", and why $\sqrt{2}$ (root of the monic $T^2 - 2$) is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ while $\tfrac12$ (root only of the non-monic $2T - 1$) is not.

Why does the leading coefficient matter so much? Because a monic equation $x^n = -(r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n)$ lets you *rewrite every high power of $x$ in terms of lower ones*. The powers $1, x, x^2, \dots$ do not run away to infinity; they collapse into the finite list $1, x, \dots, x^{n-1}$. This is the technical heart of everything: **$x$ is integral over $R$ if and only if $R[x]$ is a finite $R$-module**, generated by those first $n$ powers. The whole chapter is the systematic exploitation of this single equivalence — that an arithmetic condition (a monic equation) is the same as a finiteness condition (a finitely generated module), proved by the **determinant trick**, the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|Cayley–Hamilton theorem for modules]]. Finiteness is robust where the bare definition is fragile: sums and products of integral elements are integral not because you can guess their monic equations (you usually cannot) but because they live inside a finite module, and a finite module is closed under the ring operations.

Geometrically, this is one of the pillars of the whole subject, and it is worth installing the dictionary now. A ring map $R \to A$ is a map of spaces $\operatorname{Spec} A \to \operatorname{Spec} R$ the other way; and the central fact of this chapter is:

$$A \text{ integral over } R \;\longleftrightarrow\; \operatorname{Spec} A \to \operatorname{Spec} R \text{ is a } \textbf{finite map}: \text{ proper, with finite fibres, surjective when } R \subseteq A.$$

A **finite map** of varieties is one whose fibres are finite sets of points and which loses no points "to infinity" — the algebraic-geometry incarnation of a branched covering. The reason the fibres are finite is *exactly* that each coordinate satisfies a monic equation of some degree $n$, so over each point of the base there are at most $n$ preimages, the roots of that equation. The non-monic case is what allows points to escape: the inclusion $k[t] \hookrightarrow k[t, t^{-1}]$ inverts $t$, and geometrically it deletes the point $t = 0$ from the line — an *open immersion*, the very opposite of proper — and correspondingly $t^{-1}$ is algebraic but not integral over $k[t]$. So integrality is the algebra of maps that are *finite-to-one and proper*, and the failure of integrality is the algebra of maps that *open up* or *run off to infinity*.

The second geometric pillar is **normalization**. Take a curve with a singularity — the standard model is the cusp $y^2 = x^3$, whose coordinate ring is $k[t^2, t^3] \subseteq k[t]$ under $x \mapsto t^2$, $y \mapsto t^3$. The cusp's coordinate ring is *not integrally closed*: the element $t = y/x \in \operatorname{Frac}$ satisfies the monic $T^2 - x = 0$ (since $t^2 = x$) yet does not lie in $k[t^2, t^3]$. Passing to the **integral closure** $k[t]$ adds exactly the missing integral element $t$ and *resolves the singularity*: $\operatorname{Spec} k[t]$ is the smooth line, mapping onto the cusp by the finite map $t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$, an isomorphism away from the singular point where it unglues the cusp. This is the picture to carry through the chapter:

$$\text{integral closure of a coordinate ring} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \textbf{normalization} \text{ of a variety: the universal finite birational fix that makes it } \textbf{normal} \text{ (smooth in codimension one).}$$

The chapter is built in three movements. First, the *equivalence of integrality with module-finiteness* and its consequences: the integral elements form a subring (the **integral closure**), integrality is transitive, and finiteness is transitive. Second, the *good closure rings*: a **UFD is integrally closed** — so $\mathbb{Z}$, $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$, and every polynomial ring is normal — and integral closure commutes with localization, so being integrally closed is a **local property** (it can be checked one prime at a time). Third, the *field-theoretic payoff*: an integral extension of domains has $A$ a field iff $B$ is a field, so integral extensions preserve maximality of primes — the seed of the going-up theorem and of the Nullstellensatz. We close with a generalisation, *integrality over an ideal*, which packages the radical $\sqrt{\mathfrak a \overline A}$ into the same determinant-trick machine.

This chapter assumes fluency with [[Def - Ideal|ideals]], [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient rings]], [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime and maximal ideals]], [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domains]], [[Def - Field of Fractions|fields of fractions]], [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domains]] and [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domains]] from the [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings]] notes; with [[Def - Module|modules]], [[Def - Submodule|submodules]] and especially [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finite generation of modules]] from the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|Modules]] notes; with the [[Def - Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial|algebraic-integer]] story and the fact that [[Thm - Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers|a rational algebraic integer is an integer]] from [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8|Rings IV]]; and with [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|localization]] and the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]] from the previous chapters. The determinant trick is the one tool you cannot do without — if it is hazy, refresh it before reading the proofs.

# Concept Map

## §6.1 Integral Elements and the Integral Closure

- **[[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension]]**
	- An element $x$ of an $R$-algebra $A$ is **integral over $R$** if it satisfies a *monic* polynomial $f \in R[T]$, i.e. $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n = 0$ with $r_i \in R$; this is strictly stronger than being **algebraic** (any non-zero $f$), the gap being precisely the leading coefficient. The algebra $A$ is **integral over $R$** if every element is, and is then called an **integral extension**. The defining example: $\sqrt2$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ (monic $T^2 - 2$) but $\tfrac12$ is not (only the non-monic $2T - 1$), and over a *field* $k$ the two notions coincide because any non-zero leading coefficient is a unit and can be divided out.

- **[[Def - Finite and Finite-Type Algebra]]**
	- An $R$-algebra $A$ is **finite** if it is finitely generated as an $R$-*module* ($A = Rx_1 + \cdots + Rx_n$), and **finite-type** (finitely generated as an $R$-*algebra*) if $A = R[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ for finitely many $x_i$. Finite is strictly stronger: $k[T]$ is finite-type but not finite over $k$ (the powers $1, T, T^2, \dots$ are $k$-linearly independent). The whole chapter turns on the slogan **finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral**: a finite-type algebra is a finite module exactly when it is generated by integral elements.

- **[[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)]]**
	- For $x \in A \supseteq R$, the following are equivalent: (i) $x$ is integral over $R$; (ii) $R[x]$ is a finite $R$-module; (iii) $R[x]$ is contained in a subring that is a finite $R$-module; (iv) there is a faithful $R[x]$-module that is finite over $R$. The engine is the **[[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]]**: multiplication by $x$ on a faithful finite module satisfies its own characteristic polynomial, which is monic — manufacturing the integral equation from finiteness. The "true name" of integrality is therefore *finiteness of the generated subring*, and this is what makes the next three theorems possible.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Finite morphisms of affine schemes *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> An integral, finite-type ring map $R \to A$ is precisely a **finite morphism** $\operatorname{Spec} A \to \operatorname{Spec} R$ of affine schemes: a map that is affine and whose target is covered by opens over which $A$ is a finite free-ish module. The module-finite criterion is the algebraic content of "finite morphisms have finite fibres" — over a point $\mathfrak p$, the fibre is $\operatorname{Spec}(A \otimes_R \kappa(\mathfrak p))$, a finite-dimensional algebra over the residue field, hence finitely many points. Finite morphisms are the proper, quasi-finite maps; they are the building blocks of **branched coverings** and of Noether normalization.

- **[[Ex - The integral closure of Z in Q is Z]]** (⭐)
	- Prove $\mathbb{Z}$ is normal: a rational integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integer. Run the lowest-terms argument (write $x = a/b$ coprime, clear denominators to isolate $a^n$, track a prime $p \mid b$ to a contradiction $p \mid a$), or invoke "a UFD is integrally closed" in one line. The base case of normalization theory and the prototype for disproving integrality with a UFD.

- **[[Ex - Sums and products of algebraic integers are algebraic integers]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show the algebraic integers form a ring (Example Sheet 1 Q15(b)). The technique is the module-finite criterion: place $a, b$ in the single finite module $\mathbb{Z}[a, b]$, which integralises $a + b$ and $ab$ at once with no equation in hand. The constructive coda realises the combined monic polynomials as characteristic polynomials of the Kronecker sum $C_f \otimes I + I \otimes C_g$ and product $C_f \otimes C_g$ of companion matrices.

- **[[Ex - Z[sqrt 5] is not integrally closed]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Exhibit the missing algebraic integer $\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$ (integral via $T^2 - T - 1$, outside $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$ by its half-integer coordinate) to disprove normality, then compute $\overline{\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]} = \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2]$ by the sandwich, capping with a Euclidean-hence-UFD candidate. Shows non-normality is a "missing algebraic integer" and that $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$ is the wrong ring of integers when $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

- **[[Ex - The integral closure of k[t^2,t^3]]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute the normalization of the cuspidal cubic $y^2 = x^3$: $t = t^3/t^2$ is integral via $T^2 - t^2$ but missing from $k[t^2, t^3]$, and the closure is the smooth line $k[t]$ (sandwich capped by $k[t]$, a PID). Geometrically the finite birational map $t \mapsto (t^2, t^3)$ resolves the cusp singularity — the showcase of "non-normal $=$ singular, integral closure $=$ smooth model".

## §6.2 Normality, the Closure Subring, and Finiteness

- **[[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring]]**
	- The set $\overline{A}$ of elements of $B$ integral over $A$ is an **$A$-subalgebra** of $B$ — closed under addition and multiplication and containing $A$. The proof is pure module-finiteness: if $x, y$ are integral then $A[x, y]$ is a finite $A$-module, every element of it (in particular $x \pm y$ and $xy$) is integral by the criterion. This is the structural fact behind "sums and products of algebraic integers are algebraic integers", a statement one cannot prove by writing down monic equations directly. Moreover $\overline A$ is itself integrally closed in $B$ ($\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$): closing once already closes completely.

- **[[Def - Integral Closure and Normal Domain]]**
	- The **integral closure of $A$ in $B$** is the subring $\overline A = \{b \in B : b \text{ integral over } A\}$; $A$ is **integrally closed in $B$** if $\overline A = A$. For a domain, the **integral closure** is taken inside $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$, and $A$ is **integrally closed** or **normal** if it equals its own integral closure there. Normality is the algebraic shadow of *smoothness in codimension one*: a normal variety can only be singular in codimension $\geq 2$, so normal curves are smooth. The cusp ring $k[t^2, t^3]$ is *not* normal (its closure is $k[t]$); $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$ is not normal (its closure is $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2]$).

- **[[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness]]**
	- For a tower $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$: if $C$ is finite over $B$ and $B$ is finite over $A$, then $C$ is finite over $A$ (multiply the generating sets); and if $C$ is integral over $B$ and $B$ is integral over $A$, then $C$ is integral over $A$. The integrality half is reduced to the finiteness half: an integral equation for $c \in C$ has only finitely many coefficients $b_1, \dots, b_n \in B$, each integral over $A$, so $c$ is integral over the finite $A$-algebra $A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$, and finiteness transits. Transitivity is what makes integral closure *closed* and normalization *idempotent*.

- **[[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed]]**
	- Every [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] is integrally closed (normal). If $x = a/b \in \operatorname{Frac}(A)$ is in lowest terms (no prime divides both $a, b$) and integral, clearing denominators in $x^n + a_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n = 0$ gives $a^n = -b(\cdots)$, so any prime $p \mid b$ divides $a^n$, hence $a$ — contradicting lowest terms unless $b$ is a unit, i.e. $x \in A$. Consequence: $\mathbb{Z}$, every PID, and every polynomial ring $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ is normal — affine space and its coordinate rings are the basic normal objects. The converse fails: normal is much weaker than UFD.

- **Integral closure commutes with localization** *(bold plain text — packaged on the closure/normal-domain pages, no separate subpage)*
	- For a multiplicative set $S \subseteq A$, the integral closure of $S^{-1}A$ in $S^{-1}B$ equals $S^{-1}\overline A$: localizing and closing commute. Together with the [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local–global principle]] this gives that **being integrally closed is a local property** — a domain $A$ is normal iff $A_{\mathfrak p}$ is normal for every prime $\mathfrak p$, iff $A_{\mathfrak m}$ is normal for every maximal $\mathfrak m$ (Example Sheet 3 Q7). Normalization can thus be carried out one point at a time.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Normalization of a variety and resolution in codimension one *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The integral closure of the coordinate ring of an affine variety $X$ is the coordinate ring of its **normalization** $\tilde X \to X$ — the universal finite, birational map from a normal variety. For curves, normality equals smoothness, so normalization *resolves all singularities of a curve*: it separates the two branches of a node and unfolds the cusp $k[t^2,t^3] \subseteq k[t]$ into the smooth line. In higher dimension it only resolves codimension-one singularities (**Serre's criterion**: normal $=$ $R_1 + S_2$), leaving deeper singularities for the full resolution problem. That **a UFD is integrally closed** is why smooth varieties (whose local rings are regular, hence UFDs by Auslander–Buchsbaum) are automatically normal.

- **[[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)]]**
	- Let $A \subseteq B$ be an integral extension of domains. Then $A \cap B^\times = A^\times$ (an element of $A$ invertible in $B$ is already invertible in $A$), and consequently **$B$ is a field if and only if $A$ is a field**. Corollary: for a prime $\mathfrak q$ of $B$ lying over $\mathfrak p = \mathfrak q \cap A$, $\mathfrak q$ is maximal iff $\mathfrak p$ is maximal — passing the quotient extension $A/\mathfrak p \subseteq B/\mathfrak q$ (still integral) through the field criterion. This is the bridge to the [[Commutative Algebra VIII — Going Up and Going Down|Cohen–Seidenberg theorems]] and to Zariski's Lemma in the **Nullstellensatz**.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Going-up, lying-over, and the geometry of finite fibres *(from Commutative Algebra / Algebraic Geometry)*
> The field criterion is the technical seed of the **going-up theorem**: in an integral extension every prime of the base is hit ($\operatorname{Spec} B \to \operatorname{Spec} A$ is surjective), chains of primes lift, and incomparable primes stay incomparable in a single fibre. Geometrically these are the statements that a finite map is **surjective and closed with finite fibres**, and that **dimension is preserved** under finite maps — an integral extension does not change Krull dimension. The maximal-to-maximal corollary here is exactly "closed points map to closed points", developed fully in [[Commutative Algebra VIII — Going Up and Going Down]].

- **[[Ex - An integral domain integral over a field is a field]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove a domain integral over a field $k$ is a field, by inverting a minimal monic equation: the domain hypothesis forces a nonzero constant term, and the field hypothesis inverts it (Atiyah–Macdonald 5.7). The counterexample $k \times k$ shows the domain hypothesis is necessary. This inversion trick, fed through Noether normalization, is the engine of Zariski's lemma and the Nullstellensatz.

- **[[Ex - The finiteness lemma for A in B in C]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- The Artin–Tate lemma (Example Sheet 3 Q4): with $A$ Noetherian, $C$ finite-type over $A$, and $C$ finite over $B$, show $B$ is finite-type over $A$. The trick is to manufacture a Noetherian intermediate ring $A'$ from the finitely many structure constants, over which $C$ becomes module-finite; then $B$ is trapped as a submodule of a finite module over a Noetherian ring. All three hypotheses are load-bearing — subalgebras of finite-type algebras are not generally finite-type.

## §8 Integrality over an Ideal

- **Integrality over an ideal and the radical formula** *(bold plain text — covered on the closure/normal-domain pages as the §8 capstone, no separate subpage)*
	- An element $x \in B$ is **integral over an ideal $\mathfrak a \trianglelefteq A$** if it satisfies a monic equation $x^n + a_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n = 0$ with *all* coefficients $a_i \in \mathfrak a$. The theorem (Becker 8.2): the integral closure of $\mathfrak a$ in $B$ is exactly $\sqrt{\mathfrak a \overline A}$, the radical (in $\overline A$) of the extended ideal. So integrality over an ideal is integrality over $A$ (forcing $x \in \overline A$) *plus* a radical-membership condition $x \in \sqrt{\mathfrak a \overline A}$. Unlike $\overline A$, this set need not contain $1$ — it is an ideal of $\overline A$, not a subring. The same determinant-trick argument runs, now with $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$ feeding the Cayley–Hamilton coefficients into $\mathfrak a$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The integral closure of an ideal and Rees algebras *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> Integral dependence over an ideal $\mathfrak a$ is the foundation of the **integral closure $\overline{\mathfrak a}$ of an ideal**, a central object in the theory of **reductions**, multiplicity, and the **Rees algebra** $\bigoplus_n \mathfrak a^n t^n$. The radical formula $\sqrt{\mathfrak a \overline A}$ is the first instance of the general phenomenon that integral dependence over an ideal is detected by valuations and by the blow-up; it reappears in [[Commutative Algebra XI — Graded Rings and the Artin-Rees Lemma|graded-ring theory]] and in the analytic study of singularities.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring goals of this chapter fall into five families. The first is **proving (or disproving) that a specific element is integral** — that $\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$, that $t^{-1}$ is *not* integral over $k[t]$. The standard route is to *exhibit a monic equation* (to prove integrality) or to *invoke a normal ring* (to disprove it): if the ambient ring is a UFD/normal and the element lies in the fraction field but not the ring, it cannot be integral, by [[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed]]. The second is **computing an integral closure / a normalization** — finding $\overline{\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]} = \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2]$, or $\overline{k[t^2,t^3]} = k[t]$. One guesses the candidate closure $A'$, checks $A \subseteq A' \subseteq \operatorname{Frac}$ with $A'$ integral over $A$ and $A'$ already normal (often because $A'$ is a UFD), and concludes $A' = \overline A$. The third, the chapter's structural signature, is **transferring finiteness/integrality through a tower or operation** — that sums and products stay integral, that integrality is transitive, that a tower of finite extensions is finite; these all route through the **module-finite criterion** and the closure-is-a-subring theorem. The fourth is **deducing a field/maximality statement** — that a domain integral over a field is a field, that primes lying over maximals are maximal; the lever is the [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|domain criterion]]. The fifth is **establishing a normality/locality fact** — that a UFD is normal, that normality is local, that a prime is integrally closed; these combine [[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed]] with localization. These are the natural targets because the chapter is a *comparison* between a ring and a bigger ring it sits inside: every theorem measures how much of the larger ring is "controlled" — finitely, integrally, properly — by the smaller.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **An inclusion $R \subseteq A$ with a generating set** — the move is to ask whether the generators are integral, because finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral, so a finite-type algebra generated by integral elements is a finite module ([[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]]). **A monic equation is given, or a finite module is given** — these are interchangeable, and recognising which one a problem hands you tells you whether to run the determinant trick forwards (finite $\Rightarrow$ monic) or backwards (monic $\Rightarrow$ finite). **The ambient ring is a UFD, a PID, or a polynomial ring** — then it is normal, and the only integral elements of its fraction field are its own elements; this instantly settles "is $x$ integral?" questions and computes closures from above. **A tower $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$** — signalling transitivity: stack finiteness or integrality through the middle ring. **An integral extension of domains, plus a field somewhere** — switching on the domain criterion and the unit-comparison $A \cap B^\times = A^\times$. **A localization or a "check at each prime"** — because integral closure commutes with localization and normality is local. The discipline is to match source to target along the chapter's spine: a generating set routes through the module-finite criterion; a fraction-field element over a UFD routes through normality; a tower routes through transitivity; a field/maximality question routes through the domain criterion. The [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every integrality problem is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

1. **Turn a monic equation into a finite module, and back.** A monic relation $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n = 0$ says $x^n \in Rx^{n-1} + \cdots + R$, so $R[x] = R + Rx + \cdots + Rx^{n-1}$ is a *finite* $R$-module. Conversely, if $x$ stabilises a faithful finite module ($xM \subseteq M$), the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]] produces a monic equation for $x$. *Trigger:* you have integrality and want finiteness, or vice versa. *Pattern:* "monic of degree $n$ $\Leftrightarrow$ the first $n$ powers span".

2. **Prove closure under $+, \times$ by working inside $A[x, y]$.** To show $x \pm y$ or $xy$ is integral when $x, y$ are, do *not* hunt for their monic equations — show $A[x, y]$ is a finite $A$-module (because $A[x]$ is finite over $A$ and $A[x][y]$ is finite over $A[x]$, then transit), and invoke that every element of a finite faithful module is integral. *Trigger:* "is this combination of integral elements integral?" *Pattern:* "land everything in one finite module, then the criterion applies to all of it at once".

3. **Stack finiteness/integrality through a tower.** For $A \subseteq B \subseteq C$, finiteness multiplies (a generating set of $C/B$ times one of $B/A$ generates $C/A$) and integrality composes (via [[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness]]). *Trigger:* a chain of three rings, or "integral over something that is integral over the base". *Pattern:* "reduce $C/A$ to $C/B$ and $B/A$; collect the finitely many coefficients of an integral equation into a finite middle algebra $A' = A[b_1,\dots,b_n]$".

4. **Disprove integrality with a normal ring.** If the ambient ring $A$ is a UFD (or otherwise known normal) and $x \in \operatorname{Frac}(A) \setminus A$, then $x$ is *not* integral over $A$ — by [[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed]] there is nothing in the fraction field beyond $A$ to be integral. *Trigger:* "show this fraction is not an integer/not integral". *Pattern:* "the ring is a UFD, so its integral closure is itself; a genuine fraction cannot be integral".

5. **Compute a closure by the sandwich $A \subseteq A' \subseteq \operatorname{Frac}(A)$.** To find $\overline A$, propose $A'$, verify $A'$ is integral over $A$ (so $A' \subseteq \overline A$) and $A'$ is *already normal* (so $\overline A \subseteq A'$, since $\overline A$ is integral over $A$ hence over... — more precisely $\overline A \subseteq \overline{A'} = A'$). *Trigger:* "compute the integral closure / normalization". *Pattern:* "guess the closure, show it integral and normal; normality of the candidate caps the answer".

6. **Pass an integral extension through a quotient or a localization.** If $A \subseteq B$ is integral, so is $A/(\mathfrak q \cap A) \hookrightarrow B/\mathfrak q$ for any ideal $\mathfrak q \trianglelefteq B$, and so is $S^{-1}A \subseteq S^{-1}B$ for any multiplicative $S \subseteq A$; reduce modulo coefficients or apply $S^{-1}$ termwise to a monic equation. *Trigger:* you need integrality of a quotient or localized extension. *Pattern:* "reduce the monic equation mod $\mathfrak q$" or "divide the monic equation by $s^n$".

7. **Read maximality/field-ness off the domain criterion.** For an integral extension of domains, $B$ is a field iff $A$ is; for a prime $\mathfrak q$ over $\mathfrak p$, $\mathfrak q$ maximal iff $\mathfrak p$ maximal. *Trigger:* "show this ring is a field" or "this prime is maximal" in an integral extension. *Pattern:* "pass to $A/\mathfrak p \subseteq B/\mathfrak q$, still integral and still domains; apply the field criterion".

8. **Compare units via the integral equation.** If $a \in A$ has an inverse $b \in B$, multiply $b$'s integral equation $b^n + a_1 b^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n = 0$ by $a^{n-1}$ to get $b = -(a_1 + a_2 a + \cdots + a_n a^{n-1}) \in A$. *Trigger:* you need "invertible upstairs $\Rightarrow$ invertible downstairs". *Pattern:* "multiply the integral relation for the inverse by a high power of the element to collapse all the fractions".

9. **Localize integral closure / normality questions.** Because $\overline{S^{-1}A} = S^{-1}\overline A$ and normality is local, reduce "is $A$ normal?" to "is each $A_{\mathfrak m}$ normal?". *Trigger:* a normality question over a complicated ring, or "is integral closure preserved under localization". *Pattern:* "it suffices to check after localizing at each maximal ideal".

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Confusing "integral" with "algebraic"
> Because over a field the two coincide, it is tempting to treat them as the same in general. They are not, and the gap is the *leading coefficient*. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the element $\tfrac12$ is algebraic (root of $2T - 1$) but **not** integral: its only polynomial relations have a leading coefficient divisible by $2$, never $1$. Concretely, the set of algebraic elements of $\mathbb{Q}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ is *all* of $\mathbb{Q}$, while the set of integral elements is just $\mathbb{Z}$. The operation "drop the leading coefficient by dividing through" — legal for algebraicity over a field — is exactly what integrality forbids, because over a general ring the leading coefficient need not be a unit. The two notions agree precisely when $R$ is a field (every non-zero scalar is a unit), which is the only case where dividing out is allowed.

> [!warning] 2. Trying to add monic equations to prove sums are integral
> Given monic equations for $x$ and $y$, it is natural to want to *combine* them directly into a monic equation for $x + y$. There is no such formula in general — the degree of the relation for $x + y$ can be as large as the product of the two degrees, and writing it out means computing a resultant or a characteristic polynomial of a tensor construction (this is what Example Sheet 1 Q15(b)'s algorithm does, via $A \otimes I + I \otimes B$). The correct move is structural: $x, y$ both live in the finite $A$-module $A[x, y]$, and *every* element of a finite faithful module is integral by the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|criterion]] — so $x + y, xy$ are integral with no equation in hand. The repair: stop trying to produce the equation and instead produce the *module*; the equation then exists by Cayley–Hamilton even though you never wrote it.

> [!warning] 3. Believing finite-type implies finite
> "Finitely generated as an algebra" and "finitely generated as a module" sound interchangeable, and the words tempt you to conflate them. They differ by exactly *integrality*. The polynomial ring $k[T]$ is finite-*type* over $k$ (one algebra generator, $T$) but is **not** a finite $k$-module — the powers $1, T, T^2, \dots$ are linearly independent over $k$, so no finite set spans. The element $T$ is not integral over $k$ (it satisfies no monic — indeed no non-zero — polynomial over $k$ of bounded degree forcing a relation). The operation becomes legal exactly when *every algebra generator is integral*: a finite-type algebra is a finite module **iff** it is generated by integral elements ([[Def - Finite and Finite-Type Algebra|finite = finite-type + integral]]). Geometrically, finite-type is "a variety", finite is "a finite branched cover" — vastly different.

> [!warning] 4. Inverting an element and expecting integrality to survive
> Localization feels harmless, but inverting an element can *destroy* integrality and change the geometry from proper to open. The inclusion $k[t] \hookrightarrow k[t, t^{-1}]$ is integral nowhere: $t^{-1}$ satisfies $t \cdot T - 1 = 0$, algebraic but *not monic over $k[t]$*, and indeed $k[t, t^{-1}]$ is not a finite $k[t]$-module (the negative powers $t^{-1}, t^{-2}, \dots$ are independent). Geometrically, inverting $t$ deletes the origin from the affine line — an open immersion, the opposite of a finite proper map, and points escape "to infinity" in the fibre over $t = 0$. The lesson: integral extensions are about *adjoining roots of monic equations*, never about *inverting*; the two operations pull in opposite geometric directions (proper-finite vs. open).

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any integrality problem is to identify which of the chapter's three relations is being asked about — *is an element integral?*, *is an extension finite?*, *is a ring normal?* — because each has a characteristic route, and the routes are tied together by the one equivalence "integral $=$ module-finite".

If the problem **asks whether a specific element is integral**, you have a clean dichotomy. To prove integrality, either *exhibit a monic equation* by direct computation (for $\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2$, square it: $\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$, so $T^2 - T - 1$ works), or, when the element is built from known integral elements, *place it inside a finite module* and invoke the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|module-finite criterion]] — this is how you handle sums and products without guessing equations. To *disprove* integrality, reach for normality: if the ambient ring is a UFD or known normal and your element is a genuine fraction not in the ring, [[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed]] kills it instantly — there is nothing in $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$ beyond $A$ to be integral. The recurring trap is to attempt a direct monic equation when none exists in low degree; recognising that the element is a fraction over a UFD short-circuits the whole search.

If the problem **asks whether an extension is finite, or asks you to compute a closure**, the controlling identity is **finite $=$ finite-type $+$ integral**. To show $A = R[x_1, \dots, x_m]$ is a finite $R$-module, check each generator $x_i$ is integral; then the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|criterion]] plus [[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness|transitivity]] of finiteness assembles a finite generating set (the bounded monomials $x_1^{e_1} \cdots x_m^{e_m}$ with each $e_i < \deg$). To compute the integral closure $\overline A$, run the *sandwich*: guess a candidate $A'$ with $A \subseteq A' \subseteq \operatorname{Frac}(A)$, prove $A'$ is integral over $A$ (so it sits inside $\overline A$), and prove $A'$ is *already normal* (so it caps $\overline A$ from above, since $\overline A$ is integral over $A$ hence contained in any normal ring between $A$ and $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$). For $k[t^2, t^3]$ the candidate is $k[t]$ — integral over $k[t^2, t^3]$ because $t^2 = t \cdot t$ and $t$ satisfies $T^2 - t^2$, and normal because it is a PID. The recognition step is *guessing the closure*; normality of the guess does the verification.

If the problem **asks about fields, units, or maximal ideals in an integral extension**, you are using the [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|domain criterion]], and the strategy is to *pass to quotients and apply the field equivalence*. For "this prime $\mathfrak q$ over $\mathfrak p$ is maximal iff $\mathfrak p$ is", quotient by $\mathfrak q$: the extension $A/\mathfrak p \hookrightarrow B/\mathfrak q$ is still integral and both are domains, so by the criterion one is a field iff the other is — and "field" means "the prime is maximal". The unit-comparison $A \cap B^\times = A^\times$ is the lemma underneath, proved by multiplying the inverse's integral equation by a high power of the element. The decisive move is recognising that a question about maximality *downstairs* is a question about field-ness of a *quotient*, which the criterion handles upstairs and downstairs simultaneously.

A fourth, more computational, family **concerns normality and locality**. To prove a ring is normal, the cleanest route is to exhibit it as a UFD; failing that, check normality *locally*, prime by prime, using that integral closure commutes with localization (so being integrally closed is a [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local property]]). Example Sheet 3 Q7 — "$A$ normal iff every $A_{\mathfrak p}$ normal iff every $A_{\mathfrak m}$ normal" — is the prototype, and it lets a global normality question be reduced to local rings where the structure is simpler.

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "how much of the bigger ring does a monic equation reach?"** Proving an element integral asks whether the monic reach extends to it; computing a closure asks for the full monic reach of $A$ inside $\operatorname{Frac}(A)$; finiteness asks whether the reach is *uniformly bounded* (a single finite module); the field criterion asks what the monic reach does to invertibility. Decide whether you are *establishing* reach (exhibit a monic equation or a finite module), *bounding* it from above (invoke normality of a UFD), or *transferring* it (transitivity, quotients, localization), and the right tool is determined.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|The module-finite criterion]]**: $x$ integral over $R$ $\iff$ $R[x]$ is a finite $R$-module $\iff$ $x$ lies in some finite faithful $R[x]$-module. This is the most reused fact in the chapter because it converts an *arithmetic* condition (a monic equation, hard to produce) into a *finiteness* condition (a finitely generated module, easy to manipulate), and finiteness is preserved by sums, products, towers, quotients and localization. Its standard uses are proving the integral closure is a subring (everything lands in one finite module), proving transitivity, and disproving integrality (an infinite module means no monic equation). Reach for it whenever "integral" needs to be *used* rather than merely *checked* — the determinant trick is doing the work underneath.

- **[[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|Integral closure is a subring, and it is idempotent]]**: $\overline A$ is an $A$-subalgebra of $B$, and $\overline{\overline A} = \overline A$. Its reusability is that it lets you treat "integral over $A$" as a *closure operation* with all the expected algebra: integral elements form a ring you can compute in, and closing once closes forever. This is the technical backbone of "sums and products of algebraic integers are algebraic integers" and of the well-definedness of normalization. Whenever several integral elements appear and you want to combine them, this guarantees the result stays integral with no further checking.

- **[[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed|A UFD is integrally closed]]**: every unique factorization domain is normal. This is the geometric workhorse from above: it instantly certifies $\mathbb{Z}$, every PID, and every polynomial ring $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ as normal, which both *bounds* integral closures (nothing in $\operatorname{Frac}$ beyond the ring is integral) and *disproves* integrality of fractions in one line. Use it for every "is $x$ an integer / integral?" question where the base is a UFD, and as the reason smooth varieties are normal (regular local rings are UFDs).

- **[[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|The domain/field criterion]]**: for integral $A \subseteq B$ of domains, $B$ field $\iff$ $A$ field, and $\mathfrak q$ over $\mathfrak p$ is maximal $\iff$ $\mathfrak p$ is. Its reusability is as the bridge from integrality to the *prime-ideal* theory: it is the lemma behind lying-over, going-up, and the maximality transfer that powers the **Nullstellensatz** (Zariski's lemma) and dimension theory. The trigger is any field-ness or maximality question inside an integral extension; the reaction is "pass to the quotient domains and use the field equivalence".

- **[[Thm - Transitivity of Integrality and Finiteness|Transitivity of finiteness and integrality]]**: finite-over-finite is finite, integral-over-integral is integral. This is reused as a *composition law*: it lets you build complicated finite/integral extensions out of simple ones and decompose them back, and it is the reason integral closure is idempotent and normalization well-defined. The standard pattern is to insert a finitely generated middle ring $A[b_1, \dots, b_n]$ collecting the coefficients of an integral equation, reducing an integrality-over-$A$ question to finiteness-over-$A$.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — an integral extension is a finite map, and the integral closure is the normalization.** To a finitely generated $k$-algebra $A$ one attaches the affine variety $X = \operatorname{Spec} A$, and a ring map $R \to A$ becomes a map $X \to \operatorname{Spec} R$ of varieties the other way. The chapter's central theorem — integral $+$ finite-type $=$ finite module — says this map is a **finite morphism**: its fibres are finite (over each point, the coordinates satisfy monic equations of bounded degree, so there are boundedly many preimages), it is proper (no points escape to infinity, because a monic equation cannot have a "root at infinity" the way a non-monic one can), and it is surjective when $R \subseteq A$. The contrast is exact: inverting an element, $k[t] \hookrightarrow k[t, t^{-1}]$, is an *open immersion* that deletes a point — algebraic but not integral. And the integral closure of a coordinate ring is the coordinate ring of the **normalization** $\tilde X \to X$, the universal finite birational map from a normal variety; for the cusp $k[t^2, t^3] \subseteq k[t]$ this map unfolds the singularity, replacing the cusped curve by the smooth line and the missing element $t = y/x$ by an honest function. So this chapter is the algebraic theory of finite maps and singularity-resolution-in-codimension-one all at once.

2. **Number theory — the ring of integers of a number field is an integral closure, and normality is what makes ideal factorization work.** For a number field $K$ (a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$), the **ring of integers** $\mathcal{O}_K$ is by definition the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$ — the algebraic integers of $K$. That this is a *ring* is precisely [[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|"sums and products of algebraic integers are algebraic integers"]], a fact one cannot see by manipulating minimal polynomials but which falls out of module-finiteness. The chapter's running examples are the first cases: $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathbb{Z}$, $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt5)} = \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2]$ (so the naive guess $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$ is *not* the full ring of integers — it is not normal). The deep payoff downstream is that $\mathcal{O}_K$, being a normal Noetherian domain of dimension one, is a **Dedekind domain**, where every ideal factors uniquely into primes even when elements do not — the normality established here is exactly the hypothesis $(R_1 + S_2)$ that rescues unique factorization at the level of ideals, developed in [[Commutative Algebra XIII — Dedekind Domains and DVRs]].

3. **The determinant trick — Cayley–Hamilton for modules is the single mechanism behind the whole chapter.** The [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]] says: if $M$ is a finite $R$-module and $\phi : M \to M$ is $R$-linear with $\phi(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$ for an ideal $\mathfrak a$, then $\phi$ satisfies a monic polynomial $\phi^n + a_1 \phi^{n-1} + \cdots + a_n = 0$ with $a_i \in \mathfrak a$, obtained by expanding $\det(T \cdot \mathrm{id} - \phi)$ against the adjugate. Take $\phi$ to be "multiply by $x$" and $\mathfrak a = R$: the conclusion is a monic equation for $x$, which is *the* integrality criterion ([[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)]]). Take $\phi$ to be "multiply by $x$" and $\mathfrak a$ a genuine ideal with $xM \subseteq \mathfrak a M$: the conclusion is a monic equation with coefficients in $\mathfrak a$, which is *integrality over the ideal $\mathfrak a$* (§8, the radical formula $\sqrt{\mathfrak a \overline A}$). One linear-algebra lemma — a finite-dimensional operator satisfies its characteristic polynomial, upgraded from vector spaces to finitely generated modules — generates both the basic theory and its ideal-theoretic refinement. This same trick is the engine of [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama's lemma]], so the two chapters share a heart.

4. **Field theory and Galois theory — integral closure is the "integers" inside an algebraic extension, and the field criterion is the algebraic-extension analogue.** Over a field $k$, integral and algebraic coincide, so the integral closure of $k$ in an extension $L$ is the set of all $L$-elements algebraic over $k$ — the *algebraic closure of $k$ inside $L$*. The [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|domain criterion]] specialises to the familiar field-theoretic fact that a domain algebraic over a field is itself a field (so $k[\alpha] = k(\alpha)$ for $\alpha$ algebraic — adjoining an algebraic element already gives a field, no need to invert). Pushed one rung further, the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in a Galois extension carries an action of the Galois group permuting the primes over a given rational prime — the link between this chapter and the **decomposition/inertia** theory of [[Commutative Algebra VIII — Going Up and Going Down|going-up]] and algebraic number theory. The conceptual transfer is that "integral" is to "algebraic" as "$\mathbb{Z}$" is to "$\mathbb{Q}$": the integral closure picks out the subring playing the role of the integers in any algebraic extension.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: integrality is "algebraicity that respects the integers", and a monic equation is a finiteness machine.** Every result in this chapter is one consequence of a single upgrade — replacing "satisfies a polynomial" by "satisfies a *monic* polynomial". The reason this tiny change is so powerful is that a monic equation $x^n = -(r_1 x^{n-1} + \cdots + r_n)$ is *self-truncating*: it expresses the highest power as a combination of lower ones, so it folds the infinite list of powers $1, x, x^2, \dots$ down into a finite spanning set. A non-monic equation $r_0 x^n = -(\cdots)$ cannot do this unless $r_0$ is invertible — you would have to divide by $r_0$, leaving the ring. So **monic $=$ finitely generated $=$ no escape to infinity**, and the three faces of the chapter (arithmetic: monic equation; module-theoretic: finite module; geometric: proper finite map) are the same fact seen from three sides. Carry the slogan that **integrality is the algebra of "denominators are not allowed", and finiteness is what enforces it**: every time the theory works, it is because a monic equation has trapped the powers of an element in a finite-dimensional cage, and every time it fails ($\tfrac12$, $t^{-1}$), it is because an inversion has let them out.

**The true name of integrality is finiteness, not the monic equation.** The official definition — "there is a monic $f$ with $f(x) = 0$" — is almost never how you *use* integrality, because you rarely have the equation. The operational characterisation, the [[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|true name]], is: **$x$ is integral over $R$ exactly when $R[x]$ (equivalently, some faithful finite module containing $x$) is a finite $R$-module.** This is what you reach for in every proof. To show $x + y$ is integral, you do not produce its monic equation — you note $x, y \in A[x, y]$, a finite module, and finiteness does the rest. To show $t^{-1}$ is *not* integral over $k[t]$, you do not check all monic equations — you observe $k[t, t^{-1}]$ is not a finite $k[t]$-module. The monic equation is the *definition*; module-finiteness is the *content*, and the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]] is the bridge that makes them the same. Knowing which one a problem wants — produce an equation (rare) versus produce a module (almost always) — is the single most useful navigational fact in the chapter.

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "is this integral?" splits on whether the base is normal.** When asked whether $x \in \operatorname{Frac}(A)$ is integral over $A$, the first question to fire is *"is $A$ a UFD or otherwise normal?"* If yes, the answer is immediate: $x$ is integral iff $x \in A$, because [[Thm - A UFD is Integrally Closed|a UFD is integrally closed]] — so a genuine fraction is never integral and an element of $A$ always is, no computation required. This single reflex disposes of the entire family "show $\tfrac ab$ is/ is not an algebraic integer over $\mathbb{Z}$", "show $t^{-1}$ is not integral over $k[t]$", "is this element in the ring of integers". The complementary reflex is for *combinations*: when several integral elements are combined by $+, \times$, do not seek the resulting equation — collect them into one finite module $A[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ and let [[Thm - The Integral Closure is a Subring|"the closure is a subring"]] certify the result integral. These two reflexes — *normal base $\Rightarrow$ integrality is membership*, and *combination $\Rightarrow$ land in a finite module* — between them solve the large majority of the chapter's element-level problems, and both are downstream of the one equivalence "integral $=$ module-finite".

**Inheritance — where does normality come from?** Normality is not a property you usually verify from its definition; it is *inherited*. The cleanest source is being a **UFD**: unique factorization forces integral closure, by the lowest-terms argument. The next source is **smoothness/regularity**: a regular local ring is a UFD (Auslander–Buchsbaum), hence normal, so a smooth variety is automatically normal — which is why normality is "smoothness in codimension one", the largest part of smoothness that pure integral closure can detect. The third source is **localization and gluing**: normality is local, so a ring inherits normality from its localizations, and a variety inherits it from an open cover. When you need to know a ring is normal, do not grind through fraction-field elements; ask *where the normality is coming from* — is it a UFD, is it regular, is it locally normal — and the answer is usually one of these three. Conversely, when a ring *fails* to be normal (the cusp, $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt5]$), that failure is a precise measurement of a singularity or of a "missing" algebraic integer, and the normalization is the canonical repair.
