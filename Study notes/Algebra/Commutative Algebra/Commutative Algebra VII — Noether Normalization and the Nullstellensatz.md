# Notation Registry

**Standing conventions.** Throughout these notes *ring* means a commutative ring with $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$. This chapter has two ground fields in play, and keeping them straight is essential: $k$ is the field whose polynomials we write down (the field of *coefficients* / *definition*), and $\Omega \supseteq k$ is an **algebraically closed** field where we look for *solutions* (every nonconstant polynomial over $\Omega$ has a root in $\Omega$). The two standing examples are $k \subseteq \Omega = \mathbb{C}$ (with $k = \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R},$ or $\mathbb{C}$) and $k = \mathbb{F}_p \subseteq \Omega = \overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$. When $k = \Omega$ the prefix "$k$-" is dropped. The chapter's one slogan, stated again in Motivation: **radical ideals are varieties, primes are irreducible varieties, maximal ideals are points — and Noether normalization makes every variety a finite cover of affine space.**

- $k$ — base field (coefficients); $\Omega \supseteq k$ — an **algebraically closed** extension (where solutions live)
- $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ — the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] in $n$ indeterminates; $A, B$ — finitely generated $k$-algebras
- $\Omega^n$ — **affine $n$-space**, written $\mathbb{A}^n$ when viewed geometrically; $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ — a point
- $f(x)$ — the value of $f \in k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ at $x \in \Omega^n$; $\operatorname{ev}_x$ — evaluation at $x$
- $V(S) = \{x \in \Omega^n : f(x) = 0\ \forall f \in S\}$ — the **vanishing set** of $S$; $V(f) := V(\{f\})$
- $I(X) = \{f \in k[T_1, \dots, T_n] : f(x) = 0\ \forall x \in X\}$ — the **ideal of a set** $X \subseteq \Omega^n$
- $k[X] = k[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I(X)$ — the **coordinate ring** (ring of regular functions on $X$)
- $\mathfrak m_x = (T_1 - x_1, \dots, T_n - x_n)$ — the **maximal ideal of a point** $x$; $\operatorname{Spec}$ / $\operatorname{mSpec}$ — prime / maximal spectrum
- $\sqrt{\mathfrak a} = \{f : f^m \in \mathfrak a,\ m \geq 1\}$ — the [[Def - Radical of an Ideal and the Nilradical|radical]]; $\mathfrak a$ is **radical** if $\sqrt{\mathfrak a} = \mathfrak a$
- $\operatorname{trdeg}_k L$ — the **transcendence degree** of $L/k$; $A'$ — a polynomial subalgebra $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$
- "$A$ **finite** over $B$" — $A$ is a finitely generated $B$-*module*; "$A$ **finitely generated** over $B$" — as an *algebra*
- $F$ — the top-degree homogeneous part (leading form) of a polynomial $f$
- $\mathbb{A}^n$ — affine space; **bold plain text** (variety, scheme, structure sheaf, generic point) — forward references to algebraic geometry

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **a system of polynomial equations and the geometric shape it cuts out are the same object, viewed through the ring of functions — and this dictionary is exact, computable, and the foundation of algebraic geometry.** Up to now commutative algebra has been the study of rings for their own sake. This chapter reveals that the polynomial ring $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ and its quotients *are* the algebra of geometry: every ideal is a shape, every radical ideal is a shape recovered exactly, every prime is an irreducible shape, every maximal ideal is a point. The two theorems that make this precise — **Noether normalization** and **Hilbert's Nullstellensatz** — are the most consequential in the subject, and they are the reason "commutative algebra is the algebra of algebraic geometry".

The dictionary is built in two movements. The first, **Noether normalization**, is a structural theorem about finitely generated algebras: every one of them is a *finite module* over a polynomial ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. Geometrically, every affine variety is a *finite branched cover* of an affine space $\mathbb{A}^n$, obtained by a generic linear projection; the number $n$ is the dimension. This is the tool that tames arbitrary finitely generated algebras by exhibiting them on top of the one ring we understand completely. Its immediate corollary, **Zariski's lemma**, says a finitely generated algebra that *is a field* is a finite extension of $k$ — and that single fact is the engine of everything geometric.

The second movement is the **Nullstellensatz** itself, in three escalating forms. The *weak* form is an existence theorem — over an algebraically closed field, every consistent system of polynomial equations has a solution; equivalently, the maximal ideals of $\Omega[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ are exactly the points of $\Omega^n$. The *strong* form computes which functions vanish on a variety: $I(V(\mathfrak a)) = \sqrt{\mathfrak a}$, the radical. And the *correspondence* assembles these into the full dictionary, an order-reversing bijection stratified through the ideal hierarchy:

$$\{\text{maximal ideals}\} \subseteq \{\text{prime ideals}\} \subseteq \{\text{radical ideals}\} \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \{\text{points}\} \subseteq \{\text{irreducible varieties}\} \subseteq \{\text{algebraic sets}\}.$$

The price of admission is a hypothesis that cannot be dropped: $\Omega$ must be **algebraically closed**. Over $\mathbb{R}$, the equation $T^2 + 1 = 0$ has no solution, so a perfectly good maximal ideal has empty zero set and the dictionary collapses. Algebraic closure is exactly the guarantee that "consistent" implies "solvable", and the chapter spends real effort isolating where it is used (the Nullstellensatz) versus where mere infinitude of $k$ suffices (Noether normalization's coordinate change).

This chapter assumes you are fluent with [[Def - Ideal|ideals]], [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime and maximal ideals]] and the [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients|domain/field-quotient dictionary]] from [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings]], with the [[Def - Radical of an Ideal and the Nilradical|radical]] and [[Def - The Prime Spectrum (Spec)|prime spectrum]] from [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|localization]], with [[Def - Integral Element and Integral Extension|integral and finite extensions]] and the [[Thm - Integral Extensions and Fields (Domain Criterion)|domain/field criterion]] from [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions|integral extensions]], and with [[Def - Finitely Generated Algebra|finitely generated algebras]] and [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial rings]]. A little field theory (algebraic extensions, transcendence bases) is reviewed as needed.

# Concept Map

## §7.1 Noether Normalization

- **[[Def - Algebraic Independence and Transcendence Degree]]**
	- Elements $x_1, \dots, x_n \in A$ are $k$-**algebraically independent** if the evaluation map $k[T_1, \dots, T_n] \to A$, $T_i \mapsto x_i$, is injective — no nonzero polynomial vanishes on them, so $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ is a genuine polynomial ring. A **transcendence basis** of a field extension $L/k$ is a maximal algebraically independent set; all such have the same cardinality, the **transcendence degree** $\operatorname{trdeg}_k L$. This is "linear algebra with polynomial relations replacing linear ones": independence, basis, dimension, and additivity in towers ($\operatorname{trdeg}_k E = \operatorname{trdeg}_k L + \operatorname{trdeg}_L E$) all reappear, and $\operatorname{trdeg}$ is the algebraic shadow of geometric dimension.

- **[[Thm - Noether Normalization]]**
	- Every nonzero finitely generated $k$-algebra $A$ is a **finite module** over a polynomial subalgebra $A' = k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ with the $x_i$ algebraically independent and $n = \operatorname{trdeg}_k A$. The proof inducts on the number of generators, using a generic linear shear $x_i \mapsto x_i + c_i x_m$ to turn any algebraic relation into a monic (integral) one — the top homogeneous form $F$ of the relation becomes the leading coefficient $F(c, 1)$, made a unit by choosing $c$ to avoid its zeros (possible over an infinite field). Geometrically: every variety is a finite branched cover of $\mathbb{A}^n$ by a generic projection.

> [!tip] Finite surjection onto affine space *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Noether normalization is the algebra of **"every $d$-dimensional affine variety is a finite surjective branched cover of $\mathbb{A}^d$"**, realised as a generic linear projection $\pi : X \to \mathbb{A}^d$. Finiteness forces $\pi$ to be surjective, closed, with finite fibres, preserving the dimension of each component; the number of sheets is the degree of the function-field extension. This is the geometric backbone of **dimension theory** and the reason a complicated variety can always be studied as a cover of simple affine space.

- **[[Ex - Noether normalization of a plane curve]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Carry out the normalization of $k[X, Y]/(f)$ explicitly: shear coordinates so $f$ becomes monic in one variable, exhibit the integral equation, conclude $\dim = \operatorname{trdeg} = 1$. The elliptic curve $y^2 = x^3 + x$ needs no shear ($f$ already monic in $y$) and presents as a $2$-sheeted cover of $\mathbb{A}^1$.

- **[[Ex - Why algebraic closure is needed in the Nullstellensatz]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Separate the two hypotheses: *infinite* (for the linear shear, via the dehomogenisation lemma ES1 Q1b and zero-avoidance ES1 Q1c) versus *algebraically closed* (for solution-existence). Build the $\mathbb{R}$-counterexample $(T^2+1)$ to the Nullstellensatz, the $\mathbb{F}_q$-counterexample $T_1^q - T_1$ to zero-avoidance, and spread inclusions $\mathbb{C} \to \overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$ by reduction mod $p$ (ES3 Q8).

> [!tip] Dimension equals transcendence degree *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Because $A$ is finite over $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ and finite extensions preserve Krull dimension, $\dim A = n = \operatorname{trdeg}_k \operatorname{Frac}(A)$ for a finitely generated domain. This is the theorem **"dimension of a variety = transcendence degree of its function field"**, the cornerstone of dimension theory developed in [[Commutative Algebra XII — Dimension Theory]]: $\mathbb{A}^n$ has dimension $n$, a hypersurface has dimension $n-1$, each equation drops dimension by one.

## §7.2 The Nullstellensatz and the Dictionary

- **[[Thm - Zariski's Lemma]]**
	- If $k \subseteq K$ are fields and $K$ is finitely generated *as a $k$-algebra*, then $K$ is finite (finite-dimensional) over $k$ — hence a finite algebraic extension. The proof is normalization plus the domain/field criterion: $K$ is finite over a polynomial skeleton $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, which must therefore be a field, forcing $n = 0$. The subtlety it resolves: $k(T)$ is finitely generated as a *field* but not as an *algebra*; a single transcendental destroys finite algebra-generation. This is the engine of the weak Nullstellensatz.

- **[[Thm - The Weak Nullstellensatz]]**
	- Over algebraically closed $\Omega \supseteq k$: $V(\mathfrak a) = \varnothing \iff 1 \in \mathfrak a$. A proper ideal has a nonempty zero set; equivalently, every consistent polynomial system has a solution. In point form (over $k = \Omega$): the maximal ideals of $\Omega[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ are exactly the $\mathfrak m_x = (T_1 - x_1, \dots, T_n - x_n)$, and $x \mapsto \mathfrak m_x$ is a bijection $\Omega^n \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{mSpec}$. The proof: a maximal ideal's residue field is finite over $k$ (Zariski), embeds into $\Omega$, and the embedding is evaluation at a point. It is the algebraic-geometry analogue of the fundamental theorem of algebra.

- **[[Ex - Maximal ideals of a polynomial ring over an algebraically closed field]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Classify $\operatorname{mSpec} \Omega[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ as the points $\mathfrak m_x$, by passing a maximal ideal to its residue field, applying Zariski's lemma, and collapsing via algebraic closure. The $\mathbb{R}$-counterexample $(T^2+1)$ (residue field $\mathbb{C} \neq \mathbb{R}$) shows closure is essential.

- **[[Thm - The Strong Nullstellensatz]]**
	- Over algebraically closed $\Omega$: $I(V(\mathfrak a)) = \sqrt{\mathfrak a}$ — a polynomial vanishes on the whole variety iff a power lies in the ideal. The hard inclusion uses the **Rabinowitsch trick**: adjoin a variable $T_{n+1}$ with $T_{n+1}f - 1$ to force $f \neq 0$; the enlarged variety is empty, the weak form gives $1$ in the enlarged ideal, and clearing the denominator $f$ produces $f^m \in \mathfrak a$ — the exponent is the radical. This computes the geometry-side operation $I(V(-))$ as the purely algebraic "take the radical".

- **[[Ex - The radical as the intersection of maximal ideals containing it]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove $\sqrt{I} = \bigcap_{I \subseteq \mathfrak m}\mathfrak m$ for a finitely generated $k$-algebra (the **Jacobson** property), by rewriting $\sqrt I = I(V(I))$ via the strong Nullstellensatz and identifying maximal ideals above $I$ with points of $V(I)$. In a general ring only *primes* suffice; finitely generated algebras have enough maximal ideals.

- **[[Thm - The Nullstellensatz Correspondence (radical ideals and varieties)]]**
	- The maps $V, I$ are mutually inverse, inclusion-reversing bijections {radical ideals} $\leftrightarrow$ {algebraic sets}, refining to {primes} $\leftrightarrow$ {irreducible varieties} and {maximal ideals} $\leftrightarrow$ {points}. It is an *anti-isomorphism of lattices*: $V(\mathfrak a + \mathfrak b) = V(\mathfrak a) \cap V(\mathfrak b)$, $I(X \cup Y) = I(X) \cap I(Y)$, $I(X \cap Y) = \sqrt{I(X) + I(Y)}$. Assembled from the strong Nullstellensatz ($I(V(\mathfrak a)) = \sqrt{\mathfrak a}$) and $V(I(X)) = X$, it is the complete algebra–geometry dictionary.

- **[[Def - Affine Variety and the Vanishing Set]]**
	- $V(S) = \{x \in \Omega^n : f(x) = 0\ \forall f \in S\}$ is the **vanishing set**; an **algebraic set** is any $V(S)$, depending only on the ideal $(S)$. $V$ reverses inclusions, sends sums of ideals to intersections and products to unions, so the $V(\mathfrak a)$ are the closed sets of the **Zariski topology** on $\Omega^n$. Algebraic closure of $\Omega$ is what makes proper ideals have nonempty zero sets (the weak Nullstellensatz); over $\mathbb{R}$, $V(T^2+1) = \varnothing$.

- **[[Def - The Coordinate Ring and the Ideal of a Set]]**
	- $I(X)$ (the polynomials vanishing on $X$) is always a radical ideal; $k[X] = k[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I(X)$ is the **coordinate ring**, the ring of regular functions on $X$, always reduced. The construction encodes "spaces are their rings of functions": points are maximal ideals of $k[X]$, components are minimal primes, regular maps $X \to Y$ are algebra homomorphisms $k[Y] \to k[X]$. Non-radical ideals (with nilpotents, like $(T^2)$) are not coordinate rings of varieties — they are the seed of schemes.

- **[[Def - Irreducible Algebraic Set]]**
	- An algebraic set is **irreducible** (an **affine variety** in the strict sense) if nonempty and not a union of two proper algebraic subsets. Irreducible $\iff I(X)$ prime $\iff \Omega[X]$ a domain — the geometric atoms are the primes. Every algebraic set is a finite union of irreducible **components** (the minimal primes of $I(X)$), the geometric form of prime factorisation. Irreducible is strictly stronger than connected: the two axes $V(T_1 T_2)$ are connected but reducible.

- **[[Ex - The ideal-variety correspondence and unions and intersections]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show $I(X \cup Y) = I(X) \cap I(Y)$ always, but $I(X \cap Y) = \sqrt{I(X) + I(Y)}$ needs the radical (ES3 Q6). The parabola-tangent-to-line counterexample $I(X) + I(Y) = (T_1^2, T_2) \subsetneq (T_1, T_2)$ shows the sum's nilpotent records *intersection multiplicity*, which the radical erases.

- **[[Ex - Irreducible iff the ideal is prime]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove $X$ irreducible $\iff I(X)$ prime $\iff \Omega[X]$ a domain, by translating "$fg \in I(X)$" into the covering $X \subseteq V(f) \cup V(g)$ and using "a prime equal to a finite intersection equals a factor" (ES2 Q2a). The mechanism "vanishing of a product = union of zero loci" drives both directions.

> [!tip] The full dictionary of affine algebraic geometry *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The correspondence is the affine, classical heart of an **anti-equivalence of categories** {affine $\Omega$-varieties, regular maps} $\simeq$ {finitely generated reduced $\Omega$-algebras}$^{\mathrm{op}}$: a variety is its coordinate ring, a map is an algebra homomorphism, points are maximal ideals, dimension is Krull dimension is transcendence degree, local geometry is the local ring at a point. Dropping "reduced" — keeping nilpotents — extends varieties to **affine schemes** $\operatorname{Spec} A$, and gluing them gives general **schemes**, absorbing arithmetic ($\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}$), infinitesimals, and non-closed fields into one geometry.

> [!tip] Spec, generic points, and the Zariski topology *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Reading the correspondence on *all* primes realises $\operatorname{Spec} \Omega[T_1, \dots, T_n]$ as affine space enriched with a **generic point** for each irreducible subvariety (its closure is that subvariety). The classical points $\Omega^n$ are the *closed* points; the extra points let "a general point of the variety" become a literal point, and free the theory from needing algebraic closure — the move founding **scheme theory**. The Zariski topology appeared already from [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|localization]] as the topology on $\operatorname{Spec}$; here it is the classical topology on $\Omega^n$.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring goals cluster into five types. The first is **deciding whether a variety is empty / a system is solvable** — settled by the [[Thm - The Weak Nullstellensatz|weak Nullstellensatz]]: a system has a solution iff $1$ is not in the ideal, certifiable by a Bézout identity. The second is **computing or recognising a radical** — "is $f \in \sqrt{\mathfrak a}$?", "is $\mathfrak a$ radical?" — routed through the [[Thm - The Strong Nullstellensatz|strong Nullstellensatz]] $\sqrt{\mathfrak a} = I(V(\mathfrak a))$ and the Rabinowitsch construction. The third, the chapter's signature, is **translating geometry to algebra and back** — proving varieties equal/contained/irreducible, classifying maximal or prime ideals, decomposing into components — via the [[Thm - The Nullstellensatz Correspondence (radical ideals and varieties)|correspondence]] and its lattice arithmetic. The fourth is **establishing finiteness / structure of a finitely generated algebra** — exhibiting it as a finite module over a polynomial ring — via [[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether normalization]], the route to Noetherianity, finiteness of normalization, and dimension. The fifth is **computing a dimension** — as a transcendence degree or a length of a prime chain — via normalization plus the going-up theorems. These are the natural targets because the chapter is fundamentally a *translation technology*: every theorem says "this geometric feature is faithfully an algebraic feature".

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A finitely generated $k$-algebra is given** — the universal precondition; present it as $k[T_1, \dots, T_n]/\mathfrak a$ and Noether normalization / the Nullstellensatz apply. **The base $\Omega$ is algebraically closed** — the hypothesis that turns "consistent" into "solvable" and makes maximal ideals points; recognising it is in force tells you the full dictionary is available. **A maximal (or prime) ideal is named** — switch to its residue field (finite over $k$ by Zariski) or its variety (a point or irreducible set). **A radical / vanishing condition appears** — route through $\sqrt{\mathfrak a} = I(V(\mathfrak a))$, converting algebra to vanishing or back. **The field is merely infinite (not closed)** — enough for Noether normalization's coordinate change, but *not* for the Nullstellensatz; this distinction (infinite vs. closed) is the subtlety the chapter most insists on. The discipline is to match source to target: a "is it empty?" target routes through the weak form; a "what vanishes?" target through the strong form; a "finite structure / dimension" target through normalization; a "geometry $\leftrightarrow$ algebra" target through the correspondence.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in this chapter is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list.

1. **Present a finitely generated algebra as a polynomial-ring quotient.** Write $A = k[T_1, \dots, T_n]/\mathfrak a$; then [[Thm - Noether Normalization|normalization]] and the [[Thm - The Strong Nullstellensatz|Nullstellensatz]], stated for polynomial rings, apply, and the ideal correspondence transports statements to ideals containing $\mathfrak a$. *Trigger:* any finitely generated $k$-algebra. *Pattern:* "present as $k[T]/\mathfrak a$, lift, apply, push back down".

2. **Shear coordinates to make a relation monic (Noether normalization).** Given an algebraic relation $f(x_1, \dots, x_m) = 0$, substitute $x_i \mapsto x_i + c_i x_m$ with $c$ avoiding the zeros of the top form $F(\cdot, 1)$; the relation becomes monic in $x_m$, making it integral. *Trigger:* generators with a relation, want a finite module over fewer generators. *Pattern:* "isolate the leading form, pick a generic direction, divide by $F(c,1)$".

3. **Pass to the residue field at a maximal ideal and squeeze with Zariski's lemma.** $R/\mathfrak m$ is a field finitely generated as a $k$-algebra, hence [[Thm - Zariski's Lemma|finite over the base field]]; over $\Omega$ closed it equals $\Omega$, and $\mathfrak m$ is a point. *Trigger:* a maximal ideal of a finitely generated algebra. *Pattern:* "residue field is finite over $k$; over $\Omega$ it is $\Omega$, so $\mathfrak m = \mathfrak m_x$".

4. **Identify a $k$-algebra homomorphism $k[T] \to \Omega$ with a point.** $\varphi \leftrightarrow x = (\varphi(T_i))$, with $\varphi = \operatorname{ev}_x$ and $\ker \varphi = \mathfrak m_x$. *Trigger:* a homomorphism from a polynomial ring to a field appears. *Pattern:* "read off the image-tuple as a point".

5. **Replace a radical by the ideal of a vanishing set (strong Nullstellensatz).** $\sqrt{\mathfrak a} = I(V(\mathfrak a))$ converts radical-membership into vanishing on points. *Trigger:* a radical, nilpotence, or "$f$ vanishes on the variety". *Pattern:* "$f \in \sqrt{\mathfrak a} \iff f$ vanishes on $V(\mathfrak a)$".

6. **Run the Rabinowitsch trick.** To show $f \in \sqrt{\mathfrak a}$, adjoin $T_{n+1}$ and $T_{n+1}f - 1$, get an empty variety, apply the weak form, clear denominators. *Trigger:* proving radical-membership from vanishing. *Pattern:* "force $f \neq 0$ with one extra variable, reduce to the weak form".

7. **Push $V$ and $I$ through lattice operations.** $V(\mathfrak a + \mathfrak b) = V(\mathfrak a) \cap V(\mathfrak b)$, $V(\mathfrak a \cap \mathfrak b) = V(\mathfrak a) \cup V(\mathfrak b)$, $I(X \cup Y) = I(X) \cap I(Y)$, $I(X \cap Y) = \sqrt{I(X) + I(Y)}$. *Trigger:* a union/intersection of varieties or ideals. *Pattern:* "translate, inserting a radical at each $I \circ V$".

8. **Translate "$fg$ vanishes on $X$" into "$X \subseteq V(f) \cup V(g)$".** A product vanishes exactly where a factor does; this is the bridge between primality and irreducibility. *Trigger:* a product in $I(X)$, or a decomposition of $X$. *Pattern:* "product in the ideal = covering by two zero loci".

9. **Read dimension as transcendence degree.** After normalizing $A$ over $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, $\dim A = n = \operatorname{trdeg}_k \operatorname{Frac}(A)$. *Trigger:* a dimension computation for a finitely generated domain. *Pattern:* "count the polynomial coordinates after normalizing".

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Applying the Nullstellensatz over a non-algebraically-closed field
> Because $\mathbb{R}$ is infinite and familiar, it is tempting to use "maximal ideals are points" or "$V(\mathfrak a) = \varnothing \iff 1 \in \mathfrak a$" over $\mathbb{R}$. Both **fail**: $(T^2 + 1) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{R}[T]$ is maximal with empty real zero set, and its residue field $\mathbb{C} \neq \mathbb{R}$, so it is not a point. The obstruction is exactly that $\mathbb{R}$ is not algebraically closed — Zariski's lemma still gives a *finite* extension, but closure is needed to collapse it to the base. The repair: work over $\Omega = \overline{k}$ (where the maximal ideal becomes the Galois orbit $\{i, -i\}$), or, for general $k$, use the $k$-algebraic / $\Omega$-solution formulation with $\Omega$ closed. Conflating "infinite" with "algebraically closed" is the single most common error of the chapter.

> [!warning] 2. Writing $I(X \cap Y) = I(X) + I(Y)$ without the radical
> The clean identity $I(X \cup Y) = I(X) \cap I(Y)$ tempts the false symmetric statement $I(X \cap Y) = I(X) + I(Y)$. It is wrong: the sum of two radical ideals need not be radical. For the parabola $X = V(T_2 - T_1^2)$ and line $Y = V(T_2)$, tangent at the origin, $I(X) + I(Y) = (T_1^2, T_2) \subsetneq (T_1, T_2) = I(X \cap Y)$ — the sum contains $T_1^2$ but not $T_1$. The nilpotent records the tangency (intersection multiplicity $2$). The repair is $I(X \cap Y) = \sqrt{I(X) + I(Y)}$, always; the bare sum is the *scheme-theoretic* intersection, the radical recovers the variety.

> [!warning] 3. Confusing "finitely generated as an algebra" with "as a field"
> $k(T)$ is generated by one element *as a field*, so it looks "small", and one is tempted to call it a finitely generated $k$-algebra. It is **not**: by [[Thm - Zariski's Lemma|Zariski's lemma]], a finitely generated $k$-algebra that is a field is *finite* over $k$, whereas $k(T)$ is infinite-dimensional (it has the transcendental $T$). Generating $k(T)$ as an algebra needs infinitely many elements (all the denominators $1/p(T)$). The repair: track *which* generation is meant — algebra-generation (polynomials only, the strong condition) versus field-generation (rational functions allowed). A single transcendental kills finite algebra-generation.

> [!warning] 4. Believing $\sqrt I = \bigcap_{\text{maximal}}\mathfrak m$ in an arbitrary ring
> The Jacobson identity "$\sqrt I$ = intersection of the maximal ideals above $I$" holds for finitely generated $k$-algebras, and it is tempting to use it everywhere. In a general ring it is **false** — only $\sqrt I = \bigcap_{\text{prime}}\mathfrak p$ is universal. In the local ring $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, the intersection of maximal ideals is $p\mathbb{Z}_{(p)} \neq (0) = \sqrt{(0)}$. The repair condition is "the ring is **Jacobson**", which the [[Thm - The Weak Nullstellensatz|weak Nullstellensatz]] guarantees for finitely generated algebras (a maximal ideal at every point) but not for local rings.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any problem of this chapter is to name *which direction of the dictionary* you are using, because there are three modes and each has a characteristic route.

If the problem **is about existence or emptiness** — "does this system have a solution?", "is this variety empty?", "find a common zero" — you are in the [[Thm - The Weak Nullstellensatz|weak Nullstellensatz]] mode. The route is: a solution exists iff the ideal is proper iff $1 \notin \mathfrak a$; to prove emptiness, exhibit a Bézout identity $\sum p_i f_i = 1$; to prove non-emptiness, note the ideal is proper and produce a point via a maximal ideal. The recognition step is realising that "homomorphism to $\Omega$" = "point", so finding a solution is finding a maximal ideal whose residue field embeds in $\Omega$ — which Zariski's lemma always allows over a closed field. The single most common error here is forgetting algebraic closure.

If the problem **is about which functions vanish, or about radicals** — "is $f$ in $\sqrt{\mathfrak a}$?", "compute the radical", "is this ideal radical?" — you are in the [[Thm - The Strong Nullstellensatz|strong Nullstellensatz]] mode. The controlling identity is $\sqrt{\mathfrak a} = I(V(\mathfrak a))$: radical-membership is vanishing on the variety. To *prove* $f \in \sqrt{\mathfrak a}$ algorithmically, run Rabinowitsch (adjoin $T_{n+1}$, force $f \neq 0$, get emptiness, apply the weak form, clear denominators) — the exponent in $f^m \in \mathfrak a$ is the price of the denominators, and it is *why* the answer is the radical rather than the ideal. To recognise a radical, compute the variety and re-ideal-ise; $\mathfrak a$ is radical iff $\mathfrak a = I(V(\mathfrak a))$.

If the problem **is a translation between geometry and algebra** — "prove these varieties are equal", "classify the maximal/prime ideals", "decompose into components", "is this variety irreducible?" — you are in the [[Thm - The Nullstellensatz Correspondence (radical ideals and varieties)|correspondence]] mode, and the strategy is to use the dictionary's strata. Points are maximal ideals (pass to the residue field, squeeze with Zariski); irreducible varieties are primes (use "vanishing of a product = union of zero loci"); general algebraic sets are radicals (decompose into minimal primes = components). The lattice arithmetic translates set operations: union to intersection of ideals (free), intersection to radical of sum (costs a radical), containment of varieties to reverse-containment of radicals. The recurring trap is dropping the radical on intersections.

A fourth, more structural, family **concerns finiteness, dimension, and the structure of a finitely generated algebra** — and these route through [[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether normalization]]. To compute a dimension, normalize over a polynomial ring and count the variables (= transcendence degree). To prove a finiteness statement (Noetherian, finite normalization, the algebra is a finite module), exhibit the normalization explicitly. The core manoeuvre is the linear shear that makes the defining relation monic; over an infinite field a generic shear works, and the leading form $F$ governs which directions are good.

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "what is the same statement on the other side of the dictionary?"** A geometric question becomes an ideal question (and conversely); the art is to translate to the side where the tools live — existence to the weak form, vanishing to the strong form, structure to normalization, set operations to the lattice arithmetic. Decide which side is easier, translate, solve, and translate back, remembering that the only thing geometry adds to an ideal is its radical.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether normalization]]**: every finitely generated $k$-algebra is a finite module over a polynomial ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$. This is the most reused *structural* fact because it tames an arbitrary finitely generated algebra by exhibiting it on top of the one ring we fully understand, with a *finite* (property-transferring) extension. Reach for it whenever you need finiteness, Noetherianity, a dimension, or a base over which to induct; its proof technique — the monic-making linear shear — is itself a reusable computation. **Typical use:** computing $\dim A = \operatorname{trdeg}_k A$, proving finiteness of integral closure, setting up dimension induction, and as the engine of Zariski's lemma.

- **[[Thm - Zariski's Lemma|Zariski's lemma]]**: a finitely generated $k$-algebra that is a field is finite over $k$. Its reusability is as the bridge from "finitely generated algebra" to "finite-dimensional vector space", which is what lets residue fields be controlled. **Typical use:** classifying maximal ideals (the residue field is finite over $k$, hence a point over $\Omega$ closed), proving the weak Nullstellensatz, establishing that finitely generated algebras are Jacobson. The trigger is "a field that is a finitely generated algebra"; the reaction is "it is finite over the base".

- **[[Thm - The Strong Nullstellensatz|The strong Nullstellensatz]]** $I(V(\mathfrak a)) = \sqrt{\mathfrak a}$: this is the master identity converting the geometric operation "ideal of the vanishing set" into the algebraic operation "radical". Its reusability is total — it is the single fact that makes the dictionary computable. **Typical use:** computing radicals, deciding radical-membership (via Rabinowitsch), proving the lattice identities of the correspondence, establishing the Jacobson property. Whenever a radical and a variety are both in play, this is the first move.

- **The point form of the [[Thm - The Weak Nullstellensatz|weak Nullstellensatz]]** $\Omega^n \xrightarrow{\sim} \operatorname{mSpec}$, $x \mapsto \mathfrak m_x$: the foundational identification "points are maximal ideals". Its reusability is as the bottom rung that anchors the entire dictionary and makes "a homomorphism out of a coordinate ring is a point" precise. **Typical use:** translating between points and ideals, evaluating functions in residue fields, building the correspondence's maximal-ideal stratum, and (downstream) defining the closed points of $\operatorname{Spec}$.

- **The Nullstellensatz [[Thm - The Nullstellensatz Correspondence (radical ideals and varieties)|correspondence]]** and its lattice arithmetic: $V, I$ inverse on radical ideals / algebraic sets, with $\cup \leftrightarrow \cap$ and $\cap \leftrightarrow \sqrt{+}$. Its reusability is as the *organising principle* that lets every geometric computation become an ideal computation. **Typical use:** proving varieties equal or contained, decomposing into irreducible components (minimal primes), recognising irreducibility (primality), and translating any set-theoretic geometric statement into algebra. The trigger is "a relation among varieties"; the reaction is "translate via $V$/$I$, insert radicals at $I \circ V$".

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — this chapter *is* the foundation, and the correspondence is the dictionary.** To an affine variety $X \subseteq \Omega^n$ one attaches its [[Def - The Coordinate Ring and the Ideal of a Set|coordinate ring]] $\Omega[X] = \Omega[T_1, \dots, T_n]/I(X)$, the ring of polynomial functions on it; the [[Thm - The Nullstellensatz Correspondence (radical ideals and varieties)|Nullstellensatz correspondence]] makes this assignment an *anti-equivalence of categories* between affine varieties (with regular maps) and finitely generated reduced $\Omega$-algebras (with algebra homomorphisms, arrows reversed). Under it, a point of $X$ is a maximal ideal of $\Omega[X]$, an irreducible subvariety is a prime, the dimension of $X$ is the [[Def - Algebraic Independence and Transcendence Degree|transcendence degree]] of its function field, the local geometry near a point is the [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|local ring]] $\Omega[X]_{\mathfrak m_x}$, and a regular map $X \to Y$ is a homomorphism $\Omega[Y] \to \Omega[X]$. The construction that glues affine pieces — replacing $X$ by its **structure sheaf** of regular functions and allowing the ring to carry nilpotents — produces **schemes**, the framework of modern algebraic geometry. So this chapter is the classical, affine prototype of all of scheme theory: the literal statement that geometry and commutative algebra are the same subject.

2. **The fundamental theorem of algebra and field theory — the weak Nullstellensatz is "solutions always exist", generalised.** The fundamental theorem of algebra says one polynomial over $\mathbb{C}$ has a root; the [[Thm - The Weak Nullstellensatz|weak Nullstellensatz]] says any *consistent* finite system in any number of variables over an algebraically closed field has a common root, where "consistent" is the precise algebraic condition $1 \notin \mathfrak a$. The proof factors through [[Thm - Zariski's Lemma|Zariski's lemma]] and the field-theoretic fact that finite extensions embed into an algebraic closure. The role of algebraic closure is exactly the role it plays in the fundamental theorem: it guarantees roots exist. Over a non-closed field the theorem fails, and the residue field $\Omega[T]/\mathfrak m$ of a maximal ideal becomes the **field of definition** of a *Galois orbit* of points rather than a single point — the entry to arithmetic geometry, where one works over $\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{F}_p$ and the residue-field degrees are the interesting data.

3. **Dimension theory and transcendence — Noether normalization computes geometric dimension algebraically.** [[Thm - Noether Normalization|Noether normalization]] exhibits a finitely generated domain $A$ as a finite module over a polynomial ring $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$, and because finite (integral) extensions preserve Krull dimension via [[Commutative Algebra VIII — Going Up and Going Down|going-up and incomparability]], $\dim A = n = \operatorname{trdeg}_k \operatorname{Frac}(A)$. This is the theorem "the dimension of a variety equals the transcendence degree of its function field equals the length of the longest chain of irreducible subvarieties" — three a priori different invariants made equal. Geometrically it is the finite projection $X \to \mathbb{A}^n$: the variety covers an affine space whose dimension is manifestly $n$, and finiteness transfers the dimension. The full theory, including the Krull principal ideal theorem (a hypersurface drops dimension by one) and the Hilbert polynomial, is built on this base in [[Commutative Algebra XII — Dimension Theory]].

4. **The polynomial method in combinatorics and number theory — the Nullstellensatz exported to finite grids.** The principle "a polynomial vanishing on a large set must have algebraic structure" generalises from algebraically closed fields to combinatorial settings. Alon's *Combinatorial Nullstellensatz* controls the degrees of a polynomial vanishing on a grid $S_1 \times \dots \times S_n$, proving existence theorems (Cauchy–Davenport, the Cayley–Bacharach configurations, list-colouring bounds) by the same "vanishing forces structure" logic. The zero-avoidance lemma of [[Ex - Why algebraic closure is needed in the Nullstellensatz|this chapter's exercises]] (a nonzero polynomial over an infinite field has a non-root, Schwartz–Zippel) is the probabilistic-polynomial-identity-testing tool at the heart of this method, and the [[Ex - Why algebraic closure is needed in the Nullstellensatz|reduction-mod-$p$ technique]] (ES3 Q8) is how facts over $\mathbb{C}$ propagate to almost all characteristics, the foundational "spreading out" of arithmetic geometry.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: this chapter installs a dictionary, and every problem is a translation.** The single perspective that organises everything is that there are two parallel worlds — geometry (points, varieties, shapes cut out by equations) and algebra (ideals of the polynomial ring) — and the Nullstellensatz is the order-reversing bijection between them. The geometry side is intuitive but hard to compute; the algebra side is computable but unintuitive; and the dictionary lets you do each problem on whichever side is easier. The strata match rung for rung: maximal ideals are points, primes are irreducible varieties, radicals are algebraic sets, and the inclusion order reverses (bigger variety, smaller ideal). The deep content is that the bijection is *faithful* — the only information geometry adds to an ideal is its radical, and "take the radical" is an algebraic operation, so the two worlds carry literally the same data. The slogan to carry: **a variety and its coordinate ring are the same object, and Noether normalization shows that object is always a finite cover of affine space.**

**The true name of an ideal, completed: it is a shape, and its radical is what you can see.** The [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings]] notes gave a prime ideal the true name "kernel of a map to a domain"; [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|localization]] gave it "a point of $\operatorname{Spec}$". This chapter completes the picture: **an ideal $\mathfrak a$ is the algebra of the shape $V(\mathfrak a)$, and the radical $\sqrt{\mathfrak a}$ is the part of $\mathfrak a$ that geometry can see.** Two ideals with the same radical cut out the same variety; the difference between them — the nilpotents in $k[T]/\mathfrak a$ — is *infinitesimal* data invisible to the point set, recording multiplicity and tangency. The strong Nullstellensatz $I(V(\mathfrak a)) = \sqrt{\mathfrak a}$ is exactly the statement "geometry sees the radical and nothing more". This reorganises the whole subject: classical algebraic geometry works with radical ideals (varieties), and the leap to *schemes* is precisely the decision to keep the full ideal, letting nilpotents encode the infinitesimal geometry (a double point $V(T^2)$, a tangent intersection). Knowing whether your problem cares about the radical or the full ideal tells you whether you are doing variety theory or scheme theory.

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "maximal ideal of a finitely generated algebra" means "pass to the residue field, it is finite over $k$".** When a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$ of a finitely generated $k$-algebra appears, the reflex should fire: $R/\mathfrak m$ is a field finitely generated as a $k$-algebra, so by [[Thm - Zariski's Lemma|Zariski's lemma]] it is *finite* over $k$ — and over an algebraically closed $\Omega$ it *is* $\Omega$, making $\mathfrak m$ a point. This single reflex classifies maximal spectra, proves the weak Nullstellensatz, and establishes the Jacobson property. The complementary reflex is the **Rabinowitsch trick** for radicals: when you must show "$f$ vanishing on $V(\mathfrak a)$ implies $f^m \in \mathfrak a$", adjoin a variable $T_{n+1}$ with $T_{n+1}f - 1$ to force $f \neq 0$, reducing the strong form to the weak form. And the third reflex, for normalization: when generators satisfy a relation, **shear coordinates to make the relation monic**, turning algebraic dependence into integral dependence. These three moves — *residue field via Zariski*, *Rabinowitsch for radicals*, *shear for normalization* — between them solve the large majority of the chapter's problems, and all three are consequences of the single structural fact that finitely generated algebras are finite over polynomial rings.

**Infinite versus algebraically closed: the two hypotheses do different jobs, and keeping them apart is the chapter's hardest discipline.** Noether normalization's linear coordinate change needs only that $k$ is *infinite* (so a generic direction avoids a proper zero set); the Nullstellensatz's existence of solutions needs that $\Omega$ is *algebraically closed* (so a maximal ideal's residue field collapses to the base). The field $\mathbb{R}$ separates them cleanly: normalization and Zariski's lemma hold over $\mathbb{R}$, but the Nullstellensatz fails, because $(T^2+1)$ has residue field $\mathbb{C} \neq \mathbb{R}$. The field $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$ satisfies both (infinite and closed), which is why algebraic geometry works uniformly in all characteristics over algebraically closed fields, and why facts over $\mathbb{C}$ spread to almost every $\overline{\mathbb{F}_p}$ by reduction mod $p$. The diagnostic for any "does this work over field $K$?" question: ask *which* hypothesis the argument actually used — coordinate change (infinite suffices) or solution existence (closure required). This separation, made concrete in [[Ex - Why algebraic closure is needed in the Nullstellensatz]], is the key to porting results between characteristic zero and characteristic $p$ and is the seed of the **Lefschetz principle** and arithmetic geometry's spreading-out technology.
