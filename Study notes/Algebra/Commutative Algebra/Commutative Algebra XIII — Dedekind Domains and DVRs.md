# Notation Registry

**Standing convention.** Throughout these notes *ring* means a commutative ring with $1$, and a ring homomorphism sends $1 \mapsto 1$. This chapter is about two classes of ring that sit at the very bottom of the dimension hierarchy: the **discrete valuation rings** (DVRs), which are the one-dimensional local rings that are "as nice as possible", and the **Dedekind domains**, which are their global counterpart — one-dimensional domains every localization of which is a DVR. The single organising slogan, stated again in Motivation, is: **a Dedekind domain is the coordinate ring of a smooth affine curve, a DVR is the local ring at one of its points, and the valuation is the order of vanishing there.** Where the source writes $\operatorname{spec}$, $\operatorname{mspec}$, $\sqrt{\cdot}$, $\operatorname{Frac}$, $\operatorname{ht}$, we use $\operatorname{Spec}$, $\operatorname{mSpec}$, $\sqrt{\cdot}$, $\operatorname{Frac}$, $\operatorname{ht}$.

- $A, R$ — commutative rings with $1$, usually integral domains
- $K = \operatorname{Frac}(A)$ — the [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] of a domain $A$
- $K^\times = K \setminus \{0\}$ — the multiplicative group of the field $K$
- $v : K^\times \to \mathbb{Z}$ — a **discrete valuation**: a surjective group homomorphism with $v(x+y) \geq \min\{v(x), v(y)\}$; extended by $v(0) = \infty$
- $A_v = \{x \in K : v(x) \geq 0\}$ — the **valuation ring** of $v$
- $v_p$ — the **$p$-adic valuation** on $\mathbb{Q}$: $v_p(p^n \tfrac ab) = n$ for $p \nmid a, b$; its valuation ring is $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$
- $(A, \mathfrak{m})$ — a [[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field|local ring]] with unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$; $k = A/\mathfrak{m}$ its residue field
- $\pi$ — a **uniformizer** (uniformizing parameter): a generator of $\mathfrak{m}$ in a DVR, equivalently an element with $v(\pi) = 1$
- $\dim A$ — the [[Def - Krull Dimension and Height|Krull dimension]]; $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p}$ — the height of a prime
- $\mathfrak{p}, \mathfrak{q}$ — prime ideals; $\mathfrak{p}^n = \mathfrak{p} \cdots \mathfrak{p}$ the ideal product
- $A_\mathfrak{p} = (A \setminus \mathfrak{p})^{-1}A$ — the [[Def - Multiplicative Set and Localization|localization]] of $A$ at $\mathfrak{p}$; $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}$ — the extended ideal
- $\mathfrak{a}^c = \iota^{-1}(\mathfrak{b})$ — the [[Def - Extension and Contraction of Ideals|contraction]] of an ideal along the localization map $\iota : A \to A_\mathfrak{p}$
- $\mathcal{O}_K$ — the **ring of integers** of a number field $K$: the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$
- $[K : \mathbb{Q}]$ — the degree of the field extension $K/\mathbb{Q}$
- $\mathcal{I}(A)$ — the group of nonzero **fractional ideals** of $A$ (when $A$ is Dedekind); $\mathcal{P}(A)$ — the subgroup of principal fractional ideals
- $\operatorname{Cl}(A) = \mathcal{I}(A)/\mathcal{P}(A)$ — the **ideal class group**

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **a Dedekind domain is a ring of integers, like $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathcal{O}_K$, in which numbers may fail to factor uniquely but *ideals* always do — and the reason ideals behave is that, one prime at a time, the ring looks like the integers localized at a prime.** The story has two registers, local and global, and they are perfect mirrors of one another. Locally, the model object is $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, the integers with everything coprime to $p$ inverted: a ring with exactly two primes $(0) \subsetneq (p)$, where every nonzero element is $p^n$ times a unit, and where the single invariant of an element is "how many times $p$ divides it". That counting function $v_p$ is a *discrete valuation*, and a ring that arises this way is a *discrete valuation ring*. Globally, the model object is $\mathbb{Z}$ itself, or more interestingly the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ of a number field: a one-dimensional domain whose localization at each nonzero prime is a DVR. That is a *Dedekind domain*.

Why do we want these definitions? Because of a famous failure. In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the number $6$ factors two genuinely different ways,
$$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}),$$
and all four factors are irreducible, so *unique factorization of elements is false*. This is not a curiosity; it is the obstruction that nineteenth-century number theory ran into when trying to prove Fermat-style statements by factoring. Kummer's and Dedekind's resolution was to stop factoring numbers and start factoring *ideals*. At the level of ideals the ambiguity disappears: $(2) = \mathfrak{p}^2$, $(3) = \mathfrak{q}\bar{\mathfrak{q}}$, and the two factorizations of $6$ become the *same* prime-ideal factorization $(6) = \mathfrak{p}^2\mathfrak{q}\bar{\mathfrak{q}}$ regrouped two ways. The theorem that makes this work — every nonzero ideal of a Dedekind domain is uniquely a product of prime ideals — is the global punchline of the chapter, and it is proved by the relentless application of the local-to-global philosophy: check the factorization one prime at a time, where the ring is a DVR and the answer is obvious.

The backbone of the whole subject is the following chain of equivalent descriptions of the local object, which is the content of the [[Thm - Characterization of Discrete Valuation Rings|DVR characterization theorem]]. For a Noetherian local domain $(A, \mathfrak{m})$ of dimension $1$:
$$A \text{ is a DVR} \iff A \text{ is integrally closed} \iff \mathfrak{m} \text{ is principal} \iff \text{every nonzero ideal is a power of }\mathfrak{m} \iff \dim_k \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 1.$$
Each "$\iff$" is a different face of the same ring, and learning to move between them is most of the chapter. The two definitions of a Dedekind domain — "Noetherian, integrally closed, dimension $1$" and "every localization at a nonzero prime is a DVR" — are likewise equivalent, and the equivalence is exactly the local-to-global principle for integral closure: being integrally closed is a local property, and locally it means being a DVR.

There is a second register in which all of this is best understood, and it is geometric. To a one-dimensional domain $A$ one attaches the space $\operatorname{Spec} A$, a **curve**: its closed points are the nonzero primes, its one generic point is $(0)$. A Dedekind domain is precisely the coordinate ring of a **smooth affine curve** — in dimension one, "integrally closed" is the algebraic meaning of "no singular points". Localizing at a point gives the DVR, which is the local ring of the curve at that point, and the valuation $v$ is the **order of vanishing** of a function at the point: $v(f) = 3$ means $f$ has a triple zero there. Unique factorization of ideals says that an ideal — a **divisor** on the curve — is a sum of points with multiplicities, $\sum n_i [\mathfrak{p}_i]$. And the obstruction to *principal* ideals, the ideal class group $\operatorname{Cl}(A)$, is exactly the **Picard group** of the curve: the group of line bundles, measuring how far the curve is from having every divisor be the divisor of a function. The class number of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ being $2$ is the statement that this curve carries one nontrivial line bundle — the very same non-free locally-free ideal $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ that [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|Localization]] flagged as "freeness is not local".

This chapter assumes you are fluent with [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian rings]], [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domains]], [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PIDs]], [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|UFDs]] and the [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] from the Rings notes; with [[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field|local rings]], the [[Def - Radical of an Ideal and the Nilradical|radical]], [[Def - Multiplicative Set and Localization|localization]] and the [[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|local–global principle]] from [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|Localization]]; with [[Def - Integral Closure and Normal Domain|integral closure and normality]] from [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions|Integral Extensions]]; with [[Def - Krull Dimension and Height|Krull dimension and height]] from [[Commutative Algebra XII — Dimension Theory|Dimension Theory]]; with [[Def - Primary Ideal|primary ideals]] and primary decomposition from [[Commutative Algebra IX — Primary Decomposition|Primary Decomposition]]; and with [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama's lemma]]. The $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ characterization additionally uses the **cotangent space** $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ from [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama]].

# Concept Map

## §14.1 Discrete Valuation Rings

- **[[Def - Discrete Valuation and Valuation Ring]]**
	- A **discrete valuation** on a field $K$ is a surjective homomorphism $v : K^\times \to \mathbb{Z}$ with the ultrametric inequality $v(x+y) \geq \min\{v(x), v(y)\}$, extended by $v(0) = \infty$; one derives $v(1) = v(-1) = 0$ and $v(-x) = v(x)$. Its **valuation ring** $A_v = \{x : v(x) \geq 0\}$ is the set of elements with non-negative valuation, and an integral domain is a **discrete valuation ring (DVR)** if it is $A_v$ for some discrete valuation on its own fraction field. The basic facts: $x \in A_v^\times \iff v(x) = 0$, the nonzero ideals are exactly $(\pi^0) \supsetneq (\pi^1) \supsetneq (\pi^2) \supsetneq \cdots$ for any uniformizer $\pi$ (an element with $v(\pi) = 1$), and $\operatorname{Spec} A_v = \{(0), (\pi)\}$, so $A_v$ is a Noetherian local domain of dimension $1$.
	- The model is $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ with $v = v_p$: every nonzero rational is $p^n$ times a unit of $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, and $v_p$ counts the power of $p$. Geometrically $v$ is the order of vanishing of a function at a smooth point of a curve.

- **[[Thm - Characterization of Discrete Valuation Rings]]**
	- For a Noetherian local domain $(A, \mathfrak{m})$ of dimension $1$, the following are equivalent: (1) $A$ is a DVR; (2) $A$ is integrally closed; (3) $\mathfrak{m}$ is principal; (4) every nonzero ideal is a power of $\mathfrak{m}$; (5) there is $\pi$ with every nonzero ideal $(\pi^n)$; and (6) $\dim_k \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 1$, where $k = A/\mathfrak{m}$. The hard implication is (2) $\Rightarrow$ (3): integral-closedness is used to manufacture a uniformizer, via an element $x = b/a$ with $x^{-1}\mathfrak{m} \subseteq A$ but $x^{-1}\mathfrak{m} \not\subseteq \mathfrak{m}$. The cheap implications form the cycle $(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow(3)\Rightarrow(4)\Rightarrow(5)\Rightarrow(1)$.
	- These are the "true names" of a DVR: each is the form you reach for in a different problem — principal $\mathfrak{m}$ for computing, integral-closedness for recognizing one inside a Dedekind domain, the cotangent condition for smoothness.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The local ring of a smooth point on a curve *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> A DVR is exactly the **local ring of a nonsingular point on an algebraic curve**. The condition $\dim_k \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 1$ is the algebraic definition of **smoothness**: the Zariski cotangent space is one-dimensional, matching the dimension of the curve, so the point has a well-defined tangent line and no node or cusp. The uniformizer $\pi$ is a **local coordinate** at the point, and the valuation $v(f)$ is the **order of vanishing** of the function $f$ — the integer such that $f = (\text{unit}) \cdot \pi^{v(f)}$. A node like $\operatorname{Spec} k[x,y]/(y^2 - x^3)$ at the origin fails this: there $\dim \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 2$, the local ring is not integrally closed, and **normalization** (taking integral closure) is the operation that resolves the singularity, replacing the cusp by a smooth point whose local ring is a DVR.

- **[[Ex - Z localized at p is a DVR]]** (⭐)
	- Build the prototype DVR from scratch: verify that $v_p$ (the power of $p$ in a rational) is a discrete valuation on $\mathbb{Q}$ — well-defined and multiplicative by unique factorization in $\mathbb{Z}$, ultrametric by factoring out the minimal power of $p$ — and identify its valuation ring as $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, with uniformizer $p$, maximal ideal $(p)$, and residue field $\mathbb{F}_p$. This is the model every DVR is patterned on.

- **[[Ex - A DVR is a PID with one maximal ideal]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove the cheap implications of the characterization: principal $\mathfrak{m}$ ⇒ every ideal a power of $\mathfrak{m}$ ⇒ every ideal is $(\pi^n)$ ⇒ DVR, using the "element of exact order $t$" argument and Nakayama's $\mathfrak{m} \neq \mathfrak{m}^2$. Concludes the operational identity that a DVR is exactly a local principal ideal domain that is not a field (ES4 Q17(a)(i)).

- **[[Ex - The valuation ring of the p-adic valuation]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Settle how much the valuation ring remembers: the ring determines its valuation (since $v(x)$ is the unique $n$ with $(x) = \mathfrak{m}^n$), and isomorphic valuation rings force isomorphic fields (the fraction field is functorial). A lesson in resisting a framing that invites a counterexample to a true statement (ES4 Q17(b)).

## §14.2 Dedekind Domains and Ideal Factorization

- **[[Def - Dedekind Domain]]**
	- A **Dedekind domain** is a Noetherian integral domain of dimension $1$ satisfying one (hence all) of: (1) $A$ is integrally closed; (2) $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a DVR for every nonzero prime $\mathfrak{p}$. The equivalence is the local–global principle for integral closure: $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a Noetherian local domain of dimension $\operatorname{ht}\mathfrak{p} = 1$, so by the DVR characterization it is a DVR iff it is integrally closed, and "$A$ integrally closed iff every $A_\mathfrak{m}$ integrally closed" is the local property. Every PID is Dedekind; $\mathbb{Z}$, $k[T]$, and $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ are Dedekind, but $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$ is not (it is not integrally closed) and $k[x,y]$ is not (dimension $2$).
	- A Dedekind domain is the coordinate ring of a smooth affine curve; condition (2) says it looks like a DVR — a smooth point — everywhere locally.

- **[[Thm - A Dedekind Domain has Unique Factorization of Ideals]]**
	- In a Dedekind domain $A$, every nonzero ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ is a product $\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{p}_1^{e_1} \cdots \mathfrak{p}_n^{e_n}$ of powers of distinct nonzero primes, and the $\mathfrak{p}_i$ and exponents $e_i \geq 1$ are unique up to reordering. Two ingredients drive the proof: first, the set of $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals is exactly $\{\mathfrak{p}^n\}_{n \geq 1}$ (a $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideal localizes to a power of $\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}$ in the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$, and contracts back to $\mathfrak{p}^n$); second, distinct nonzero primes are coprime and maximal, so the primary decomposition $\mathfrak{a} = \bigcap \mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i}$ collapses to a product by the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules|Chinese Remainder Theorem]]. The exponent $e_i$ is the local valuation $v_i(\mathfrak{a})$ read in the DVR $A_{\mathfrak{p}_i}$.
	- This is the precise sense in which "Dedekind = where ideals factor uniquely". It is the substitute for unique factorization of elements, recovering it exactly when every ideal is principal.

- **[[Thm - The Ring of Integers of a Number Field is Dedekind]]**
	- If $K$ is a number field (a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$) and $\mathcal{O}_K$ is its ring of integers (the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$), then $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a Dedekind domain. The proof checks the three Dedekind axioms: $\mathcal{O}_K$ is integrally closed by transitivity of integrality; it has dimension $1$ because $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathcal{O}_K$ is integral and integral extensions preserve dimension; and it is Noetherian because $\mathcal{O}_K \cong \mathbb{Z}^{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, hence finitely generated, hence a Noetherian $\mathbb{Z}$-module and a fortiori a Noetherian ring. The free-module fact $\mathcal{O}_K \cong \mathbb{Z}^{[K:\mathbb{Q}]}$ is the deepest input and is non-examinable.
	- This is the theorem that justifies the whole edifice: it is *why* number theory takes place in Dedekind domains, and it is the source of the term "ring of integers".

- **[[Thm - Localization of a Dedekind Domain is a DVR]]**
	- For a Dedekind domain $A$ and a nonzero prime $\mathfrak{p}$, the localization $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a DVR. This is one half of the definition's equivalence, but it is the workhorse: it is what lets every "is this ideal a power of $\mathfrak{p}$?" question be answered by passing to the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$, computing with the valuation $v_\mathfrak{p}$, and contracting back. The valuation $v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a})$ recovered this way is exactly the exponent of $\mathfrak{p}$ in the prime factorization of $\mathfrak{a}$, and a $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideal is contracted from $A_\mathfrak{p}$ (this uses that $\mathfrak{p} \cap (A\setminus\mathfrak{p}) = \varnothing$).

- **[[Def - Fractional Ideal and the Ideal Class Group]]**
	- A **fractional ideal** of a domain $A$ with fraction field $K$ is a nonzero $A$-submodule $\mathfrak{a} \subseteq K$ with $d\mathfrak{a} \subseteq A$ for some $d \neq 0$ (clearing denominators makes it an honest ideal). When $A$ is Dedekind the nonzero fractional ideals form an abelian **group** $\mathcal{I}(A)$ under multiplication, with inverse $\mathfrak{a}^{-1} = \{x \in K : x\mathfrak{a} \subseteq A\}$ and identity $A$ — every fractional ideal is uniquely $\prod \mathfrak{p}^{n_\mathfrak{p}}$ with $n_\mathfrak{p} \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $\mathcal{I}(A)$ is the free abelian group on the primes. The principal fractional ideals $(x) = xA$ form a subgroup $\mathcal{P}(A)$, and the quotient $\operatorname{Cl}(A) = \mathcal{I}(A)/\mathcal{P}(A)$ is the **ideal class group**. It is trivial iff $A$ is a PID, so its size measures the failure of unique factorization.
	- Geometrically $\mathcal{I}(A)$ is the group of **divisors** on the curve, $\mathcal{P}(A)$ the **principal divisors** (divisors of functions), and $\operatorname{Cl}(A)$ the **Picard group** of line bundles.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Divisors, the Picard group, and line bundles *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> On a smooth curve $C = \operatorname{Spec} A$, a **divisor** is a finite formal sum $\sum n_i [\mathfrak{p}_i]$ of closed points with integer coefficients — and unique factorization of ideals says this is *the same data* as a nonzero fractional ideal, via $\mathfrak{a} \mapsto \sum v_{\mathfrak{p}_i}(\mathfrak{a})[\mathfrak{p}_i]$. So the divisor group is $\mathcal{I}(A)$, the free abelian group on the points. The divisor of a rational function $f$ is $\operatorname{div}(f) = \sum_\mathfrak{p} v_\mathfrak{p}(f)[\mathfrak{p}]$ — its zeros minus its poles, with multiplicity — and these **principal divisors** are $\mathcal{P}(A)$. The quotient $\operatorname{Cl}(A) = \mathcal{I}(A)/\mathcal{P}(A)$ is the **divisor class group**, which for a smooth curve equals the **Picard group** $\operatorname{Pic}(C)$ of isomorphism classes of **line bundles**: a fractional ideal *is* a rank-one locally-free module, hence a line bundle, and two are isomorphic iff they differ by a principal ideal. The class group being nontrivial is the geometric statement that the curve carries a line bundle with no global trivialization — the same phenomenon that **Serre–Swan** turns into the nontriviality of a vector bundle, here in its simplest rank-one form.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Ramification and the different *(from Algebraic Number Theory)*
> When a prime $p \in \mathbb{Z}$ is extended to $\mathcal{O}_K$, its factorization $(p) = \mathfrak{p}_1^{e_1}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_g^{e_g}$ records how the prime **splits, ramifies, or stays inert**: the exponents $e_i$ are the **ramification indices**, and a prime with some $e_i > 1$ is *ramified*. The example $(2) = \mathfrak{p}^2$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ shows $2$ is ramified. Geometrically this is the branching of the finite map of curves $\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal{O}_K \to \operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}$; the locus where ramification happens is cut out by the **discriminant**, and the finer invariant measuring it is the **different ideal**. Unique factorization of ideals is exactly what gives these indices a meaning, and the fundamental identity $\sum_i e_i f_i = [K : \mathbb{Q}]$ (with $f_i$ the residue degrees) is its first consequence.

- **[[Ex - Fractional ideals form a group]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove that the nonzero fractional ideals of a Dedekind domain form an abelian group, verifying the only nontrivial axiom — invertibility $\mathfrak{a}(A:\mathfrak{a}) = A$ — *locally*, where at each prime it is the trivial DVR cancellation $(\pi^n)(\pi^{-n}) = A_\mathfrak{p}$. Identifies $\mathcal{I}(A)$ as the free abelian group on the primes, and notes that invertibility of all fractional ideals characterizes Dedekind domains.

- **[[Ex - Unique factorization of ideals in Z[sqrt -5] into primes]]** (⭐⭐)
	- The canonical worked example: factor $(2) = \mathfrak{p}^2$ (ramified), $(3) = \mathfrak{q}\bar{\mathfrak{q}}$ (split), $(1\pm\sqrt{-5}) = \mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q},\,\mathfrak{p}\bar{\mathfrak{q}}$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ by reducing $x^2+5$ modulo each prime (Dedekind's criterion), and watch both factorizations of $6$ regroup to the single ideal factorization $(6) = \mathfrak{p}^2\mathfrak{q}\bar{\mathfrak{q}}$. The norm proves $\mathfrak{p}$ is non-principal.

- **[[Ex - The class group measures failure of unique factorization]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Prove the headline equivalence "trivial class group ⇔ PID ⇔ UFD" (the hard arrow UFD ⇒ PID using that dimension one makes every nonzero prime minimal, hence principal in a UFD), then compute $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ by showing $[\mathfrak{p}]$ has order $2$ — $\mathfrak{p}^2 = (2)$ is principal but $\mathfrak{p}$ is not, by the norm obstruction $a^2+5b^2 \neq 2$.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring goals of this chapter cluster into five types. The first is **recognizing a ring as a DVR or a Dedekind domain** — taking a ring presented concretely (a localization, a ring of integers, a coordinate ring) and certifying it against one of the equivalent definitions. The standard route is to verify the three Dedekind axioms (Noetherian, integrally closed, dimension $1$) or, locally, to produce a uniformizer; the [[Thm - Characterization of Discrete Valuation Rings|DVR characterization]] supplies whichever of the five equivalent conditions is easiest to check. The second is **computing the prime factorization of an ideal** — given $\mathfrak{a}$, finding the primes and exponents in $\mathfrak{a} = \prod \mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i}$; the lever is [[Thm - Localization of a Dedekind Domain is a DVR|localize at each 𝔭]], read off $e_i = v_{\mathfrak{p}_i}(\mathfrak{a})$ in the DVR, and reassemble. The third, the chapter's signature, is **establishing unique factorization of ideals** itself, or its consequences — that a Dedekind domain with trivial class group is a PID, that ideals satisfy a "to contain is to divide" calculus. The fourth is **proving fractional ideals form a group** — exhibiting inverses, which is where the Dedekind hypothesis is genuinely needed (a general domain has fractional ideals but they are not invertible). The fifth is **computing or bounding the class group** — showing $\operatorname{Cl}(A)$ is nontrivial by producing a non-principal ideal, or trivial by showing every ideal is principal. These are natural because Dedekind theory is fundamentally a *factorization theory*: every theorem says either "ideals factor" or "here is the obstruction to factoring elements".

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A ring is given as integrally closed of dimension $1$** — the cleanest source, immediately routing to "Dedekind" and unlocking unique factorization. **A ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ appears** — and the move is to invoke [[Thm - The Ring of Integers of a Number Field is Dedekind|𝒪_K is Dedekind]], after which all ideal-factorization machinery is available; recognizing the number-theoretic setting is the whole trigger. **A local ring with one nonzero prime is in play** — switch on the DVR characterization, hunt for a uniformizer or check $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$. **A prime $\mathfrak{p}$ is named in a Dedekind domain** — localize to the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$, where the valuation $v_\mathfrak{p}$ turns every ideal question into integer arithmetic, then contract using that $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals are contracted from $A_\mathfrak{p}$. **A specific non-principal ideal is exhibited** (like $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$) — the source for class-group nontriviality. The discipline is to match source to target along the local–global axis: a global factorization target routes through localization to the DVR and back; a "recognize the ring" target routes through the equivalent characterizations; a class-group target routes through fractional ideals and the search for a (non-)principal generator. The [[Commutative Algebra XIII — Dedekind Domains and DVRs#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every Dedekind/DVR problem is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Everything is self-contained.

1. **Read off valuation arithmetic.** In a DVR with valuation $v$, every nonzero $x$ is $u\pi^{v(x)}$ for a unit $u$ and uniformizer $\pi$, so $v(xy) = v(x) + v(y)$, $v(x+y) \geq \min\{v(x), v(y)\}$ with *equality when $v(x) \neq v(y)$*, and $x \mid y \iff v(x) \leq v(y)$. Divisibility, units, and ideal containment all collapse to comparing integers. *Trigger:* any multiplicative or divisibility question inside a DVR. *Pattern:* "replace $x$ by $v(x) \in \mathbb{Z}$ and compute".

2. **Localize at a prime to reach a DVR, then contract.** A question about an ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ in a Dedekind domain near a prime $\mathfrak{p}$ becomes a question about $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}$ in the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$, where it is trivial; the answer is pulled back because $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals are [[Thm - Localization of a Dedekind Domain is a DVR|contracted from A_𝔭]]. *Trigger:* "is $\mathfrak{a}$ a power of $\mathfrak{p}$?", "what is the exponent of $\mathfrak{p}$ in $\mathfrak{a}$?". *Pattern:* "$e_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}) = v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p})$, computed in the DVR".

3. **Manufacture a uniformizer from integral-closedness.** To show $\mathfrak{m}$ is principal in a Noetherian local domain of dimension $1$, find $x = b/a \in \operatorname{Frac}(A)$ with $x^{-1}\mathfrak{m} \subseteq A$ (an ideal) but $x^{-1}\mathfrak{m} \not\subseteq \mathfrak{m}$ (escapes the maximal ideal, so equals $A$); then $\mathfrak{m} = (x)$. The escape uses $x^{-1} \notin A$, i.e. $x^{-1}$ not integral, which integral-closedness guarantees. *Trigger:* proving condition (3) of the DVR characterization from condition (2). *Pattern:* "take $a \in \mathfrak{m}$, $\mathfrak{m}^t \subseteq Aa \not\supseteq \mathfrak{m}^{t-1}$, pick $b \in \mathfrak{m}^{t-1}\setminus Aa$".

4. **Trade primary decomposition for a product via coprimality.** In a Dedekind domain distinct nonzero primes are *maximal and pairwise coprime*, so $\mathfrak{p}_1^{e_1} \cap \cdots \cap \mathfrak{p}_n^{e_n} = \mathfrak{p}_1^{e_1}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_n^{e_n}$ by the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules|CRT]]: a minimal primary decomposition *is* a prime factorization. *Trigger:* you have $\mathfrak{a}$ written as an intersection of primary ideals. *Pattern:* "coprime $\Rightarrow$ intersection $=$ product".

5. **Invert a fractional ideal.** For a fractional ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of a Dedekind domain, $\mathfrak{a}^{-1} = (A : \mathfrak{a}) = \{x \in K : x\mathfrak{a} \subseteq A\}$ satisfies $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{a}^{-1} = A$; in factored form $(\prod \mathfrak{p}^{n_\mathfrak{p}})^{-1} = \prod \mathfrak{p}^{-n_\mathfrak{p}}$. *Trigger:* you need to cancel an ideal, solve $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{x} = \mathfrak{b}$, or pass to the group $\mathcal{I}(A)$. *Pattern:* "multiply both sides by $\mathfrak{a}^{-1}$, just like dividing".

6. **Translate "contains" into "divides".** For ideals of a Dedekind domain, $\mathfrak{a} \supseteq \mathfrak{b} \iff \mathfrak{a} \mid \mathfrak{b}$ (i.e. $\mathfrak{b} = \mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{c}$ for an integral ideal $\mathfrak{c}$), because in each $A_\mathfrak{p}$ containment is $v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a}) \leq v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{b})$. *Trigger:* a containment of ideals you want to make multiplicative. *Pattern:* "to contain is to divide; the bigger ideal is the smaller divisor".

7. **Detect a uniformizer via $\mathfrak{m} \neq \mathfrak{m}^2$.** In a DVR $\mathfrak{m} \supsetneq \mathfrak{m}^2$ strictly (Nakayama), so any $\pi \in \mathfrak{m}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^2$ generates $\mathfrak{m}$; this is how (4) $\Rightarrow$ (5) produces $\pi$. *Trigger:* condition (4) holds and you want an explicit uniformizer. *Pattern:* "pick $\pi \in \mathfrak{m}\setminus\mathfrak{m}^2$; then $(\pi) = \mathfrak{m}$".

8. **Certify Dedekind by the three axioms.** To prove a domain is Dedekind, show separately: Noetherian (Hilbert basis or finite module), integrally closed (transitivity of integrality, or "UFD $\Rightarrow$ integrally closed"), and dimension $1$ (integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ or a PID). *Trigger:* "show $A$ is a Dedekind domain". *Pattern:* "split into Noetherian $+$ normal $+$ $\dim 1$".

9. **Use the radical to find the power.** In a Noetherian ring every ideal contains a power of its radical: if $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}} = \mathfrak{m}$ then $\mathfrak{m}^t \subseteq \mathfrak{a}$ for some $t$. In a DVR this pins $\mathfrak{a}$ between consecutive powers of $\mathfrak{m}$. *Trigger:* you know $\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}}$ but not $\mathfrak{a}$. *Pattern:* "$\sqrt{\mathfrak{a}} = \mathfrak{m} \Rightarrow \mathfrak{m}^t \subseteq \mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{m}^s$, then localize to find the exact exponent".

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming elements factor uniquely because ideals do
> Unique factorization of *ideals* in a Dedekind domain is so clean that one is tempted to conclude the ring is a UFD. It is not, in general: $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ has unique ideal factorization yet $6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ shows elements do not factor uniquely. The gap is precisely the **ideal class group**: a Dedekind domain is a UFD if and only if $\operatorname{Cl}(A)$ is trivial, which for a Dedekind domain is the same as being a PID. The repair condition is therefore "$\operatorname{Cl}(A) = 0$" — and the failure is exactly that the non-principal ideal $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ has order $2$ in the class group. Element factorization being non-unique is the *shadow* of ideal factorization being unique but ideals being non-principal.

> [!warning] 2. Treating every one-dimensional Noetherian local domain as a DVR
> Dimension $1$, Noetherian, and local feel like enough to be a DVR, since those are three of the hypotheses. The missing fourth — **integral closure** — is not automatic and is exactly what can fail. The cusp ring $A = k[t^2, t^3]_{(t^2, t^3)}$ is a Noetherian local domain of dimension $1$ that is *not* a DVR: it is not integrally closed (its normalization is $k[t]_{(t)}$, a DVR), and correspondingly $\dim_k \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 2$, not $1$, so $\mathfrak{m}$ is not principal. The operation becomes legal exactly when you add "integrally closed" (equivalently $\dim_k \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 1$). The geometric content: the local ring at a *singular* point of a curve is one-dimensional but not a DVR.

> [!warning] 3. Inverting a fractional ideal in a non-Dedekind domain
> Because fractional ideals of any domain can be multiplied, it is tempting to assume $\mathfrak{a}^{-1} = (A : \mathfrak{a})$ always satisfies $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{a}^{-1} = A$. This is exactly the Dedekind property and *fails* in general. In $A = k[x,y]$ (dimension $2$, so not Dedekind) the maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m} = (x,y)$ has $(A : \mathfrak{m}) = A$, so $\mathfrak{m}\cdot(A:\mathfrak{m}) = \mathfrak{m} \neq A$ — the ideal is not invertible. The operation becomes legal precisely when $A$ is a Dedekind domain, which is the content of "[[Def - Fractional Ideal and the Ideal Class Group|fractional ideals form a group]]": invertibility of *every* nonzero fractional ideal is equivalent to the Dedekind condition. So invertibility is not a free lunch; it is the defining miracle.

> [!warning] 4. Believing the valuation is extra data beyond the ring
> Since a DVR is defined as the valuation ring of some $v$, one expects the valuation to be a choice attached to the ring. In fact $v$ is *uniquely determined by $A$*: $v(x) = n$ iff $(x) = \mathfrak{m}^n$, so the valuation is recovered from the ideal structure alone, and any two discrete valuations with the same valuation ring on the same field are equal. The repair is to notice there is nothing to repair — the data is redundant. This is why one may speak of "the" valuation of a DVR, and why $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ knows $v_p$ without being told.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The first decision in any problem of this chapter is to locate yourself on the local–global axis, because every tool is one of two kinds: a *local* tool that works inside a DVR, or a *global* tool that assembles local answers over all primes.

If the problem **asks you to recognize or build a DVR**, you are working locally, and the move is to pick the cheapest of the five equivalent conditions in the [[Thm - Characterization of Discrete Valuation Rings|characterization theorem]]. If you are handed a valuation, condition (1) is immediate. If you are handed a local ring and want to *prove* it is a DVR, the usual route is to exhibit a principal maximal ideal (condition 3) by finding a uniformizer, or — when the ring sits inside a known field of fractions — to verify integral-closedness (condition 2) and let the theorem do the rest. The one genuinely creative step in the entire chapter lives here: the implication (2) $\Rightarrow$ (3), where integral-closedness is converted into a uniformizer by choosing $x = b/a$ with $b \in \mathfrak{m}^{t-1}\setminus Aa$, $t$ minimal with $\mathfrak{m}^t \subseteq Aa$. If instead you want the *smoothness* face, check $\dim_k \mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 1$; this is often the fastest in geometric examples, where $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ is a concrete vector space you can compute a basis for.

If the problem **asks you to factor an ideal or compute an exponent**, you are using the global-to-local-to-global cycle, and the strategy is fixed. Step one: localize at the relevant prime $\mathfrak{p}$, passing to the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$ via [[Thm - Localization of a Dedekind Domain is a DVR|the localization theorem]]. Step two: in the DVR, the ideal $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p}$ is just $(\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p})^{e}$ for $e = v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a})$ — read off the exponent by valuation arithmetic. Step three: contract back, using that $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals are contracted from $A_\mathfrak{p}$, so $\mathfrak{p}^e = (\mathfrak{p}^e A_\mathfrak{p})^c$ and the local exponent is the global one. Doing this for every prime in the support assembles the factorization $\mathfrak{a} = \prod \mathfrak{p}^{e_\mathfrak{p}}$. The trap to avoid is trying to factor directly in $A$; the whole point of Dedekind theory is that you should *never* factor in $A$, only in its DVR localizations where factoring is trivial.

If the problem **asks about fractional ideals, invertibility, or the class group**, you are in the group $\mathcal{I}(A)$, and the strategy is to think of ideals as integer-vectors of exponents. By unique factorization, $\mathcal{I}(A) \cong \bigoplus_\mathfrak{p} \mathbb{Z}$ is the free abelian group on the primes, with multiplication adding exponent-vectors and inversion negating them. Principal ideals $\mathcal{P}(A)$ are the image of $K^\times$, i.e. the divisors of functions. So a class-group question is a question about the cokernel $\operatorname{Cl}(A) = \mathcal{I}(A)/\mathcal{P}(A)$: to show a class is trivial, exhibit a generator (find $x$ with $\mathfrak{a} = (x)$); to show it is nontrivial, prove no such generator exists, usually by a norm argument (in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, the norm $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$ never equals $2$ or $3$, so $(2,1+\sqrt{-5})$ is not principal). The class group is finite for a ring of integers, and bounding it is the entry to genuine number theory.

The meta-strategy that threads all of this: **every question in this chapter is the question "what does this ring look like one prime at a time, and how do the answers glue?"** A DVR is the answer at a single smooth point; a Dedekind domain is a coherent family of such answers over a whole curve; unique factorization is the statement that the gluing is lossless; and the class group is the precise measure of what global information the local answers fail to capture. Decide whether you are computing at one point (use the valuation) or assembling over all points (use unique factorization and the class group), and the right tool is determined.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Characterization of Discrete Valuation Rings|The DVR characterization]]**: for a Noetherian local domain of dimension $1$, "DVR $=$ integrally closed $=$ principal $\mathfrak{m}$ $=$ ideals are powers of $\mathfrak{m}$ $=$ $\dim_k\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 1$". This is the most reused fact because it gives five interchangeable handles on the same object, and almost every local computation picks whichever is convenient: the principal-$\mathfrak{m}$ form for explicit arithmetic, integral-closedness for recognizing a DVR inside a Dedekind domain, the cotangent form for smoothness checks. Reach for it whenever a one-dimensional local domain appears and you need to know whether it is "as nice as possible".

- **[[Thm - Localization of a Dedekind Domain is a DVR|Localization gives a DVR]]**: $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a DVR for each nonzero prime of a Dedekind domain. Its reusability is as a *reduction principle*: it converts any local ideal question in a Dedekind domain into integer arithmetic in a DVR, with $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals contracting cleanly back. This is the technical engine behind unique factorization and behind every exponent computation; whenever a prime is named in a Dedekind domain, this is the first move.

- **[[Thm - A Dedekind Domain has Unique Factorization of Ideals|Unique factorization of ideals]]**: every nonzero ideal is uniquely $\prod \mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i}$. Its reusability is that it turns the multiplicative monoid of ideals into a *free* structure — ideals become exponent-vectors, "contains" becomes "divides", and gcd/lcm become min/max of exponents. This is what makes the fractional-ideal group, the class group, and "to contain is to divide" all available, and it is the substitute for element factorization throughout number theory.

- **[[Thm - The Ring of Integers of a Number Field is Dedekind|𝒪_K is Dedekind]]**: the ring of integers of any number field is a Dedekind domain. Its reusability is that it is the *gateway hypothesis*: the instant you recognize a ring of integers, the entire factorization toolkit switches on. It is why algebraic number theory is the study of Dedekind domains, and it is the source of all the standard worked examples ($\mathbb{Z}[i]$, $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_n]$).

- **The fractional-ideal group $\mathcal{I}(A) \cong \bigoplus_\mathfrak{p}\mathbb{Z}$ and the class group $\operatorname{Cl}(A)$**: nonzero fractional ideals form a free abelian group on the primes, and $\operatorname{Cl}(A) = \mathcal{I}(A)/\mathcal{P}(A)$ measures failure of principality. Their reusability is conceptual *and* computational: any cancellation argument among ideals ("$\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{c} = \mathfrak{b}\mathfrak{c} \Rightarrow \mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{b}$") is just subtraction in a free group, and any question of unique factorization of *elements* reduces to triviality of the class group.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic geometry — a Dedekind domain is a smooth affine curve, a DVR is the local ring at a smooth point, and the valuation is order of vanishing.** To a one-dimensional domain $A$ one attaches the curve $\operatorname{Spec} A$, whose closed points are the nonzero primes and whose generic point is $(0)$. The condition "integrally closed", which in dimension one is the same as **normal**, is the algebraic meaning of *smooth*: at a smooth point the local ring $A_\mathfrak{p}$ is a DVR, its maximal ideal is principal (generated by a **local coordinate** $\pi$), and the valuation $v_\mathfrak{p}(f)$ is the **order of vanishing** of the function $f$ at the point — positive for a zero, negative for a pole. A singular point, like the cusp of $y^2 = x^3$, has a non-DVR local ring with $\dim_k\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 = 2$; taking the integral closure **normalizes** the curve, smoothing the singularity and replacing the bad local ring by a DVR. So this chapter is the local theory of curves: the DVR is the infinitesimal model of a smooth point, and "Dedekind" is "smooth everywhere".

2. **Algebraic number theory — $\mathcal{O}_K$ is the Dedekind domain where Kummer's ideal numbers live, and factorization of $(p)$ is splitting and ramification.** The ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of a number field is Dedekind by [[Thm - The Ring of Integers of a Number Field is Dedekind|the integrality argument]], and unique factorization of ideals is the rigorous form of Kummer's "ideal numbers" — the fictitious factors invented to restore unique factorization to rings like $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ where elements factor ambiguously. Extending a rational prime, $(p)\mathcal{O}_K = \prod \mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i}$, records its arithmetic: the **ramification index** $e_i$, the **residue degree** $f_i = [\mathcal{O}_K/\mathfrak{p}_i : \mathbb{F}_p]$, and the identity $\sum e_i f_i = [K:\mathbb{Q}]$. The prime $2$ ramifying in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ as $\mathfrak{p}^2$ is the simplest instance, and the whole apparatus of class field theory is built on the factorization theorem proved here.

3. **Module theory and the local–global principle — invertible ideals are rank-one projective modules, and the class group is the simplest nontrivial $\operatorname{Pic}$.** A fractional ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ of a Dedekind domain is, as an $A$-module, **finitely generated, torsion-free, and locally free of rank one**: at each prime $\mathfrak{a}A_\mathfrak{p} = (\pi^{n})A_\mathfrak{p} \cong A_\mathfrak{p}$ is free, yet globally $\mathfrak{a}$ need not be free — it is free iff principal. This is exactly the phenomenon [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization|Localization]] flagged: *freeness is not a local property*, and the non-free locally-free ideal $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ is the witness. Such a module is a **projective module** of rank one, and the set of isomorphism classes is the **Picard group** $\operatorname{Pic}(A) = \operatorname{Cl}(A)$ — under the **Serre–Swan** dictionary, a rank-one projective module is a line bundle. The class group is therefore the first nonzero instance of the gap between locally free and free, and number theory's class number is a count of line bundles.

4. **The dimension and integrality chapters — Dedekind is the bottom rung of the regularity ladder, where normal already means smooth.** A Dedekind domain is exactly a Noetherian normal ([[Def - Integral Closure and Normal Domain|integrally closed]]) domain of [[Def - Krull Dimension and Height|Krull dimension]] $1$. In higher dimension, "normal" and "smooth" diverge — a normal surface can be singular in codimension $2$ — but in dimension $1$ they coincide, which is *why* the one-dimensional theory is so clean and why DVRs admit five equivalent characterizations. The DVR is also the local ring whose [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma|cotangent space]] $\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2$ has dimension equal to the Krull dimension, the defining condition of a **regular local ring**; DVRs are precisely the one-dimensional regular local rings, the seed of the general theory of regularity that [[Commutative Algebra XII — Dimension Theory|Dimension Theory]] opens.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a DVR is the integers near one prime, and everything else is bookkeeping.** Every object in this chapter is built from the single template $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ — the integers with one prime kept and all others inverted. In that ring there is one prime to count against, one valuation $v_p$ measuring divisibility by it, and one uniformizer $p$ such that every element is a unit times a power of $p$. A general DVR is this template with $p$ renamed $\pi$ and $\mathbb{Z}$ replaced by an abstract local ring; a Dedekind domain is a whole *family* of these templates, one per prime, sewn together so that an element's behaviour at each prime is governed by the corresponding DVR. Unique factorization of ideals is the statement that the sewing loses no information: knowing the valuation $v_\mathfrak{p}(\mathfrak{a})$ at every prime determines $\mathfrak{a}$ completely. And the class group is the precise statement of what *global* information — which combinations of local exponents come from an actual element — the local pictures fail to determine on their own. The slogan to carry: **a Dedekind domain is a curve's worth of copies of $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$, and the class group counts the ways the local copies refuse to glue into global principal ideals.**

**The true name of integral-closedness in dimension one is smoothness, and it is what makes the valuation well-defined.** One usually meets "integrally closed" as a technical condition — the ring contains all elements of its fraction field satisfying a monic polynomial over it. The [[Thm - Characterization of Discrete Valuation Rings|DVR characterization]] reveals its operational meaning: in a one-dimensional local domain, integral-closedness is *exactly* the condition that the maximal ideal is principal, i.e. that there is a single uniformizer measuring everything, i.e. that the point is **smooth**. The reason a singular point fails to be a DVR is that it has *too many* directions in its cotangent space ($\dim_k\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^2 > 1$), so no single element can serve as a coordinate, and the order-of-vanishing function becomes ill-defined — at a node, a function can vanish "to order $1$ along one branch and order $2$ along the other". Integral closure repairs this by separating the branches (normalization), restoring a single well-defined valuation per point. So "integrally closed" is the algebraic certificate that order-of-vanishing makes sense, which is why it is the load-bearing hypothesis in both the DVR characterization and the definition of a Dedekind domain.

**A trigger-reaction pattern worth burning in: "factor an ideal" means "localize at each prime, count in the DVR, reassemble".** When a problem asks for the prime factorization of an ideal, the exponent of a given prime, or whether one ideal divides another, the reflex should fire automatically — *do not factor in $A$*; instead pass to the DVR $A_\mathfrak{p}$ for each prime in sight, where the ideal becomes a power of the maximal ideal and its exponent is just an integer read off the valuation, then contract back using that $\mathfrak{p}$-primary ideals come from $A_\mathfrak{p}$. The complementary reflex is for the class group: when asked whether a ring is a PID or a UFD, the reaction is "compute $\operatorname{Cl}(A)$", and to show it nontrivial, produce a non-principal ideal and prove non-principality by a *norm* obstruction — the norm is multiplicative, so a principal ideal $(x)$ has norm $N(x)$, and an ideal whose norm is not a value of the norm form cannot be principal. These two reflexes — *localize to a DVR to factor*, and *use a norm to obstruct principality* — between them solve the large majority of the chapter's computational problems, and both are direct consequences of the single fact that a Dedekind domain is locally, at each prime, a discrete valuation ring.
