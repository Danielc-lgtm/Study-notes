# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Let $R$ be a ring, $M$ a [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] $R$-module with generators $m_1,\dots,m_n$, and $f : M \to M$ an $R$-linear map (so $f \in \operatorname{End}_R(M) = \operatorname{Hom}_R(M,M)$, the noncommutative $R$-algebra of endomorphisms, with $f^k$ the $k$-fold composite and $f^0 = \operatorname{id}_M$). Let $\mathfrak a \trianglelefteq R$ be an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]], and $\mathfrak a M = \{\sum_i a_i m_i : a_i \in \mathfrak a,\ m_i \in M\}$ the [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] generated by products. We write $\mathbf m = (m_1,\dots,m_n)^{\mathsf T}$ for the column of generators, $I_n$ for the identity matrix, $\operatorname{adj}(Q)$ for the **adjugate** (classical adjoint) of $Q$ — the matrix of signed cofactors, satisfying $\operatorname{adj}(Q)\,Q = (\det Q) I_n$ over any commutative ring — and $T$ for a formal indeterminate, used to make $M$ an [[Def - Polynomial Ring|$R[T]$]]-module. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Cayley–Hamilton for modules, the determinant trick).** Let $M$ be a finitely generated $R$-module, $f : M \to M$ an $R$-linear endomorphism, and $\mathfrak a \trianglelefteq R$ an ideal with
> $$f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M.$$
> Then there exist $n \geq 1$ and elements $a_1, \dots, a_n \in \mathfrak a$ such that, as an identity in $\operatorname{End}_R(M)$,
> $$f^n + a_1 f^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{n-1} f + a_n \operatorname{id}_M = 0.$$
> That is, $f$ satisfies a **monic** polynomial of degree $n$ whose lower coefficients all lie in $\mathfrak a$, where $n$ may be taken to be the number of generators of $M$.

The case $\mathfrak a = R$ recovers the classical statement that an endomorphism of a module generated by $n$ elements satisfies a monic degree-$n$ polynomial over $R$. The power of the theorem is the refinement that the lower coefficients lie in the *prescribed* ideal $\mathfrak a$ whenever $f$ compresses $M$ into $\mathfrak a M$.

---

# Motivation

The classical Cayley–Hamilton theorem — a square matrix over a field satisfies its own characteristic polynomial — is usually proved by an argument that feels like a coincidence of linear algebra. The content of this chapter's version is that the theorem is not about fields at all, and that the *refinement* "the coefficients lie in $\mathfrak a$" is where the real power lives. It is the single engine from which all of Nakayama's lemma is built, and it is reused, almost verbatim, to prove the foundational theorem of integral extensions ("integral over $R$" $=$ "lives in a finitely generated $R$-module").

The question it answers is: *given that a linear map $f$ shrinks a finitely generated module into an ideal multiple of itself, how rigidly is $f$ constrained?* Intuitively, if $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$, then $f$ is "small" — its image is buried inside the ideal — and you expect this smallness to be expressible as an algebraic relation. The theorem makes this precise and quantitative: $f$ satisfies a monic polynomial of controlled degree, and the smallness is visible in the coefficients all lying in $\mathfrak a$. When you specialise $f = \operatorname{id}$ and $\mathfrak a M = M$ (so the hypothesis $f(M) = M \subseteq \mathfrak a M$ holds), the relation becomes $(1 + a_1 + \dots + a_n)\operatorname{id} = 0$ — an element of $\mathfrak a$ shifted by $1$ annihilates the whole module, which is exactly the input Nakayama needs.

The reason it deserves a dedicated page, rather than being folded into Nakayama, is its reusability. The same matrix manipulation, with $f$ chosen to be *multiplication by an element* $x$ of a larger ring, proves that an element satisfying $xM \subseteq M$ for a faithful finitely generated module $M$ is **integral** — this is the gateway to [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions|integral extensions]], normalisation, and the geometry of finite morphisms. So the determinant trick is not a lemma in service of one theorem; it is a load-bearing structural fact about finitely generated modules that the rest of commutative algebra leans on repeatedly.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The theorem requires the precondition $A$: *$M$ finitely generated and $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$.* Recognising when this holds is the skill.

The first disguised source is **$\mathfrak a M = M$** — the Nakayama hypothesis. The property $B$ is "$\mathfrak a$ fills $M$", and the bridge to $A$ is to take $f = \operatorname{id}_M$: then $f(M) = M = \mathfrak a M \subseteq \mathfrak a M$, so $A$ holds with this $\mathfrak a$. The non-obvious part is choosing $f$ to be the *identity*, which seems to throw away the generality of "any $f$" — but it is exactly the specialisation that turns the polynomial relation into "$1 - a$ annihilates $M$". *Example problem:* prove $M = 0$ when $\mathfrak a M = M$ and $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ — this is [[Thm - Nakayama's Lemma]].

The second disguised source is **a surjective endomorphism $\varphi : M \to M$**, viewed through the polynomial ring. The property $B$ is "$\varphi$ is onto", and the bridge is to make $M$ an $R[T]$-module with $T$ acting as $\varphi$: surjectivity says $\varphi(M) = M$, i.e. $T \cdot M = M$, i.e. $(T)M = M = (T)M$, so $A$ holds over $R[T]$ with the ideal $\mathfrak a = (T)$ and $f = \operatorname{id}$ (or directly with $f = \varphi$, $\mathfrak a = (T)$ contracted). The non-obviousness: an endomorphism with *no* visible ideal becomes one with $f(M) \subseteq (T)M$ once you let $T$ act as $f$. *Example problem:* a surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module is injective — [[Ex - A surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module is injective]].

The third disguised source is **an element $x$ of an $R$-algebra $A$ with $xM \subseteq M$ for a finitely generated faithful $R$-module $M \subseteq A$.** The property $B$ is "multiplication by $x$ stabilises a finitely generated module", and the bridge is to take $f = (\text{multiplication by } x)$ and $\mathfrak a = R$: then $f(M) \subseteq M = R M$, so $A$ holds, and the resulting monic polynomial $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \dots + r_n = 0$ (read off via faithfulness) is exactly an **integral equation** for $x$. *Example problem:* a finite ring extension is integral — the foundational result of [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]].

The fourth disguised source is **a free module embedding $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$**, or a generating set of size $n$ for $R^n$. The property $B$ is "an inclusion or self-surjection of free modules", and the bridge writes the relevant map as a matrix and feeds it to the trick to control the rank without dimension. *Example problem:* $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m \Rightarrow n \leq m$ over any nonzero ring — [[Ex - Every generating set of size n for R^n is a basis]].

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion $C$ is the monic relation $f^n + a_1 f^{n-1} + \dots + a_n = 0$ with $a_i \in \mathfrak a$.

Combine $C$ with **$f = \operatorname{id}$ and $\mathfrak a M = M$**. The relation collapses to $(1 + a_1 + \dots + a_n)\operatorname{id}_M = 0$, i.e. $(1 - a)m = 0$ for all $m$ with $a = -(a_1+\dots+a_n) \in \mathfrak a$. The further result $E$ is that a *single* element $1 - a$ annihilates $M$ — the corollary that powers Nakayama. The combination is nonobvious because it extracts a degree-$1$ consequence from a degree-$n$ polynomial by specialising the variable.

Combine $C$ with **$\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$**. Then the annihilator $1 - a$ from the previous combination is a *unit*, by [[Thm - The Jacobson Radical via Units]], so $M = 0$. The further result $E$ is Nakayama's lemma itself. Nonobvious because it converts "an element annihilates $M$" into "$M = 0$" using the invertibility supplied by the Jacobson radical.

Combine $C$ with **faithfulness of $M$**. If $M$ is a faithful module and $f$ is multiplication by $x \in A \supseteq R$, then the relation $f^n + r_1 f^{n-1} + \dots + r_n = 0$ holds as an endomorphism, and faithfulness lets you erase $M$ and read it as a relation $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \dots + r_n = 0$ in $A$. The further result $E$ is an integral equation for $x$: $x$ is integral over $R$. Nonobvious because the endomorphism relation lives in $\operatorname{End}_R(M)$ and faithfulness is exactly what transports it back to $A$.

# Why Is It True

Forget the formalism for a moment and think about what "$f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$" buys you. It says: when you apply $f$ to a generator $m_i$, the result is an $\mathfrak a$-combination of the generators, $f(m_i) = \sum_j P_{ij} m_j$ with all $P_{ij} \in \mathfrak a$. So $f$, restricted to its action on the generators, *is a matrix $P$ with entries in $\mathfrak a$*. The finitely generated hypothesis is what makes this matrix finite. Now you have a system of linear equations: the generators $m_i$ satisfy $\sum_j (T \delta_{ij} - P_{ij}) m_j = 0$ where "$T$" stands for the operator $f$. This is a homogeneous linear system $(TI - P)\mathbf m = 0$ over the ring $R[T]$ (with $T$ acting as $f$ on $M$), and it has a *nonzero* solution, namely $\mathbf m$ itself — the generators are not all zero (assuming $M \neq 0$).

When a homogeneous system has a nonzero solution, classical linear algebra says the determinant of the coefficient matrix vanishes. That intuition is *almost* right and is the heart of the matter, but it needs care over a ring where you cannot divide. The fix is the adjugate: rather than "determinant zero", you multiply the equation $(TI - P)\mathbf m = 0$ on the left by $\operatorname{adj}(TI - P)$ and use the identity $\operatorname{adj}(Q)Q = (\det Q)I$, valid over *any* commutative ring with no division required. This turns the system into $(\det(TI - P))\,m_i = 0$ for every generator, hence $\det(TI - P)$ annihilates all of $M$. And $\det(TI - P)$ is the characteristic polynomial of $P$ — monic of degree $n$, with its lower coefficients built from the entries of $P$, which all live in $\mathfrak a$. Substituting back $T = f$ gives the stated relation.

**The one-line mechanism: $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$ makes $f$ a matrix over $\mathfrak a$, and the adjugate identity $\operatorname{adj}(Q)Q = (\det Q)I$ converts "$f$ annihilates the generators" into "$\det(TI - f)$ annihilates the module" without ever dividing.** Everything else is bookkeeping: the coefficients lie in $\mathfrak a$ because they are sums of products of entries of $P$, and each entry is in $\mathfrak a$.

---

# What Makes This Hard

The non-obvious step is recognising that the equation $(TI - P)\mathbf m = 0$ should be hit with the *adjugate*, not solved by inverting $TI - P$ — over $R[T]$ you cannot invert, and the whole point is that the adjugate identity sidesteps division. Most people get stuck trying to import the field-theoretic "determinant zero" argument literally, or worrying that $T = f$ is "not really an element of a ring". The fix to the latter is the clean move of making $M$ a genuine $R[T]$-module with $T$ acting as $f$, so that $TI - P$ is an honest matrix over the commutative ring $R[T]$ and the adjugate identity applies on the nose. The most common error is to forget that the coefficients must be tracked into $\mathfrak a$ — the *whole refinement* — and to prove only the bare monic relation.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:**
Pick generators, express $f$ on them as a matrix $P$ with entries in $\mathfrak a$, package the relations as $(TI - P)\mathbf m = 0$ over $R[T]$ with $T$ acting as $f$, multiply by the adjugate to get $\det(TI - P)$ annihilating $M$, and read off the monic characteristic polynomial whose lower coefficients lie in $\mathfrak a$.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Turn $f$ into a matrix over $\mathfrak a$.** Show $f(m_i) = \sum_j P_{ij} m_j$ with $P_{ij} \in \mathfrak a$.
   - *Hint:* $f(m_i) \in f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$, and every element of $\mathfrak a M$ is an $\mathfrak a$-combination of the generators.
   - *Why needed:* It produces the finite matrix $P$ that the determinant acts on; without finite generation there is no matrix.

2. **Package as a linear system over $R[T]$.** Make $M$ an $R[T]$-module with $T \cdot m := f(m)$, and rewrite step 1 as $(TI_n - P)\mathbf m = 0$.
   - *Hint:* $T m_i = f(m_i) = \sum_j P_{ij} m_j$, i.e. $\sum_j (T\delta_{ij} - P_{ij}) m_j = 0$.
   - *Why needed:* It places the relation inside a commutative ring $R[T]$ where the adjugate identity is available.

3. **Apply the adjugate.** Left-multiply by $\operatorname{adj}(TI_n - P)$ and use $\operatorname{adj}(Q)Q = (\det Q)I$ to get $\det(TI_n - P)\,m_i = 0$ for all $i$.
   - *Hint:* $\operatorname{adj}(Q)\,(Q\mathbf m) = (\det Q)\,\mathbf m$, and $Q\mathbf m = 0$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the division-free substitute for "determinant zero" and is what makes the argument work over a ring.

4. **Read off the polynomial.** Since the $m_i$ generate, $\det(TI_n - P)$ annihilates all of $M$; expand it as $T^n + a_1 T^{n-1} + \dots + a_n$ with $a_i \in \mathfrak a$, and substitute $T = f$.
   - *Hint:* The coefficient of $T^{n-k}$ is $(-1)^k$ times the sum of $k \times k$ principal minors of $P$, a sum of products of entries of $P$, all in $\mathfrak a$.
   - *Why needed:* It delivers the monic relation with coefficients controlled in $\mathfrak a$ — the full statement.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: $f$ acts as a matrix with entries in $\mathfrak a$
> **Statement:** If $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_n$ and $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$, there is a matrix $P = (P_{ij}) \in M_n(R)$ with every $P_{ij} \in \mathfrak a$ and $f(m_i) = \sum_{j=1}^n P_{ij} m_j$ for each $i$.
>
> **Hint:** Each $f(m_i)$ lies in $\mathfrak a M$, which by definition consists of finite sums $\sum a_j x_j$ with $a_j \in \mathfrak a$; expand each $x_j$ in the generators.
>
> **Why needed:** It converts the abstract endomorphism $f$ into a concrete finite matrix over $\mathfrak a$ — the object the determinant is taken of. This is the only place finite generation is used.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Fix a finite generating set $m_1,\dots,m_n$ of $M$. For each $i$, $f(m_i) \in f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$. By definition of $\mathfrak a M$, $f(m_i) = \sum_{k} a_k x_k$ for finitely many $a_k \in \mathfrak a$ and $x_k \in M$. Writing each $x_k = \sum_j r_{kj} m_j$ in the generators ($r_{kj} \in R$) and collecting, $f(m_i) = \sum_j \big(\sum_k a_k r_{kj}\big) m_j = \sum_j P_{ij} m_j$ where $P_{ij} = \sum_k a_k r_{kj}$. Since $\mathfrak a$ is an ideal, each $a_k r_{kj} \in \mathfrak a$ and so $P_{ij} \in \mathfrak a$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The adjugate identity holds over any commutative ring
> **Statement:** For any $Q \in M_n(S)$ over a commutative ring $S$, $\operatorname{adj}(Q)\,Q = Q\,\operatorname{adj}(Q) = (\det Q)\,I_n$.
>
> **Hint:** Cofactor expansion of $\det Q$ along a row, and the fact that expanding along the "wrong" row gives $0$ (it computes the determinant of a matrix with a repeated row).
>
> **Why needed:** It is the division-free replacement for "if $Q\mathbf v = 0$ with $\mathbf v \neq 0$ then $\det Q = 0$". Over a ring we cannot invert $Q$; the adjugate is what lets us extract $\det Q$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $\operatorname{adj}(Q)_{ij} = C_{ji}$ be the $(j,i)$ cofactor of $Q$, i.e. $(-1)^{i+j}$ times the $(j,i)$ minor. The $(i,k)$ entry of $\operatorname{adj}(Q)\,Q$ is $\sum_j C_{ji} Q_{jk} = \sum_j (-1)^{i+j}(\text{minor}_{ji})Q_{jk}$. For $k = i$ this is the cofactor expansion of $\det Q$ along column $i$, giving $\det Q$. For $k \neq i$ it is the cofactor expansion of the determinant of the matrix obtained from $Q$ by replacing column $i$ with column $k$ — a matrix with two equal columns, whose determinant is $0$. Hence $\operatorname{adj}(Q)Q = (\det Q)I_n$. These are polynomial identities in the entries of $Q$ with integer coefficients, so they hold over every commutative ring (they hold over $\mathbb Z[Q_{ij}]$ and specialise). The identity $Q\operatorname{adj}(Q) = (\det Q)I_n$ is symmetric.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: A monic characteristic polynomial with coefficients in $\mathfrak a$
> **Statement:** $\det(T I_n - P) = T^n + a_1 T^{n-1} + \dots + a_n$ is monic of degree $n$ with each $a_i \in \mathfrak a$, where $P$ has entries in $\mathfrak a$.
>
> **Hint:** Expand the determinant; the coefficient of $T^{n-k}$ is $\pm$ the sum of $k\times k$ principal minors of $P$, each a sum of products of $k$ entries of $P$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is what tracks the smallness of $f$ (image in $\mathfrak a M$) into the smallness of the coefficients (in $\mathfrak a$) — the refinement that distinguishes this from the bare classical statement.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > The determinant $\det(TI_n - P)$ is a sum over permutations $\sigma$ of $\prod_i (T\delta_{i\sigma(i)} - P_{i\sigma(i)})$. The identity permutation contributes $\prod_i (T - P_{ii}) = T^n + (\text{lower order})$, giving the monic leading term $T^n$. Every monomial of degree $< n$ in $T$ arises by selecting, in at least one factor, the $-P_{i\sigma(i)}$ term rather than $T$; thus the coefficient of $T^{n-k}$ ($k \geq 1$) is an $R$-linear combination of products of $k$ entries $P_{i\sigma(i)} \in \mathfrak a$. Since $\mathfrak a$ is an ideal, any such product (indeed any product involving at least one factor in $\mathfrak a$) lies in $\mathfrak a$, so each $a_k \in \mathfrak a$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_n$ be finitely generated, $f : M \to M$ $R$-linear with $f(M) \subseteq \mathfrak a M$.
>
> **Step 0 — the $R[T]$-module structure.** Make $M$ a module over the polynomial ring $R[T]$ by letting $T$ act as $f$: for $p(T) = \sum_k c_k T^k \in R[T]$ and $m \in M$, set $p(T)\cdot m = \sum_k c_k f^k(m)$. This is well-defined because any choice of image for $T$ extends uniquely to an $R$-algebra homomorphism $R[T] \to \operatorname{End}_R(M)$ (the polynomial ring is free on one generator); commutativity is not an issue with a single variable. So $TI_n - P \in M_n(R[T])$ is a matrix over a commutative ring.
>
> **Step 1 (Lemma 1).** Since $f(m_i) \in \mathfrak a M$ and the $m_j$ generate $M$, there is $P = (P_{ij}) \in M_n(R)$ with $P_{ij} \in \mathfrak a$ and $f(m_i) = \sum_j P_{ij} m_j$. In the $R[T]$-module $M$ this reads $T m_i = \sum_j P_{ij} m_j$, i.e.
> $$\sum_{j=1}^n (T\delta_{ij} - P_{ij})\, m_j = 0 \qquad (i = 1,\dots,n),$$
> which is the matrix equation $(TI_n - P)\,\mathbf m = 0$ with $\mathbf m = (m_1,\dots,m_n)^{\mathsf T}$.
>
> **Step 2 (Lemma 2).** Set $Q = TI_n - P \in M_n(R[T])$. Multiply $Q\mathbf m = 0$ on the left by $\operatorname{adj}(Q)$ and apply $\operatorname{adj}(Q)Q = (\det Q)I_n$:
> $$(\det Q)\, m_i = 0 \qquad (i = 1,\dots,n).$$
> Since the $m_i$ generate $M$, the element $\det Q \in R[T]$ — acting through the $R[T]$-module structure — annihilates every element of $M$:
> $$\det(TI_n - P)\cdot m = 0 \quad \text{for all } m \in M.$$
>
> **Step 3 (Lemma 3).** Expand $\det(TI_n - P) = T^n + a_1 T^{n-1} + \dots + a_n$ with each $a_i \in \mathfrak a$. The action of $T^k$ is $f^k$, so the annihilation statement is, as an identity in $\operatorname{End}_R(M)$,
> $$f^n + a_1 f^{n-1} + \dots + a_{n-1} f + a_n \operatorname{id}_M = 0. \qquad \blacksquare$$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Integral elements are roots of monic polynomials (number theory / algebraic geometry).** Let $R \subseteq A$ be rings and $x \in A$ such that $x M \subseteq M$ for some finitely generated faithful $R$-submodule $M \subseteq A$. Apply the trick with $f$ equal to multiplication by $x$ and $\mathfrak a = R$: faithfulness lets you read the resulting relation $x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} + \dots + r_n = 0$ ($r_i \in R$) in $A$, proving $x$ is integral over $R$. The nonobvious recognition is that "stabilises a finitely generated faithful module" is the disguised precondition $A$ of the theorem — this is the whole content of [[Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions]].

**Eigenvalue containment for matrices over a ring (linear algebra).** For a matrix $P \in M_n(R)$ acting on $M = R^n$, the theorem with $f = P$, $\mathfrak a = R$ recovers classical Cayley–Hamilton: $P$ satisfies its characteristic polynomial $\det(TI - P)$. The refinement says that if every entry of $P$ lies in an ideal $\mathfrak a$ (a "small perturbation"), then so do the non-leading characteristic coefficients — a quantitative bound on how the spectrum can move, applied for instance to matrices over $\mathbb Z/p^k$ in coding theory and cryptography.

**Rank invariance of free modules (topology, via Serre–Swan).** Apply the trick to control a surjection or inclusion of free modules: it forces $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m \Rightarrow n \leq m$ over *any* nonzero commutative ring, with no dimension theory ([[Ex - Every generating set of size n for R^n is a basis]]). Under the Serre–Swan correspondence this is "a vector bundle has a well-defined rank", and the nonobvious point is that the rank invariant survives over rings with no fields in sight, supplied entirely by the determinant.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton (Minimal-Polynomial Form)|Classical Cayley–Hamilton (linear algebra)]]** — this is the specialisation $M = R^n$, with $f$ a matrix and $\mathfrak a = R$. The module statement strips away the field hypothesis: the same adjugate identity $\operatorname{adj}(Q)Q = (\det Q)I$ that proves the classical theorem over a field works verbatim over any commutative ring, because it is a polynomial identity. The module version's extra content is the ideal-tracking of coefficients, invisible in the field case where $\mathfrak a = R$.

- **[[Thm - Nakayama's Lemma|Nakayama's lemma]]** — the immediate corollary, obtained by taking $f = \operatorname{id}$ and $\mathfrak a M = M$. The determinant trick is the *proof* of Nakayama, not merely a tool used by it: specialising the variable to the identity collapses the degree-$n$ relation to $(1 - a)\operatorname{id} = 0$, and the Jacobson-radical hypothesis makes $1 - a$ invertible. Everything Nakayama does is downstream of this page.

- **[[Thm - Characterizations of Integrality (Module-Finite Criterion)|Module-finite criterion for integrality]]** — the theorem reused with $f$ = multiplication by a candidate integral element. The equivalence "integral $\iff$ contained in a finitely generated module" is the determinant trick in one direction (build the monic equation from the stabilised module) and an easy direction in reverse. This is how the determinant trick becomes the foundation of integral extensions and normalisation.

- **[[Def - Finitely Generated Module|Finite generation]]** — the precondition that makes the whole argument run: it bounds the size of the matrix $P$, hence the degree of the characteristic polynomial. Drop it and there is no finite matrix to take a determinant of, and the conclusion genuinely fails (the Prüfer module).

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Integral extensions and finite morphisms *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Applying the trick to multiplication-by-$x$ proves that an element stabilising a finitely generated faithful module is **integral**, the algebraic backbone of **finite morphisms** of varieties — maps whose fibres are finite. Integral closure becomes **normalisation**, the resolution of certain singularities, and the going-up/going-down theorems govern how primes (points) lie over each other. The theory is **Commutative Algebra VI — Integral Extensions** and **VIII — Going Up and Going Down**.

> [!tip] Krull's principal ideal theorem *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The determinant trick reappears in the proof of **Krull's height theorem** (the principal ideal theorem), the statement that a hypersurface drops dimension by exactly one — the cornerstone of **dimension theory**. The same "finitely generated module forced to satisfy a monic relation" mechanism controls the chains of primes. See **Commutative Algebra XII — Dimension Theory**.
