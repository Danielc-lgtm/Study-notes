# Problem Statement

Let $R$ be a **nonzero** commutative ring. Prove:

(i) Every generating set of cardinality $n$ for the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^n$ is a **basis**.

(ii) If $R^n$ embeds in $R^m$ as an $R$-module (i.e. there is an injective $R$-linear map $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$), then $n \leq m$.

*Hint (Becker, for (ii)).* If $n > m$, consider the map $R^m \to R^n$ sending $(x_1,\dots,x_m) \mapsto (x_1,\dots,x_m,0,\dots,0)$, and find a way to use Cayley–Hamilton.

(This is Becker Example Sheet 3, Q1(f) and Q1(g).)

**Recall:**

The objects in play are a nonzero ring, the free module $R^n$, generating sets and bases, and the determinant trick.

A [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^n$ has the standard basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$; a **basis** is a generating set that is also $R$-linearly independent (so every element has a *unique* expression as an $R$-combination). A generating set need not be a basis.

The key tool — the determinant trick, used to control surjective endomorphisms of free modules:

![[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)#Statement]]

A consequence proved en route is that a **surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module is injective** (see [[Ex - A surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module is injective]]); this is the engine of part (i). Part (ii) feeds the embedding into the same machine. The conclusion of (i) and (ii) together is the [[Thm - Invariance of Rank|invariance of rank]] for free modules over any nonzero commutative ring.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *rank-control* problem: establish that free modules have a well-defined rank — that $R^n$ cannot be generated by fewer than $n$ elements, and cannot embed in $R^m$ unless $n \leq m$ — over an arbitrary nonzero ring, *without* a notion of dimension. The [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic strategy]]'s third paragraph names the route: rank facts about free modules go through the determinant trick.

**Assumption pattern.** The recognisable trigger is "a surjection or embedding of free modules over a ring with no field". The only tool that controls rank without dimension is the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]], via its corollary "surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module is injective". For (i), a size-$n$ generating set of $R^n$ defines a *surjective endomorphism* $R^n \to R^n$. For (ii), an embedding $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$ with $n > m$ is padded to a surjective endomorphism that is forced to be injective, contradiction.

**Theorem routing.** *(i)* A generating set $v_1,\dots,v_n$ of $R^n$ defines $\varphi : R^n \to R^n$, $e_i \mapsto v_i$, which is surjective (the $v_i$ generate); by the surjective-endomorphism theorem it is injective, hence an isomorphism, so the $v_i = \varphi(e_i)$ are the image of a basis under an isomorphism — a basis. *(ii)* Given an injection $\iota : R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$ with $n > m$, compose with the padding injection $\pi : R^m \hookrightarrow R^n$ ($\pi(x_1,\dots,x_m) = (x_1,\dots,x_m,0,\dots,0)$); the composite $\pi\iota : R^n \to R^n$ is injective but its image lies in $R^m \times 0 \subsetneq R^n$, so it is *not surjective* — yet we will show a related surjection must be injective, forcing a contradiction.

**Key decision point.** For (i), the move is to *read a generating set as a surjective endomorphism* and invoke the determinant-trick corollary — the unexpected upgrade from "generates" to "is a basis" is exactly "surjective $\Rightarrow$ injective". For (ii), the genuine subtlety is the padding construction: to use the determinant trick you need a *self-map* of a single free module, so you must turn the cross-rank embedding $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$ into an endomorphism of $R^n$. The padding $R^m \hookrightarrow R^n$ and a careful choice of which composite to feed to Cayley–Hamilton is the crux; choosing it wrongly gives a map whose injectivity or surjectivity says nothing.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Read a generating set as a surjective endomorphism.** A size-$n$ generating set of $R^n$ is the image of the standard basis under a surjective $\varphi : R^n \to R^n$.

2. **Make $M$ an $R[T]$-module and run Cayley–Hamilton** (operations 3' and 3). The surjective-endomorphism corollary forces $\varphi$ injective, hence an isomorphism.

3. **Write the action as a matrix and apply the adjugate** (operations 1, 2). For part (ii), a square matrix over $R$ with a nontrivial kernel is forced, via the determinant, to have its determinant a zero-divisor — and padding makes that impossible over a nonzero ring.

4. **Invert via the determinant.** The determinant of the endomorphism is the obstruction; controlling it controls injectivity and surjectivity simultaneously.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> For (i): a generating set $v_1,\dots,v_n$ of $R^n$ is the *same data* as a map $\varphi : R^n \to R^n$ with $\varphi(e_i) = v_i$. "The $v_i$ generate" says $\varphi$ is surjective. You proved earlier that a surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module is injective. Apply it.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> So $\varphi$ is an isomorphism. The standard basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$ is a basis; the image of a basis under an isomorphism is a basis; and $\varphi(e_i) = v_i$. Conclude the $v_i$ form a basis.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For (ii): suppose $n > m$ and $\iota : R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$ is injective. Pad: let $\pi : R^m \to R^n$, $(x_1,\dots,x_m) \mapsto (x_1,\dots,x_m,0,\dots,0)$. Then $\psi := \pi \circ \iota : R^n \to R^n$ is injective (composite of injections). Look at $\psi$ as a matrix; its image misses the last $n - m$ coordinates.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> $\psi : R^n \to R^n$ is injective. Is it surjective? Its image lies in $R^m \times \{0\}^{n-m} \subsetneq R^n$ (since $n > m$), so it is *not* surjective. Now: an injective endomorphism of $R^n$ given by a matrix $A$ has $\det A$ a non-zero-divisor (if $\det A$ were a zero-divisor, $c \cdot \det A = 0$ for some $c \neq 0$ would produce a kernel vector via the adjugate). But a non-surjective $\psi$ forces $\det A$ to be a zero-divisor or zero. Contradiction over a nonzero ring — unless $n \leq m$.

# Solution

Part (i) reads a generating set as a surjective endomorphism and invokes "surjective $\Rightarrow$ injective". Part (ii) pads the cross-rank embedding into an endomorphism of $R^n$ and uses the determinant to show injectivity and non-surjectivity cannot coexist, forcing $n \leq m$.

**Step 1 (part i): A size-$n$ generating set defines a surjective endomorphism of $R^n$.**

Generators $v_1,\dots,v_n$ give $\varphi : R^n \to R^n$, $e_i \mapsto v_i$, which is surjective.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $v_1,\dots,v_n$ generate $R^n$. Define the $R$-linear map $\varphi : R^n \to R^n$ on the standard basis by $\varphi(e_i) = v_i$ and extend linearly. For any $w \in R^n$, since the $v_i$ generate, $w = \sum_i r_i v_i = \sum_i r_i \varphi(e_i) = \varphi(\sum_i r_i e_i)$, so $w \in \operatorname{im}\varphi$. Hence $\varphi$ is surjective.

**Step 2 (part i): The surjective endomorphism is injective, so $\varphi$ is an isomorphism and the $v_i$ form a basis.**

By the determinant-trick corollary, $\varphi$ surjective $\Rightarrow$ injective; an isomorphism carries the standard basis to a basis.

> [!note]- Derivation
> $R^n$ is finitely generated, and $\varphi : R^n \to R^n$ is a surjective endomorphism. By [[Ex - A surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module is injective|the surjective-endomorphism theorem]] — itself a consequence of [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|Cayley–Hamilton]] (let $T$ act as $\varphi$; surjectivity gives $(T)R^n = R^n$; the trick yields an inverse) — $\varphi$ is injective. So $\varphi$ is bijective, an $R$-module isomorphism.
>
> The standard basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$ is a basis of $R^n$, and an isomorphism sends a basis to a basis: $\varphi(e_1),\dots,\varphi(e_n) = v_1,\dots,v_n$ are $R$-linearly independent (if $\sum r_i v_i = 0$ then $\varphi(\sum r_i e_i) = 0$, so $\sum r_i e_i = 0$ by injectivity, so all $r_i = 0$) and generate (given). Hence $v_1,\dots,v_n$ is a basis. $\blacksquare$ (i)

**Step 3 (part ii): Pad a cross-rank embedding into an endomorphism of $R^n$.**

Assume $n > m$ and $\iota : R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$ injective. The padding $\pi : R^m \hookrightarrow R^n$ gives an injective $\psi = \pi\iota : R^n \to R^n$ that is not surjective.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Suppose for contradiction $n > m$ and there is an injective $R$-linear $\iota : R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$. Define the injective padding map
> $$\pi : R^m \to R^n, \qquad \pi(x_1,\dots,x_m) = (x_1,\dots,x_m,\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{n-m}).$$
> $\pi$ is injective. The composite $\psi := \pi \circ \iota : R^n \to R^n$ is injective (a composite of injective maps). But $\operatorname{im}\psi \subseteq \operatorname{im}\pi = R^m \times \{0\}^{n-m} \subsetneq R^n$ (proper since $n - m \geq 1$ and $R \neq 0$, so the last coordinate is never hit). Thus $\psi$ is an **injective but non-surjective** endomorphism of $R^n$.

**Step 4 (part ii): The determinant forbids an injective non-surjective endomorphism here.**

Writing $\psi$ as a matrix $A$, injectivity forces $\det A$ to be a non-zero-divisor, but non-surjectivity (the last coordinate is never an image) makes $\det A$ a zero-divisor or zero — impossible over $R \neq 0$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Represent $\psi$ by a matrix $A \in M_n(R)$ (its columns are $\psi(e_1),\dots,\psi(e_n)$). Two facts about $A$:
>
> *Injectivity $\Rightarrow \det A$ is a non-zero-divisor.* Suppose $c \cdot \det A = 0$ for some $c \in R$. Consider the vector $w = \operatorname{adj}(A)\cdot(c\, e_1)$... more directly: the adjugate identity $A\,\operatorname{adj}(A) = (\det A) I$ shows that for any $v$, $A(\operatorname{adj}(A) v) = (\det A) v$. If $\det A$ is a zero-divisor, say $c \det A = 0$ with $c \neq 0$, then $A(\operatorname{adj}(A)(c\,e_1)) = c(\det A)e_1 = 0$; either $\operatorname{adj}(A)(c\, e_1) \neq 0$ is a kernel vector (contradicting injectivity), or $\operatorname{adj}(A)(c\, e_1) = 0$ for all unit vectors, which forces $c\,\operatorname{adj}(A) = 0$, and then expanding $A\operatorname{adj}(A) = (\det A)I$ gives $c\det A = 0$ consistently while a finer analysis (Becker/McCoy's theorem: a square matrix over a commutative ring is injective iff $\det A$ is a non-zero-divisor) yields that $\det A$ must be a non-zero-divisor. We use this clean criterion: **over a commutative ring, the endomorphism $A$ of $R^n$ is injective $\iff \det A$ is a non-zero-divisor.** Since $\psi$ is injective, $\det A$ is a non-zero-divisor.
>
> *Non-surjectivity contradicts this.* The last standard basis vector $e_n \in R^n$ is not in $\operatorname{im}\psi$ (every image has last coordinate $0$, but $e_n$ has last coordinate $1 \neq 0$). So $A$ is not surjective. By the same determinant criterion in its surjectivity form, **$A$ is surjective $\iff \det A$ is a unit**; and a square matrix over a commutative ring that is injective is automatically surjective when... no — the sharp statement we need is: if $\det A$ is a non-zero-divisor then... it need not be a unit. So instead argue directly: $A$ surjective means $\det A \in R^\times$; here $A$ is *not* surjective, so $\det A \notin R^\times$.
>
> Combine with the cleanest route. By part (i)/the surjective-endomorphism theorem, *any surjective endomorphism of $R^n$ is injective*; we now need the converse obstruction. The decisive fact is **McCoy's theorem**: for $A \in M_n(R)$ over a commutative ring, $A$ is injective as a map $R^n \to R^n$ if and only if $\det A$ is a non-zero-divisor. Our $\psi$ has matrix $A$ whose image misses $e_n$; but more usefully, consider the columns of $A$ — they are $\psi(e_i)$, all lying in $R^m \times \{0\}^{n-m}$, so the last row of $A$ is entirely zero. A matrix with a zero row has $\det A = 0$. But $\det A = 0$ is a zero-divisor (indeed $1 \cdot \det A = 0$ with $1 \neq 0$ in the nonzero ring $R$), so by McCoy's theorem $A = \psi$ is *not injective* — contradicting Step 3.
>
> The contradiction shows the assumption $n > m$ is impossible. Hence $n \leq m$. $\blacksquare$ (ii)

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** Over a nonzero commutative ring $R$: (i) any size-$n$ generating set of $R^n$ is a basis; (ii) $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m \Rightarrow n \leq m$.
>
> *(i)* Given generators $v_1,\dots,v_n$ of $R^n$, the map $\varphi : R^n \to R^n$, $e_i \mapsto v_i$, is surjective. A surjective endomorphism of a finitely generated module is injective (Cayley–Hamilton: let $T$ act as $\varphi$; $(T)R^n = R^n$ gives a polynomial inverse). So $\varphi$ is an isomorphism, and $v_i = \varphi(e_i)$ is the image of the standard basis under an isomorphism, hence a basis.
>
> *(ii)* Suppose $n > m$ and $\iota : R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$ is injective. Let $\pi : R^m \to R^n$ be the padding $(x_1,\dots,x_m)\mapsto(x_1,\dots,x_m,0,\dots,0)$, injective. Then $\psi = \pi\iota : R^n \to R^n$ is injective. Its matrix $A$ has last row zero (all images lie in $R^m\times\{0\}^{n-m}$), so $\det A = 0$. By McCoy's theorem (a square matrix over a commutative ring is injective iff its determinant is a non-zero-divisor), $\det A = 0$ being a zero-divisor in the nonzero ring $R$ makes $A$ *not* injective — contradicting injectivity of $\psi$. Hence $n \leq m$. $\blacksquare$
>
> *(Remark.)* McCoy's theorem is the determinant-trick content made sharp: the adjugate identity $A\operatorname{adj}(A) = (\det A)I$ shows that if $\det A$ is a non-zero-divisor and $Av = 0$ then $(\det A)v = \operatorname{adj}(A)Av = 0$ forces $v = 0$ (injectivity), and conversely a determinant that is a zero-divisor $c\det A = 0$ produces a nonzero kernel vector. This is exactly the adjugate manipulation of [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)]].

# Key Takeaways

**A generating set of $R^n$ of size $n$ is a surjective endomorphism in disguise, and "surjective $\Rightarrow$ injective" upgrades it to a basis.** The conceptual leap in part (i) is identifying a generating set $\{v_1,\dots,v_n\}$ with the endomorphism $\varphi(e_i) = v_i$; then "generates" is "surjective" and the determinant-trick corollary forces "injective", i.e. linear independence, i.e. basis. This is the trigger to carry: *a square generating set is a surjective self-map; over any ring, that makes it a basis.* The same identification powers the invariance of rank — you cannot generate $R^n$ with fewer than $n$ elements (a smaller set, padded with zeros, would give a non-injective surjection, impossible). Over a field this is dimension-counting; the content here is that it survives over *any* nonzero commutative ring, because the determinant substitutes for dimension.

**To use the determinant trick across different ranks, pad the cross-rank map into a self-map of one free module.** Part (ii)'s real difficulty is that Cayley–Hamilton needs an *endomorphism* — a self-map — but the embedding $R^n \hookrightarrow R^m$ goes between different modules. The padding $R^m \hookrightarrow R^n$ converts it into $\psi : R^n \to R^n$, where the determinant is defined and the trick applies. This padding-to-a-self-map manoeuvre is the reusable technique: whenever you want to apply a square-matrix fact (determinant, characteristic polynomial, trace) to a non-square situation, embed into the larger square shape and track what the padding forces (here, a zero row, hence zero determinant). The diagnostic: a map whose image misses a coordinate has that coordinate's row zero in any matrix representation, killing the determinant.

**McCoy's theorem is the sharp form of the determinant trick: injectivity of a square matrix equals "$\det$ is a non-zero-divisor".** The load-bearing fact behind part (ii) is that over a commutative ring, a square matrix $A$ is injective (as $R^n \to R^n$) exactly when $\det A$ is a non-zero-divisor — proved by the adjugate identity $A\operatorname{adj}(A) = (\det A)I$, the same identity that drives Cayley–Hamilton. This refines the field statement "$A$ injective $\iff \det A \neq 0$" to a ring statement "$A$ injective $\iff \det A$ not a zero-divisor", and surjectivity sharpens to "$\iff \det A$ a unit". For spaced practice, hold the trichotomy: over a commutative ring, $\det A$ being a unit, a non-zero-divisor, or a zero-divisor governs whether $A$ is an isomorphism, injective, or has a kernel. All three are read off the single element $\det A$ — the determinant trick's gift of controlling rank without dimension. This is the algebraic backbone of "**free modules have a well-defined rank**", the **Serre–Swan** shadow of "vector bundles have a well-defined rank". Compare [[Thm - Invariance of Rank]] for the statement over the special rings where it was first proved, now extended to all nonzero commutative rings.
