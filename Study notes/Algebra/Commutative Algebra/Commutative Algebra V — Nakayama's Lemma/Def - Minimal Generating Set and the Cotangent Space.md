# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Throughout, $(R, \mathfrak m)$ is a [[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field|local ring]] with residue field $k = R/\mathfrak m$, and $M$ is a [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] $R$-module. We write $\mathfrak m M = \{\sum_i a_i m_i : a_i \in \mathfrak m,\ m_i \in M\}$ for the submodule generated by products, $M/\mathfrak m M$ for the quotient (a $k$-vector space), $\bar x = x + \mathfrak m M$ for the image of $x \in M$, and $\dim_k(-)$ for vector-space dimension over $k$. When $M = \mathfrak m$ we get $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$, the **cotangent space**, and we write $\mu(M)$ for the minimal number of generators of $M$. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma]].

This is a compound page: it defines two interlocking notions — a **minimal generating set** of $M$ over a local ring, and the **cotangent space** $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ that arises by specialising to $M = \mathfrak m$ — because the second is the geometrically central instance of the first, and the invariant ($\dim_k$) that makes "minimal" well-defined is the same in both.

# Axiom Motivation

Over a field, "minimal generating set" needs no thought: it is a basis, every basis has the same size (the dimension), and any spanning set contains one. Over a general ring this all collapses. A [[Def - Module|module]] need not have a basis at all; a "minimal" generating set — one with no element removable — can have *different sizes* (over $\mathbb Z$, the group $\mathbb Z/6$ is generated minimally by $\{1\}$, size $1$, but also by $\{2, 3\}$, size $2$, and neither element of the second set is removable); and the number of generators is not an isomorphism invariant. The notion is a mess. The point of this page is that **over a local ring the mess disappears**, for one reason: Nakayama makes generation of $M$ equivalent to spanning of the single vector space $M/\mathfrak m M$, and vector spaces *do* have a well-defined dimension. So "minimal generating set" recovers all its good behaviour, and the size becomes the honest invariant $\dim_k M/\mathfrak m M$.

Why reduce modulo $\mathfrak m$ specifically, and why $\mathfrak m M$ rather than some other submodule? Because $\mathfrak m M$ is exactly the "negligible" part that Nakayama controls. An element $x \in M$ and an element $x' = x + (\text{something in } \mathfrak m M)$ generate the same thing modulo $\mathfrak m M$, and Nakayama's lemma promotes "same modulo $\mathfrak m M$" to "same generating power": by [[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal]], a set generates $M$ iff its image generates $M/\mathfrak m M$. The quotient by $\mathfrak m M$ is the *coarsest* reduction that still detects generation, and it is a vector space because $R/\mathfrak m = k$ is a field. Quotient by a smaller submodule and you lose the field (the quotient is not a vector space); quotient by a larger one (say $M$ itself) and you lose all information. $\mathfrak m M$ is the Goldilocks reduction.

Now specialise to the case that gives the definition its geometric soul. Take $M = \mathfrak m$ — the maximal ideal, which is a finitely generated module when $R$ is Noetherian. The reduction is $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$, and the number of generators of the ideal $\mathfrak m$ becomes $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$. This is forced to be a meaningful invariant by the same Nakayama argument, and it has a name — the **embedding dimension** — because of what it computes geometrically. If $R = \mathcal O_p$ is the local ring of functions at a point $p$ of a variety, then $\mathfrak m$ is the functions vanishing at $p$, $\mathfrak m^2$ is the functions vanishing *to second order*, and the quotient $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is the space of *first-order parts* of vanishing functions — the differentials at $p$. This is the **cotangent space**: a function vanishing at $p$ has a well-defined differential, $\mathfrak m^2$ is exactly the kernel of "take the differential", and $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is the image. The reason to define $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ and not, say, $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^3$ is that the differential is *first-order* data, and first order is precisely "modulo second-order vanishing".

The last thing to motivate is why the cotangent space, not the tangent space, is the primary object — why $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ rather than its dual. The honest answer is algebraic naturality: $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is *built directly* from the ring, by an ideal quotient, with no choices, whereas the tangent space $(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)^*$ requires dualising. Functions pull back along maps, so cotangent vectors are **contravariant** and arise first from the algebra; tangent vectors are their duals. This matches the differential-geometry experience that $df$ is more primitive than $\partial/\partial x$ in the algebraic setting, and it is why one *defines* the cotangent space and *derives* the tangent (Zariski) space as its dual. A point is smooth exactly when this cotangent space has the "right" dimension, equal to the dimension of the variety; the failure — too many cotangent directions — is the algebraic signature of a singularity, and it is detected entirely by the linear algebra this definition sets up.

# The Definition

Let $(R, \mathfrak m)$ be a local ring with residue field $k = R/\mathfrak m$.

## Minimal generating set

Let $M$ be a finitely generated $R$-module. A generating set $\{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$ of $M$ is a **minimal generating set** if no proper subset generates $M$. Equivalently, by [[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal]] and Nakayama, $\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$ is a minimal generating set if and only if the images $\bar x_1, \dots, \bar x_n$ form a $k$-**basis** of the vector space $M/\mathfrak m M$. Consequently every minimal generating set of $M$ has the same cardinality
$$\mu(M) := \dim_k\big(M/\mathfrak m M\big),$$
the **minimal number of generators** of $M$, and any generating set has at least $\mu(M)$ elements.

## The cotangent space

The **cotangent space** of the local ring $(R, \mathfrak m)$ is the $k$-vector space
$$\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2,$$
obtained by taking $M = \mathfrak m$ in the above (using that $\mathfrak m \cdot \mathfrak m = \mathfrak m^2$, so the reduction $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m \cdot \mathfrak m$ is $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$). When $R$ is Noetherian, $\mathfrak m$ is finitely generated and
$$\operatorname{embdim}(R) := \mu(\mathfrak m) = \dim_k\big(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\big)$$
is the **embedding dimension** of $R$ — the minimal number of generators of $\mathfrak m$. The $k$-dual $(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)^*$ is the **Zariski (tangent) space** of $R$.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The reduction $M \mapsto M/\mathfrak m M$ is the [[Commutative Algebra II — Tensor Products|extension of scalars]] $M \mapsto M \otimes_R k$, i.e. base change along the quotient map $R \to k = R/\mathfrak m$. This is the structural reason $M/\mathfrak m M$ is a $k$-vector space: tensoring a module with a quotient ring lands in modules over that quotient ring, and a module over a field is a vector space. The functor $(-) \otimes_R k$ is right exact, and applying it to a surjection $R^n \twoheadrightarrow M$ from a free module ([[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|finite generation]]) gives a surjection $k^n \twoheadrightarrow M/\mathfrak m M$ — which is why $\dim_k M/\mathfrak m M \leq n$ for any generating set of size $n$, the lower-bound half of minimality. The cotangent space is then $(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2) = \mathfrak m \otimes_R k$, the **conormal module** of the point — the algebraic version of the conormal bundle of a submanifold. Its formation is contravariant: a local homomorphism $(R,\mathfrak m) \to (S, \mathfrak n)$ induces $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2 \to \mathfrak n/\mathfrak n^2$ pulling cotangent vectors back, dual to pushing tangent vectors forward.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The cleanest compression: **over a local ring, a module is "a vector space plus negligible corrections", and the minimal generators are a basis of that vector space.** The whole difficulty of module theory — no bases, ill-defined generator counts — is quarantined into the corrections $\mathfrak m M$, which Nakayama certifies as removable. So $\mu(M) = \dim_k M/\mathfrak m M$ is the module-theoretic stand-in for dimension.

**True name:** the true name of the cotangent space $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is "**the space of differentials at the point**" — the first-order parts of functions vanishing there. The ideal $\mathfrak m$ is "functions vanishing at $p$", $\mathfrak m^2$ is "functions vanishing to order $\geq 2$", and the quotient is "the linear approximation $df(p)$". This is why $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ counts coordinates and why its excess over the dimension measures singularity.

This is literally the algebraic version of the **cotangent space** in differential geometry. For a smooth manifold and a point $p$, the cotangent space $T_p^*M$ is the quotient of the maximal ideal $\mathfrak m_p$ of germs vanishing at $p$ by $\mathfrak m_p^2$ — exactly the same construction, with $C^\infty$ germs in place of regular functions. The Zariski tangent space $(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2)^*$ is the algebraic $T_pM$, defined so as to make sense even at singular points where no chart exists. The **embedding dimension** is the smallest $N$ such that the variety embeds in $\mathbb A^N$ near $p$, matching the geometric meaning of "how many coordinates the point needs".

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — a free module over a local ring.** For $M = R^n$ over local $(R,\mathfrak m)$, the reduction is $R^n/\mathfrak m R^n = (R/\mathfrak m)^n = k^n$, so $\mu(R^n) = n$. The standard basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$ is a minimal generating set, and no fewer than $n$ elements generate $R^n$ — recovering, over any local ring, the rank invariance of free modules. This is the local case behind the global exercise that every size-$n$ generating set of $R^n$ is a basis.

**Is an instance — the cotangent space of $k[[x_1,\dots,x_n]]$.** For the formal power series ring $R = k[[x_1,\dots,x_n]]$, local with $\mathfrak m = (x_1,\dots,x_n)$, the cotangent space $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ has basis $\bar x_1,\dots,\bar x_n$, so $\operatorname{embdim}(R) = n$. Since the Krull dimension is also $n$, this is a **regular** (smooth) point — the model of a smooth point of $n$-dimensional space. The differentials $\bar x_i$ are exactly the coordinate $1$-forms $dx_i$.

**Is NOT an instance (minimal sets of different sizes) — the failure over a non-local ring.** Over $R = \mathbb Z$, the module $M = \mathbb Z/6$ has the minimal generating set $\{1\}$ of size $1$ and the irredundant generating set $\{2, 3\}$ of size $2$ (neither $2$ nor $3$ alone generates, since $\gcd$ with $6$ must be $1$). So over a non-local ring "minimal" generating sets can have different cardinalities and $\mu$ is ill-defined. This is precisely the pathology that locality cures: there is no single residue field to reduce to, because $\mathbb Z$ has many maximal ideals.

**Is an instance — a singular point has excess embedding dimension.** For the node $R = k[x,y]/(xy)$ localized at $\mathfrak m = (x,y)$, the cotangent space $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is spanned by $\bar x, \bar y$ — the relation $xy \in \mathfrak m^2$ kills no linear term — so $\operatorname{embdim} = 2$, while the Krull dimension is $1$. The excess $2 > 1$ detects the singularity at the origin: the node needs two coordinates though it is a curve.

**Corollary — Nakayama makes $\mu(M)$ well-defined.** Because $\{x_i\}$ generates $M$ iff $\{\bar x_i\}$ spans $M/\mathfrak m M$, and a spanning set of a vector space has $\geq \dim$ elements with equality iff it is a basis, the minimal generating sets are exactly the lifts of bases and all have size $\dim_k M/\mathfrak m M$. The local hypothesis is essential — it is what provides the single residue field.

**Calibration check.** Verify $\mu(R^n) = n$ over a local ring by computing $R^n/\mathfrak m R^n = k^n$. Confirm that $\{2,3\}$ is an irredundant generating set of $\mathbb Z/6$ of size $2$, and explain why this does not contradict $\mu = \dim_k$ (no single residue field). For $R = k[[x,y]]/(y^2 - x^3)$ at $(x,y)$, compute $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ and confirm it exceeds the dimension $1$, certifying the cusp as singular.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Regular local rings and smooth varieties *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> A Noetherian local ring is **regular** when $\dim_k \mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2 = \dim R$ (embedding dimension equals Krull dimension); these are the algebraic models of **smooth points**. The cotangent space defined here is the exact obstruction — a point is singular precisely when it needs more cotangent directions than its dimension. Regular local rings are integrally closed, are UFDs (Auslander–Buchsbaum), and carry the **Jacobian criterion** for smoothness. The dimension theory is developed in **Commutative Algebra XII — Dimension Theory**.

> [!tip] Kähler differentials and the cotangent complex *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The cotangent space $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is the fibre at the point of the **sheaf of Kähler differentials** $\Omega_{R/k}$ — the algebraic cotangent bundle, built to make $d : R \to \Omega_{R/k}$ the universal derivation. The conormal exact sequence and the **cotangent complex** generalise this to relative and derived settings, and the whole deformation theory of a variety is controlled by these differentials. This page's $\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2$ is the first term.

> [!tip] Minimal free resolutions and Betti numbers *(from Homological Algebra)*
> Over a Noetherian local ring, repeatedly lifting a basis of a fibre to a minimal generating set produces the **minimal free resolution** of a module, unique up to isomorphism; the ranks of its free modules are the **Betti numbers** $\beta_i = \dim_k \operatorname{Tor}_i^R(M, k)$, with $\beta_0 = \mu(M)$ the minimal number of generators computed here. Nakayama is exactly what makes the resolution minimal: the maps have entries in $\mathfrak m$.
