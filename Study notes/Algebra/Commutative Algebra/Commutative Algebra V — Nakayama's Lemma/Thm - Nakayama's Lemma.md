# Notation

All rings are commutative with $1$. Let $R$ be a ring, $M$ a [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] $R$-module, $\mathfrak a \trianglelefteq R$ an ideal, and $J(R) = \bigcap_{\mathfrak m} \mathfrak m$ the [[Def - The Jacobson Radical|Jacobson radical]]. We write $\mathfrak a M$ for the [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] generated by products $a m$ ($a \in \mathfrak a$, $m \in M$), $N \subseteq M$ for a submodule, and $M/N$ for the [[Def - Quotient Module|quotient module]]. For a [[Def - Local Ring and Residue Field|local ring]] we write $(R, \mathfrak m)$, where $J(R) = \mathfrak m$. The full registry is on [[Commutative Algebra V — Nakayama's Lemma]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Nakayama's lemma).** Let $M$ be a finitely generated $R$-module and $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ an ideal with $\mathfrak a M = M$. Then $M = 0$.

> **Corollary (submodule form).** Let $M$ be finitely generated, $N \subseteq M$ a submodule, and $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ with $\mathfrak a M + N = M$. Then $N = M$.

The submodule form is the version used in practice: it says a submodule $N$ that accounts for all of $M$ "modulo the small ideal $\mathfrak a$" is already all of $M$. The local-ring special case ($\mathfrak a = \mathfrak m$, so the hypothesis becomes $\mathfrak m M = M$ or $\mathfrak m M + N = M$) is the one most often invoked, and it is the engine behind the residue-field reduction $M \rightsquigarrow M/\mathfrak m M$.

---

# Motivation

Nakayama's lemma is the theorem that makes "reduce to the residue field, then lift" a legitimate proof strategy, and that strategy is the spine of commutative algebra. Modules are hard because they lack bases; vector spaces are easy because they have them; and the only thing standing between a finitely generated module $M$ over a local ring and the vector space $M/\mathfrak m M$ is the submodule $\mathfrak m M$. Nakayama is the precise statement that this gap is *not* the whole module — that you cannot fill $M$ up with $\mathfrak m M$ — and therefore that the reduction $M/\mathfrak m M$ never trivialises a nonzero module. From this single fact flow all the lifting theorems: a spanning set of the vector space generates the module, a basis lifts to minimal generators, a surjection of fibres lifts to a surjection of modules.

The role the theorem plays is *finishing*. Almost every local computation in the subject ends with a line of the form "$\mathfrak m M = M$" (or "$\mathfrak m M + N = M$"), having been set up by localizing and reducing, and Nakayama closes it: $M = 0$ (or $N = M$). It is the period at the end of the sentence. The [[Commutative Algebra IV — Localization#Problem-Solving Strategy|localization chapter]] already advertised this: "prove it locally" means "localize at a maximal ideal, then use Nakayama". This is where that promise is cashed.

Why should it be true at all? Because finite generation makes the module *rigid*. If $\mathfrak a M = M$ then every generator is an $\mathfrak a$-combination of the generators, and feeding this self-referential system through the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]] produces a single element $1 - a$ (with $a \in \mathfrak a$) that annihilates everything. When $\mathfrak a$ is inside the Jacobson radical, $1 - a$ is a *unit* — by [[Thm - The Jacobson Radical via Units]] — and a unit annihilating a module forces the module to vanish. The two hypotheses are exactly the two things this argument needs: finite generation to build the matrix, and smallness of $\mathfrak a$ to make $1 - a$ invertible. Drop either and the theorem is false, as the standard counterexamples show.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The theorem requires the precondition $A$: *$M$ finitely generated and $\mathfrak a M = M$ with $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$.* Recognising disguised forms of "$\mathfrak a$ fills $M$" is the skill.

The first disguised source is **a local ring with $\mathfrak m M = M$**. The property $B$ is "$(R,\mathfrak m)$ local and reducing $M$ mod $\mathfrak m$ gives zero", i.e. $M/\mathfrak m M = 0$, which is $\mathfrak m M = M$. Since $J(R) = \mathfrak m$, the precondition $A$ holds, and Nakayama gives $M = 0$. The non-obvious recognition is that "the fibre $M \otimes k$ vanishes" is literally "$\mathfrak m M = M$". *Example problem:* a finitely generated module with zero fibre at a point has zero stalk there.

The second disguised source is **a spanning condition modulo a submodule**, $\mathfrak m M + N = M$. The property $B$ is "$N$ generates $M$ modulo $\mathfrak m$", and the bridge is to pass to $M/N$: then $\mathfrak m(M/N) = (\mathfrak m M + N)/N = M/N$, so the submodule form applies and $N = M$. *Example problem:* lifting a residue-field spanning set to a generating set — [[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal]].

The third disguised source is **a global module after localization**. The property $B$ is "$M$ over a general ring $R$, with $M_{\mathfrak m} = \mathfrak m M_{\mathfrak m}$ at a maximal ideal $\mathfrak m$". Localizing makes $R_{\mathfrak m}$ local, so $J(R_{\mathfrak m}) = \mathfrak m R_{\mathfrak m}$, and Nakayama applies to $M_{\mathfrak m}$. The bridge is *localize first to enlarge the Jacobson radical*. *Example problem:* proving a finitely generated module is zero by checking each localization is zero.

The fourth disguised source is **a surjection that should be an isomorphism**. The property $B$ is "$\varphi : M \twoheadrightarrow M$ surjective"; setting $N = \ker$-related data and feeding to the determinant trick (via $R[T]$) recovers a Nakayama-type conclusion. *Example problem:* surjective endomorphisms of finitely generated modules are injective.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion $C$ is "$M = 0$" (or "$N = M$").

Combine $C$ with **a candidate generating set**. If $x_1,\dots,x_n$ span $M/\mathfrak m M$ and $N = \sum R x_i$, then $\mathfrak m M + N = M$, so the submodule form gives $N = M$: the $x_i$ generate. The further result $E$ is the generator-lifting theorem, the workhorse of the chapter. Nonobvious because it converts a vector-space spanning statement into a module-generation statement.

Combine $C$ with **the residue-field dimension**. Once you know a minimal generating set has size $\dim_k M/\mathfrak m M$, comparing two modules' fibre dimensions controls maps between them: a map inducing an isomorphism on fibres is, by Nakayama applied to the cokernel, surjective. The further result $E$ is "fibrewise iso $\Rightarrow$ surjective" — the basis of upper semicontinuity of rank. Nonobvious because fibre data is far weaker-looking than module data.

Combine $C$ with **flatness or freeness criteria**. A finitely generated flat module over a local ring is free, proved by lifting a fibre basis and using Nakayama to show the lift is a basis (the relations vanish by flatness, the spanning by Nakayama). The further result $E$ is "finitely generated projective = locally free". Nonobvious because it bridges a global property (projective) to a local one (free) through the residue field.

# Why Is It True

The cleanest way to feel why Nakayama holds is to imagine trying to violate it. Suppose $M \neq 0$ is finitely generated with $\mathfrak m M = M$, over a local ring. Take a *minimal* generating set $x_1,\dots,x_n$ — minimal in the sense of fewest elements. Since $\mathfrak m M = M$, the last generator $x_n$ is itself in $\mathfrak m M$, so $x_n = a_1 x_1 + \dots + a_n x_n$ with all $a_i \in \mathfrak m$. Rearrange: $(1 - a_n) x_n = a_1 x_1 + \dots + a_{n-1} x_{n-1}$. Now $a_n \in \mathfrak m = J(R)$, so $1 - a_n$ is a *unit*; divide by it, and $x_n$ becomes a combination of $x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}$. But then those $n - 1$ elements already generate $M$, contradicting minimality. The only escape is $n = 0$, i.e. $M = 0$. This is the proof in miniature, and it shows exactly where each hypothesis is spent: finite generation gives a *finite, minimal* generating set to run induction on; the Jacobson-radical hypothesis makes $1 - a_n$ invertible so you can solve for $x_n$.

**The one-line mechanism: $\mathfrak m M = M$ lets you write the last generator in terms of all of them with coefficients in $\mathfrak m$, and $1 - (\text{coefficient of itself})$ is a unit, so it dissolves into the others — minimality then forces no generators at all.** The slick version replaces this induction with the determinant trick: Cayley–Hamilton at $f = \operatorname{id}$ produces in one stroke the element $1 - a$ that annihilates everything, and the Jacobson radical inverts it.

The submodule form is the same statement viewed in the quotient. If $\mathfrak m M + N = M$, then in $M/N$ we have $\mathfrak m(M/N) = M/N$, and $M/N$ is finitely generated (a quotient of $M$), so Nakayama gives $M/N = 0$, i.e. $N = M$. There is no new content — just the observation that "fills modulo $N$" becomes "fills" after quotienting by $N$.

---

# What Makes This Hard

The conceptual hurdle is not the proof but *remembering the two hypotheses and why neither is optional*. The non-obvious step in the slick proof is feeding $f = \operatorname{id}$ into the determinant trick — using a theorem about general endomorphisms to study the identity, which feels like overkill until you see it produces the annihilator $1 - a$ for free. The most common error is to apply Nakayama without checking finite generation (the Prüfer module $\mathbb Z[\tfrac 1p]/\mathbb Z$ has $pM = M$, $M \neq 0$) or without checking $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ (over $\mathbb Z$, $J = (0)$, so $(p)$ is too big and "$pM = M$" carries no conclusion). When the module is not finitely generated, the right move is to *look for a counterexample*, not to push harder on the proof.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:**
Apply the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]] to $f = \operatorname{id}_M$ with the ideal $\mathfrak a$; the hypothesis $\mathfrak a M = M$ gives $f(M) = M \subseteq \mathfrak a M$, so there is $a \in \mathfrak a$ with $(1 - a)m = 0$ for all $m$. Then $1 - a$ is a unit because $a \in \mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$, forcing $M = 0$. For the submodule form, quotient by $N$.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Get a single annihilator.** Show there is $a \in \mathfrak a$ with $(1-a)m = 0$ for all $m \in M$.
   - *Hint:* Determinant trick with $f = \operatorname{id}$: the relation $1 + a_1 + \dots + a_n = 0$ on $\operatorname{id}_M$ is $(1 - a)\operatorname{id}_M = 0$ with $a = -(a_1 + \dots + a_n) \in \mathfrak a$.
   - *Why needed:* It converts the module condition $\mathfrak a M = M$ into a single ring element $1 - a$ killing $M$.

2. **Invert $1 - a$.** Show $1 - a \in R^\times$, hence $M = 0$.
   - *Hint:* $a \in \mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$, so by [[Thm - The Jacobson Radical via Units]], $1 - a$ is a unit; multiply $(1-a)m = 0$ by $(1-a)^{-1}$.
   - *Why needed:* It is where the Jacobson-radical hypothesis is spent; without it $1 - a$ need not be invertible.

3. **Submodule form.** Deduce $\mathfrak a M + N = M \Rightarrow N = M$.
   - *Hint:* Apply the bare lemma to the finitely generated module $M/N$, using $\mathfrak a(M/N) = (\mathfrak a M + N)/N = M/N$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the practically used version; the proof reduces it to the bare case by quotienting.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: A single Jacobson-shifted annihilator
> **Statement:** If $M$ is finitely generated and $\mathfrak a M = M$, there exists $a \in \mathfrak a$ with $(1 - a)m = 0$ for all $m \in M$.
>
> **Hint:** Apply the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|determinant trick]] to $f = \operatorname{id}_M$ (legal since $\operatorname{id}(M) = M = \mathfrak a M \subseteq \mathfrak a M$).
>
> **Why needed:** It is the bridge from the module hypothesis to a ring element; everything after is inverting that element.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Since $\mathfrak a M = M$, the map $f = \operatorname{id}_M$ satisfies $f(M) = M = \mathfrak a M \subseteq \mathfrak a M$. By the determinant trick there are $a_1,\dots,a_n \in \mathfrak a$ with $\operatorname{id}^n + a_1 \operatorname{id}^{n-1} + \dots + a_n \operatorname{id} = 0$ in $\operatorname{End}_R(M)$. As $\operatorname{id}^k = \operatorname{id}$, this is $(1 + a_1 + \dots + a_n)\operatorname{id}_M = 0$, i.e. $(1 - a)\operatorname{id}_M = 0$ with $a = -(a_1 + \dots + a_n) \in \mathfrak a$. Thus $(1 - a)m = 0$ for every $m \in M$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: A Jacobson-shifted unit kills only zero
> **Statement:** If $a \in J(R)$ and $(1 - a)m = 0$ for all $m \in M$, then $M = 0$.
>
> **Hint:** $1 - a$ is a unit by [[Thm - The Jacobson Radical via Units]]; multiply through by its inverse.
>
> **Why needed:** It is where $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ is used; it converts "an element annihilates $M$" into "$M = 0$".
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Since $a \in \mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$, the element $1 - a$ is a unit of $R$ by the unit characterisation of the Jacobson radical. Let $u = (1 - a)^{-1}$. For any $m \in M$, $(1 - a)m = 0$, so $m = u(1-a)m = u \cdot 0 = 0$. As $m$ was arbitrary, $M = 0$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Passing to the quotient gives the submodule form
> **Statement:** If $M$ is finitely generated, $N \subseteq M$, and $\mathfrak a M + N = M$ with $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$, then $N = M$.
>
> **Hint:** Show $\mathfrak a (M/N) = M/N$ and apply the bare lemma to $M/N$.
>
> **Why needed:** It produces the version actually invoked in generator-lifting and freeness arguments.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > $M/N$ is finitely generated (a quotient of the finitely generated $M$). In $M/N$, $\mathfrak a(M/N) = \{\sum a_i \bar m_i\} = (\mathfrak a M + N)/N = M/N$ by hypothesis. By the bare Nakayama lemma applied to $M/N$ and $\mathfrak a$, $M/N = 0$, i.e. $N = M$.

---

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $M$ be finitely generated and $\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$ with $\mathfrak a M = M$.
>
> **Bare lemma.** By Lemma 1, there is $a \in \mathfrak a$ with $(1 - a)m = 0$ for all $m \in M$. Since $a \in \mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$, Lemma 2 gives $M = 0$.
>
> Explicitly: the determinant trick applied to $\operatorname{id}_M$ (valid because $\operatorname{id}_M(M) = M = \mathfrak a M$) yields $(1 + a_1 + \dots + a_n)\operatorname{id}_M = 0$ with $a_i \in \mathfrak a$; setting $a = -(a_1+\dots+a_n) \in \mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$, the element $1 - a$ is a unit ([[Thm - The Jacobson Radical via Units]]), and $(1-a)m = 0$ forces $m = 0$ for every $m$. Hence $M = 0$.
>
> **Submodule form.** Suppose instead $N \subseteq M$ with $\mathfrak a M + N = M$. By Lemma 3, $\mathfrak a(M/N) = (\mathfrak a M + N)/N = M/N$, and $M/N$ is finitely generated, so the bare lemma gives $M/N = 0$, i.e. $N = M$. $\blacksquare$
>
> ---
> **Why the hypotheses are necessary.** Finite generation: over $\mathbb Z$, $M = \mathbb Z[\tfrac1p]/\mathbb Z$ has $pM = M$ but $M \neq 0$; or over $\mathbb Z_{(p)}$, $M = \mathbb Q$ has $\mathfrak m M = M$ with $M \neq 0$ — neither is finitely generated. Jacobson smallness: over $\mathbb Z$ (where $J(\mathbb Z) = 0$), $\mathfrak a = (p)$ is not in $J(\mathbb Z)$, and $M = \mathbb Z/q$ ($q \neq p$ prime) has $pM = M$, $M \neq 0$.

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**A coherent sheaf is generated near a point by sections that fill the fibre (algebraic geometry).** For a coherent sheaf $\mathcal F$ with stalk $M = \mathcal F_p$ a finitely generated $\mathcal O_p$-module, sections whose images span the fibre $M/\mathfrak m M$ generate the stalk by the submodule form of Nakayama. The nonobvious recognition is that "spanning the fibre" is the disguised source $\mathfrak m M + N = M$; this underlies upper semicontinuity of rank and the local generation of sheaves.

**Finitely generated flat modules over a local ring are free (homological algebra).** Lift a basis of the fibre $M/\mathfrak m M$ to elements of $M$; Nakayama makes them generate, and flatness makes the relations among them vanish, so they form a basis. The nonobvious step is that Nakayama supplies *spanning* while flatness supplies *independence* — two halves of "free" from two different hypotheses, glued at the residue field.

**Lifting idempotents and Hensel's lemma (number theory).** Over a complete local ring, an approximate solution of a polynomial equation that is non-degenerate modulo $\mathfrak m$ lifts to an exact one; the Nakayama-style control of $1 + \mathfrak m$ as units is what makes the Newton iteration converge. The nonobvious application is that "solvable mod $\mathfrak m$ $\Rightarrow$ solvable" for smooth equations is the same rigidity that Nakayama expresses for modules.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton for Modules (Determinant Trick)|The determinant trick]]** — the *proof* of Nakayama, not just a tool it uses. Specialising the trick to $f = \operatorname{id}$ converts $\mathfrak a M = M$ into the single annihilator $1 - a$. Every line of Nakayama beyond that is inverting $1 - a$, which is the next bridge.

- **[[Thm - The Jacobson Radical via Units|The unit characterisation of J(R)]]** — supplies the invertibility of $1 - a$ from $a \in J(R)$. This is the *entire* role of the hypothesis "$\mathfrak a \subseteq J(R)$": it is the licence to invert. Remove it and $1 - a$ may fail to be a unit, and the lemma collapses.

- **[[Thm - Generators Modulo the Maximal Ideal|Generators modulo the maximal ideal]]** — the headline application of the submodule form. Spanning $M/\mathfrak m M$ is "$\mathfrak m M + N = M$", which Nakayama upgrades to "$N = M$", i.e. generation. This is how the residue-field reduction becomes a generating-set construction.

- **[[Thm - The Local-Global Principle|The local–global principle]]** — Nakayama is the local engine the local–global principle feeds. "Prove it locally" reduces a global statement to local rings, where $\mathfrak m = J(R)$ is large and Nakayama bites; assembling the local conclusions is the local–global half. The two together are the central two-step method of the subject.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Minimal free resolutions and Betti numbers *(from Homological Algebra)*
> Over a Noetherian local ring, iterating "lift a fibre basis to a minimal generating set" — each step justified by Nakayama — builds the **minimal free resolution** of a module, unique up to isomorphism, with the free ranks being the **Betti numbers** $\beta_i = \dim_k \operatorname{Tor}_i^R(M,k)$. Nakayama is exactly what forces the differentials to have entries in $\mathfrak m$, which is the definition of minimality.

> [!tip] Smoothness, upper semicontinuity of rank, and coherent sheaves *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Nakayama makes the fibre dimension $\dim_k \mathcal F_p/\mathfrak m_p \mathcal F_p$ of a coherent sheaf **upper semicontinuous** — it can only jump up on closed sets — and underlies the construction of the **cotangent sheaf** and the **Jacobian criterion** for smoothness. The local generation it provides is the technical heart of coherent-sheaf theory; the geometry is developed in **Commutative Algebra XII — Dimension Theory**.
