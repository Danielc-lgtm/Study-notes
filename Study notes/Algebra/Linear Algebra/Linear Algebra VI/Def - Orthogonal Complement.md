# Notation

$V$ is an inner product space over $\mathbf{F}$. $U \subseteq V$ is a subset (often a subspace) of $V$. The orthogonal complement is denoted $U^\perp$, read "$U$ perp". See [[Linear Algebra VI — §6 Inner Product Spaces]] for the full notation registry.

---

# Axiom Motivation

We have a subset $U \subseteq V$ and want to single out all the vectors in $V$ that are perpendicular to *every* vector in $U$. This is the natural geometric question: given a "thing" $U$, what is "everything orthogonal to it"? In $\mathbb{R}^3$, if $U$ is a line through the origin, "everything orthogonal to $U$" should be a plane through the origin perpendicular to that line; if $U$ is a plane, then "everything orthogonal to $U$" is a line. The orthogonal complement is the precise algebraic formulation of "everything perpendicular to $U$".

Why "for every $u \in U$" rather than "for some $u \in U$"? Because we want $U^\perp$ to be a subspace, and we want it to behave well with respect to operations on $U$. If we only required "orthogonal to some element", the resulting set would not be a subspace (the union of perpendiculars to each $u$ is not closed under addition) and would not have the duality property $(U^\perp)^\perp = U$. Requiring orthogonality to *every* element of $U$ is what makes $U^\perp$ a subspace, and is what gives the [[Thm - Orthogonal Decomposition|orthogonal decomposition]] $V = U \oplus U^\perp$ for finite-dimensional $U$.

Why allow $U$ to be a subset, not just a subspace? Because the operation $U \mapsto U^\perp$ is naturally defined on arbitrary subsets, and the perpendicular to any subset coincides with the perpendicular to the subspace it spans. This is the content of the identity $\{v_1, \dots, v_m\}^\perp = \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_m)^\perp$: orthogonality to all vectors in a list is the same as orthogonality to all of their linear combinations. So the orthogonal complement only "sees" the subspace generated by $U$, and there is no loss of generality in restricting to [[Def - Subspace|subspaces]] — but admitting subsets makes the definition more flexible.

Why is $U^\perp$ a subspace? Three checks: (i) the zero vector is orthogonal to everything, so $0 \in U^\perp$; (ii) if $v, w \in U^\perp$ and $u \in U$, then $\langle u, v + w\rangle = \langle u, v\rangle + \langle u, w\rangle = 0 + 0 = 0$, so $v + w \in U^\perp$; (iii) if $v \in U^\perp$ and $\lambda \in \mathbf{F}$, then $\langle u, \lambda v\rangle = \bar\lambda \langle u, v\rangle = 0$, so $\lambda v \in U^\perp$. The subspace structure is automatic from the linearity of the inner product in the second slot (modulo conjugates).

The orthogonal complement has two faces that should be kept in mind. The **algebraic** face is the linear-algebraic operation $U \mapsto U^\perp$ on [[Def - Subspace|subspaces]] of $V$. The **geometric** face is "everything perpendicular to $U$". The two coincide for finite-dimensional inner product spaces (and finite-dimensional or closed subspaces of Hilbert spaces), but diverge in infinite [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]] for non-closed subspaces. This divergence is the key infinite-dimensional subtlety: see the warning callout in the parent topic page.

# The Definition

Let $V$ be an inner product space and $U \subseteq V$ a subset. The **orthogonal complement** of $U$ in $V$ is

$$
U^\perp = \{ v \in V : \langle u, v\rangle = 0 \text{ for every } u \in U \}.
$$

That is, $U^\perp$ is the set of vectors in $V$ that are orthogonal to every vector in $U$.

$U^\perp$ depends on the ambient space $V$ as well as on $U$, but $V$ is usually clear from context and omitted from the notation.

**Properties:**

1. **$U^\perp$ is always a subspace of $V$**, regardless of whether $U$ itself is a subspace.
2. $\{0\}^\perp = V$.
3. $V^\perp = \{0\}$.
4. $U \cap U^\perp \subseteq \{0\}$ (the only vector orthogonal to itself is $0$). When $U$ is a subspace, $U \cap U^\perp = \{0\}$.
5. If $G \subseteq H$, then $H^\perp \subseteq G^\perp$ (the operation reverses inclusions).
6. $\{v_1, \dots, v_m\}^\perp = \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_m)^\perp$ — the orthogonal complement of a set equals the orthogonal complement of its span.

For **finite-dimensional** subspaces $U$ of $V$ (or, in infinite [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]], for closed subspaces), the [[Thm - Orthogonal Decomposition|orthogonal decomposition theorem]] gives:

7. $V = U \oplus U^\perp$ (every $v \in V$ decomposes uniquely as $v = u + w$ with $u \in U$ and $w \in U^\perp$).
8. $\dim U^\perp = \dim V - \dim U$ when $V$ is finite-dimensional.
9. $(U^\perp)^\perp = U$.
10. $U^\perp = \{0\}$ if and only if $U = V$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**The annihilator.** In duality theory, for a subspace $U \subseteq V$ (no inner product needed), the **annihilator** is $U^0 = \{\varphi \in V^* : \varphi|_U = 0\}$ — the linear functionals that vanish on $U$. When $V$ is finite-dimensional with an inner product, the Riesz isomorphism $V \cong V^*$ identifies $U^0$ with $U^\perp$. So the orthogonal complement is the inner-product version of the annihilator — the "perpendicular" with respect to the canonical pairing $V \otimes V^* \to \mathbf{F}$.

**Topological orthogonal complement.** In an arbitrary topological vector space (without an inner product), one can still define a notion of "perpendicular subspace" via duality, but the geometry of "perpendicular" requires the inner product. In Hilbert spaces, the orthogonal complement of a *closed* subspace is well-behaved; for non-closed subspaces, $(U^\perp)^\perp = \overline{U}$ (the closure of $U$), so the double-perp can be strictly larger than $U$.

**Generalisations.** The orthogonal complement extends straightforwardly to inner-product modules over fields, sesquilinear forms over $\mathbb{C}$, and pseudo-inner products on Minkowski space (where it remains a subspace but loses the trivial-intersection property — vectors can be "perpendicular to themselves").

**True name:** the orthogonal complement is the **kernel of the projection-to-dual** map $V \to U^*$ defined by $v \mapsto \langle\cdot, v\rangle|_U$. Equivalently, it is the inner-product realization of the annihilator: the dual subspace pulled back along $V \cong V^*$.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the orthogonal complement of a line is the perpendicular plane.** Let $U = \operatorname{span}((2, 3, 5))$. Then
$$
U^\perp = \{ (x, y, z) : 2x + 3y + 5z = 0 \},
$$
the plane through the origin with normal vector $(2, 3, 5)$. The dimension count agrees: $\dim U = 1$, $\dim U^\perp = 2$, $\dim V = 3$.

**Is an instance: in $\mathbb{R}^3$, the orthogonal complement of a plane is the perpendicular line.** Let $U = \{(x, y, z) : 2x + 3y + 5z = 0\}$. Then
$$
U^\perp = \operatorname{span}((2, 3, 5)) = \{(2t, 3t, 5t) : t \in \mathbb{R}\}.
$$
This is the converse of the previous example, and the symmetric statement $(U^\perp)^\perp = U$ shows that the two perp operations invert each other.

**Is an instance: orthogonal complement of a coordinate subspace.** In $\mathbf{F}^5$ with the Euclidean inner product, let $U = \{(a, b, 0, 0, 0) : a, b \in \mathbf{F}\}$. Then $U^\perp = \{(0, 0, x, y, z) : x, y, z \in \mathbf{F}\}$ — the complementary coordinate subspace.

**Is an instance: orthogonal complement of an orthonormal-basis subspace.** If $e_1, \dots, e_m, f_1, \dots, f_n$ is an orthonormal basis of $V$, then $\operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_m)^\perp = \operatorname{span}(f_1, \dots, f_n)$. This is the cleanest example: orthonormal bases split $V$ naturally into a chosen subspace and its perp.

**Is an instance: in $L^2[-\pi, \pi]$, the orthogonal complement of the even functions is the odd functions.** The space $L^2[-\pi, \pi]$ splits as a direct sum of even and odd functions. The two subspaces are orthogonal complements of each other because $\int_{-\pi}^\pi f_{\text{even}}(x) g_{\text{odd}}(x)\, dx = 0$ for any even $f$ and odd $g$ (the integrand is odd).

**Is NOT an instance — pathology in infinite dimensions: $U^\perp = \{0\}$ without $U = V$.** Let $V = C[-1, 1]$ with $\langle f, g\rangle = \int_{-1}^1 fg$. Let $U = \{f \in V : f(0) = 0\}$ — the subspace of continuous functions vanishing at $0$. Then $U^\perp = \{0\}$: any continuous $g$ orthogonal to every $f \in U$ must satisfy $\int_{-1}^1 fg = 0$ for every such $f$, which forces $g = 0$ on $[-1, 0) \cup (0, 1]$, hence $g = 0$ by continuity. But $U \neq V$: the constant function $1$ is in $V$ and not in $U$. So $V = U \oplus U^\perp$ fails — even though both $U \cap U^\perp = \{0\}$ and $U^\perp$ is a subspace. The repair condition is that the decomposition holds for **closed** subspaces in Hilbert spaces, or any subspace in finite dimensions. See [[Linear Algebra VI — §6 Inner Product Spaces#Bridges|the Bridges section]].

**Corollary ($U^\perp$ is always a subspace).** The zero vector is in $U^\perp$, and the set is closed under addition and scalar multiplication, by the linearity of $\langle u, \cdot\rangle$ in the second slot (modulo conjugates that do not affect membership in $\ker$).

**Corollary (orthogonal complement of a span).** $\{v_1, \dots, v_m\}^\perp = \operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_m)^\perp$. *Proof:* $w$ is orthogonal to every $v_k$ if and only if $w$ is orthogonal to every linear combination (by linearity of $\langle\cdot, w\rangle$).

**Corollary (dimension formula for finite-dimensional $V$).** $\dim U + \dim U^\perp = \dim V$. *Proof:* the orthogonal decomposition theorem.

**Corollary (double-perp).** For finite-dimensional $U$, $(U^\perp)^\perp = U$. *Proof:* $U \subseteq (U^\perp)^\perp$ is immediate from the definitions; the reverse inclusion uses dimension counting. In infinite dimensions, $(U^\perp)^\perp$ is the closure $\overline{U}$.

**Calibration check.** Three verifications: (i) in $\mathbb{R}^3$, compute the orthogonal complement of $\operatorname{span}((1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0))$ and verify it equals the $z$-axis; (ii) verify that $(\{(1, 0)\})^\perp = (\operatorname{span}((1, 0)))^\perp$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ — both equal the $y$-axis; (iii) check that for $V = \mathbb{R}^4$ and $U = \operatorname{span}((1, 2, 3, -4), (-5, 4, 3, 2))$, $\dim U^\perp = 2$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Orthogonal Projection and Best Approximation *(direct downstream)*
> Once $V = U \oplus U^\perp$ is in hand, the [[Def - Orthogonal Projection|orthogonal projection]] $P_U : V \to V$ is defined by $v = u + w \mapsto u$, and the [[Thm - Best Approximation by Orthogonal Projection|best-approximation theorem]] $\|v - P_U v\| \leq \|v - u\|$ for all $u \in U$ follows from Pythagoras applied to $v - u = (v - P_U v) + (P_U v - u)$. The orthogonal complement is the entire reason orthogonal projection exists.

> [!tip] Hilbert Projection Theorem *(from Functional Analysis)*
> In a Hilbert space, the orthogonal decomposition $H = U \oplus U^\perp$ holds for **closed** subspaces $U$ — and more generally, for any closed convex subset, every $v \in H$ has a unique nearest point in the set (the **Hilbert projection theorem**). This generalizes the finite-dimensional fact in two ways: from subspaces to convex subsets, and from finite to infinite dimensions with the closedness assumption. The projection theorem is the engine behind separating-hyperplane theorems, the Hahn-Banach theorem in Hilbert space, and the duality theory of $H^*$.

> [!tip] Adjoint Operators *(from Linear Algebra VII)*
> The adjoint $T^* \in \mathcal{L}(W, V)$ of $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ between inner product spaces is the unique map satisfying $\langle Tv, w\rangle = \langle v, T^* w\rangle$. Two cornerstone identities relate ranges and kernels via orthogonal complements: $(\operatorname{range} T)^\perp = \ker T^*$ and $(\ker T)^\perp = \operatorname{range} T^*$. This is the **fundamental theorem of linear algebra** in inner-product form: the four fundamental subspaces of a linear map come in two perpendicular pairs. See [[Linear Algebra VII — §7 Operators on Inner Product Spaces]].
