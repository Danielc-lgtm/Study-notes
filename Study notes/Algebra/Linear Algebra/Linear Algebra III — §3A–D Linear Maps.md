# Notation Registry

Throughout this chapter $\mathbf{F}$ denotes a field, always either $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ unless said otherwise, and $U, V, W$ denote vector spaces over $\mathbf{F}$. When dimension is finite the symbols $\dim U, \dim V, \dim W$ are the usual non-negative integers; we write $\dim V = n$, $\dim W = m$ when the dimensions need names. A linear map is denoted by capital Latin letters $T, S, R, E$, and when a single space is involved we write $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ rather than $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, V)$.

- $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ — the set of all linear maps $V \to W$; itself a vector space over $\mathbf{F}$ (see [[Def - Linear Map]]).
- $\mathcal{L}(V) = \mathcal{L}(V, V)$ — linear maps from $V$ to itself, called **operators**; a ring under composition.
- $T(v)$ or $Tv$ — the image of $v \in V$ under $T$; the second notation is preferred whenever $T$ is linear.
- $\operatorname{null} T = \{v \in V : Tv = 0\}$ — the **null space** (or **kernel**) of $T$; a subspace of $V$.
- $\operatorname{range} T = \{Tv : v \in V\}$ — the **range** (or **image**) of $T$; a subspace of $W$.
- $I$ or $I_V$ — the **identity operator** on $V$, $Iv = v$.
- $T^{-1}$ — the inverse of $T$, when $T$ is invertible.
- $ST$ — the **composition** $S \circ T$, defined when $T$ maps into the domain of $S$; for linear maps we always drop the $\circ$.
- $\mathbf{F}^{m, n}$ — the vector space of $m$-by-$n$ matrices with entries in $\mathbf{F}$, of dimension $mn$.
- $A_{j, k}$ — the entry of a matrix $A$ in row $j$, column $k$ (rows first, columns second).
- $A_{j, \cdot}$ — the $j$-th row of $A$ as a $1$-by-$n$ matrix; $A_{\cdot, k}$ — the $k$-th column as an $m$-by-$1$ matrix.
- $\mathcal{M}(T)$ or $\mathcal{M}(T, (v_1, \dots, v_n), (w_1, \dots, w_m))$ — the **matrix of $T$** with respect to the chosen bases of domain and codomain; the $k$-th column is the coordinate vector of $Tv_k$ in the $W$-basis.
- $\mathcal{M}(v)$ — the **matrix of a vector** $v$, the $n$-by-$1$ column of its coordinates in a chosen basis.
- $\operatorname{rank} A$ — the **rank** of a matrix $A$; equal both to the column rank and the row rank.
- $\dim \operatorname{range} T$ — the **rank** of a linear map; equal to $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{M}(T)$ for any choice of bases.
- $A^{\mathrm{t}}$ — the transpose of $A$.
- $V \cong W$ — $V$ and $W$ are **isomorphic**: there exists an invertible linear map $V \to W$.
- $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ — the group of invertible operators on $V$ under composition; in basis-dependent form, $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{F})$ the group of invertible $n$-by-$n$ matrices.

**Standing convention.** A "linear map" always means an $\mathbf{F}$-linear map between $\mathbf{F}$-vector spaces. Whenever we speak of "the matrix of $T$" or "the matrix of $v$" without further qualification, a basis has been chosen for each space in sight; the matrix is *not* a property of $T$ alone but of the pair (map, choice of bases). When $\mathbf{F}^n$ appears, the standard basis is in force unless said otherwise. Indices on matrices are always row-first then column-second.

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: **a linear map is what you get when you take a basis of $V$, send each basis vector wherever you like in $W$, and then extend by linearity**. Everything else in this chapter — null spaces, ranges, matrices, matrix multiplication, invertibility, isomorphism, change of basis — is bookkeeping that follows from this single fact.

A linear map $T : V \to W$ is the right notion of "function between vector spaces". The previous chapters set up vector spaces, bases, and dimension, but a single vector space by itself is inert: you cannot do interesting mathematics with a static structure, you need maps between structures. A linear map is one that respects the two operations defining a vector space, $T(u + v) = Tu + Tv$ and $T(\lambda v) = \lambda Tv$, and the linear-map lemma in [[Thm - Linear Map Determined by Action on Basis]] turns this respect-the-operations condition into a constructive recipe: pick any basis $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ of $V$ and any vectors $w_1, \ldots, w_n$ of $W$ at all, then there exists exactly one linear map sending $v_k \mapsto w_k$. A linear map is finite data — $n$ vectors in $W$ — once a basis of $V$ is chosen, and that finiteness is what makes linear algebra computable.

The central theorem of this chapter, and arguably of all finite-dimensional linear algebra, is the **fundamental theorem of linear maps**, also known as the rank–nullity theorem:

$$\dim V \;=\; \dim \operatorname{null} T \;+\; \dim \operatorname{range} T.$$

Every structural fact about linear maps on finite-dimensional spaces is a consequence. From this single conservation law you derive, line by line: that in equal finite dimensions injectivity implies surjectivity and vice versa ([[Thm - Injectivity Equals Surjectivity in Finite Dimensions]]); that two finite-dimensional vector spaces are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension ([[Thm - Two Vector Spaces Isomorphic iff Same Dimension]]); that dimension is the complete invariant of a finite-dimensional vector space up to isomorphism; that $\dim \mathcal{L}(V, W) = mn$ once you choose any matrix representation. The rank–nullity theorem is to linear algebra what Lagrange's theorem is to finite group theory — the one counting fact from which most structural rigidity is forced.

The other thread running through these four sections is the **platonic-vs-representation** distinction. A linear map $T : V \to W$ is a platonic object: it exists before any basis is chosen, and it does not change when bases are reshuffled. A matrix $\mathcal{M}(T) \in \mathbf{F}^{m, n}$ is a *representation* of $T$ — its description after choosing a basis of $V$ and a basis of $W$. Change the bases and the matrix changes; the linear map does not. Matrix multiplication is the rule that makes composition of representations agree with composition of maps, $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$; this is not a coincidence but the *definition* of matrix multiplication, reverse-engineered from the demand that the bookkeeping be consistent. The change-of-basis formula $A = C^{-1} B C$ in [[Thm - Change of Basis Formula]] is the rule for translating between two representations of the same operator. Almost every confusion in linear algebra is a category error confusing the platonic object with one of its representations; the discipline of this chapter is to keep the two straight.

A reader entering this topic should have refreshed: [[Linear Algebra I — §1 Vector Spaces]] for the vector-space axioms, subspaces, and direct sums; [[Linear Algebra II — §2 Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces]] for span, linear independence, basis, and dimension. The group-theory analogue throughout is [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]] — the rank–nullity theorem is the dimension-counting shadow of the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem|first isomorphism theorem]], and the categorical formalism of $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ as "morphisms in the category of vector spaces" mirrors the morphism structure of groups.

# Concept Map

## §3A The Vector Space of Linear Maps

- **[[Def - Linear Map]]**
	- A **linear map** $T : V \to W$ is a function satisfying additivity $T(u + v) = Tu + Tv$ and homogeneity $T(\lambda v) = \lambda Tv$. Equivalently, $T$ preserves arbitrary linear combinations, $T(\sum \lambda_k v_k) = \sum \lambda_k Tv_k$ — this is the "true name" of linearity and the form one actually uses. The set of all such maps is denoted $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$; it carries pointwise addition and scalar multiplication and becomes itself a vector space over $\mathbf{F}$. The non-examples are instructive: $f(x) = x + 1$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is not linear because $f(0) \neq 0$, and $f(x) = x^2$ fails homogeneity except at $\lambda = 0, 1$.

- **[[Thm - Linear Map Determined by Action on Basis]]**
	- The **linear-map lemma**: given a basis $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ of $V$ and *any* vectors $w_1, \ldots, w_n \in W$, there exists a unique linear map $T : V \to W$ with $Tv_k = w_k$ for every $k$. This is the constructive heart of the subject: a linear map is determined by, and freely constructed from, its action on a basis. It is the reason a linear map is finite data once a basis is fixed, and it is the source of every existence proof in this chapter.

- **[[Ex - A linear map from R to R is multiplication by a scalar]]** (⭐)
	- Show that every linear map $T : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is of the form $Tx = \lambda x$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$, and identify $\lambda$.

- **[[Ex - Sum of injective linear maps need not be injective]]** (⭐)
	- Exhibit injective linear maps $S, T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ whose sum $S + T$ is not injective, showing that "is injective" is not a subspace condition on $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$.

- **[[Ex - Linear maps preserve linear combinations]]** (⭐)
	- Prove that the two axioms of linearity together imply $T(\sum_{k=1}^n \lambda_k v_k) = \sum_{k=1}^n \lambda_k Tv_k$ for every finite list of vectors and scalars — the "true name" form of linearity.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Endomorphism Ring *(from Ring Theory)*
> Composition gives $\mathcal{L}(V) = \mathcal{L}(V, V)$ a multiplication, distributive over the addition that already lives there. The result is a (non-commutative when $\dim V \geq 2$) **ring**, the **endomorphism ring** $\operatorname{End}(V)$. Every result of [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues|operator theory]] — eigenvalues, the minimal polynomial, Cayley–Hamilton — is really the structure theory of this ring's commutative subalgebras $\mathbf{F}[T]$. The two-sided ideals of $\operatorname{End}(V)$ are particularly clean: for finite-dimensional $V$ they are only $\{0\}$ and $\operatorname{End}(V)$ itself.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Linear Representation *(from Representation Theory)*
> A linear representation of a group $G$ on a vector space $V$ is, by definition, a [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphism]] $G \to \operatorname{GL}(V)$, where $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ is the group of invertible linear maps under composition. Once you have $\mathcal{L}(V)$ and the notion of invertibility, the entire subject of representation theory — characters, irreducibles, decomposition, the Peter–Weyl theorem — is available. The action of $G$ on $V$ is "abstract symmetry made concrete as linear symmetry".

## §3B Null Spaces and Ranges

- **[[Def - Null Space and Range]]**
	- The **null space** of $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is $\operatorname{null} T = \{v \in V : Tv = 0\}$, a subspace of $V$; the **range** is $\operatorname{range} T = \{Tv : v \in V\}$, a subspace of $W$. The null space measures the failure of injectivity — $T$ is injective if and only if $\operatorname{null} T = \{0\}$ — and the range measures the actual image of $T$ in $W$. Examples: the differentiation map $D : \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})$ has null space equal to the constants and range equal to all of $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})$; the multiplication-by-$x^2$ map has null space $\{0\}$ and range the polynomials with no constant or linear term.

- **[[Def - Rank of a Linear Map]]**
	- The **rank** of $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is $\dim \operatorname{range} T$, and the **nullity** is $\dim \operatorname{null} T$. Once a basis is fixed in $V$ and $W$, the rank of $T$ equals the rank of its matrix $\mathcal{M}(T)$, which is the dimension of the column span of $\mathcal{M}(T)$ in $\mathbf{F}^{m,1}$. A striking theorem says the row rank of a matrix equals the column rank, so "the rank" is a single well-defined number; this collapse is what makes the [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps|rank–nullity theorem]] symmetric in $V$ and $W$.

- **[[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps]]**
	- **Rank–nullity.** For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ with $V$ finite-dimensional, $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{null} T + \dim \operatorname{range} T$. This is the central theorem of finite-dimensional linear algebra: it says that whatever dimension is lost to the null space is gained back exactly in the range, so dimension is a conserved quantity moving from domain into image. From it follow injectivity-equals-surjectivity in equal finite dimensions, the dimension classification of finite-dimensional vector spaces, the dimension formula for $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$, and almost every structural rigidity result in the chapter.

- **[[Ex - Rank of a product is bounded by individual ranks]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove that $\dim \operatorname{range} ST \leq \min\{\dim \operatorname{range} S, \dim \operatorname{range} T\}$. The corollary $\operatorname{rank}(AB) \leq \min\{\operatorname{rank} A, \operatorname{rank} B\}$ for matrices follows immediately.

- **[[Ex - Null space and range of a projection]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For an idempotent operator $P \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ (satisfying $P^2 = P$), prove $V = \operatorname{null} P \oplus \operatorname{range} P$.

- **[[Ex - Existence of a linear map with prescribed null space and range]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For $X \subseteq V$ and $Y \subseteq W$ subspaces (with $V$ finite-dimensional, $Y$ finite-dimensional), there exists $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ with $\operatorname{null} T = X$ and $\operatorname{range} T = Y$ iff $\dim X + \dim Y = \dim V$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: First Isomorphism Theorem for Modules *(from Module Theory)*
> The rank–nullity theorem is the dimension-counting shadow of a sharper statement: there is an isomorphism $V / \operatorname{null} T \cong \operatorname{range} T$, the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem|first isomorphism theorem]] applied in the category of vector spaces (equivalently, of $\mathbf{F}$-modules). Taking dimensions of both sides recovers rank–nullity. The same statement generalises to all [[Def - Module|modules]] over a ring, where one only gets a quotient isomorphism (dimensions need not even make sense), and to all [[Def - Ring|rings]] via the first isomorphism theorem for rings.

## §3C Matrices

- **[[Def - Matrix of a Linear Map]]**
	- After choosing a basis $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ of $V$ and a basis $w_1, \ldots, w_m$ of $W$, the **matrix of $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$** is the $m$-by-$n$ matrix $\mathcal{M}(T)$ whose $k$-th column lists the coordinates of $Tv_k$ in the $W$-basis: $Tv_k = \sum_j A_{j, k} w_j$. The matrix is the representation of the platonic object $T$ in the chosen bases — it depends on the bases as much as on $T$. The matrix of a vector $v \in V$ is the column $\mathcal{M}(v)$ of its coordinates in the $V$-basis, and the central computational identity is $\mathcal{M}(Tv) = \mathcal{M}(T)\,\mathcal{M}(v)$: every linear map *acts* as left-multiplication by its matrix on coordinate vectors.

- **[[Def - Matrix Multiplication]]**
	- For $A \in \mathbf{F}^{m, n}$ and $B \in \mathbf{F}^{n, p}$, the **product** $AB \in \mathbf{F}^{m, p}$ has entries $(AB)_{j, k} = \sum_{r=1}^{n} A_{j, r} B_{r, k}$ — the dot product of row $j$ of $A$ with column $k$ of $B$. The formula looks arbitrary; the point of the [[Thm - Composition Corresponds to Matrix Multiplication|composition theorem]] is that this is the *only* definition under which $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$, so matrix multiplication is reverse-engineered from the demand that representation respect composition. Three equivalent views are useful: row-times-column for a single entry, column $k$ of $AB$ equals $A$ times column $k$ of $B$, and $Ax$ as a linear combination of the columns of $A$ with coefficients from $x$.

- **[[Thm - Composition Corresponds to Matrix Multiplication]]**
	- For $T \in \mathcal{L}(U, V)$ and $S \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ with fixed bases, $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\,\mathcal{M}(T)$. The point is twofold: it explains why the bizarre-looking row-times-column rule is *the* definition of matrix product (it has to be, to make the bookkeeping consistent), and it is the operational version of "linear maps act like matrix multiplication". The chain rule of multivariate calculus is this theorem applied to total derivatives, and matrix multiplication is non-commutative because composition of operators is non-commutative.

- **[[Ex - Matrix of a sum of linear maps is the sum of matrices]]** (⭐)
	- Show that the matrix-of-a-linear-map assignment $\mathcal{M} : \mathcal{L}(V, W) \to \mathbf{F}^{m, n}$ is itself a linear map: $\mathcal{M}(S + T) = \mathcal{M}(S) + \mathcal{M}(T)$ and $\mathcal{M}(\lambda T) = \lambda \mathcal{M}(T)$.

- **[[Ex - The space of linear maps has dimension mn]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that $\dim \mathcal{L}(V, W) = (\dim V)(\dim W)$ by exhibiting the isomorphism $\mathcal{L}(V, W) \to \mathbf{F}^{m, n}$ given by $T \mapsto \mathcal{M}(T)$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Matrix Group and Lie Group *(from Lie Theory)*
> The set $\mathbf{F}^{n, n}$ is more than a vector space: it carries an associative multiplication, and its invertible elements form a group. The **general linear group** $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{F})$ and its subgroups — $\operatorname{SL}_n$ (determinant 1), $\operatorname{O}(n)$ (orthogonal), $\operatorname{U}(n)$ (unitary), $\operatorname{Sp}_n$ (symplectic) — are the foundational **matrix Lie groups**. Once you have matrix multiplication, the geometry of these groups (as smooth manifolds with a compatible group structure) is the entire subject of Lie theory.

## §3D Invertibility and Isomorphism

- **[[Def - Invertibility and Isomorphism]]**
	- A linear map $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is **invertible** if there exists $S \in \mathcal{L}(W, V)$ with $ST = I_V$ and $TS = I_W$; the unique such $S$ is denoted $T^{-1}$. An **isomorphism** is an invertible linear map; two vector spaces are **isomorphic**, written $V \cong W$, if some isomorphism between them exists. Invertibility is equivalent to being both injective and surjective ([[Thm - Injectivity Equals Surjectivity in Finite Dimensions]] strengthens this in finite dimensions: either alone suffices). Isomorphism is the equivalence relation under which vector-space theory is conducted — "the same up to relabelling".

- **[[Def - Change of Basis Matrix]]**
	- For two bases $u_1, \ldots, u_n$ and $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ of the same space $V$, the **change-of-basis matrix** from $u$ to $v$ is $C = \mathcal{M}(I, (u_1, \ldots, u_n), (v_1, \ldots, v_n))$ — the matrix of the identity operator viewed as a map from "$V$ with $u$-basis" to "$V$ with $v$-basis". Its $k$-th column lists the $v$-coordinates of $u_k$. $C$ is always invertible, and $C^{-1}$ is the change-of-basis matrix from $v$ to $u$. Concretely: $C$ converts $u$-coordinates of a vector into $v$-coordinates.

- **[[Thm - Injectivity Equals Surjectivity in Finite Dimensions]]**
	- For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ with $\dim V = \dim W < \infty$, the following are equivalent: $T$ is invertible, $T$ is injective, $T$ is surjective. In the important special case $W = V$, this collapses to the slogan **injective ⟺ surjective for operators on a finite-dimensional space**, a finite-dimensional rigidity that fails dramatically in infinite dimensions (the multiplication-by-$x$ operator on $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})$ is injective but not surjective; the backward shift is surjective but not injective). The proof is one line from [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps|rank–nullity]].

- **[[Thm - Two Vector Spaces Isomorphic iff Same Dimension]]**
	- Two finite-dimensional vector spaces over the same field are isomorphic if and only if they have the same dimension. So **dimension is the complete invariant** of a finite-dimensional vector space: every $n$-dimensional $\mathbf{F}$-space is isomorphic to $\mathbf{F}^n$. As a corollary, $\mathcal{L}(V, W) \cong \mathbf{F}^{m, n}$ (via $T \mapsto \mathcal{M}(T)$, choosing bases), and so $\dim \mathcal{L}(V, W) = mn$.

- **[[Thm - Change of Basis Formula]]**
	- For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and two bases of $V$ with change-of-basis matrix $C$ from $u$ to $v$, the matrices $A = \mathcal{M}(T, (u))$ and $B = \mathcal{M}(T, (v))$ are related by $A = C^{-1} B C$. So the matrix of an operator in a new basis is obtained by **conjugation** by the change-of-basis matrix. This makes "similar matrices" — matrices related by $A = C^{-1} B C$ for some invertible $C$ — precisely "matrices of the same operator in different bases", and it sets up the entire program of finding a basis in which the matrix of $T$ is *simple* (diagonal, upper-triangular, Jordan-normal — see [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]] and beyond).

- **[[Ex - Composition of invertible linear maps is invertible]]** (⭐)
	- For invertible $S \in \mathcal{L}(U, V)$ and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$, the composition $TS$ is invertible with $(TS)^{-1} = S^{-1} T^{-1}$ — the "socks-and-shoes" reversal.

- **[[Ex - Matrix of identity in two bases is invertible]]** (⭐)
	- The change-of-basis matrices in opposite directions are mutual inverses: $\mathcal{M}(I, (u), (v))^{-1} = \mathcal{M}(I, (v), (u))$, by applying the composition theorem to $I \circ I = I$.

- **[[Ex - Inverse of a triangular matrix is triangular]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that if $A$ is an invertible upper-triangular square matrix, then $A^{-1}$ is also upper-triangular, and its diagonal entries are the reciprocals of those of $A$. The lower-triangular version is identical.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Jacobian *(from Multivariate Analysis)*
> The **total derivative** $Df_x : T_x M \to T_{f(x)} N$ of a smooth map $f$ between manifolds at a point $x$ is a [[Def - Linear Map|linear map]] between tangent spaces. In coordinate charts (a choice of basis), its matrix is the **Jacobian** — the matrix of partial derivatives $(\partial f_j / \partial x_k)$. The chain rule $D(g \circ f)_x = Dg_{f(x)} \circ Df_x$ is exactly [[Thm - Composition Corresponds to Matrix Multiplication|composition corresponds to matrix multiplication]], translated to derivatives. The platonic object is the total derivative; the Jacobian is its representation in coordinate bases; change of basis on the Jacobian is the chain-rule transformation rule for charts.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises in this chapter cluster around a small list of recurring goals. The most common is **proving injectivity, surjectivity, or invertibility** of a specific linear map — typically by computing its null space or its range and reading the answer off [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps|rank–nullity]]. A second goal is **identifying a dimension**: $\dim \operatorname{null} T$, $\dim \operatorname{range} T$, $\dim \mathcal{L}(V, W)$, or the dimension of some constructed subspace; here the conservation law of rank–nullity is the workhorse, often combined with the [[Def - Linear Map|linear-map lemma]] to construct maps with prescribed kernel and image. A third is **identifying a vector space up to isomorphism**, that is, finding an isomorphism with a familiar space (typically $\mathbf{F}^n$ or $\mathbf{F}^{m, n}$) and so reducing an abstract problem to coordinates. A fourth is **establishing or computing a matrix representation** — choosing bases, computing $\mathcal{M}(T)$, transferring the calculation to matrix algebra, and converting back. A fifth, which becomes central in later chapters, is **finding a basis in which $\mathcal{M}(T)$ is simple**: the change-of-basis formula $A = C^{-1} B C$ says any matrix can be replaced by any similar matrix, and the project of operator theory is to find the simplest representative of each similarity class. These five — prove-(in)jectivity, count-dimensions, identify-up-to-isomorphism, build-matrix-representation, simplify-by-changing-basis — are the targets, and they recur because each is one way to pin a linear map down: a linear map is understood when you know its rank, its kernel, its image, its similarity class, and a clean representative in some basis.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The assumptions in these problems are equally stereotyped, and the recurring routes between them and the targets are the substance of the chapter. **A homomorphism (linear map) is given or can be built** — once you have $T$, [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps|rank–nullity]] hands you a divisibility-style constraint on dimensions, and the [[Def - Linear Map|linear-map lemma]] often lets you *construct* a homomorphism by prescribing its action on a basis. **The dimensions of domain and codomain are given**, especially when they are equal and finite; equal dimensions trigger [[Thm - Injectivity Equals Surjectivity in Finite Dimensions]], converting half-statements into full ones, and trigger the dimension-classification of isomorphism. **A basis is given or can be chosen** — once a basis is named, every linear map is replaced by a matrix and every vector by a column, and a structural problem becomes a computational one. **An action on a specific generating set is given** — values $Tv_k$ on a basis specify $T$ uniquely, and any additional condition on $T$ becomes a condition on those values. **The map factors through a small space** — if $T = SR$ with $\dim$ of the intermediate space small, then $\dim \operatorname{range} T \leq$ that small dimension, a frequent source of rank bounds. The recurring move is to route source to target: a constructed homomorphism routes through rank–nullity to a dimension formula; equal dimensions plus injectivity routes through the inj/surj equivalence to invertibility; a chosen basis routes through matrix representation to a computation. The [[Linear Algebra III — §3A–D Linear Maps#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan and try each. Everything is self-contained: a reader with no linear-algebra background should be able to follow each operation.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Specify a linear map by its action on a basis.** Given a basis $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ of $V$ and any choice of vectors $w_1, \ldots, w_n \in W$ (no constraints at all), there exists a unique linear map $T : V \to W$ with $Tv_k = w_k$. This is the [[Thm - Linear Map Determined by Action on Basis|linear-map lemma]], and it is the construction tool: whenever a problem requires "produce a linear map with property P", solve for what the values on a basis must be and then invoke the lemma. *Trigger:* you need a map with prescribed null space, prescribed range, or prescribed values at some specific vectors. *Pattern:* "define $T$ on the basis $v_1, \ldots, v_n$ by $Tv_k = $ (your choice), extend linearly".

2. **Compute the null space and range by reduction to coordinates.** Pick a basis, write down the matrix $\mathcal{M}(T)$, and read off $\operatorname{null} T$ as the solutions of $\mathcal{M}(T) x = 0$ and $\operatorname{range} T$ as the column span of $\mathcal{M}(T)$. *Trigger:* any computational question about kernels, images, or ranks of a concretely given map. *Pattern:* abstract problem → matrix problem → solve homogeneous system → translate solution back to vectors.

3. **Apply rank–nullity to convert one dimension into another.** The conservation law $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{null} T + \dim \operatorname{range} T$ in [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps]] is the bread-and-butter dimension calculator. *Trigger:* you want a dimension you do not yet know, and you can find any one of $\dim V$, $\dim \operatorname{null} T$, $\dim \operatorname{range} T$. *Pattern:* "by rank–nullity, $\dim X = \dim V - \dim Y$".

4. **Convert injectivity to surjectivity (or vice versa) using equal finite dimension.** In [[Thm - Injectivity Equals Surjectivity in Finite Dimensions]], once $\dim V = \dim W$ and both are finite, injective is equivalent to surjective is equivalent to invertible. *Trigger:* a problem asks for surjectivity (the harder property) and you can prove injectivity (the easier one), or you want invertibility and have either one. *Pattern:* "$T$ is injective by [...]; since $\dim V = \dim W < \infty$, $T$ is also surjective, hence invertible".

5. **Identify a vector space up to isomorphism by counting dimensions.** Two finite-dimensional $\mathbf{F}$-spaces are isomorphic iff their dimensions agree ([[Thm - Two Vector Spaces Isomorphic iff Same Dimension]]). *Trigger:* you must show $V \cong W$, or you want to replace an awkward space with a familiar one like $\mathbf{F}^n$ or $\mathbf{F}^{m,n}$. *Pattern:* compute $\dim V$, compute $\dim W$, compare, then either exhibit the explicit isomorphism or appeal to the theorem.

6. **Pass between a linear map and its matrix.** Once bases are chosen, $\mathcal{M} : \mathcal{L}(V, W) \to \mathbf{F}^{m, n}$ is itself a linear isomorphism. Composition becomes matrix multiplication, inversion becomes matrix inversion, sum becomes matrix sum. *Trigger:* abstract calculation is stuck; you want to deploy computational tools. *Pattern:* fix bases, replace $T$ by $\mathcal{M}(T)$, replace $v$ by $\mathcal{M}(v)$, work in $\mathbf{F}^{m,n}$ and $\mathbf{F}^{n,1}$, then translate the answer back if needed.

7. **Use $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$ to turn composition into multiplication.** The [[Thm - Composition Corresponds to Matrix Multiplication|composition theorem]] is the operational reason matrix algebra exists. *Trigger:* you have a composition of linear maps and want to compute, or you have a product of matrices and want to interpret. *Pattern:* "$T = T_1 T_2 \cdots T_k$, so $\mathcal{M}(T) = \mathcal{M}(T_1) \cdots \mathcal{M}(T_k)$".

8. **Change basis to simplify.** Replace the matrix $B = \mathcal{M}(T, (v))$ in the $v$-basis by $A = C^{-1} B C = \mathcal{M}(T, (u))$ in a better-chosen $u$-basis ([[Thm - Change of Basis Formula]]). *Trigger:* the current matrix is awkward (full of nonzero entries, no apparent structure) and you suspect a "good" basis would make it diagonal, upper-triangular, or block-diagonal. *Pattern:* identify a basis adapted to $T$ — typically eigenvectors, invariant-subspace adapted, or kernel-and-complement adapted — and conjugate.

9. **Decompose a domain via $V = \operatorname{null} T \oplus U$ for some complement $U$.** Every finite-dimensional vector space and every subspace has a complement, so one can always write $V = \operatorname{null} T \oplus U$ for some $U$; the restriction $T|_U : U \to \operatorname{range} T$ is then an isomorphism. *Trigger:* you need to "lift" something from the range back to the domain, or you want to split the action of $T$ cleanly into its "killed" part and its "preserved" part. *Pattern:* "choose a complement $U$ of $\operatorname{null} T$ in $V$; then $T|_U$ is an isomorphism onto $\operatorname{range} T$".

10. **Build new linear maps by sum, composition, restriction, and extension.** $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is a vector space — closed under sum and scalar multiplication — and admits composition with maps into and out of it; restriction to a subspace yields a new linear map; the linear-map lemma extends a map defined on a subspace (when given the values on a basis of the subspace) to one on the whole space. *Trigger:* you need a map with several properties at once. *Pattern:* combine simpler maps with known properties — the engine of constructive proofs.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating "rank-nullity" as also applying when $V$ is infinite-dimensional.
> Rank–nullity is the statement $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{null} T + \dim \operatorname{range} T$, and it requires $V$ to be finite-dimensional — otherwise $\dim V$ is not even a single number to put on the left. The standard counterexample is the multiplication-by-$x$ map $T : \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})$: $\operatorname{null} T = \{0\}$ has dimension $0$, $\operatorname{range} T$ consists of polynomials with no constant term and has the same (infinite!) dimension as the source, but the source itself is the bigger thing — there is no single integer making the equation hold. The operation becomes legal exactly when the source space (or null space and range together) is finite-dimensional. In infinite dimensions one must instead state the quotient version $V / \operatorname{null} T \cong \operatorname{range} T$, which always holds, and dimension counting is replaced by cardinality of bases.

> [!warning] 2. Concluding that a linear operator is invertible because it is injective, when the domain is infinite-dimensional.
> The slogan "injective ⟺ surjective for an operator" is a *finite-dimensional* phenomenon and fails badly in infinite dimensions. The multiplication-by-$x^2$ operator on $\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})$ is injective (only the zero polynomial is killed) but not surjective — the constant polynomial $1$ is not in the range. The backward shift $(x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots) \mapsto (x_2, x_3, \ldots)$ on $\mathbf{F}^\infty$ is surjective but not injective. The operation becomes legal exactly when $\dim V = \dim W < \infty$, which is the precise hypothesis of [[Thm - Injectivity Equals Surjectivity in Finite Dimensions]]. In functional analysis one studies what extra conditions (compactness, boundedness on a Banach space, the Fredholm alternative) partially rescue the slogan.

> [!warning] 3. Identifying a vector space with its matrix representation.
> "$T$ *is* the matrix $\mathcal{M}(T)$" is a fast-and-loose identification that beginners make and then pay for. The matrix depends on a choice of basis; the linear map does not. Two different bases give two different matrices for the same map (related by [[Thm - Change of Basis Formula|change of basis]]), and conflating them leads to "proofs" that use a particular basis without realising it. The operation becomes legal when the basis is fixed and named, and the discipline is to write $\mathcal{M}(T, (v_1, \ldots, v_n), (w_1, \ldots, w_m))$ with the bases explicit whenever there is any ambiguity. The single biggest source of confusion in elementary linear algebra is this category error.

> [!warning] 4. Multiplying matrices in the wrong order — or assuming $AB = BA$.
> Matrix multiplication is non-commutative: for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ one has $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ but $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, so $AB \neq BA$. The order matters because it tracks the order of composition: $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$, with $S$ first because $S$ is applied *after* $T$. Reversing the matrix order corresponds to reversing the composition order, a different map entirely. The operation becomes legal exactly when both matrices arise from operators that commute as functions ($ST = TS$); commutativity is a substantive constraint, not a default.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic are won at the moment you classify what kind of problem you are looking at, so begin there. Most exercises are one of five types, and each has a characteristic source pattern and a characteristic route to the target.

If the problem **asks you to prove a specific linear map is injective, surjective, or invertible** — by far the most common task — your primary instrument is [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps|rank–nullity]]. The route is to identify either the null space or the range (whichever is easier), compute its dimension, and let the conservation law $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{null} T + \dim \operatorname{range} T$ supply the rest. To prove injectivity, show $\operatorname{null} T = \{0\}$; to prove surjectivity in finite dimensions with $\dim V = \dim W$, prove injectivity (cheaper) and invoke [[Thm - Injectivity Equals Surjectivity in Finite Dimensions]]. When the dimensions differ, you must address surjectivity directly: either by exhibiting preimages of a spanning set of $W$, or by observing $\dim \operatorname{range} T = \dim W$ from rank–nullity. The cheap structural shortcuts to scan for first: is $T$ visibly $S \circ R$ with $R$ surjective and $S$ surjective? Is $T$ visibly bijective on a basis? Does $T$ have a one-sided inverse $S$ with $ST = I$ or $TS = I$? Each is a structural argument that needs no computation.

If the problem **asks you to find or bound a dimension** — typically $\dim \operatorname{null} T$, $\dim \operatorname{range} T$, $\dim \mathcal{L}(V, W)$, or the dimension of some constructed subspace — the assumption pattern is that you have, or can build, a linear map between known-dimension spaces, and the route runs through rank–nullity again. The decision is which dimension to compute directly: the null space tends to be easier (a linear system to solve), the range tends to be harder (must exhibit elements). If both are awkward, build the map's matrix and compute its rank. For dimensions of "subspaces of $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$" — e.g., maps with $U \subseteq \operatorname{null} T$ — the move is to build a restriction map $\mathcal{L}(V, W) \to \mathcal{L}(U, W)$ and apply rank–nullity *to that map*. This pattern — apply rank–nullity to a *cleverly chosen meta-map* — is the most reusable trick in the chapter.

If the problem **asks you to identify a quotient space, or to identify a vector space up to isomorphism** — "show $V \cong W$", "what is $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{F}^n, \mathbf{F})$" — the strategy is to count dimensions and appeal to [[Thm - Two Vector Spaces Isomorphic iff Same Dimension]]. The technique is to either (i) compute both dimensions and observe they agree, or (ii) construct an explicit isomorphism, ideally using the [[Def - Linear Map|linear-map lemma]] by specifying values on a basis. The dimension argument is faster but less informative; the explicit isomorphism is slower but reveals the structure. When the question asks "what *is* this space", an explicit isomorphism to $\mathbf{F}^n$ for some $n$ is the right answer, not just "it has dimension $n$". The first-isomorphism-theorem template — find a surjective linear map $V \to W$ with kernel equal to the subspace you're quotienting by — extends to the linear algebra setting just as in [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2|group theory]].

If the problem **asks you to compute a matrix or to perform a calculation with matrices** — composing maps, inverting a matrix, finding the matrix of an operator in a new basis — the strategy is to choose bases carefully and use [[Def - Matrix Multiplication|matrix multiplication]], [[Thm - Composition Corresponds to Matrix Multiplication|composition equals product]], and [[Thm - Change of Basis Formula|change of basis]] as the computational tools. The decision is which basis to use. A bad basis gives a complicated matrix; a basis adapted to the structure of $T$ (eigenvectors, basis of $\operatorname{null} T$ plus complement, basis adapted to an invariant subspace decomposition) gives a clean matrix. For change-of-basis problems, the formula $A = C^{-1} B C$ is mechanical: compute $C$ as the matrix of $I$ between bases (its columns are the coordinates of the new basis in the old), invert it, and conjugate.

If the problem **asks for a structural fact about $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ itself** — "the noninvertible maps are not a subspace", "$\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ has dimension $mn$", "characterise the two-sided ideals of $\mathcal{L}(V)$" — the strategy is to exploit that $\mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is itself a vector space, and to use the matrix isomorphism $\mathcal{L}(V, W) \cong \mathbf{F}^{m, n}$ to translate to a concrete computational space. The technique is to fix bases of $V$ and $W$, work in matrices, and translate back. The matrix isomorphism converts statements about linear maps into statements about $m$-by-$n$ matrices over a field, which are much more concrete.

A meta-strategy threads through all of the above: **when stuck, choose bases**. The platonic-vs-representation principle is that a basis-free statement is conceptually clean but computationally inert; choosing a basis converts the problem into one about matrices and columns, where the toolkit is much richer. Conversely, when the matrix calculation is messy, lift back to the basis-free statement and look for structural shortcuts. The skill of the chapter is fluently moving back and forth: basis-free for clarity, basis-fixed for calculation. Every question in this chapter, at root, is the question **"what does this linear map do, and which basis makes that visible?"**

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps|Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps (rank–nullity)]]**: $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{null} T + \dim \operatorname{range} T$. This is *the* theorem of finite-dimensional linear algebra, used the way [[Thm - Lagrange's Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] is used in group theory: it is a free conservation law that converts one unknown dimension into another. Reach for it the moment any linear map is in sight and any dimension is requested; it usually solves the problem in one line. Its most powerful disguised use is *negative* — ruling out the existence of linear maps with prescribed kernel and range by a dimension obstruction, exactly the role Lagrange plays for subgroups.

- **[[Thm - Linear Map Determined by Action on Basis|Linear-map lemma]]**: a linear map is uniquely determined by, and freely constructible from, its values on a basis. This is the *construction tool* — whenever you need a linear map with property P, you ask "what must the values on a basis be?" and build it from there. The matching computational fact is the matrix isomorphism: once a basis is fixed, $T$ is just $n$ vectors in $W$, equivalently a column $n$-tuple in $W^n$, equivalently an $m$-by-$n$ matrix. Recognise its applicability whenever the problem says "show there exists a linear map such that...".

- **[[Thm - Injectivity Equals Surjectivity in Finite Dimensions|Injectivity ⟺ surjectivity in equal finite dimensions]]**: if $\dim V = \dim W < \infty$, then $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ is injective iff surjective iff invertible. The reusable move is to *prove the easier of the three* and get the others for free. Most often injectivity is easier (one equation to check: $Tv = 0 \Rightarrow v = 0$), and surjectivity is the harder property you actually wanted. It is the structural counterpart of the pigeonhole principle: there is no room for slack between domain and codomain when their dimensions agree. Internalising this slogan and recognising its failure in infinite dimensions are both essential.

- **[[Thm - Two Vector Spaces Isomorphic iff Same Dimension|Dimension is the complete invariant]]**: $V \cong W$ iff $\dim V = \dim W$ (finite). The reusable consequence is that every $n$-dimensional space *is* $\mathbf{F}^n$, up to isomorphism — every abstract problem about finite-dimensional vector spaces can be replaced by a concrete problem about $\mathbf{F}^n$ once a basis is chosen. The typical use is to dissolve a calculation in an unfamiliar space by transporting it to coordinates, then transporting the answer back. Combined with the matrix isomorphism, $\mathcal{L}(V, W) \cong \mathbf{F}^{m, n}$ has dimension $mn$.

- **[[Thm - Composition Corresponds to Matrix Multiplication|Composition equals matrix product]]** and **[[Thm - Change of Basis Formula|change of basis is conjugation]]**: $\mathcal{M}(ST) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(T)$, and $\mathcal{M}(T, (u)) = C^{-1} \mathcal{M}(T, (v)) C$ for the basis-change matrix $C$. The pair is reusable as the *translation dictionary* between the world of abstract linear maps and the world of matrices. Composition of operators is matrix multiplication; change of basis is similarity. Every operator-theoretic concept in [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues|later chapters]] — eigenvalues, diagonalisability, the minimal polynomial, Jordan form — is a question about similarity classes of matrices, which is to say the operator's identity as the bases vary.

# Bridges

1. **[[Def - Group]] and [[Def - Homomorphism]] — the general linear group.** The set $\operatorname{GL}(V)$ of *invertible* linear operators on $V$, under composition, is a group: composition is associative, the identity operator is the identity element, and every element has an inverse by definition. The non-invertible operators are excluded precisely because they fail the inverse axiom. Once $V$ is finite-dimensional, $\operatorname{GL}(V) \cong \operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{F})$ — the group of invertible $n$-by-$n$ matrices under matrix multiplication. A **linear representation** of a group $G$ on $V$ is exactly a [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphism]] $G \to \operatorname{GL}(V)$, and so the entire subject of representation theory is "the study of homomorphisms from a fixed group $G$ into matrix groups". The kernel of such a representation is a normal subgroup of $G$; faithful representations are exactly the injective ones, and they embed $G$ as a subgroup of some $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbf{F})$ — the linear-algebra analogue of Cayley's theorem.

2. **[[Def - Ring]] and [[Def - Ring Homomorphism]] — the endomorphism ring.** The set $\mathcal{L}(V) = \mathcal{L}(V, V)$ of operators on a single space carries two compatible operations: pointwise addition makes it a vector space (already), and composition makes it an associative product. The two distribute: $(S + T) R = SR + TR$ and $R(S + T) = RS + RT$. The result is a (non-commutative when $\dim V \geq 2$) **ring**, called the **endomorphism ring of $V$** and denoted $\operatorname{End}(V)$ or $\operatorname{End}_\mathbf{F}(V)$. Choosing a basis identifies it with the matrix ring $M_n(\mathbf{F}) = \mathbf{F}^{n, n}$. The ideals of this ring are extremely restricted — for finite-dimensional $V$, the only two-sided ideals are $\{0\}$ and the whole ring (Exercise 17 in LADR §3A makes this precise). The minimal polynomial of an operator $T$ in [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]] is the generator of the kernel of the ring homomorphism $\mathbf{F}[x] \to \operatorname{End}(V)$, $p(x) \mapsto p(T)$ — so the minimal polynomial *is* a [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring-homomorphism]] phenomenon.

3. **[[Def - Module]] and [[Def - Module Homomorphism]] — a linear map is a module homomorphism.** A [[Def - Module|module]] over a ring $R$ generalises a vector space (which is a module over a *field*); a [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]] is the structure-preserving map. When the ring $R$ is the field $\mathbf{F}$, the modules are exactly $\mathbf{F}$-vector spaces and the module homomorphisms are exactly linear maps — so "linear map" is literally a special case of "module homomorphism", and every theorem of this chapter is the field-coefficient case of a module-theoretic theorem. The [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps|rank–nullity theorem]] is the dimension-counting shadow of the first isomorphism theorem $V / \operatorname{null} T \cong \operatorname{range} T$, which holds for any module homomorphism with the same proof. Over a **PID** (like $\mathbf{F}[x]$, encoded into linear algebra as "a vector space equipped with an operator"), the structure theorem for finitely generated modules specialises to the Jordan-form theorem of [[Linear Algebra VIII — §8 Operators on Complex Vector Spaces]] — Jordan blocks are the elementary divisors of $V$ as an $\mathbf{F}[x]$-module.

4. **[[Def - The Total Derivative and Differentiability]] — the derivative is a linear map.** For a smooth map $f : M \to N$ between manifolds and a point $x \in M$, the **total derivative** $Df_x : T_x M \to T_{f(x)} N$ is by definition the unique linear map between tangent spaces approximating $f$ to first order. In local coordinates (a basis on each tangent space), its matrix is the **Jacobian** $(\partial f_j / \partial x_k)$, an instance of the matrix of a linear map. The **chain rule** $D(g \circ f)_x = Dg_{f(x)} \circ Df_x$ is exactly [[Thm - Composition Corresponds to Matrix Multiplication|composition corresponds to matrix multiplication]] for total derivatives, and the chain rule in coordinates — "Jacobian of a composition equals the product of Jacobians" — is matrix multiplication. The implicit function theorem (in [[Multivariate Analysis I — Differentiation in Several Variables]]) is the calculus statement that "the total derivative is invertible at $x$" implies "$f$ is locally invertible near $x$" — a smooth version of "invertibility transfers from the linearisation to the map", with [[Thm - Injectivity Equals Surjectivity in Finite Dimensions]] underlying the rigidity in finite dimensions. So multivariate calculus is, infinitesimally, this chapter.

5. **[[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem]] (group theory) — the algebraic source of rank–nullity.** For groups, the first isomorphism theorem says $G / \ker \varphi \cong \operatorname{im} \varphi$ for any homomorphism $\varphi$; the proof transfers verbatim from groups to rings to modules to vector spaces, replacing "normal subgroup" by "ideal", "submodule", or "subspace" respectively. For a linear map $T : V \to W$, this is $V / \operatorname{null} T \cong \operatorname{range} T$, and taking dimensions of both sides yields $\dim V - \dim \operatorname{null} T = \dim \operatorname{range} T$ — the [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps|rank–nullity theorem]]. So rank–nullity is not a primitive theorem about dimensions; it is the dimensional shadow of a much older isomorphism between a quotient and an image. The bridge runs the same way for [[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem|second]] and [[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem|third]] isomorphism theorems, which in the vector-space context become dimension-relation formulas for sums and quotients of subspaces.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: rank–nullity is the conservation law from which everything else is bookkeeping.** Almost every structural theorem in this chapter is a one- or two-line consequence of $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{null} T + \dim \operatorname{range} T$. Injectivity-equals-surjectivity in equal dimensions: rank–nullity plus the observation that $\dim \operatorname{null} T = 0$ and $\dim \operatorname{range} T = \dim W$ are equivalent when $\dim V = \dim W$. Two finite-dimensional spaces are isomorphic iff their dimensions agree: rank–nullity plus the existence of an injective map between any two equal-dimension spaces (construct one on a basis). $\dim \mathcal{L}(V, W) = mn$: rank–nullity applied to the matrix isomorphism. Even the change-of-basis formula, which looks like a calculation, is really the statement that a single platonic object (the operator $T$) has many representations, all of the same rank — and the rank is the same because the rank of an operator is basis-free, which is itself a corollary of rank–nullity. The whole chapter is the elaboration of a single dimension-conservation law, and a reader who internalises this can reconstruct everything else.

**The true name of a linear map is "the assignment of values on a basis, extended by linearity".** The formal definition of a linear map — preserves addition and scalar multiplication — is the right thing to *check*, but the wrong thing to *think*. Operationally, a linear map $T : V \to W$ is the data of $n$ vectors $Tv_1, \ldots, Tv_n \in W$ (the images of a chosen basis of $V$), with the rule that this data uniquely determines a linear map by linear extension. This is the content of the linear-map lemma, and it is what makes a linear map *finite data* despite being a function between potentially infinite sets. The matrix $\mathcal{M}(T)$ is just the names of those $n$ vectors written down as columns in the $W$-basis; matrix multiplication is the rule for composing two such data sets. Every construction in this chapter — null space, range, matrix, change of basis — becomes clean and obvious once you think of a linear map as "$n$ vectors in $W$, plus the agreement to extend by linearity".

**Platonic vs. representation: a linear map is the object, a matrix is one of its descriptions.** A linear map $T : V \to W$ is a function between vector spaces; it exists before any basis is chosen, and choosing a basis no more changes it than naming a country changes its borders. A matrix $\mathcal{M}(T)$ is a *description* of $T$ — its expression in two specific bases, one for the domain and one for the codomain. Different bases give different matrices for the same map (related by the change-of-basis formula $A = C^{-1} B C$), and the same matrix can describe different maps if the bases differ. The deep payoff of this distinction is in the next several chapters: similar matrices (those differing by conjugation) represent the *same* operator, so the eigenvalues, characteristic polynomial, minimal polynomial, trace, determinant, and rank are properties of the operator and not of any particular matrix description. The work of [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues|Chapter 5]] onward is to find, for each operator, a basis in which the matrix is *as simple as possible* — diagonal if you can, upper-triangular if you must, Jordan blocks in the worst case. The simpler the matrix, the more visible the operator's intrinsic properties.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: see a linear map, reach for rank–nullity.** This is the most useful single procedural association in finite-dimensional linear algebra. The moment a problem hands you a linear map $T \in \mathcal{L}(V, W)$ with $V$ finite-dimensional, the equation $\dim V = \dim \operatorname{null} T + \dim \operatorname{range} T$ is free, and it is almost always the key step. The other automatic reactions worth installing: **see an $n$-by-$n$ matrix and want invertibility — check injectivity, get surjectivity free**; **see a composition $ST$ — reach for $\operatorname{rank}(ST) \leq \min(\operatorname{rank} S, \operatorname{rank} T)$**; **see "two spaces isomorphic" — count dimensions**; **see "a linear map with prescribed values on a basis" — invoke the linear-map lemma to assert existence and uniqueness**; **see an awkward matrix — change basis to simplify**. Each is a one-sentence procedural shortcut that triggers on a visible feature of the problem; collecting them is the difference between "knowing linear algebra" and "doing linear algebra fluently".

**Inheritance: dimension is the only invariant, so everything else has to come from somewhere.** When studying a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ over $\mathbf{F}$, the *only* invariant property — the only thing you can say about $V$ without using extra structure — is its dimension. Everything else that looks like a property of $V$ alone (a basis, an inner product, a norm, a specific decomposition) is actually a piece of extra data, not intrinsic to $V$. Operators on $V$ have many invariants (rank, trace, determinant, eigenvalues, the minimal polynomial), but those are properties of the operator, not of $V$. This is why "every $n$-dimensional space is $\mathbf{F}^n$ up to isomorphism" feels both true and unsatisfying: it is true, and it does mean that *as vector spaces alone* all $n$-dimensional spaces are interchangeable — but in practice you study operators on those spaces, and operators do see the structure. The discipline is to remember that any structural fact about $V$ that is not pure dimension is using something outside the vector-space axioms.
