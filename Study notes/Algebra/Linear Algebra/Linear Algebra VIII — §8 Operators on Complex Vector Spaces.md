# Notation Registry

Throughout this topic $\mathbf{F}$ denotes either $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and $V$ is a finite-dimensional nonzero vector space over $\mathbf{F}$. The substantive results, however, require $\mathbf{F} = \mathbb{C}$; this is the standing convention of the chapter. Wherever a result holds for both fields it is flagged; otherwise read $\mathbf{F} = \mathbb{C}$.

> [!warning] Convention: complex field unless stated otherwise
> This chapter is mostly about $\mathbf{F} = \mathbb{C}$. The reason is the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: only over $\mathbb{C}$ does every operator have an eigenvalue, and only over $\mathbb{C}$ does the generalized eigenspace decomposition (and therefore the Jordan form) exhaust $V$. The nilpotent-operator results in §8A and the Jordan-basis theorem for nilpotent operators (§8C) are valid over $\mathbb{R}$ as well; the upgrade to all operators requires $\mathbb{C}$.

- $\mathcal{L}(V)$ — the space of linear operators $T : V \to V$
- $T^k$ — the $k$-fold composition $T \circ T \circ \cdots \circ T$; $T^0 = I$
- $I$ or $I_V$ — the identity operator on $V$
- $\lambda \in \mathbf{F}$ — typically a (potential) eigenvalue
- $\operatorname{null} S$ and $\operatorname{range} S$ — the null space (kernel) and range (image) of an operator $S \in \mathcal{L}(V)$
- $E(\lambda, T) = \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)$ — the **eigenspace** for $\lambda$
- $G(\lambda, T) = \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^{\dim V}$ — the **generalized eigenspace** for $\lambda$
- $\operatorname{mult}(\lambda) = \dim G(\lambda, T)$ — the **multiplicity** (= algebraic multiplicity) of $\lambda$
- $\dim E(\lambda, T)$ — the **geometric multiplicity** of $\lambda$
- $p_T(z) = (z - \lambda_1)^{d_1} \cdots (z - \lambda_m)^{d_m}$ — the **characteristic polynomial** of $T$, with $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$ the distinct eigenvalues and $d_k = \operatorname{mult}(\lambda_k)$
- $m_T(z)$ — the **minimal polynomial** of $T$ (the monic polynomial of least degree with $m_T(T) = 0$)
- $J_k(\lambda)$ — the **Jordan block** of size $k$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$: $\lambda$ on the diagonal, $1$ on the superdiagonal, $0$ elsewhere
- $\operatorname{tr} A$ — the **trace** of a square matrix $A$ (sum of diagonal entries)
- $\operatorname{tr} T$ — the **trace** of an operator (the trace of its matrix in any basis; well-defined by basis change)
- $\mathcal{M}(T, (v_1, \dots, v_n))$ — the matrix of $T$ in the ordered basis $v_1, \dots, v_n$

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **a non-diagonalizable operator on a complex vector space is just a direct sum of "eigenvalue times identity plus a small nilpotent thing"**, and that decomposition is the Jordan form. Every other result in this chapter is a consequence or a refinement of that statement.

The story begins from a defeat. In [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]] we discovered that on a complex space every operator has an eigenvalue, and we hoped that operators would be classified by their eigenvalues — that a basis of eigenvectors would always exist, reducing every $T$ to a diagonal matrix. This hope is wrong: the operator $T(z_1, z_2) = (z_2, 0)$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ has $0$ as its only eigenvalue, the eigenspace $E(0, T)$ is one-dimensional, and there is no basis of eigenvectors. An eigenvector "decomposition" of $\mathbb{C}^2$ is impossible because there are not enough eigenvectors. Yet there must be *some* description of $T$; it is, after all, a small operator on a small space. What is it?

The remedy is to enlarge the kit. Instead of insisting that $(T - \lambda I) v = 0$, ask only that $(T - \lambda I)^k v = 0$ for some $k$. Such a vector $v$ is a **generalized eigenvector**, and the set of all of them (plus zero) is the **generalized eigenspace** $G(\lambda, T) = \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^{\dim V}$. On the example above the generalized eigenspace $G(0, T)$ is the whole of $\mathbb{C}^2$, because $T^2 = 0$. The headline theorem of the chapter — the **generalized eigenspace decomposition** — says this remedy always works on a complex space:

$$V \;=\; G(\lambda_1, T) \,\oplus\, \cdots \,\oplus\, G(\lambda_m, T),$$

with each $G(\lambda_k, T)$ invariant under $T$ and with $T - \lambda_k I$ nilpotent on $G(\lambda_k, T)$. So every operator on a complex space, on each generalized eigenspace, *looks like* an eigenvalue plus a nilpotent — and that is a structure we can completely describe.

This structural backbone gives the **Jordan form**. On each generalized eigenspace the restricted nilpotent $T - \lambda_k I$ has a particularly nice basis — a chain $v_1, v_2, \dots$ with $(T - \lambda_k I) v_j = v_{j-1}$ — and stitching these chains together across all generalized eigenspaces produces a basis of $V$ in which the matrix of $T$ is block-diagonal with each block of the form $J_k(\lambda) = \lambda I + N$, where $N$ has $1$s on the superdiagonal and zeros elsewhere. That basis is a **Jordan basis** and the matrix is the **Jordan form**. The Jordan form is to operators on complex spaces what the prime factorization is to integers: a canonical decomposition into indivisible pieces, unique up to the order of the pieces. The same theorem — same statement, same content — is the **primary decomposition** of the $\mathbb{C}[x]$-module $V$ (where $x$ acts as $T$) into a direct sum of cyclic modules $\mathbb{C}[x]/(x - \lambda)^k$; the Jordan basis is just an explicit generator-and-power-of-$T$ basis for each cyclic summand. See `[[Thm - Jordan Normal Form]]` and `[[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem]]` in `[[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]]` for the module-theoretic side.

The chapter then collects three corollaries that the Jordan form makes free. First, **every invertible operator on a complex space has a square root**: split into generalized eigenspaces, factor as $\lambda_k (I + N_k/\lambda_k)$, and observe that $I + (\text{nilpotent})$ has a square root by truncating the Taylor series of $\sqrt{1 + x}$ — the series ends because the nilpotent vanishes after finitely many powers. Second, the **characteristic polynomial** $p_T(z) = \prod (z - \lambda_k)^{d_k}$ (with $d_k$ the multiplicity) has $T$ as a root: this is the **Cayley–Hamilton theorem**, and Axler's proof is now transparent because $p_T(T)$ acts on each $G(\lambda_k, T)$ as the nilpotent $(T - \lambda_k I)^{d_k}$ raised to high enough a power to kill the generalized eigenspace. Third, the **trace** equals the sum of the eigenvalues counted with multiplicity. The trace will become more important than its definition lets on: it is the simplest invariant of $T$ that can be read off any matrix representation without diagonalising — for any matrix $A$ representing $T$ in any basis, $\operatorname{tr} A$ is the same number — and it is the negative of the coefficient of $z^{n-1}$ in the characteristic polynomial.

The audience for this topic is assumed to have all of [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]] in working memory: eigenvalues, eigenvectors, polynomials of operators, minimal polynomial, upper-triangular form on $\mathbb{C}$, and diagonalisable operators. The chapter does not use inner products at all — it is purely linear-algebraic — and an inner product (with the spectral theorem from [[Linear Algebra VII — §7 Operators on Inner Product Spaces]]) is the cleanest *sufficient* condition for diagonalisability, but it is not necessary, and the present chapter is about operators that lack the spectral theorem's saving grace. If you have studied [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2]] and [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]] then this chapter is the structure theorem for finitely generated torsion modules over the PID $\mathbb{C}[x]$, written out in linear-algebra notation; if not, the connection will be made explicit but is not a prerequisite.

# Concept Map

## §8A Generalized Eigenvectors and Nilpotent Operators

- **[[Def - Generalized Eigenvector]]**
	- For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ and $\lambda$ an eigenvalue, a **generalized eigenvector** is a nonzero $v \in V$ with $(T - \lambda I)^k v = 0$ for some positive integer $k$. Equivalently $(T - \lambda I)^{\dim V} v = 0$, since the null spaces of the powers stabilise by dimension. Generalized eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent, and on a complex space they always assemble into a basis. The chain of generalized eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_k$ with $(T - \lambda I) v_j = v_{j-1}$ is the explicit basis for a Jordan block.

- **[[Def - Generalized Eigenspace]]**
	- The **generalized eigenspace** is $G(\lambda, T) = \{v \in V : (T - \lambda I)^k v = 0 \text{ for some } k\}$, equivalently $G(\lambda, T) = \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^{\dim V}$. It is a $T$-invariant subspace containing the eigenspace $E(\lambda, T) = \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)$, equal to it when $T$ is diagonalisable and strictly larger otherwise. Its dimension is the **multiplicity** (= algebraic multiplicity) of $\lambda$. The operator $T - \lambda I$ restricted to $G(\lambda, T)$ is nilpotent — this is what makes generalized eigenspaces the right building block.

- **[[Thm - Null Spaces of Powers Stabilize]]**
	- For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$, the sequence $\{0\} = \operatorname{null} T^0 \subseteq \operatorname{null} T^1 \subseteq \cdots$ is increasing; once two consecutive terms agree, all later terms agree; and equality must occur by the index $\dim V$ at the latest. As a corollary, $V = \operatorname{null} T^{\dim V} \oplus \operatorname{range} T^{\dim V}$. The intuition is dimensional: a strictly increasing chain of subspaces in $V$ adds at least one dimension at each step, so it cannot strictly increase beyond $\dim V$ steps.

- **[[Def - Nilpotent Operator]]**
	- An operator $N \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is **nilpotent** if $N^k = 0$ for some positive integer $k$; equivalently, every vector in $V$ is a generalized eigenvector of $N$ for the eigenvalue $0$. By the stabilisation result, if any power vanishes then $N^{\dim V} = 0$. Nilpotent operators are exactly the operators whose minimal polynomial is $z^m$ for some $m$, exactly the operators whose only eigenvalue is $0$ (over $\mathbb{C}$), and exactly the operators that admit an upper-triangular matrix with zeros on the diagonal. They are the chapter's building block: on each generalized eigenspace, $T - \lambda I$ is nilpotent.

- **[[Ex - A nilpotent operator on V satisfies T^dim V = 0]]** (⭐)
	- Show directly from the null-space stabilisation that if any power of $N$ is zero then $N^{\dim V} = 0$; deduce the equivalence of the three characterisations of nilpotence (zero power, only eigenvalue zero on $\mathbb{C}$, upper-triangular with zero diagonal).

- **[[Ex - Jordan form of a 3x3 nilpotent matrix]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Find the Jordan form (and a Jordan basis) of the explicit nilpotent matrix on $\mathbb{C}^3$ given by $A = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 9 & 0 \\ -7 & 9 & 6 \\ 4 & 0 & -6 \end{pmatrix}$. Verify $A^3 = 0$, $A^2 \neq 0$, locate a vector with $A^2 v \neq 0$, and read off the Jordan basis as $A^2 v, A v, v$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Functional Calculus on Operators *(from Functional Analysis)*
> Once you have generalized eigenspaces, you can apply functions other than polynomials to $T$. The **holomorphic functional calculus** assigns to any function $f$ holomorphic on the spectrum of $T$ an operator $f(T)$; on each generalized eigenspace it is the Taylor expansion of $f$ around $\lambda_k$ applied to $\lambda_k I + (\text{nilpotent})$, which terminates because the nilpotent piece dies. Square roots, logarithms, and $e^T$ are all instances. The infinite-dimensional analogue runs through the Riesz–Dunford integral representation.

## §8B Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition

- **[[Def - Algebraic and Geometric Multiplicity]]**
	- The **algebraic multiplicity** of an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $T$ is $\dim G(\lambda, T) = \dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^{\dim V}$, and the **geometric multiplicity** is $\dim E(\lambda, T) = \dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)$. The geometric multiplicity is at most the algebraic one, with equality exactly when $T$ is diagonalisable on $G(\lambda, T)$. The algebraic multiplicity also counts how many times $\lambda$ appears on the diagonal of any upper-triangular matrix for $T$, and on a complex space the algebraic multiplicities sum to $\dim V$.

- **[[Thm - Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition]]**
	- For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ on a complex space, $V = G(\lambda_1, T) \oplus \cdots \oplus G(\lambda_m, T)$ where $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$ are the distinct eigenvalues. Each $G(\lambda_k, T)$ is $T$-invariant, and $(T - \lambda_k I)|_{G(\lambda_k, T)}$ is nilpotent. This is the *structure theorem for operators on complex spaces* — exactly the primary decomposition of the $\mathbb{C}[x]$-module $V$ into a direct sum of primary cyclic factors. Every Jordan-form, characteristic-polynomial, square-root, and trace result in the chapter follows from it.

- **Characteristic polynomial.** With multiplicities $d_k = \dim G(\lambda_k, T)$, the **characteristic polynomial** of $T$ is $p_T(z) = (z - \lambda_1)^{d_1} \cdots (z - \lambda_m)^{d_m}$. It has degree $\dim V$, its zeros are precisely the eigenvalues, and the Cayley–Hamilton theorem says $p_T(T) = 0$. The characteristic polynomial is a multiple of the minimal polynomial, and they have the same zeros (= the eigenvalues). See `[[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton (Minimal-Polynomial Form)]]` for the proof via generalized eigenspaces — much cleaner than the determinant proof we will give in [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants]].

- **Block-diagonal form with upper-triangular blocks.** With respect to a basis adapted to the generalized eigenspace decomposition, the matrix of $T$ is block diagonal with each block a $d_k \times d_k$ upper-triangular matrix having $\lambda_k$ on every diagonal entry. This is the half-way house between the upper-triangular form of [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]] and the Jordan form of §8C. It is the matrix translation of the generalized eigenspace decomposition.

- **[[Ex - Sum of algebraic multiplicities equals dimension]]** (⭐)
	- Prove that on a complex vector space, $\sum_{\lambda} \dim G(\lambda, T) = \dim V$, and deduce the analogous bound on the number of distinct eigenvalues. Direct consequence of the generalized eigenspace decomposition and the dimension formula for direct sums.

- **[[Ex - Minimal polynomial divides any polynomial that kills the operator]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Use the division algorithm to show $m_T \mid q$ for any polynomial $q$ with $q(T) = 0$; apply to the characteristic polynomial to get $m_T \mid p_T$, and verify the zeros of $m_T$ are exactly the eigenvalues. The argument uses no generalized eigenspaces — it is a polynomial-arithmetic fact about $\mathbb{C}[x]$ — but the application to $p_T$ links to this chapter.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Primary Decomposition *(from Module Theory / Commutative Algebra)*
> The generalized eigenspace decomposition is literally the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]] of $V$ regarded as a $\mathbb{C}[x]$-module under $T$. Each $G(\lambda_k, T)$ is the $(x - \lambda_k)$-primary summand; the minimal polynomial of $T$ on $G(\lambda_k, T)$ is $(x - \lambda_k)^{k_k}$ for some exponent $k_k$, and the structure theorem for modules over a PID factors the whole $\mathbb{C}[x]$-module $V$ into a direct sum of $\mathbb{C}[x]/(x - \lambda_k)^{k_{k,i}}$. The linear-algebraic and module-theoretic pictures are the same theorem.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Holomorphic Functional Calculus *(from Functional Analysis)*
> For any function $f$ holomorphic on a neighbourhood of the spectrum of $T$, the operator $f(T)$ is well-defined and equals $\sum_k f(\lambda_k I + N_k)|_{G(\lambda_k, T)} = \sum_k \sum_j \frac{f^{(j)}(\lambda_k)}{j!} N_k^j |_{G(\lambda_k, T)}$, a finite sum since $N_k$ is nilpotent. In infinite dimensions one replaces the sum by a contour integral $f(T) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint f(z) (zI - T)^{-1}\, dz$.

## §8C Jordan Form and Square Roots

- **[[Def - Jordan Basis and Jordan Form]]**
	- A **Jordan basis** for $T$ is a basis of $V$ in which the matrix of $T$ is block diagonal with each block a **Jordan block** $J_k(\lambda)$ — $\lambda$ on the diagonal, $1$s on the superdiagonal, $0$s elsewhere. The matrix in such a basis is the **Jordan form** of $T$. The number and sizes of the Jordan blocks for each eigenvalue are invariants of $T$, so the Jordan form is unique up to the order of the blocks; the basis itself is not unique. The Jordan form is the precise statement that "an operator on a complex space is an eigenvalue plus a nilpotent shift, on each generalized eigenspace".

- **[[Thm - Existence of Jordan Form]]**
	- For $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ on a complex space there exists a Jordan basis. The heart of the proof is the special case of a nilpotent operator: starting from a vector $u$ with $T^{m-1} u \neq 0$ (where $m$ is the smallest power that kills $T$), one builds a chain $u, Tu, \dots, T^{m-1} u$ and a complementary invariant subspace, and induction on dimension does the rest. The general case follows by applying the nilpotent result to each restricted $(T - \lambda_k I)|_{G(\lambda_k, T)}$ and assembling.

- **[[Thm - Square Root of an Invertible Operator on a Complex Space]]**
	- Every invertible $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ on a complex space has a square root. The strategy: on each generalized eigenspace write $T|_{G(\lambda_k, T)} = \lambda_k (I + N_k / \lambda_k)$ with $N_k = (T - \lambda_k I)|_{G(\lambda_k, T)}$ nilpotent (using invertibility to ensure $\lambda_k \neq 0$); a square root of $I + N$ exists for any nilpotent $N$ by truncating the Taylor series of $\sqrt{1 + x}$; multiply by a complex square root of $\lambda_k$. The same template gives $k$th roots for any $k \geq 1$.

- **[[Ex - Operators with the same characteristic polynomial need not have the same Jordan form]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Exhibit on $\mathbb{C}^4$ two operators with characteristic polynomial $(z - 1)(z - 5)^3$ but different Jordan forms: one with a $3 \times 3$ Jordan block for $\lambda = 5$ and minimal polynomial $(z - 1)(z - 5)^3$; another with a $2 \times 2$ and a $1 \times 1$ block for $\lambda = 5$ and minimal polynomial $(z - 1)(z - 5)^2$. The characteristic polynomial knows the multiplicities; the Jordan form needs in addition the block-size partition for each eigenvalue, equivalently the dimensions $\dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^k$ for each $k$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Matrix Exponential *(from ODE Theory)*
> For $T = D + N$ with $D$ diagonalisable and $N$ nilpotent commuting (which is the Jordan decomposition), $e^{tT} = e^{tD} e^{tN}$ where $e^{tN}$ is a polynomial in $tN$ of degree at most $\dim V - 1$. On each generalized eigenspace, $e^{tT}|_{G(\lambda, T)} = e^{\lambda t} \sum_{j=0}^{m-1} \frac{(tN)^j}{j!}$. The factors $t e^{\lambda t}, t^2 e^{\lambda t}, \dots$ that appear in solutions of linear ODEs $\dot x = Ax$ with repeated eigenvalues are direct images of the Jordan block structure: a Jordan block of size $k$ for $\lambda$ contributes a $k$-dimensional family of solutions polynomial-in-$t$ of degree at most $k - 1$ times $e^{\lambda t}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Stability of Fixed Points *(from Dynamical Systems)*
> Linearising a smooth dynamical system $\dot x = f(x)$ at a fixed point $x_0$ gives $\dot \xi = (Df)_{x_0} \xi$ (cf. [[Def - The Total Derivative and Differentiability|total derivative]] and [[Def - Partial Derivatives and the Jacobian Matrix|Jacobian]]). The Jordan form of $(Df)_{x_0}$ determines the stability and the qualitative behaviour: real parts of the eigenvalues control exponential growth/decay, and a Jordan block of size $\geq 2$ with eigenvalue having real part $0$ is the borderline case where stability cannot be decided by spectrum alone (and a $t e^{0}$ — i.e. linear-in-$t$ — secular term appears).

## §8D Trace

- **[[Def - Trace]]**
	- For a square matrix $A$ the **trace** is the sum of the diagonal entries, $\operatorname{tr} A = \sum_i A_{i,i}$. The fundamental algebraic property is $\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA)$, which lets one define the **trace of an operator** $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ as the trace of any matrix representing it: by the change-of-basis formula $B = C^{-1} A C$ and $\operatorname{tr}(C^{-1} A C) = \operatorname{tr}(A C C^{-1}) = \operatorname{tr} A$, the value is basis-independent. The trace is the simplest non-trivial linear invariant of operators.

- **[[Thm - Trace Equals Sum of Eigenvalues]]**
	- On a complex space, $\operatorname{tr} T$ equals the sum of the eigenvalues of $T$ counted with multiplicity. It also equals the negative of the coefficient of $z^{\dim V - 1}$ in the characteristic polynomial. Together with the determinant (which equals the product of the eigenvalues with multiplicity — see [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants]]), the trace pins down the elementary symmetric polynomials of the eigenvalues, and via Newton's identities one can read off the power sums $\sum_k \lambda_k^p$ as well.

- **[[Ex - Trace is basis-independent and equals tr of any matrix]]** (⭐)
	- Verify directly from $\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \operatorname{tr}(BA)$ that for any invertible $C$, $\operatorname{tr}(C^{-1} A C) = \operatorname{tr} A$, hence the trace of an operator (as the trace of any of its matrices) is well-defined. Establish in addition $\operatorname{tr}(\lambda T) = \lambda \operatorname{tr} T$, $\operatorname{tr}(S + T) = \operatorname{tr} S + \operatorname{tr} T$, and $\operatorname{tr}(ST) = \operatorname{tr}(TS)$ — the trace is a linear functional on $\mathcal{L}(V)$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Trace as a Lie Algebra Functional *(from Lie Theory)*
> The trace is the differential of the determinant at the identity: $\det(I + \varepsilon X) = 1 + \varepsilon \operatorname{tr} X + O(\varepsilon^2)$. The kernel of the trace functional on $n \times n$ matrices is the **special linear Lie algebra** $\mathfrak{sl}(n) = \{X : \operatorname{tr} X = 0\}$, and the splitting $\mathfrak{gl}(n) = \mathfrak{sl}(n) \oplus \mathbb{F} \cdot I$ at the Lie-algebra level mirrors the matrix-group decomposition $\mathrm{GL}_n / \mathrm{SL}_n \cong \mathbb{F}^\times$ at the Lie-group level (via $\det$). The relation $\operatorname{tr}([X, Y]) = \operatorname{tr}(XY - YX) = 0$ is the Lie-algebraic version of $\operatorname{tr}(ST) = \operatorname{tr}(TS)$.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The questions one asks about an operator on a complex space have a striking uniformity once the generalized eigenspace machinery is in place. The first target is **classify the operator up to similarity**: find its Jordan form, equivalently the multiset of pairs (eigenvalue, Jordan-block-size partition). Two operators are similar — represent the same operator in different bases — exactly when they have the same Jordan form, so this target is the ultimate question about a single operator. The second target is **compute a polynomial of the operator**, especially the characteristic polynomial, the minimal polynomial, or expressions like $e^{tT}$ that arise from convergent power series: this routes through generalized eigenspaces because polynomials act block-by-block. The third target is **show an operator has a square root, a $k$th root, or a logarithm**, which is the same as taking a function holomorphic on the spectrum and applying it; the existence is automatic on each generalized eigenspace via a truncated Taylor series. The fourth target is **read off invariants from a matrix or a partial description**: from any matrix the trace gives the sum of eigenvalues, the determinant the product, and the characteristic polynomial the elementary symmetric polynomials of the eigenvalues — so the eigenvalues can be recovered as roots without ever diagonalising. The fifth target is **decide whether an operator is diagonalisable**: equivalently, whether $G(\lambda, T) = E(\lambda, T)$ for every $\lambda$, or whether the minimal polynomial has distinct roots. These five — classify, compute polynomial, extract root or function, read off invariants, decide diagonalisability — are the moves that almost every problem in the chapter is asking for.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypothesis pattern in chapter 8 problems is equally uniform. **The eigenvalues of $T$ are given** (or computable from a small matrix): this is the strongest source because the generalized eigenspace decomposition then immediately splits $V$ and reduces every question to nilpotent operators. **A polynomial annihilating $T$ is given**, frequently the characteristic or minimal polynomial: this constrains the Jordan form sharply — the exponents of $(z - \lambda)$ in the characteristic polynomial are the multiplicities, the exponents in the minimal polynomial are the sizes of the largest Jordan blocks. **The dimensions $\dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^k$ are given for various $k$**: from these one reads off the entire Jordan block structure for the eigenvalue $\lambda$ — the size-$j$ blocks contribute exactly $\dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^j - \dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^{j-1}$ to the cumulative dimension. **$T$ is invertible**, or equivalently $0$ is not an eigenvalue: this is the precondition for taking roots and for the connection $G(\lambda, T) = G(1/\lambda, T^{-1})$. **$T$ acts on a small space and a matrix is given explicitly**, where the strategy is to factor the characteristic polynomial, compute the null spaces of powers of $T - \lambda I$, and read off the Jordan form. The routing pattern is the constant: every source converts via the generalized eigenspace decomposition into a list of restricted nilpotent operators, and every target is some computation on those nilpotents.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves that almost every problem in chapter 8 is built from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything is self-contained.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Decompose into generalized eigenspaces.** Given $T$ on a complex space with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$, write $V = G(\lambda_1, T) \oplus \cdots \oplus G(\lambda_m, T)$ and study $T$ on each piece separately. *Trigger:* any global question about $T$, especially when it involves polynomials of $T$ or eigenvalue-related quantities. *Pattern:* "by the [[Thm - Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition|generalized eigenspace decomposition]], it suffices to prove the claim on each $G(\lambda_k, T)$, and on $G(\lambda_k, T)$ the operator $T$ is $\lambda_k I + N_k$ with $N_k$ nilpotent." This is the first move on essentially every problem of the chapter.

2. **Replace $T$ by $\lambda I + N$ on a generalized eigenspace.** After decomposing, work with the pair (eigenvalue, nilpotent) instead of the original $T$. *Trigger:* you have descended to a generalized eigenspace and want to compute. *Pattern:* a polynomial $q(T)$ acts on $G(\lambda_k, T)$ as $q(\lambda_k I + N_k)$, which is the Taylor expansion $\sum_j \frac{q^{(j)}(\lambda_k)}{j!} N_k^j$ — a *finite* sum because $N_k^{\dim G} = 0$. Functions holomorphic on the spectrum, square roots, exponentials, logarithms — all reduce to this finite Taylor computation.

3. **Use the null-space-stabilisation chain.** The strictly increasing sequence $\operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^k$ must stabilise by $k = \dim V$ (in fact at the multiplicity, but the dimension is the easy universal bound). *Trigger:* a problem mentions powers of $T - \lambda I$ or null spaces of powers. *Pattern:* "the sequence $\dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^k$ is non-decreasing, capped at $\dim V$, and stops growing once two consecutive terms agree — see [[Thm - Null Spaces of Powers Stabilize]]." The increments $\dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^k - \dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^{k-1}$ are the **dual partition** of the Jordan-block partition for $\lambda$.

4. **Read off the Jordan structure from the null-space dimensions.** For each eigenvalue $\lambda$, the number of Jordan blocks of size $\geq j$ for $\lambda$ equals $\dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^j - \dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^{j-1}$. *Trigger:* you need the Jordan form but want to avoid hunting for an explicit Jordan basis. *Pattern:* compute the four numbers $\dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^k$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ until they stabilise; the successive differences give the partition (number of blocks of each size); the partition reconstructs the Jordan form.

5. **Use polynomial constraints.** If $q(T) = 0$ for a polynomial $q$, then $q$ is a multiple of the minimal polynomial $m_T$, and the zeros of $m_T$ are exactly the eigenvalues. *Trigger:* the problem gives $T^k = 0$, $T^2 = T$, $T^k = I$, or any polynomial equation in $T$. *Pattern:* from $q(T) = 0$ extract the possible eigenvalues (zeros of $q$) and the maximal Jordan-block sizes (the exponents in the minimal polynomial divide those in $q$). For $T$ idempotent ($T^2 = T$), this immediately forces eigenvalues $0$ and $1$ and forces $T$ to be diagonalisable.

6. **Square-root by Taylor truncation.** A square root of $I + N$ for nilpotent $N$ exists and is given by the truncated Taylor series $\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \binom{1/2}{j} N^j$ where $k$ is any integer with $N^k = 0$. *Trigger:* the problem asks for a square root, $k$th root, logarithm, or exponential of an invertible operator. *Pattern:* write $T = \lambda(I + N/\lambda)$ on each generalized eigenspace, take $\sqrt{\lambda}$ (any choice in $\mathbb{C}$), take the Taylor series of $\sqrt{1 + x}$ truncated to fewer than $\dim V$ terms, glue.

7. **Read the trace and determinant off any matrix.** The trace and determinant are basis-independent; they equal the sum and product of the eigenvalues with multiplicity. *Trigger:* you need a single eigenvalue or a constraint on eigenvalues and don't want to diagonalise. *Pattern:* compute $\operatorname{tr} T$ from any matrix, equate with $\sum d_k \lambda_k$; if you also know $\det T$, you have two equations for the eigenvalues; if you know any other elementary symmetric function (or any other power-sum, since $\operatorname{tr} T^j = \sum d_k \lambda_k^j$), you can finish via Newton's identities.

8. **Build a Jordan basis from chains.** For each eigenvalue $\lambda$, start with vectors in $\operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^k \setminus \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^{k-1}$ where $k$ is the largest needed, then propagate down by applying $(T - \lambda I)$ to get a length-$k$ chain ending at an eigenvector. Repeat with shorter chains until all of $G(\lambda, T)$ is covered. *Trigger:* the problem asks for an explicit Jordan basis or matrix. *Pattern:* compute the partition by null-space dimensions (operation 4); for each part of size $k$, pick a vector $v$ with $(T - \lambda I)^k v = 0$ but $(T - \lambda I)^{k-1} v \neq 0$ and take the chain $(T - \lambda I)^{k-1} v, \dots, (T - \lambda I) v, v$; collect all chains.

9. **Pass to a $T$-invariant complement.** If $U \subseteq V$ is $T$-invariant of small dimension, there exists a $T$-invariant complement $W$ with $V = U \oplus W$ — this is exactly what makes the Jordan-basis induction work for nilpotent $T$. *Trigger:* you want to induct on $\dim V$ using an invariant subspace. *Pattern:* find a linear functional $\varphi$ that detects $U$ (i.e. $\varphi(T^{m-1} u) \neq 0$ for a vector $u$ generating $U$), set $W = \{v : \varphi(T^k v) = 0$ for all relevant $k\}$, verify dimensional balance.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming $E(\lambda, T) = G(\lambda, T)$
> It is tempting to conflate eigenspaces and generalized eigenspaces — they agree when $T$ is diagonalisable, which is the main case examples are drawn from. They do not agree in general: for $T(z_1, z_2) = (z_2, 0)$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ the only eigenvalue is $0$, $E(0, T) = \operatorname{span}(e_1)$ is one-dimensional, but $G(0, T) = \mathbb{C}^2$ is the whole space because $T^2 = 0$. The repair is to remember the equivalence $E(\lambda, T) = G(\lambda, T)$ for every $\lambda \iff T$ is diagonalisable $\iff$ the minimal polynomial has distinct roots; without that equivalence in hand, work with $G$, not $E$.

> [!warning] 2. Believing the characteristic polynomial determines the operator up to similarity
> It does not. The two operators on $\mathbb{C}^4$ with matrices $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ both have characteristic polynomial $(z - 1)(z - 5)^3$ but they have different Jordan forms (a $3 \times 3$ block versus a $2 \times 2$ and a $1 \times 1$ block for $\lambda = 5$) and so are not similar. The repair is the Jordan form, which is the full similarity invariant; equivalently, the pair (characteristic polynomial, minimal polynomial) is *not* enough in general — see [[Ex - Operators with the same characteristic polynomial need not have the same Jordan form]] — and one needs the entire partition for each eigenvalue.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming every operator on a real space has a Jordan form
> The Jordan form requires $\mathbf{F} = \mathbb{C}$: it requires every operator to have an eigenvalue, which is the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra. The operator on $\mathbb{R}^2$ given by $90^\circ$ rotation has no real eigenvalues and no Jordan form over $\mathbb{R}$. The repair is either to complexify ($V \otimes_\mathbb{R} \mathbb{C}$, run the complex theory, descend) or to settle for the **real Jordan form** in which complex-conjugate eigenvalue pairs $a \pm bi$ contribute $2 \times 2$ blocks $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ -b & a \end{pmatrix}$ instead of $1 \times 1$ blocks. Most of §8C goes through over $\mathbb{R}$ provided every eigenvalue is real; the failure mode is real eigenvalues being insufficient, not the nilpotent machinery.

> [!warning] 4. Computing a square root by "taking the square root of each matrix entry"
> A square root of an operator $T$ is an operator $R$ with $R^2 = T$. Entry-wise square-rooting is not a linear operation and does not yield $R^2 = T$ — it does not even make sense for negative entries over $\mathbb{R}$. The correct route is the structural one: split $V$ into generalized eigenspaces, factor as $\lambda(I + N/\lambda)$, take Taylor-truncated square roots of $I + N/\lambda$, and a complex square root of $\lambda$. The repair condition that the entry-wise calculation glosses over is the obvious one — that the eigenvalues are nonzero, equivalently $T$ is invertible; this is genuinely necessary, as $T(z_1, z_2, z_3) = (z_2, z_3, 0)$ on $\mathbb{C}^3$ has $0$ as its only eigenvalue and no square root, despite being a perfectly nice nilpotent operator.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

Almost every problem in chapter 8 begins with the same move, and it is so reliable that one can write it down as a meta-strategy: **decompose into generalized eigenspaces and study the nilpotent on each piece**. The first paragraph of any solution should be "by the [[Thm - Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition|generalized eigenspace decomposition]], it suffices to handle the case of an operator $\lambda I + N$ on a single generalized eigenspace, with $N$ nilpotent". If after this reduction the problem is still hard, the second move is invariably to introduce the nilpotent's Jordan basis or its null-space chain.

Within that meta-strategy the specific routes are short. If the problem **asks for the Jordan form or for similarity invariants**, compute $\dim \operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^k$ for each eigenvalue and each $k \geq 1$ until the sequence stabilises; the consecutive differences give the partition of block sizes by **operation 4** of [[Linear Algebra VIII — §8 Operators on Complex Vector Spaces#Legal Operations|Legal Operations]]. Two operators are similar exactly when these partitions agree for every eigenvalue, so similarity-questions reduce to this calculation. The minimal polynomial gives the *largest* block for each eigenvalue and the characteristic polynomial gives the *sum* of the block sizes (the multiplicity), so these two together determine the Jordan form when each multiplicity is at most $3$ but are insufficient in general — the discrepancy starts at multiplicity $4$.

If the problem **asks for $f(T)$** for some polynomial or holomorphic function, the route is to expand $f$ as a Taylor series around each eigenvalue and apply the result block-by-block: on $G(\lambda_k, T)$ the operator $f(T)|_{G(\lambda_k, T)}$ is $\sum_j \frac{f^{(j)}(\lambda_k)}{j!} N_k^j$, a finite sum because $N_k$ is nilpotent. For the characteristic polynomial $p_T$ specifically, this calculation kills every term (because $(T - \lambda_k I)^{d_k}|_{G(\lambda_k, T)} = 0$ as $d_k = \dim G(\lambda_k, T) \geq$ nilpotency index), giving the Cayley–Hamilton theorem essentially for free.

If the problem **asks for a square root, $k$th root, or logarithm** of an invertible operator, the strategy is exactly the previous one applied to the function $\sqrt{1 + x}$, $\sqrt[k]{1 + x}$, or $\log(1 + x)$ around $x = 0$, after factoring $T|_{G(\lambda_k, T)} = \lambda_k (I + N_k / \lambda_k)$. The Taylor series at $0$ has finite radius of convergence in general — but applied to a nilpotent the series is a finite sum and convergence is automatic. This is the canonical example of the trigger-reaction pattern "want a function of an invertible operator $\to$ split into eigenvalue plus nilpotent, expand, glue".

If the problem **asks you to find eigenvalues without diagonalising**, the trace and determinant routes are available. The trace gives the sum of eigenvalues with multiplicity, the determinant gives the product, and the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial give the elementary symmetric polynomials of the eigenvalues. Newton's identities convert these into the power sums $\operatorname{tr} T^j = \sum_k d_k \lambda_k^j$. When the problem already tells you most of the eigenvalues and asks for one missing one, the trace alone is usually enough — see [[Exercise Index - §8D Trace|Exercise 8D.7]] for the prototypical example.

If the problem **asks whether $T$ is diagonalisable**, the cheapest sufficient condition is that the minimal polynomial has distinct roots; the cheapest necessary-and-sufficient condition is that every generalized eigenspace equals the eigenspace, $G(\lambda, T) = E(\lambda, T)$ for every $\lambda$. On a complex space, $T$ is diagonalisable iff its minimal polynomial factors into *distinct* linear factors $(z - \lambda_1) \cdots (z - \lambda_m)$, with each $\lambda_k$ appearing with exponent $1$. So the test is to compute the minimal polynomial and check whether it has repeated roots.

The meta-strategy that threads through every problem is the **trigger-reaction "$T$ is given $\to$ decompose into generalized eigenspaces and replace by $\lambda I + N$"**. Every question in this chapter is really a question about the pair (eigenvalue, nilpotent), and the chapter's substance is the various things one can do with that pair. Once that pair is in hand, the rest is bookkeeping.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition|Generalized Eigenspace Decomposition]]**: $V = G(\lambda_1, T) \oplus \cdots \oplus G(\lambda_m, T)$ on a complex space. The single most-used theorem in the chapter. Reach for it whenever a question about $T$ can be reformulated as a question about each piece separately — which is almost always. The typical use is to convert a global question into the simpler question of how $T$ acts on a generalized eigenspace, where $T - \lambda I$ is nilpotent and amenable to direct calculation. Recognising disguised invocations: the moment polynomials of $T$, functions of $T$, or eigenvalue-related quantities appear, this is the theorem.

- **[[Thm - Existence of Jordan Form|Existence of Jordan Form]]**: every operator on a complex space has a basis in which its matrix is Jordan. This is the strongest similarity invariant — two complex matrices are similar iff they have the same Jordan form up to block ordering. Its typical use is in showing that some property holds for *all* operators by verifying it for Jordan blocks (whose structure is completely explicit) and then assembling. Examples: $\det(\lambda I - T) = p_T(\lambda)$, Cayley–Hamilton, the trace-equals-sum-of-eigenvalues identity, the existence of roots — all of these become block-by-block verifications.

- **[[Thm - Trace Equals Sum of Eigenvalues|Trace Equals Sum of Eigenvalues]]**: $\operatorname{tr} T = \sum d_k \lambda_k$ on a complex space. The cheapest invariant of an operator — it costs nothing, it is read off any matrix without diagonalising, and it combines linearly. The typical use is to extract an eigenvalue you don't yet know from a constraint, or to verify that an asserted eigenvalue is right. Reach for it whenever a problem gives a matrix and asks about eigenvalues; reach for the *family* $\operatorname{tr} T, \operatorname{tr} T^2, \operatorname{tr} T^3, \dots$ when more than one symmetric function is needed.

- **Null-space stabilisation** (`[[Thm - Null Spaces of Powers Stabilize]]`): $\operatorname{null}(T - \lambda I)^k$ stops growing by $k = \dim V$, with the successive dimensions encoding the Jordan-block sizes. The typical use is to extract the entire Jordan structure of $T$ at $\lambda$ from a finite list of null-space dimensions — a purely computational procedure, no basis-hunting required. Reach for it whenever the problem gives partial null-space information ("$\dim \operatorname{null} T^4 = 8$ and $\dim \operatorname{null} T^6 = 9$") or asks for the partition of block sizes.

- **The polynomial constraint dictionary** (`[[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton (Minimal-Polynomial Form)]]` and the minimal-polynomial-divides-anything-killing-$T$ lemma): a polynomial $q$ with $q(T) = 0$ controls the Jordan form through its factorisation. The exponents of $(z - \lambda)$ in $q$ bound from above the exponents in the minimal polynomial, which equal the *maximal* Jordan-block sizes. The typical use is to bound or characterise the Jordan structure when $T$ satisfies a known polynomial equation: idempotents $T^2 = T$ are diagonalisable with eigenvalues in $\{0, 1\}$; involutions $T^2 = I$ are diagonalisable with eigenvalues in $\{\pm 1\}$ over $\mathbb{C}$; projections, reflections, and roots-of-unity-actions all fall out of this dictionary.

# Bridges

1. **Module Theory and the Structure Theorem for Modules over a PID** — the generalized eigenspace decomposition is the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]] in disguise. Regard $V$ as a [[Def - Module|module]] over the polynomial ring $\mathbb{C}[x]$, where $x$ acts as $T$ — this is the construction `[[Def - The Module of a Linear Operator]]` formalises. Then $V$ is a finitely generated torsion module over the PID $\mathbb{C}[x]$ (torsion because the minimal polynomial annihilates it), and the [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem for modules over a PID]] expresses $V$ as a direct sum of *primary cyclic modules* $\mathbb{C}[x] / (x - \lambda_k)^{n_{k,i}}$. Each summand $\mathbb{C}[x] / (x - \lambda)^n$ is exactly a Jordan block of size $n$ for eigenvalue $\lambda$: a generator $\bar 1$ together with its images $\bar x, \bar{x}^2, \dots$ under multiplication by $x$ forms a chain, and in this basis $x$ acts as $\lambda I + N$ where $N$ is the nilpotent shift. So the Jordan form *is* the primary cyclic decomposition. The advantage of the module-theoretic viewpoint is that it works over any PID — replacing $\mathbb{C}[x]$ by $\mathbb{Z}$ gives the classification of finitely generated abelian groups (`[[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups]]`), and replacing $\mathbb{C}[x]$ by $\mathbb{R}[x]$ gives the **rational canonical form** (`[[Thm - Rational Canonical Form]]`), valid over any field. The disadvantage is that one loses concreteness; this chapter chooses concreteness, and pays the price of being restricted to $\mathbb{C}$ for the headline result. See `[[Ex - Possible Jordan forms from the minimal and characteristic polynomials]]` for a calculation that lives natively in module-theoretic language.

2. **Polynomial Rings and the PID $\mathbb{C}[x]$** — the algebraic engine of the entire chapter is that $\mathbb{C}[x]$ is a principal ideal domain ([[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]], [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]]). The minimal polynomial of $T$ is the monic generator of the ideal $\{q \in \mathbb{C}[x] : q(T) = 0\}$, which is principal because $\mathbb{C}[x]$ is a PID. The factorisation of the minimal polynomial into linear factors — which is where the *complex* hypothesis enters via the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra — is exactly the factorisation of the ideal generator into primes in $\mathbb{C}[x]$, and the primary decomposition of $V$ is the Chinese Remainder Theorem for $\mathbb{C}[x]$ applied to the cyclic module $\mathbb{C}[x] / (m_T(x))$. The same pattern recurs in number theory: the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups is the Chinese Remainder Theorem for $\mathbb{Z}$. Whenever you find yourself working with $\mathbb{C}[x]$, $\mathbb{R}[x]$, $\mathbb{Z}$, or $k[x]$ for a field $k$, the same package of results — primary decomposition, structure theorem, minimal-polynomial-as-ideal-generator — applies, because they are all PIDs.

3. **First Isomorphism Theorem and the Quotient $\mathbb{C}[x]/(p)$** — the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem|first isomorphism theorem]] specialises to a description of generalized eigenspaces. Each generalized eigenspace $G(\lambda, T)$ is invariant under $T$, and the restricted $T|_{G(\lambda, T)}$ has minimal polynomial $(x - \lambda)^{k}$ for some $k$; consequently, the map $\mathbb{C}[x] \to \mathcal{L}(G(\lambda, T))$ sending a polynomial $p$ to $p(T)|_{G(\lambda, T)}$ has kernel exactly the ideal $((x - \lambda)^k)$, and the first isomorphism theorem gives an embedding $\mathbb{C}[x] / (x - \lambda)^k \hookrightarrow \mathcal{L}(G(\lambda, T))$. If $G(\lambda, T)$ happens to be a cyclic module — equivalently, a single Jordan block — then this map identifies $G(\lambda, T)$ with $\mathbb{C}[x] / (x - \lambda)^k$ as a $\mathbb{C}[x]$-module. The generalized eigenspace decomposition is "modular arithmetic on $\mathbb{C}[x]$-modules": $V \cong \bigoplus_i \mathbb{C}[x]/((x - \lambda_{k(i)})^{m_i})$, and the explicit isomorphism is what a Jordan basis provides.

4. **Dynamical Systems and the Total Derivative** — for a smooth flow $\dot x = f(x)$ with fixed point $x_0$ (so $f(x_0) = 0$), linearisation at $x_0$ gives the operator $(Df)_{x_0} : T_{x_0} M \to T_{x_0} M$ on the tangent space, the [[Def - The Total Derivative and Differentiability|total derivative]] ([[Def - Partial Derivatives and the Jacobian Matrix|Jacobian]] in coordinates). The qualitative behaviour of the flow near $x_0$ is determined by the spectrum of $(Df)_{x_0}$: real parts of eigenvalues determine exponential growth or decay along the corresponding generalized eigenspace, imaginary parts determine rotation, and **Jordan blocks of size $\geq 2$ produce secular terms** of the form $t^j e^{\lambda t}$ in the solution of the linearised equation — these are the so-called *resonance* terms that complicate normal-form theory. The solution of the linear ODE $\dot \xi = (Df)_{x_0} \xi$ is $\xi(t) = e^{t (Df)_{x_0}} \xi_0$, which on each generalized eigenspace equals $e^{\lambda_k t} \sum_{j=0}^{d_k - 1} \frac{(t N_k)^j}{j!} \xi_0$, exhibiting the polynomial-in-$t$ behaviour explicitly. Stability theory of fixed points (the **Hartman–Grobman theorem** and its refinements) is built on top of this linear-algebra foundation; whenever stability of a dynamical system is in question, the Jordan structure of the Jacobian is what one is implicitly studying.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: an operator on a complex space is "eigenvalue plus nilpotent" on each generalized eigenspace.** This single sentence drives the whole chapter. Everything else — the Jordan form, the characteristic polynomial, the existence of square roots, the trace identity — is a corollary of the generalized eigenspace decomposition combined with the structural description "$T|_{G(\lambda, T)} = \lambda I + N$ with $N$ nilpotent". The unifying frame survives the move to infinite dimensions in the form of the **Jordan–Chevalley decomposition** of operators into a commuting sum of a diagonalisable and a nilpotent piece, and survives the move to Lie theory in the form of the semisimple–unipotent decomposition. To understand chapter 8 is to internalise this frame so deeply that "decompose into generalized eigenspaces" is the first thing you reach for whenever you see a complex operator that need not be diagonalisable.

**The true name of "generalized eigenvector" is "vector in the kernel of $(T - \lambda I)^k$ for some $k$".** The official definition — "$v \neq 0$ with $(T - \lambda I)^k v = 0$" — is unmemorable, and the equivalent "$(T - \lambda I)^{\dim V} v = 0$" is operational but unilluminating. The right way to think of a generalized eigenvector is as the *endpoint* of a chain $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k$ with $(T - \lambda I) v_j = v_{j-1}$ and $v_1$ an honest eigenvector — that is, the explicit basis of a Jordan block. Generalized eigenvectors are eigenvectors-plus-cascading-corrections; if you find an honest eigenvector $w$ of $T$ but the eigenspace is short of the multiplicity, the generalized eigenvectors are the vectors you need to make up the difference, and they are found by solving $(T - \lambda I) v = w$, then $(T - \lambda I) v' = v$, and so on until the equation has no solution. This procedural picture is what makes Jordan-basis hunting tractable in practice.

**The Jordan form is the prime factorisation of an operator.** The integers are prime-factored into prime powers $p^k$, and any two integers with the same prime factorisation are equal; an operator on a complex space is "prime-factored" into Jordan blocks $J_k(\lambda)$, and any two operators with the same multiset of Jordan blocks are similar. The analogy is exact and goes deeper: both are instances of the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID, applied to $\mathbb{Z}$ in the case of integers and to $\mathbb{C}[x]$ in the case of operators. The Jordan block $J_k(\lambda)$ corresponds to the cyclic module $\mathbb{C}[x] / (x - \lambda)^k$, the prime-power summand for the prime $(x - \lambda)$ in $\mathbb{C}[x]$. Everything we know about integer factorisation transfers — uniqueness, computability from invariants, the analogue of the Chinese Remainder Theorem (here the generalized eigenspace decomposition itself), the analogue of the Euler totient (the number of Jordan-basis choices). It is a remarkably tight correspondence, and recognising it once removes the mystery from canonical-form theorems everywhere.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: "have a non-diagonalisable operator $\to$ generalized eigenspaces are still enough".** When you see an operator that fails to have a basis of eigenvectors, the immediate move is to ask whether it has a basis of *generalized* eigenvectors — and on a complex space the answer is always yes. This converts every operator into a manageable piecewise-nilpotent structure. The pattern is the chapter's central problem-solving habit, and it is the basis for all subsequent procedures (Jordan basis, square root, $e^{tT}$, etc.).

**A trigger-reaction pattern: "want a function of an invertible operator $\to$ split into eigenvalue plus nilpotent, expand the function as a Taylor series, multiply".** Whenever a problem asks for $\sqrt{T}$, $T^{1/k}$, $\log T$, $e^{tT}$, or $f(T)$ for some holomorphic $f$ defined on the spectrum, the route is the same and the answer is essentially automatic. The Taylor expansion of $f$ around each eigenvalue applied to $\lambda I + N$ is a *finite* sum because $N$ is nilpotent; the only ingredient one has to supply is the function value and finitely many derivatives at each eigenvalue. The deep payoff of this pattern is the holomorphic functional calculus, but even in finite dimensions and for concrete problems it is the right move.

**The trace, the determinant, and the characteristic polynomial are different views of the same eigenvalue data.** The eigenvalues with multiplicity are encoded in the characteristic polynomial $p_T(z) = z^n - (\operatorname{tr} T) z^{n-1} + \cdots + (-1)^n \det T$. The trace is the sum of eigenvalues, the determinant is the product, and the intermediate coefficients are the elementary symmetric polynomials. From any matrix of $T$ one can read off all of these without diagonalising; conversely, knowing all of them is exactly knowing the eigenvalues (with multiplicity). The trace gets all the attention because it is the cheapest to compute — sum of diagonal entries — but it is one entry in a longer dictionary, and Newton's identities convert between elementary symmetric polynomials and power sums $\operatorname{tr} T^j$. When a problem gives you partial spectral information, identify which entry of this dictionary you have and which you want; the conversion is usually a one-liner.
