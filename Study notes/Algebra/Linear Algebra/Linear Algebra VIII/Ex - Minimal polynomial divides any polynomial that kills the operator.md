# Problem Statement

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbf{F}$ and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Let $m_T(z)$ be the minimal polynomial of $T$ — the monic polynomial of least positive degree such that $m_T(T) = 0$.

Prove that for any polynomial $q \in \mathbf{F}[z]$ with $q(T) = 0$, $m_T$ divides $q$ in $\mathbf{F}[z]$.

Apply this to the characteristic polynomial $p_T$ on a complex vector space: deduce that $m_T \mid p_T$, and that the zeros of $m_T$ are exactly the eigenvalues of $T$ (the same zeros as those of $p_T$, but possibly with smaller multiplicities).

**Recall:**

The objects are operators on a finite-dimensional space and polynomials applied to them.

A **polynomial of an operator** $p(T)$ is defined for $p(z) = a_0 + a_1 z + \cdots + a_n z^n \in \mathbf{F}[z]$ by $p(T) = a_0 I + a_1 T + \cdots + a_n T^n$. The map $p \mapsto p(T)$ is a [[Def - Ring|ring]] homomorphism $\mathbf{F}[z] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$. See [[Def - Polynomial of an Operator]].

The **minimal polynomial** $m_T$ is the unique monic polynomial of least positive degree such that $m_T(T) = 0$. It exists (the set $\{p : p(T) = 0\}$ is nonempty in finite [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]], because $\mathcal{L}(V)$ has finite [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] and so the powers $I, T, T^2, \dots, T^{(\dim V)^2}$ are linearly dependent, giving a nonzero polynomial vanishing on $T$) and is unique. See [[Def - Minimal Polynomial]] and [[Thm - Existence and Uniqueness of Minimal Polynomial]].

The **division algorithm** in $\mathbf{F}[z]$: for any polynomials $q, m \in \mathbf{F}[z]$ with $m \neq 0$, there exist unique $s, r \in \mathbf{F}[z]$ such that $q = s m + r$ and either $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg m$. See [[Thm - Division Algorithm for Polynomials (LA)]].

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *divisibility result in the polynomial [[Def - Ring|ring]] $\mathbf{F}[z]$* problem, lifted to the setting of operators via the substitution $z \mapsto T$. The class is drilled by all the foundational results that use the polynomial ring's PID structure to extract divisibility from minimality. The general meta-strategy: when you have a minimal object satisfying some property and an arbitrary object satisfying the same property, divide the arbitrary by the minimal and show the remainder must vanish.

**Assumption pattern.** The hypothesis is "$q(T) = 0$ for some polynomial $q$" — equivalently, $q$ is in the kernel of the evaluation map $p \mapsto p(T)$. We want to show $q$ is in the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] generated by $m_T$. This is the standard route: the kernel of evaluation is an ideal in the PID $\mathbf{F}[z]$, hence principal, hence generated by *one* polynomial. That polynomial, normalised to be monic, is the minimal polynomial.

**Theorem routing.** Single chain: [[Thm - Division Algorithm for Polynomials (LA)|division algorithm]] $\to$ minimality of $m_T$ $\to$ conclusion. Divide $q$ by $m_T$ to get $q = s m_T + r$ with $\deg r < \deg m_T$ or $r = 0$. Plug in $T$: $0 = q(T) = s(T) m_T(T) + r(T) = 0 + r(T) = r(T)$. So $r(T) = 0$. If $r \neq 0$, then $r$ is a nonzero polynomial of degree less than $m_T$ vanishing on $T$, contradicting the minimality of $m_T$. Hence $r = 0$, and $m_T \mid q$.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious move is to *use the division algorithm to set up a contradiction with minimality*. Students sometimes try to prove the result by direct manipulation of $q$ and $m_T$, but the division algorithm is the right tool: it produces a candidate "remainder" $r$ whose evaluation must be $0$, and the minimality of $m_T$ forces $r$ to be zero. Without the division algorithm, the proof has no natural footing. With it, the proof is essentially automatic.

# Solution

The strategy is to divide $q$ by $m_T$ and use the minimality of $m_T$ to force the remainder to be zero.

**Step 1: Apply the division algorithm.**

By [[Thm - Division Algorithm for Polynomials (LA)|the division algorithm]] in $\mathbf{F}[z]$, there exist unique $s, r \in \mathbf{F}[z]$ with $q = s m_T + r$ and either $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg m_T$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The division algorithm is a standard result in $\mathbf{F}[z]$: divide $q$ by the nonzero polynomial $m_T$ and write down the quotient and remainder. The remainder has lower degree than the divisor.

**Step 2: Evaluate at $T$ and use $q(T) = m_T(T) = 0$.**

Plug $T$ into the equation $q = s m_T + r$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The map $p \mapsto p(T)$ is a ring homomorphism $\mathbf{F}[z] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$ (see [[Def - Polynomial of an Operator]]). Hence $q(T) = (s m_T + r)(T) = s(T) m_T(T) + r(T)$. Substituting $q(T) = 0$ (hypothesis) and $m_T(T) = 0$ (by definition of minimal polynomial),
> $$0 = s(T) \cdot 0 + r(T) = r(T).$$

**Step 3: Conclude $r = 0$ by minimality.**

Suppose $r \neq 0$. Then $r$ is a nonzero polynomial of degree $< \deg m_T$ with $r(T) = 0$. But $m_T$ was assumed to be a polynomial of *least positive degree* with $m_T(T) = 0$, contradicting the existence of $r$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Suppose for contradiction $r \neq 0$. Let $c$ be the leading coefficient of $r$ — nonzero since $r \neq 0$. Then $\tilde r = r / c$ is a monic polynomial of the same degree as $r$, with $\tilde r(T) = r(T) / c = 0 / c = 0$.
>
> So $\tilde r$ is a monic polynomial with $\tilde r(T) = 0$ and $\deg \tilde r = \deg r < \deg m_T$. This contradicts the minimality of $m_T$ as the *monic polynomial of least positive degree* such that $m_T(T) = 0$.
>
> Hence $r = 0$, and $q = s m_T$, so $m_T \mid q$.

**Step 4: Apply to the characteristic polynomial.**

On a complex vector space, the Cayley–Hamilton theorem gives $p_T(T) = 0$, so $m_T \mid p_T$. The zeros of $m_T$ are then a subset of the zeros of $p_T$, hence a subset of the eigenvalues; conversely the zeros of $m_T$ are exactly the eigenvalues, because if $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue then $(z - \lambda) \mid m_T$ (using that $m_T(T) v = 0$ for an eigenvector $v$, $m_T(\lambda) v = m_T(T) v = 0$, so $m_T(\lambda) = 0$). Hence the zeros of $m_T$ and the zeros of $p_T$ coincide (both are the set of eigenvalues), though the multiplicities in $m_T$ may be smaller.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By the Cayley–Hamilton theorem ([[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton (Minimal-Polynomial Form)]]), $p_T(T) = 0$ on a complex space. Applying the result we just proved with $q = p_T$, we get $m_T \mid p_T$.
>
> Since both polynomials have the eigenvalues as roots — $p_T$ by definition, and $m_T$ because $(z - \lambda) \mid m_T$ for every eigenvalue $\lambda$ — and $m_T$ divides $p_T$, the multisets of roots satisfy: roots of $m_T \subseteq$ roots of $p_T$, with multiplicities of each root in $m_T$ at most the multiplicity in $p_T$. The roots without multiplicity are the same (both are the eigenvalues), but the multiplicities can be smaller in $m_T$.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbf{F}$ and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$, and let $m_T$ be the minimal polynomial of $T$. Suppose $q \in \mathbf{F}[z]$ with $q(T) = 0$.
>
> By the division algorithm in $\mathbf{F}[z]$, there exist unique $s, r \in \mathbf{F}[z]$ with $q = s m_T + r$ and either $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg m_T$.
>
> Evaluating at $T$:
> $$0 = q(T) = s(T) m_T(T) + r(T) = s(T) \cdot 0 + r(T) = r(T).$$
>
> Suppose for contradiction $r \neq 0$. Then $r / c$ (where $c$ is the leading coefficient of $r$) is a monic polynomial of degree $\deg r < \deg m_T$ with $(r/c)(T) = 0$. This contradicts the minimality of $m_T$.
>
> Hence $r = 0$, so $q = s m_T$, so $m_T \mid q$.
>
> **Application to the characteristic polynomial.** On a complex space, the Cayley–Hamilton theorem gives $p_T(T) = 0$. Applying the result with $q = p_T$: $m_T \mid p_T$. Combined with the (separately proved) fact that the zeros of $m_T$ include every eigenvalue, the zeros of $m_T$ and $p_T$ coincide as *sets*, though possibly with different multiplicities.
>
> $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Algebraic interpretation: kernel of evaluation
> The result is the structural statement that **the kernel of the evaluation homomorphism $\mathbf{F}[z] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$ sending $p \mapsto p(T)$ is the principal ideal generated by $m_T$.** Since $\mathbf{F}[z]$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]], every ideal is principal, hence the kernel is generated by *one* polynomial, which (after normalising to be monic) is $m_T$ by minimality of degree. The result of the exercise is exactly the statement "the kernel of the evaluation map is the ideal $(m_T)$", which is the cleanest algebraic formulation. The proof we gave is essentially the proof that $\mathbf{F}[z]$ is a PID, specialised to this kernel ideal.

# Key Takeaways

**The minimal polynomial is the ideal generator for the kernel of evaluation.** The set of polynomials $q \in \mathbf{F}[z]$ with $q(T) = 0$ is an ideal of $\mathbf{F}[z]$ — closed under addition and under multiplication by arbitrary polynomials. Since $\mathbf{F}[z]$ is a PID, this ideal is principal, generated by a single polynomial. The generator is unique up to a unit (i.e., up to a nonzero scalar in $\mathbf{F}$), and the choice that is monic is *the* minimal polynomial. So **the minimal polynomial is the canonical generator of the ideal $\{q : q(T) = 0\}$**, and the divisibility result is the assertion that every element of the ideal is a multiple of the generator. This perspective generalises: for any element $T$ of any $\mathbf{F}$-algebra, the kernel of evaluation $\mathbf{F}[z] \to A$ is a principal ideal in $\mathbf{F}[z]$, generated by the minimal polynomial of $T$. The reusable diagnostic is to think of "minimal polynomial" as "ideal generator", not as "polynomial of least degree" — the latter is the definition but the former is the conceptual content.

**The division algorithm is the workhorse of polynomial-ring proofs.** Almost every divisibility result in $\mathbf{F}[z]$ (and more generally in any Euclidean domain) uses the division algorithm: divide, evaluate, force the remainder to be zero by minimality. The pattern recurs in: proof that $\mathbf{F}[z]$ is a PID; proofs that [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] are principal in any Euclidean domain; the proof of unique factorisation in $\mathbf{F}[z]$; the construction of greatest common divisors; the construction of the field of fractions; and more. The transferable lesson is to recognise the *division-algorithm structure* in any divisibility question: there is a divisor (the minimal/distinguished object), a dividend (the arbitrary object), and the conclusion is that the dividend is a multiple of the divisor. The proof is "divide and force the remainder to vanish".

**The minimal polynomial has the same zeros as the characteristic polynomial, with possibly smaller multiplicities.** On a complex space, $m_T \mid p_T$, and the zeros of $m_T$ are exactly the eigenvalues (same as the zeros of $p_T$). The multiplicities can be smaller in $m_T$: the multiplicity of $\lambda$ in $p_T$ is the algebraic multiplicity = $\dim G(\lambda, T)$, while the multiplicity of $\lambda$ in $m_T$ is the size of the *largest Jordan block* at $\lambda$. These coincide iff there is a single Jordan block at $\lambda$. The pair $(p_T, m_T)$ thus encodes coarse information about the Jordan structure: the algebraic multiplicities (from $p_T$) and the largest block size at each eigenvalue (from $m_T$). The finer partition information requires the full kernel-dimension sequence. The transferable insight: characteristic and minimal polynomials are two *coordinates* of the Jordan structure — not enough to specify it completely, but enough for many problems.
