# Notation

Throughout, $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$, $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is an operator, and $p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_m x^m \in F[x]$ is a polynomial. For an integer $m \geq 0$, $T^m$ denotes the $m$-fold composite $T \circ T \circ \cdots \circ T$ ($m$ factors), with $T^0 = I$ by convention. When $T$ is invertible, $T^{-m} = (T^{-1})^m$. The full registry is on the parent page [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]].

# Axiom Motivation

The pivotal observation of the chapter is that an operator $T$ is in a deep sense "the action of $x$" — a scalar that operates on $V$. So just as we can multiply vectors by ordinary scalars (elements of $F$) and add the results, we can "multiply" them by polynomials in $T$ — that is, take linear combinations of iterates of $T$. The polynomial $p(T)$ is what we get when we make this idea precise.

Concretely: given a polynomial $p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_m x^m$, **substitute $T$ for $x$**:
$$p(T) = a_0 I + a_1 T + a_2 T^2 + \cdots + a_m T^m.$$
This is a linear combination of the operators $I, T, T^2, \ldots, T^m$, each of which is a well-defined element of $\mathcal{L}(V)$. So $p(T)$ is itself an operator on $V$.

The motivation for taking this seriously — rather than treating it as a curious notational device — is that the assignment $p \mapsto p(T)$ has rich algebraic properties: it is **a ring homomorphism**. Sums of polynomials map to sums of operators, products of polynomials map to compositions of operators, and scalar multiples are respected. This means the polynomial ring $F[x]$ acts on $V$ — through the operator $T$ — in a way that respects all the algebraic structure of $F[x]$. This is the bridge to the [[Def - The Module of a Linear Operator|$F[x]$-module]] viewpoint: a vector space $V$ together with an operator $T$ is the same data as an $F[x]$-module structure on $V$ with $x$ acting as $T$.

The homomorphism property has a profound consequence: **any two polynomials in the same operator $T$ commute**, i.e. $p(T) q(T) = q(T) p(T)$. This is because the polynomials $p$ and $q$ commute as elements of the commutative ring $F[x]$, and the homomorphism $F[x] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$ transports this commutativity to the images $p(T)$ and $q(T)$. The point is non-trivial: distinct operators rarely commute (the matrix $\mathcal{L}(V)$ is a *non*-commutative ring), but the subring generated by $T$ — that is, $\{p(T) : p \in F[x]\}$ — *is* commutative. The polynomial ring is the natural "abelian quotient" of $\mathcal{L}(V)$ along the chosen operator $T$.

A further structural fact: applying a polynomial to $T$ **respects the null space and range relationships** in a useful way. If $p(T)v = 0$, then $T$ also kills the orbit of $v$ in a controlled way (specifically, $T \ker p(T) \subseteq \ker p(T)$ — the kernel of $p(T)$ is $T$-invariant), and the range of $p(T)$ is similarly $T$-invariant. This is why polynomials in $T$ produce $T$-invariant subspaces *en masse*: every kernel and every range of a $p(T)$ is invariant under $T$, and by choosing $p$ cleverly one can produce structurally meaningful invariant pieces (eigenspaces, generalized eigenspaces, etc.).

What constraints does the polynomial ring impose on the operator? Through the **evaluation homomorphism** $p \mapsto p(T)$, the polynomial ring $F[x]$ maps into the finite-dimensional algebra $\mathcal{L}(V)$ (which has dimension $(\dim V)^2$). Since $F[x]$ is infinite-dimensional and $\mathcal{L}(V)$ is finite-dimensional, the homomorphism cannot be injective — its kernel must contain non-trivial polynomials. The kernel is an ideal of $F[x]$, principal because $F[x]$ is a PID, and its monic generator is the [[Def - Minimal Polynomial|minimal polynomial]] of $T$. This is the foundational existence argument: there *is* a nontrivial polynomial relation in $T$, because the polynomial ring is too big to fit into operators on a finite-dimensional space without collapse.

The notation $p(T)$ deserves a brief comment. It is **not** the value of $p$ at the operator $T$ — operators are not scalars. It is the result of formally substituting $T$ for $x$ in the polynomial expression, interpreting powers as compositions and scalar multiplication as left-multiplication of the operator by a scalar. The notation is suggestive but not literal: $p(T)$ is an *operator*, $p$ is a polynomial.

# The Definition

Let $V$ be a vector space over $F$, let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$, and let $p \in F[x]$ be a polynomial $p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots + a_m x^m$.

**Powers of $T$.** For nonnegative integer $k$, $T^k \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is defined by
$$T^0 = I, \qquad T^k = T \circ T^{k-1} \text{ for } k \geq 1.$$
When $T$ is invertible, $T^{-k} = (T^{-1})^k$. These powers satisfy the exponent rules $T^j T^k = T^{j+k}$ and $(T^j)^k = T^{jk}$ for nonnegative integers (and arbitrary integers when $T$ is invertible).

**Polynomial of an operator.** The **polynomial $p$ applied to $T$**, written $p(T)$, is the operator
$$p(T) = a_0 I + a_1 T + a_2 T^2 + \cdots + a_m T^m \in \mathcal{L}(V).$$

**Evaluation map.** The map
$$\Phi_T : F[x] \longrightarrow \mathcal{L}(V), \qquad p \longmapsto p(T)$$
is a ring homomorphism. That is, for $p, q \in F[x]$ and $c \in F$:
- $(p + q)(T) = p(T) + q(T)$;
- $(cp)(T) = c \cdot p(T)$;
- $(pq)(T) = p(T) \circ q(T)$;
- the constant polynomial $1$ is sent to the identity operator $I$.

**Commutativity in $T$.** A consequence of the homomorphism property: for any $p, q \in F[x]$,
$$p(T) \circ q(T) = (pq)(T) = (qp)(T) = q(T) \circ p(T).$$
That is, any two polynomials in the same operator commute.

**Invariance of kernels and ranges.** For any $p \in F[x]$, the subspaces $\ker p(T)$ and $\operatorname{im} p(T)$ are both $T$-invariant.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The evaluation map $\Phi_T : F[x] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$ is the unique $F$-algebra homomorphism sending $x$ to $T$. This is the **universal property of the polynomial ring** $F[x]$: it is the free commutative $F$-algebra on one generator, and any choice of an element $T$ in an $F$-algebra $A$ induces a unique algebra homomorphism $F[x] \to A$ sending $x$ to $T$.

For us, $A = \mathcal{L}(V)$, the algebra of operators on $V$, and the homomorphism $\Phi_T$ encodes "the polynomial functions of $T$". The image of $\Phi_T$ is the smallest subalgebra of $\mathcal{L}(V)$ containing $T$ — equivalently, the polynomial ring generated by $T$, often denoted $F[T]$. By the universal property, this image is isomorphic to $F[x]/\ker \Phi_T$, the quotient of the polynomial ring by its kernel.

The kernel of $\Phi_T$ is an ideal of $F[x]$, and because $F[x]$ is a PID, this ideal is principal: $\ker \Phi_T = (m_T)$ for a unique monic polynomial $m_T$ — the [[Def - Minimal Polynomial|minimal polynomial]] of $T$. So
$$F[T] \;\cong\; F[x]/(m_T)$$
as $F$-algebras. This identification is the algebraic core of the chapter: the subalgebra of $\mathcal{L}(V)$ generated by $T$ is a finite-dimensional commutative $F$-algebra of dimension exactly $\deg m_T$, and its structure is determined by how $m_T$ factors. When $m_T$ has distinct linear factors (over $F$), the quotient is a product of fields $F[x]/(m_T) \cong \prod F[x]/(x - \lambda_k) \cong \prod F$, and $T$ is diagonalizable. When $m_T$ has repeated factors, the quotient has nilpotent elements, and $T$ has Jordan blocks.

The viewpoint also clarifies the dictionary between operators and $F[x]$-modules. Specifying an operator $T$ on $V$ is the same as specifying the action of $F[x]$ on $V$ — concretely, the action of $x$ — through the homomorphism $\Phi_T$. So:
$$\big\{(V, T) : V \text{ vector space}, T \in \mathcal{L}(V)\big\} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \big\{F[x]\text{-modules}\big\}.$$
The polynomial $p(T)$ is the action of the polynomial $p \in F[x]$ on the module $V$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** $p(T)$ is **the action of the polynomial $p$ on $V$**, where $V$ has been given an $F[x]$-module structure with $x$ acting as $T$. From this perspective, $\ker p(T)$ is the **submodule of $V$ killed by $p$**, and $\operatorname{im} p(T)$ is the **submodule scaled by $p$**. The whole formalism is module-theoretic, and the spectrum / minimal-polynomial / Jordan-form / rational-canonical-form package is the module-theoretic structure theorem for finitely-generated $F[x]$-modules.

In **functional analysis**, the polynomial-of-an-operator construction generalises to the **functional calculus**: given a bounded operator $T$ on a Banach space and a function $f$ holomorphic in a neighbourhood of the spectrum $\sigma(T)$, the operator $f(T)$ is defined by a Cauchy integral
$$f(T) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma f(z)(zI - T)^{-1}\, dz,$$
where $\gamma$ is a contour encircling $\sigma(T)$. The polynomial functional calculus $p \mapsto p(T)$ is the special case of this when $f = p$ is a polynomial (the integrand is a rational function of $z$, and the residues at the poles of $(zI - T)^{-1}$ recover the polynomial). The **spectral mapping theorem** $\sigma(f(T)) = f(\sigma(T))$ is the functional-calculus version of the finite-dimensional fact that the eigenvalues of $p(T)$ are $\{p(\lambda) : \lambda \in \sigma(T)\}$.

In **representation theory**, $\Phi_T$ is the representation of $F[x]$ — viewed as a one-generator algebra — on $V$. Representations of $F[x]$ are exactly $F[x]$-modules, so this returns the same correspondence as above. The point of interest is that the **simple representations** of $F[x]$ (in our finite-dimensional setting) are the modules $F[x]/(p)$ for $p$ a monic irreducible polynomial, with dimension $\deg p$; the **indecomposable representations** are the modules $F[x]/(p^k)$ for monic irreducible $p$. The whole classification of indecomposable $F[x]$-modules over the algebraic closure $\overline F$ becomes the classification of Jordan blocks, $J_k(\lambda) \cong \overline F[x]/((x - \lambda)^k)$.

In **commutative algebra**, the **localisation** of $F[x]$ at a prime ideal $(x - \lambda)$ is $F[x]_{(x-\lambda)}$, a local ring with residue field $F$. The module $E(\lambda, T) = \ker(T - \lambda I)$ is the **simple part** of the localisation of $V_T$ at the prime $(x - \lambda)$; the generalised eigenspace $G(\lambda, T) = \ker (T - \lambda I)^{\dim V}$ is the **whole** localisation at $(x - \lambda)$, equivalently the support of $V$ at the prime $(x - \lambda)$. This is the bridge to the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]].

# Examples / Corollaries

**The constant polynomial $p(x) = c$.** Then $p(T) = cI$, the scalar-multiplication-by-$c$ operator. So constant polynomials act as scalar matrices.

**Linear polynomial $p(x) = x - \lambda$.** Then $p(T) = T - \lambda I$. The kernel $\ker(T - \lambda I)$ is the [[Def - Eigenvalue and Eigenvector|eigenspace]] $E(\lambda, T)$. So the eigenspace construction is the simplest polynomial-of-an-operator construction.

**Quadratic example: $p(x) = x^2 + 1$ applied to a real rotation.** Let $T(x, y) = (-y, x)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ — the $90°$ rotation. Then $T^2(x, y) = T(-y, x) = (-x, -y) = -(x, y)$, so $T^2 = -I$, hence $T^2 + I = 0$ as operators, i.e. $p(T) = 0$ for $p = x^2 + 1$. This says the minimal polynomial of the $90°$ rotation divides $x^2 + 1$, and in fact equals $x^2 + 1$ (the only smaller polynomial — degree $1$ — would say $T = \lambda I$ for some real $\lambda$, which is false).

**Differentiation: $D : \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})$, $Dp = p'$.** For the polynomial $f(x) = 7 - 3x + 5x^2$, the operator $f(D) = 7I - 3D + 5D^2$, that is, for any polynomial $q$,
$$(f(D))(q) = 7q - 3q' + 5q''.$$
This is a linear differential operator on the polynomial space, of order $2$.

**Restriction to a basis: matrix computations.** If $T$ has matrix $A$ in a basis of $V$, then $p(T)$ has matrix $p(A)$ in the same basis, where $p(A) = a_0 I + a_1 A + a_2 A^2 + \cdots$ is the polynomial-of-a-matrix construction. So polynomial-of-an-operator and polynomial-of-a-matrix are compatible under the basis isomorphism $\mathcal{L}(V) \cong M_n(F)$.

**Commutativity: $p(T) q(T) = q(T) p(T)$ always.** Take $p = x$, $q = x + 1$. Then $p(T) = T$, $q(T) = T + I$, and indeed $T(T + I) = T^2 + T = (T + I)T$ — they commute. More generally, since $F[x]$ is commutative and $\Phi_T$ is a homomorphism, any two polynomials in the same operator commute. This is *not* true of distinct operators: $T$ and $S$ on the same space need not commute unless special structure (like being in the same polynomial expression) forces it.

**Non-example: not every operator commuting with $T$ is a polynomial in $T$.** The reverse implication of "polynomial in $T$ ⟹ commutes with $T$" is false. The identity $I$ on $V$ commutes with every operator, including ones that are not polynomials in $T$. More substantially, if $T$ has distinct eigenvalues with one-dimensional eigenspaces and $S$ acts as different scalars on the various eigenspaces, $S$ commutes with $T$ but need not be a polynomial in $T$ unless the assignment $\lambda \mapsto \text{(scalar by which } S \text{ acts on } E(\lambda, T))$ is polynomial — which Lagrange interpolation makes possible when there are finitely many eigenvalues, but the operator-version is more delicate. The precise statement is that the **commutant** of $T$ (the set of operators commuting with $T$) is generally larger than the **polynomial algebra** $F[T]$; equality holds in the special case where $T$ has distinct eigenvalues (one Jordan block per eigenvalue).

**Corollary: $\ker p(T)$ and $\operatorname{im} p(T)$ are $T$-invariant.** *(Calibration check.)* For $v \in \ker p(T)$, compute $p(T)(Tv) = (p(x) \cdot x)(T)(v) = (x p(x))(T)(v)$, but also $(xp(x))(T)(v) = T(p(T)v) = T(0) = 0$. So $Tv \in \ker p(T)$. For $v \in \operatorname{im} p(T)$, write $v = p(T)w$; then $Tv = T(p(T)w) = p(T)(Tw) \in \operatorname{im} p(T)$.

**Corollary: $p(T)v = p(\lambda)v$ for an eigenvector $v$ of $T$.** If $Tv = \lambda v$, then $T^k v = \lambda^k v$ for all $k \geq 0$ (induction), so $p(T)v = (\sum a_k T^k) v = (\sum a_k \lambda^k) v = p(\lambda) v$. This is the **spectral mapping** at the level of eigenvalues: eigenvectors of $T$ are eigenvectors of $p(T)$, with eigenvalue $p(\lambda)$. *(Calibration check.)*

**Calibration check.** If you have absorbed the definition, you should be able to verify quickly: (a) $T \circ (a I + bT) = aT + bT^2 = (aI + bT) \circ T$ — i.e. $T$ commutes with any linear polynomial in $T$ — by direct expansion; (b) for $p(x) = x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1)$, the kernel $\ker p(T) = \ker(T - I)(T + I)$ contains both $E(1, T)$ and $E(-1, T)$; (c) if $T^2 = T$ (so $T$ is a projection), then $T(T - I) = 0$, so $(x^2 - x)(T) = 0$, hence the minimal polynomial of $T$ divides $x^2 - x = x(x - 1)$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Minimal Polynomial *(from Linear Algebra V, §5B)*
> The kernel of the evaluation map $\Phi_T : F[x] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$ is a principal ideal of $F[x]$, and its monic generator is the [[Def - Minimal Polynomial|minimal polynomial]] of $T$. This is the foundational structural invariant of $T$, encoding everything from eigenvalues to diagonalizability to canonical-form data.

> [!tip] $F[x]$-Module of an Operator *(from Modules II)*
> Specifying an operator on $V$ is the same as specifying an $F[x]$-module structure on $V$, with $x$ acting as $T$. The polynomial $p(T)$ is the action of $p$ on the module. See [[Def - The Module of a Linear Operator]]. The whole structure theory of operators on finite-dimensional spaces — including the Jordan and rational canonical forms — is the structure theory of finitely generated $F[x]$-modules, transported through this dictionary.

> [!tip] Holomorphic Functional Calculus *(from Functional Analysis)*
> The polynomial functional calculus $p \mapsto p(T)$ extends, for bounded operators on Banach spaces, to a calculus $f \mapsto f(T)$ for $f$ holomorphic in a neighbourhood of the spectrum, via a Cauchy integral. The **spectral mapping theorem** $\sigma(f(T)) = f(\sigma(T))$ is the generalisation of "eigenvalues of $p(T)$ are $p(\lambda)$" to this broader setting. The whole subject of operator algebras takes the functional calculus as a foundational tool.
