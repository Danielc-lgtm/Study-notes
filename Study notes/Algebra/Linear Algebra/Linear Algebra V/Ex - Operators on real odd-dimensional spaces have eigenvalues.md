# Problem Statement

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space of *odd* dimension, and let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. Prove that $T$ has at least one real eigenvalue.

**Recall:**

An **eigenvalue** of an operator $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is a scalar $\lambda \in F$ such that there exists $v \in V$ with $v \neq 0$ and $Tv = \lambda v$. See [[Def - Eigenvalue and Eigenvector]].

A real polynomial of odd degree always has at least one real root, by the [[Def - Division Algorithm and Factorization|factorization over ℝ]]: every nonconstant real polynomial factors as $c(x - \lambda_1) \cdots (x - \lambda_m)(x^2 + b_1 x + c_1) \cdots (x^2 + b_M x + c_M)$. The degree is $m + 2M$, so odd degree forces $m \geq 1$, giving at least one real linear factor.

![[Def - Minimal Polynomial#The Definition]]

The minimal polynomial of $T$ has degree at most $\dim V$, and (by [[Thm - Eigenvalues are Zeros of the Minimal Polynomial]]) its roots in $F$ are exactly the eigenvalues of $T$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a **structural existence problem** in the chapter's "find an eigenvalue without computing one" mode. The framework over $\mathbb{R}$ is more delicate than over $\mathbb{C}$: not every real operator has a real eigenvalue (rotation on $\mathbb{R}^2$ has none), but the parity of $\dim V$ — odd vs. even — interacts with the polynomial structure to force eigenvalues when dim is odd.

**Assumption pattern.** The signal is the *parity* of $\dim V$. Over $\mathbb{R}$, a polynomial of *even* degree might have no real roots (like $z^2 + 1$); but a polynomial of *odd* degree must have at least one real root (by the intermediate value theorem applied to the polynomial as a continuous real-valued function). The minimal polynomial of $T$ has $\deg m_T \leq \dim V$, so when $\dim V$ is odd, $m_T$ has degree at most $\dim V$; but a priori $\deg m_T$ might be even. The trick is to combine the parity argument with the *real factorization* structure of $m_T$ — and to use the recursion that the "irreducible quadratic factor" of $m_T$ produces an *even-dimensional* invariant subspace.

**Theorem routing.** The route is via the [[Def - Minimal Polynomial|minimal polynomial]] and the [[Def - Division Algorithm and Factorization|real factorization theorem]]. Either:
- (Approach A) Argue directly: $m_T$ as a real polynomial of degree $\leq \dim V$ factors into linear and irreducible quadratic factors. If *all* factors of $m_T$ are irreducible quadratic, then $\deg m_T$ is even. So if $m_T$ "covers" all of $V$ (in a sense to be made precise), the parity of $\dim V$ matters. The cleanest implementation uses an **invariant-subspace lemma**: each irreducible quadratic factor of $m_T$ contributes an *even-dimensional* invariant subspace, so the "$m_T$-quadratic" parts span an even-dimensional subspace of $V$. The complementary part is generated by linear factors of $m_T$ and therefore has at least one real eigenvalue.
- (Approach B) Inductive: Pick a vector $v \in V$ and look at the polynomial $p$ annihilating $v$ (the per-vector annihilator). If $p$ has a real root, we have a real eigenvalue. Otherwise, $p$ factors entirely into irreducible quadratics. Use this to build an *even-dimensional* $T$-invariant subspace containing $v$; then descend on the quotient (which is also odd-dimensional, since odd $-$ even $=$ odd). Continue.

We follow Approach B (the LADR proof, Theorem 5.34), which uses an induction-on-dimension argument with the key auxiliary result that **on an invariant subspace, $T - \lambda I$ acts as a non-injective map iff $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue, and the null space of $T^2 + bT + cI$ with $b^2 < 4c$ has even dimension**.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious move is to **use induction on the dimension of $V$**, stepping down by units of 2 (not 1) to keep the dimension odd at each step. The induction step: if $m_T$ has no real linear factor, then $m_T = q(z) \cdot (z^2 + bz + c)$ for some $b^2 < 4c$, and the subspace $\operatorname{range}(T^2 + bT + cI)$ is $T$-invariant of strictly smaller dimension that is **still odd**, by a key lemma about kernels of $T^2 + bT + cI$ having even dimension. Then apply the inductive hypothesis to the smaller invariant subspace.

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Build a polynomial relation from a dependent list of iterates** (operation 1). Used to produce the per-vector annihilator polynomial or, equivalently, the minimal polynomial of the restriction.

2. **Factor an annihilating polynomial to expose an eigenvalue** (operation 2). The factorization over $\mathbb{R}$ has linear and irreducible-quadratic factors; the linear factors give real eigenvalues.

3. **Translate operator equations to divisibility of $m_T$** (operation 4). The structural argument uses $m_T$'s factorization to extract the eigenvalue.

4. **Restrict to an invariant subspace** (operation 9). The key inductive step descends from $V$ to an invariant subspace of smaller, still odd, dimension.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> A real polynomial of *odd* degree has at least one real root (by the intermediate value theorem, or by the [[Def - Division Algorithm and Factorization|real factorization theorem]]). Apply this to the [[Def - Minimal Polynomial|minimal polynomial]] of $T$ when its degree is odd.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> The minimal polynomial $m_T$ has degree at most $\dim V$, but its degree need not equal $\dim V$ — and need not even share its parity. So we cannot just say "$\deg m_T$ is odd because $\dim V$ is odd". The argument must dig deeper.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> If $m_T$ has *no* real linear factor, $m_T$ factors entirely into irreducible quadratic factors, so $\deg m_T$ is even. The key auxiliary fact: for an irreducible quadratic factor $z^2 + bz + c$ (with $b^2 < 4c$), the operator $T^2 + bT + cI$ has **even-dimensional null space**. This is the key lemma — prove it separately.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Induction on $\dim V$. Base case $\dim V = 1$: every nonzero vector is an eigenvector for the unique scalar $\lambda$ such that $Tv = \lambda v$.
>
> Inductive step (with step $2$): Suppose $\dim V = n \geq 3$ is odd, and the result holds for all odd-dimensional spaces of dimension $< n$.
> If $m_T$ has a real linear factor $z - \lambda$, then $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue (by [[Thm - Eigenvalues are Zeros of the Minimal Polynomial]]) — done.
> Otherwise, $m_T$ has an irreducible quadratic factor $z^2 + bz + c$. Use it to find an even-dimensional invariant subspace; the quotient is odd-dimensional and smaller, so induction applies.

# Solution

The plan is to induct on $\dim V$ in steps of $2$. At each step, if $m_T$ has a real linear factor, we are done (a real root of $m_T$ is a real eigenvalue). Otherwise, $m_T$ has an irreducible quadratic factor $z^2 + bz + c$ (with $b^2 < 4c$). Use the lemma that $\ker(T^2 + bT + cI)$ has *even* dimension to construct an even-dimensional $T$-invariant subspace, then induct on the quotient. The "trick" is that subtracting an even number from an odd one keeps the result odd, so we eventually reach a smaller odd-dimensional space, where the inductive hypothesis gives the eigenvalue.

**Step 1: A real polynomial of odd degree has a real root.**

By the [[Def - Division Algorithm and Factorization|real factorization theorem]], every nonconstant real polynomial factors into linear and irreducible-quadratic factors. The degree equals $\#\text{linear} + 2 \cdot \#\text{quadratic}$. So odd degree forces $\#\text{linear} \geq 1$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Apply [[Def - Division Algorithm and Factorization|the real factorization theorem]]: every nonconstant $p \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ factors as
> $$p(x) = c (x - \lambda_1) \cdots (x - \lambda_m) (x^2 + b_1 x + c_1) \cdots (x^2 + b_M x + c_M),$$
> with $c, \lambda_i, b_j, c_j \in \mathbb{R}$ and $b_j^2 < 4 c_j$ for each $j$. The degree of $p$ is $m + 2M$. If this is odd, then $m + 2M$ is odd, hence $m$ is odd (since $2M$ is even), hence $m \geq 1$. So $p$ has at least one real root $\lambda_1$.

**Step 2: The null space of $T^2 + bT + cI$ (for $b^2 < 4c$) has even dimension.**

This is the central lemma. The proof uses that the polynomial $z^2 + bz + c$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ and induct on dimension.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $U = \ker(T^2 + bT + cI)$. By [[Def - Polynomial of an Operator|the invariance of polynomial-kernels]], $U$ is $T$-invariant. Replacing $V$ with $U$ and $T$ with $T|_U$, it suffices to prove: if $T^2 + bT + cI = 0$ on $V$ (i.e., $U = V$) and $b^2 < 4c$, then $\dim V$ is even.
>
> *No eigenvector exists for $T$ on $V$.* Suppose $v \in V$ is a nonzero eigenvector with $Tv = \lambda v$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$. Then by the relation $T^2 + bT + cI = 0$ applied to $v$:
> $$0 = (T^2 + bT + cI)v = (\lambda^2 + b\lambda + c) v.$$
> Since $v \neq 0$, $\lambda^2 + b\lambda + c = 0$. But $z^2 + bz + c$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ (since $b^2 < 4c$), so no real root exists. Contradiction. So $T$ has no eigenvectors on $V$.
>
> *Build $V$ from $2$-dimensional invariant pieces.* Take any $w \in V$ with $w \neq 0$. Consider $W = \operatorname{span}(w, Tw)$. Then:
> - $\dim W = 2$: otherwise $Tw$ would be a scalar multiple of $w$, making $w$ an eigenvector — contradiction.
> - $W$ is $T$-invariant: $Tw \in W$ by definition, and $T(Tw) = T^2 w = -bTw - cw \in W$ (using the relation).
>
> So we have a $2$-dimensional $T$-invariant subspace $W$.
>
> Now grow this. Let $U$ be a maximal $T$-invariant even-dimensional subspace of $V$ containing $W$; suppose $U \neq V$. Pick $w' \in V \setminus U$ and consider $W' = \operatorname{span}(w', Tw')$. As before, $\dim W' = 2$ and $W'$ is $T$-invariant. Now $U \cap W'$ is a $T$-invariant subspace of $W'$; if it were $1$-dimensional, it would be spanned by an eigenvector — but no eigenvectors exist, so $U \cap W' = 0$ (since it cannot be all of $W'$ — that would mean $w' \in U$, contradicting $w' \notin U$).
>
> Then $U + W'$ is $T$-invariant and has dimension $\dim U + 2$ (still even), strictly bigger than $U$ — contradicting maximality. So $U = V$, and $\dim V$ is even.

**Step 3: Induct on $\dim V$ to extract the real eigenvalue.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Induct on $\dim V$, in steps of $2$. *Base case* $\dim V = 1$: any nonzero $v \in V$ satisfies $Tv = \lambda v$ for the unique scalar $\lambda$ with $Tv = \lambda v$ (since $Tv \in V = \operatorname{span}(v)$). So $\lambda$ is a real eigenvalue.
>
> *Inductive step* $\dim V = n \geq 3$, $n$ odd, and the result holds for all odd-dimensional real vector spaces of dimension $< n$.
>
> *Case 1: $m_T$ has a real linear factor.* By [[Thm - Eigenvalues are Zeros of the Minimal Polynomial]], real roots of $m_T$ are real eigenvalues of $T$. So a real linear factor of $m_T$ corresponds to a real eigenvalue, done.
>
> *Case 2: $m_T$ has no real linear factor.* By the real factorization theorem, $m_T$ factors entirely into irreducible quadratic factors over $\mathbb{R}$. Pick one such factor, say $z^2 + bz + c$ (with $b^2 < 4c$). Then $m_T(z) = (z^2 + bz + c) \cdot q(z)$ for some real polynomial $q$ of degree $\deg m_T - 2$.
>
> Now $m_T(T) = 0$, so $(T^2 + bT + cI) q(T) = 0$. In particular, $q(T)$ maps $V$ into $\ker(T^2 + bT + cI)$. Equivalently, $\operatorname{range}(q(T)) \subseteq \ker(T^2 + bT + cI)$.
>
> Set $W = \operatorname{range}(T^2 + bT + cI)$. We claim $W$ is a proper $T$-invariant subspace of $V$ of odd dimension, on which we can induct.
>
> - $W$ is $T$-invariant by [[Def - Polynomial of an Operator|invariance of polynomial-ranges]].
> - $W \neq V$: by the assumption that $q(T) \neq 0$ (since $\deg q < \deg m_T$, by minimality of $m_T$), there is some $v$ with $q(T)v \neq 0$, hence $q(T)v \in \ker(T^2 + bT + cI) \setminus \{0\}$, so $\ker(T^2 + bT + cI) \neq 0$, hence $W = \operatorname{range}(T^2 + bT + cI) \neq V$ (by rank-nullity). [Actually, more carefully: we need that the kernel is nontrivial, which follows from this argument; then $W$ has dimension $\dim V - \dim \ker < \dim V$.]
> - $\dim W$ is odd: by [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Linear Maps|rank-nullity]], $\dim V = \dim \ker(T^2 + bT + cI) + \dim W$. By Step 2, $\dim \ker(T^2 + bT + cI)$ is even. So $\dim W = \dim V - (\text{even})$ has the same parity as $\dim V$, which is odd. So $\dim W$ is odd.
>
> Since $W$ is a proper $T$-invariant subspace of odd dimension less than $\dim V$, the inductive hypothesis applies to $T|_W$: $T|_W$ has a real eigenvalue $\lambda$ with eigenvector $w \in W \subseteq V$. So $\lambda$ is a real eigenvalue of $T$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Let $V$ be a real vector space of odd finite dimension $n \geq 1$ and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. We prove $T$ has a real eigenvalue by induction on $n$.
>
> *Base case $n = 1$:* Any nonzero $v \in V$ satisfies $Tv \in V = \operatorname{span}(v)$, so $Tv = \lambda v$ for the unique scalar $\lambda$. $\lambda$ is a real eigenvalue with eigenvector $v$.
>
> *Inductive step:* Suppose the result holds for all odd-dimensional real vector spaces of dimension less than $n \geq 3$. Let $V$ be of odd dimension $n$ and $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$.
>
> Consider the minimal polynomial $m_T \in \mathbb{R}[z]$, with $\deg m_T \geq 1$. By the [[Def - Division Algorithm and Factorization|real factorization theorem]], $m_T$ factors as a product of linear and irreducible-quadratic factors:
> $$m_T(z) = c (z - \lambda_1) \cdots (z - \lambda_k) (z^2 + b_1 z + c_1) \cdots (z^2 + b_l z + c_l),$$
> with $b_j^2 < 4 c_j$.
>
> *Case 1: $k \geq 1$.* Then $m_T$ has a real root $\lambda_1$, which by [[Thm - Eigenvalues are Zeros of the Minimal Polynomial]] is an eigenvalue of $T$. So $\lambda_1$ is a real eigenvalue, done.
>
> *Case 2: $k = 0$.* Then $m_T$ has only irreducible-quadratic factors. Pick one such factor $z^2 + bz + c$ (with $b^2 < 4c$), so $m_T(z) = q(z)(z^2 + bz + c)$ for some real polynomial $q$ of degree $\deg m_T - 2 < \deg m_T$.
>
> Consider the subspace $U = \ker(T^2 + bT + cI)$ and $W = \operatorname{range}(T^2 + bT + cI)$. Both are $T$-invariant by [[Def - Polynomial of an Operator|invariance of polynomial-kernels-and-ranges]]. By rank-nullity, $\dim U + \dim W = \dim V = n$.
>
> *Sub-claim 1: $U$ is nonzero.* We have $m_T(T) = q(T)(T^2 + bT + cI) = 0$, so $q(T)$ maps $V$ into $U$. The polynomial $q$ has $\deg q = \deg m_T - 2 < \deg m_T$, so $q(T) \neq 0$ by minimality of $m_T$. So there is $v$ with $q(T)v \neq 0$, and $q(T)v \in U$. Hence $U \neq 0$.
>
> *Sub-claim 2: $\dim U$ is even.* This is Step 2 above. Briefly: on $U$, the operator $T^2 + bT + cI$ is zero by construction, so $T$ satisfies the irreducible-quadratic relation $T^2 + bT + cI = 0$ on $U$. The argument of Step 2 (Axler 5.33) — $T$ has no eigenvectors on $U$ (since the quadratic is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$, no real root exists) and $U$ is built from $2$-dimensional invariant pieces — gives $\dim U$ even.
>
> *Sub-claim 3: $\dim W$ is odd and $\dim W < n$.* $\dim W = n - \dim U$. Since $n$ is odd and $\dim U$ is even, $\dim W$ is odd. Since $U \neq 0$, $\dim W < n$.
>
> *Conclude:* By the inductive hypothesis applied to $T|_W$ on the odd-dimensional space $W$ of dimension $< n$, $T|_W$ has a real eigenvalue $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ with eigenvector $w \in W$. Then $w \in W \subseteq V$ and $Tw = (T|_W) w = \lambda w$, so $\lambda$ is a real eigenvalue of $T$. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Illegal but tempting alternative route
> One might try to argue: "the minimal polynomial has degree at most $\dim V$, which is odd; so $\deg m_T$ is odd; so $m_T$ has a real root by Step 1." This is **wrong** because $\deg m_T$ is bounded by $\dim V$ but is not equal to $\dim V$ — and the parity of $\deg m_T$ can differ from that of $\dim V$. For instance, on $\mathbb{R}^3$ with $T = \text{(rotation in } xy\text{-plane)} \oplus \text{(zero on } z\text{-axis)}$, the minimal polynomial is $m_T = (z^2 + 1) z$ of degree $3$ (odd!) — but in other configurations, the minimal polynomial could be just $z^2 + 1$ of degree $2$ (even). The argument *would* go through if we knew $\deg m_T$ is odd, but we do not. The actual proof uses the *space* of dimension odd as the inductive variable, with the $\ker$-has-even-dimension lemma keeping us in the odd-dimensional regime.

# Key Takeaways

**The parity of $\dim V$ over $\mathbb{R}$ is a non-trivial obstruction to eigenvalue existence.** Over $\mathbb{C}$, parity does not matter — every operator has an eigenvalue regardless of $\dim V$. Over $\mathbb{R}$, the rotation example on $\mathbb{R}^2$ shows that *even* dimension permits eigenvalue-less operators, while *odd* dimension forces real eigenvalues. The parity argument is genuinely useful: it gives a quick existence proof in odd dimensions, with no need to factor any specific polynomial. The same idea appears in topology (the **hairy ball theorem**: every continuous vector field on an even-dimensional sphere has a zero; every odd-dimensional sphere admits non-vanishing vector fields) and in dynamics (odd-dimensional fixed-point theorems by parity arguments). The pattern "odd dimension forces a real eigenvalue / fixed point / zero" recurs frequently.

**The induction step trick: descend by $2$ to keep the dimension odd.** The core insight of the proof is *step size two*. A single application of $T^2 + bT + cI$ on $V$ produces a kernel of even dimension and a range of odd dimension; descending to the range keeps us in the odd-dimensional regime where the inductive hypothesis applies. This "step by two" induction is a common pattern when parity matters: in number theory (the Chinese remainder theorem with even/odd parts), in combinatorics (parity proofs), and in algebra (proving statements about characteristic-$p$ rings by induction on $p$).

**Real vs. complex behaviour: the irreducible-quadratic factors are the "missing" eigenvalues.** The minimal polynomial of a real operator can have irreducible-quadratic factors $z^2 + bz + c$ — and these correspond to *no real eigenvalues* but to "complex-conjugate pairs of eigenvalues" $\lambda, \overline\lambda$ that exist over the complexification. The number of irreducible-quadratic factors of $m_T$ over $\mathbb{R}$ equals the number of complex-conjugate eigenvalue pairs of $T_\mathbb{C}$ (the complexified operator). So over $\mathbb{R}$, the "spectrum" of $T$ has two parts: real eigenvalues (linear factors of $m_T$) and complex-conjugate pairs (quadratic factors). The exercise shows that odd dimension forces at least one of the linear factors to be present.

**Convergent strategy for other parity problems.** The structural pattern of this proof — "real polynomial of odd degree must have a real root; use this with a divisibility/dimension argument" — recurs in similar problems: showing certain real operators have real eigenvalues, certain real polynomials have prescribed root structure, certain real vector bundles admit certain sections. Whenever you see a parity hypothesis (odd dimension, odd characteristic, odd degree), think about reducing to a "real-polynomial-of-odd-degree-has-real-root" lemma.
