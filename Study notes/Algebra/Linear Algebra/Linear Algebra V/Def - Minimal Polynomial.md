# Notation

Throughout, $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$, $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is an operator on $V$. The polynomial ring $F[x]$ is the polynomial ring in one indeterminate over $F$. The **minimal polynomial** of $T$ is denoted $m_T$ (sometimes $p_T$, but $p_T$ frequently denotes the characteristic polynomial, so we use $m_T$ to avoid confusion). A polynomial is **monic** if its leading coefficient is $1$. The full registry is on the parent page [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]].

---

# Axiom Motivation

The minimal polynomial is the **fundamental algebraic invariant of an operator**. Its existence rests on an interplay between two structural facts: (i) $\mathcal{L}(V)$ has finite dimension $(\dim V)^2$ over $F$, so the powers $I, T, T^2, \ldots, T^{(\dim V)^2}$ cannot all be linearly independent — they form $(\dim V)^2 + 1$ vectors in a $(\dim V)^2$-dimensional space; (ii) the ring $F[x]$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] via the [[Thm - Division Algorithm for Polynomials (LA)|division algorithm]], so any ideal it produces has a unique monic generator. The interplay of (i) and (ii) is the definition.

Let me unpack this. Fact (i) says there is a nontrivial polynomial relation $a_0 I + a_1 T + \cdots + a_n T^n = 0$ for some $n \leq (\dim V)^2$, hence some nonzero polynomial $p \in F[x]$ with $p(T) = 0$. (Sharper bound: the existence proof in [[Thm - Existence and Uniqueness of Minimal Polynomial]] shows $n \leq \dim V$ suffices, by a finer argument that uses iterates of a single vector rather than the full $\mathcal{L}(V)$.) Such a $p$ is called an **annihilating polynomial** of $T$.

Fact (ii) gives the punchline. The set
$$\operatorname{Ann}(T) = \{p \in F[x] : p(T) = 0\} \subseteq F[x]$$
is an **ideal** of $F[x]$ — by direct check, it is closed under addition (sum of annihilators is an annihilator) and absorbs multiplication ($q \cdot p$ has $(qp)(T) = q(T) p(T) = q(T) \cdot 0 = 0$). Since $F[x]$ is a PID, this ideal is generated by a single polynomial, and we normalise the generator to be **monic** to make it unique. This unique monic generator is the **minimal polynomial** of $T$.

More structurally, $\operatorname{Ann}(T)$ is the **kernel of the evaluation map** $\Phi_T : F[x] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$, $p \mapsto p(T)$. By the first isomorphism theorem for rings,
$$F[x] / \operatorname{Ann}(T) \;\cong\; F[T] \;\subseteq\; \mathcal{L}(V),$$
where $F[T]$ is the subalgebra of $\mathcal{L}(V)$ generated by $T$. So:

> **The minimal polynomial $m_T$ is the monic generator of the kernel of the evaluation map $F[x] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$, $p \mapsto p(T)$.**

This is the **true name** of the minimal polynomial — the structural description that explains every property it has.

Why does the minimal polynomial have the properties we want?

1. **Uniqueness comes from monicness:** any two monic generators of a principal ideal differ by a unit, and units in $F[x]$ are nonzero constants; if both have leading coefficient $1$, they must be equal.

2. **Minimality of degree comes from the Euclidean structure:** if $p, q$ both annihilate $T$ and $q$ has smaller degree, then $q$ is also in the ideal, and any nonzero element of smallest degree in the ideal generates it (by the division algorithm: divide any other annihilator $p$ by $q$, get $p = q s + r$ with $\deg r < \deg q$; since $p$ and $q$ both annihilate $T$, so does $r$; but $r$ has smaller degree than the minimal, forcing $r = 0$). So the smallest-degree monic annihilator *is* the generator.

3. **Divisibility property:** $p(T) = 0$ if and only if $m_T \mid p$. This is just restating "$\operatorname{Ann}(T) = (m_T)$". It is the most-used property of $m_T$ in practice: any polynomial equation in $T$ tells you $m_T$ divides that polynomial.

4. **Eigenvalue connection:** the roots of $m_T$ in $F$ are exactly the eigenvalues of $T$. The proof uses the factor theorem (a polynomial vanishes at $\lambda$ iff $(x - \lambda)$ divides it). See [[Thm - Eigenvalues are Zeros of the Minimal Polynomial]].

5. **Bound on degree:** $\deg m_T \leq \dim V$. This is the [[Thm - Existence and Uniqueness of Minimal Polynomial|existence theorem]]'s sharper estimate; the argument uses iterates of a single vector.

Why is the minimal polynomial monic? Pure convention — we want a *unique* generator, and a principal ideal of $F[x]$ has many generators (any nonzero scalar multiple of one is another). Monic is the natural canonical choice: it makes the leading coefficient $1$, and the convention propagates through the chapter (the characteristic polynomial will also be monic, the irreducible factors are monic, etc.).

A subtlety: the minimal polynomial *depends on the field*. If $T$ is an operator on a real vector space $V$ and we complexify (extend scalars to $\mathbb{C}$, getting an operator $T_\mathbb{C}$ on $V_\mathbb{C}$), the minimal polynomial does *not* change as a polynomial. It is the same polynomial in $\mathbb{R}[x] \subseteq \mathbb{C}[x]$; but how it factors *does* change. Over $\mathbb{R}$ it might be irreducible quadratic; over $\mathbb{C}$ it splits into two linear factors. This field-of-factorization distinction is what makes operators "behave better" over $\mathbb{C}$.

# The Definition

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$ and let $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$.

**Minimal polynomial.** The **minimal polynomial** of $T$, denoted $m_T \in F[x]$, is the unique monic polynomial of smallest positive degree such that $m_T(T) = 0$.

**Equivalent formulation (the structural one).** The minimal polynomial is the monic generator of the principal ideal
$$\operatorname{Ann}(T) = \{p \in F[x] : p(T) = 0\} \;=\; \ker\!\big(\Phi_T : F[x] \to \mathcal{L}(V)\big),$$
where $\Phi_T(p) = p(T)$ is the evaluation map.

**Key properties:**

1. **Existence and uniqueness.** $m_T$ exists, is unique, has degree $\leq \dim V$, and is the unique monic polynomial of smallest degree annihilating $T$. See [[Thm - Existence and Uniqueness of Minimal Polynomial]].

2. **Divisibility (universal annihilator).** For any $q \in F[x]$:
$$q(T) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m_T \mid q.$$
This is the divisibility property characterising the minimal polynomial: it divides every annihilating polynomial.

3. **Eigenvalues.** The roots of $m_T$ in $F$ are exactly the eigenvalues of $T$. Over $\mathbb{C}$, $m_T$ factors as
$$m_T(z) = (z - \lambda_1)(z - \lambda_2) \cdots (z - \lambda_m),$$
where the $\lambda_k$ are *all* the eigenvalues of $T$ (possibly with repetitions). See [[Thm - Eigenvalues are Zeros of the Minimal Polynomial]].

4. **Restriction.** If $U \leq V$ is $T$-invariant, the minimal polynomial of the restriction $T|_U$ divides the minimal polynomial of $T$. Equivalently, $m_{T|_U}$ divides $m_T$.

5. **Algebraic isomorphism.** $F[T] \cong F[x] / (m_T)$ as $F$-algebras, where $F[T]$ is the subalgebra of $\mathcal{L}(V)$ generated by $T$ over $F$. In particular, $\dim_F F[T] = \deg m_T$.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The minimal polynomial of $T$ is the **monic generator of the annihilator ideal** of $V$ as a finitely generated $F[x]$-module (with $x$ acting as $T$). In module-theoretic language, the annihilator of an $F[x]$-module $M$ is
$$\operatorname{ann}_{F[x]}(M) = \{p \in F[x] : p \cdot m = 0 \text{ for all } m \in M\},$$
which is an ideal of $F[x]$. For $M = V_T$ (the operator's module), this is exactly the kernel of $\Phi_T$, and the monic generator is $m_T$.

The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID — see [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain]] — decomposes $V_T$ as a direct sum of cyclic modules:
$$V_T \;\cong\; F[x]/(f_1) \oplus F[x]/(f_2) \oplus \cdots \oplus F[x]/(f_s), \qquad f_1 \mid f_2 \mid \cdots \mid f_s.$$
The polynomials $f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_s$ are the **invariant factors** of $T$. The smallest factor $f_1$ is **not** the minimal polynomial; the *largest* is. **The minimal polynomial equals the last invariant factor $f_s$.** This is because the annihilator of the cyclic module $F[x]/(f_k)$ is the ideal $(f_k)$, and the annihilator of a direct sum is the intersection of the annihilators, which in a PID is the ideal generated by the LCM — but since $f_1 \mid \cdots \mid f_s$, the LCM is just $f_s$.

The **characteristic polynomial** of $T$ — defined later via determinants — turns out to equal the product $f_1 f_2 \cdots f_s$ of all invariant factors. So the minimal polynomial divides the characteristic polynomial, and their ratio is $f_1 f_2 \cdots f_{s-1}$. This explains the relationship "minimal polynomial $\mid$ characteristic polynomial" — the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton (Minimal-Polynomial Form)|Cayley-Hamilton theorem]] in the Axler approach.

In the **primary decomposition** of $V_T$ — splitting along prime factors of the annihilator — the picture is: for each distinct eigenvalue $\lambda$ (over an algebraically closed field), the corresponding "primary component" is the generalised eigenspace, and the primary component is itself a direct sum of cyclic modules $F[x]/((x - \lambda)^{k})$ — one for each Jordan block of size $k$ at $\lambda$. The minimal polynomial's exponent at $(x - \lambda)$ equals the size of the **largest** Jordan block at $\lambda$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** The minimal polynomial is the **monic generator of the kernel of the evaluation homomorphism $F[x] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$, $p \mapsto p(T)$**. Every property of $m_T$ is a consequence of this single structural description. Equivalently (in module-theoretic language): the monic generator of the annihilator ideal $\operatorname{ann}_{F[x]}(V_T)$.

The minimal polynomial has *exact analogues* across mathematics:

- **Algebraic number theory:** the minimal polynomial of an algebraic number $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is the monic generator of the ideal $\{p \in \mathbb{Q}[x] : p(\alpha) = 0\}$. The exact same PID-generator construction, with $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ in place of $F[x]$. See [[Def - Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial]] and [[Thm - The Minimal Polynomial Generates the Kernel Ideal]] from [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8]].

- **Field theory:** for $\alpha$ in a field extension $L/F$, the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $F$ is the monic generator of $\ker(F[x] \to L, p \mapsto p(\alpha))$. Its degree is the degree of the extension $F(\alpha)/F$.

- **Functional analysis:** for a compact normal operator $T$ on a Hilbert space with finite spectrum, there is a polynomial $p$ with $p(T) = 0$, and the minimal such $p$ plays a role analogous to the finite-dimensional minimal polynomial. For general bounded operators (with infinite spectrum), no such polynomial exists, but the analogous role is played by the **functional calculus**.

- **Commutative algebra:** the annihilator ideal of a module $M$ over a commutative ring $R$ is $\operatorname{ann}_R(M) = \{r \in R : r \cdot M = 0\}$. The minimal polynomial is the generator of this annihilator when $R = F[x]$ and the annihilator is principal.

The principal underlying pattern is: **whenever you have a ring acting on a module, and the action factors through a quotient ring, the kernel of the quotient is structurally meaningful, and in a PID it has a unique monic generator.**

# Examples / Corollaries

**Scalar operator: $T = \lambda I$.** The polynomial $m_T = x - \lambda$ annihilates $T$, and no nonzero constant does (unless $T = 0$, in which case $m_T = x$ for $\lambda = 0$, *not* $1$). So $m_T = x - \lambda$. Degree $1$, one eigenvalue $\lambda$, eigenspace $V$.

**Diagonal operator with distinct eigenvalues.** If $T$ is diagonalizable with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k$, then $m_T = (x - \lambda_1) \cdots (x - \lambda_k)$ — one linear factor per *distinct* eigenvalue, no repetitions. (Repeated eigenvalues correspond to higher-dimensional eigenspaces but do *not* increase the multiplicity in $m_T$.) See [[Ex - Minimal polynomial of a diagonal matrix]].

**Diagonal operator with repeated eigenvalues: e.g. $T = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.** This is $2I$, so $m_T = x - 2$, not $(x - 2)^2$. The minimal polynomial sees the *set* of eigenvalues, weighted by the largest Jordan block.

**Jordan block: $T = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{pmatrix}$.** Then $T - \lambda I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $(T - \lambda I)^2 = 0$. So $m_T$ divides $(x - \lambda)^2$ but does not divide $x - \lambda$. Hence $m_T = (x - \lambda)^2$. The Jordan-block size $2$ becomes the exponent of $(x - \lambda)$ in $m_T$.

**Rotation on $\mathbb{R}^2$ by $90°$: $T(x, y) = (-y, x)$.** We have $T^2 = -I$, so $T^2 + I = 0$, hence $m_T \mid x^2 + 1$. The polynomial $x^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$, so its monic divisors are $1$ and itself. Since $m_T \neq 1$ (the zero operator is not $T$), $m_T = x^2 + 1$. Over $\mathbb{R}$, $T$ has no eigenvalues; the roots of $m_T$ live in $\mathbb{C}$.

**Differentiation on $\mathcal{P}_n(\mathbb{R})$ (polynomials of degree $\leq n$).** The operator $D$ satisfies $D^{n+1} = 0$ on this space (differentiation lowers degree by $1$, so after $n+1$ applications everything is killed), and no lower power vanishes (since $D^n(x^n) = n!$). So $m_D = x^{n+1}$, the maximum nilpotent.

**Projection: $T^2 = T$.** Then $T^2 - T = 0$, so $m_T \mid x^2 - x = x(x - 1)$. The monic divisors of $x(x - 1)$ are $1, x, x - 1, x(x - 1)$. Excluding $1$, we have three cases: $m_T = x$ (means $T = 0$), $m_T = x - 1$ (means $T = I$), $m_T = x(x - 1)$ (means $T$ is a non-trivial projection). In the third case, $T$ is diagonalizable with eigenvalues $0$ and $1$ (distinct linear factors of $m_T$).

**$T^k = I$.** Then $m_T \mid x^k - 1$. Over $\mathbb{C}$, $x^k - 1 = \prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(x - \zeta^j)$ where $\zeta = e^{2\pi i / k}$, so $m_T$ is a product of distinct linear factors — therefore $T$ is **diagonalizable** over $\mathbb{C}$. This gives the famous result: every finite-order operator on a complex vector space is diagonalizable. See [[Ex - Powers of an operator and the minimal polynomial]].

**Non-example: $m_T$ is not in general a complete invariant of similarity.** Two operators on $\mathbb{C}^4$ can have the same $m_T = x^2$ but be non-similar: one might be $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ & 0 \\ & & 0 & 1 \\ & & & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (two $2 \times 2$ Jordan blocks at $0$), the other $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ & 0 \\ & & 0 \\ & & & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (one $2 \times 2$ Jordan block plus two $1 \times 1$ zero blocks). Both have minimal polynomial $x^2$ but the first has rank $2$ and the second rank $1$, so they are not similar. The full similarity invariant is the *list of invariant factors* (or equivalently, the [[Thm - Jordan Normal Form|Jordan form]]). See [[Ex - Operators with the same minimal polynomial need not be similar]].

**Calibration check.** If you have absorbed the definition: (a) for the operator on $\mathbb{C}^3$ with matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, you can deduce $m_T = (x - 2)^2(x - 3)$ by computing $(T - 2I)(T - 3I)$ and observing it is not zero but $(T - 2I)^2(T - 3I) = 0$; (b) you can identify the units of $F[x]$ as nonzero constants, and use this to verify monicness gives a unique generator of the principal annihilator; (c) you recognise the structural slogan: *the minimal polynomial sees the spectrum (set of eigenvalues) and the largest Jordan block at each, but not the full Jordan structure.*

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Conditions for Diagonalizability *(from Linear Algebra V, §5D)*
> $T$ is diagonalizable $\iff$ $m_T$ is a product of **distinct** linear factors in $F[x]$. This is the practical test for diagonalizability — checkable without finding eigenvectors. See [[Thm - Conditions for Diagonalizability]].

> [!tip] Cayley-Hamilton Theorem *(from Linear Algebra V, §5E + Linear Algebra IX)*
> The minimal polynomial **divides** the characteristic polynomial (once the latter is defined via determinants). The Cayley-Hamilton theorem in its classical form — $p_T(T) = 0$ for the characteristic polynomial $p_T$ — follows immediately from this divisibility. See [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton (Minimal-Polynomial Form)]].

> [!tip] Jordan Normal Form *(from Modules II — §3.4)*
> The minimal polynomial determines the eigenvalues of $T$ and the maximum Jordan block size at each. The full Jordan structure requires more invariants (the elementary divisors), but the minimal polynomial is the most informative single polynomial associated to $T$. See [[Thm - Jordan Normal Form]].

> [!tip] Rational Canonical Form *(from Modules II — §3.4)*
> The minimal polynomial is the **last invariant factor** $f_s$ in the decomposition $V_T \cong \bigoplus F[x]/(f_k)$ with $f_1 \mid \cdots \mid f_s$. The other invariant factors $f_1, \ldots, f_{s-1}$ are the additional invariants needed for a complete similarity classification. See [[Thm - Rational Canonical Form]].

> [!tip] Annihilator of an $F[x]$-Module *(from Module Theory)*
> The minimal polynomial is the generator of $\operatorname{ann}_{F[x]}(V_T)$ when $V$ is viewed as an $F[x]$-module via $T$. See [[Def - Annihilator (Dual Space)]]. The annihilator construction makes sense for modules over arbitrary rings; the principal-generator description requires the ring to be a PID, which $F[x]$ is.

> [!tip] Holomorphic Functional Calculus *(from Functional Analysis)*
> For bounded operators on Banach spaces, the polynomial $p(T)$ generalises to $f(T)$ for $f$ holomorphic in a neighbourhood of the spectrum. The minimal polynomial is the simplest annihilating function: $f(T) = 0$ exactly when $m_T$ divides $f$ (in the finite-dimensional case). The spectral mapping theorem $\sigma(f(T)) = f(\sigma(T))$ is the generalisation of "eigenvalues of $p(T)$ are $p(\lambda)$" from this chapter.
