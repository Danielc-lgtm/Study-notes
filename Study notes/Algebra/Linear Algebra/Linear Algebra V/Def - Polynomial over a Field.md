# Notation

Throughout, $F$ is a field (almost always $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$). The indeterminate is $x$ or $z$ (the choice is purely conventional — Axler uses $z$ in Chapter 4 to emphasise the complex case and switches to $x$ when polynomials are applied to operators). The set of all polynomials over $F$ is written $\mathcal{P}(F)$ or, equivalently, $F[x]$; the set of polynomials of degree at most $m$ is $\mathcal{P}_m(F)$. For a polynomial $p$, $\deg p$ is its degree. A **zero** or **root** of $p$ is an element $\lambda \in F$ with $p(\lambda) = 0$. A polynomial is **monic** if its leading coefficient is $1$. The full registry is on the parent page [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]].

# Axiom Motivation

Polynomials are the simplest non-trivial example of an algebraic object built freely on a field. The motivation has two layers: a *concrete* one (polynomials as functions you can evaluate) and a *structural* one (polynomials as the universal free commutative algebra on one generator). Both layers matter, and learning to switch between them is the whole game.

Start with the concrete picture. A polynomial $p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_m x^m$ is a finite formal expression — a list of coefficients, all but finitely many zero. To *evaluate* $p$ at a scalar $\lambda \in F$ means to substitute: $p(\lambda) = a_0 + a_1 \lambda + \cdots + a_m \lambda^m$. The evaluation map $F \to F$, $\lambda \mapsto p(\lambda)$, makes a polynomial into a function. One could *define* a polynomial to be this function, identifying the formal expression with the function it produces. Over fields like $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$ this identification is harmless: a polynomial that is zero as a function is zero as a formal expression. Over finite fields it is not — the function $x \mapsto x^p - x$ on $\mathbb{F}_p$ is identically zero, but the formal expression $x^p - x$ is nonzero (of degree $p$). So one had better commit to the formal-expression definition, with the understanding that over infinite fields one can interchange the two viewpoints.

What axioms must we put on the formal expression? We want at least three operations: **addition** (componentwise on the coefficient lists, so $\deg(p+q) \leq \max(\deg p, \deg q)$), **scalar multiplication** ($c \cdot \sum a_k x^k = \sum (c a_k) x^k$), and — the key step — **multiplication of polynomials** ($\sum a_j x^j \cdot \sum b_k x^k = \sum_n \big(\sum_{j+k=n} a_j b_k\big) x^n$). The first two make $\mathcal{P}(F)$ a vector space; all three make it a commutative [[Def - Ring|ring]]. Multiplication is the operation that makes polynomials worth studying — without it, $\mathcal{P}(F)$ is just an infinite-dimensional vector space, and there are many of those. With multiplication, it is the **polynomial ring** $F[x]$, with rich structural features that the next definition (the [[Def - Division Algorithm and Factorization|division algorithm]]) immediately exploits.

Could we strengthen the axioms? Drop commutativity of multiplication, and we get the **noncommutative polynomial ring** $F\langle x\rangle$ — but with only one indeterminate $x$, commutativity is forced anyway. Drop associativity of multiplication, and the ring axioms collapse. Drop the requirement that coefficients lie in a field and allow only a commutative ring $R$ — we get $R[x]$, the polynomial ring over a ring. This is a real generalisation: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, $\mathbb{R}[x, y] = \mathbb{R}[x][y]$, and so on. But for the purposes of linear algebra, the field hypothesis is essential because it makes $F[x]$ a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] (every [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] is generated by a single polynomial), and this PID property is the structural backbone of eigenvalue theory: it is what guarantees the existence of the [[Def - Minimal Polynomial|minimal polynomial]] of an operator.

What about the degree? The convention $\deg 0 = -\infty$ is the right one because it makes the degree-bound rules $\deg(p+q) \leq \max(\deg p, \deg q)$ and $\deg(pq) = \deg p + \deg q$ uniformly true. The bound on the sum is sharp — $p$ and $-p$ have degree $m$ each but sum to $0$ of degree $-\infty < m$ — and the additivity of degree under product is the calculation that *requires* $F$ to be an integral domain (so that the product of the two leading coefficients is nonzero); in a field, all nonzero elements are units, so this is automatic.

The decisive structural property — the one that makes $F[x]$ behave so much better than its generalisations $F[x, y]$ and $R[x]$ — is the **degree-decrease principle for division**: if you divide a polynomial $p$ by a nonzero polynomial $s$, the remainder has degree strictly less than $\deg s$. This is the content of the [[Thm - Division Algorithm for Polynomials (LA)|division algorithm]], and it is what makes $F[x]$ a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] (with $\deg$ as the Euclidean function), hence a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]], hence a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|UFD]]. The whole edifice of the chapter — uniqueness of the minimal polynomial, factorization theorems over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$, the very existence of canonical forms for operators — rests on this single property.

A final calibration: a polynomial is **not** the same as a power series. The polynomial $1 + x + x^2 + x^3 + \cdots$ does not exist in $F[x]$, only in the larger ring $F[[x]]$ of formal power series. The defining feature of a polynomial is *finite* support — only finitely many coefficients are nonzero. This is what makes evaluation an honest operation in $F$ (a power series cannot in general be evaluated outside of a region of convergence) and what gives the degree a finite numerical meaning.

# The Definition

This is a compound page: it defines the **polynomial ring** $\mathcal{P}(F) = F[x]$ together with its **degree** function, the relations between $\mathcal{P}_m(F)$ [[Def - Subspace|subspaces]], and the notion of a **monic polynomial** and a **zero** of a polynomial — these are introduced together and none is fully usable without the others.

**Polynomial.** A **polynomial** over a field $F$ in the indeterminate $x$ is a finite formal expression
$$p(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \cdots + a_m x^m$$
with coefficients $a_0, a_1, \ldots, a_m \in F$. Two polynomials are equal if their coefficients agree term by term. The set of all polynomials over $F$ is denoted $\mathcal{P}(F)$ or $F[x]$.

**Operations.** Addition and scalar multiplication are coefficient-wise:
$$\Big(\sum_k a_k x^k\Big) + \Big(\sum_k b_k x^k\Big) = \sum_k (a_k + b_k) x^k, \qquad c \cdot \sum_k a_k x^k = \sum_k (c a_k) x^k.$$
Multiplication of polynomials is by the Cauchy product:
$$\Big(\sum_j a_j x^j\Big)\Big(\sum_k b_k x^k\Big) = \sum_n \Big(\sum_{j+k=n} a_j b_k\Big) x^n.$$
Under these, $\mathcal{P}(F)$ is a commutative ring with identity (the constant polynomial $1$), and an $F$-vector space (under addition and scalar multiplication).

**Degree.** The **degree** of a nonzero polynomial $p = \sum_{k=0}^m a_k x^k$ with $a_m \neq 0$ is $\deg p = m$. By convention $\deg 0 = -\infty$. The degree satisfies
$$\deg(p + q) \leq \max(\deg p, \deg q), \qquad \deg(pq) = \deg p + \deg q,$$
with the second equality holding because $F$ is a field (in particular has no zero divisors).

**[[Def - Subspace|Subspaces]] by degree.** For each nonnegative integer $m$, the set
$$\mathcal{P}_m(F) = \{p \in \mathcal{P}(F) : \deg p \leq m\}$$
is a subspace of $\mathcal{P}(F)$ of [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] $m+1$, with basis $\{1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^m\}$.

**Monic polynomial.** A nonzero polynomial $p = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_m x^m$ with $a_m \neq 0$ is **monic** if $a_m = 1$. Every nonzero polynomial $p$ has a unique monic associate $p/a_m$.

**Zero of a polynomial.** An element $\lambda \in F$ is a **zero** (or **root**) of a polynomial $p$ if $p(\lambda) = 0$, where the evaluation $p(\lambda)$ is defined by substitution: $p(\lambda) = a_0 + a_1 \lambda + a_2 \lambda^2 + \cdots + a_m \lambda^m$. The evaluation map $\operatorname{ev}_\lambda : \mathcal{P}(F) \to F$, $p \mapsto p(\lambda)$, is a ring homomorphism.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The polynomial ring $F[x]$ is the **free commutative $F$-algebra on one generator**. Concretely, this means it satisfies a universal property: for every commutative $F$-algebra $A$ and every element $a \in A$, there is a unique $F$-algebra homomorphism
$$F[x] \longrightarrow A, \qquad x \longmapsto a.$$
Explicitly, this homomorphism is the evaluation $p \mapsto p(a)$ — the same construction as the evaluation of a polynomial at a scalar, but now allowing $a$ to be any element of any $F$-algebra. (An $F$-algebra is a ring $A$ together with a ring homomorphism $F \to A$ landing in the centre; informally, a ring on which the scalars from $F$ act commutatively.)

This universal property characterises $F[x]$ up to unique isomorphism. It is what licenses the central construction of the present chapter: applying a polynomial to an operator $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$. The space $\mathcal{L}(V)$ is an $F$-algebra (an $F$-vector space and a ring under composition, with $F$ acting through scalar identity multiples), so the universal property delivers a unique algebra homomorphism
$$F[x] \longrightarrow \mathcal{L}(V), \qquad x \longmapsto T,$$
which is exactly $p \mapsto p(T)$. The kernel of this homomorphism is an ideal of $F[x]$, principal because $F[x]$ is a PID, and its monic generator is the **minimal polynomial** of $T$. Every result in §5B about the minimal polynomial is, structurally, a result about the kernel of this universal homomorphism.

The viewpoint also clarifies the difference between the polynomial $p(x)$ as a formal expression and the function $\lambda \mapsto p(\lambda)$ as an element of $F^F$. The "polynomial as a function" view corresponds to looking at the evaluation map $F[x] \to F^F$, and the question "is this map injective?" is the question "is a polynomial determined by its values?". Over infinite fields, yes; over finite fields, no (the kernel of the evaluation map on $\mathbb{F}_p$ contains $x^p - x$).

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** A polynomial over $F$ is **a finite-support function $\mathbb{N} \to F$** — a sequence of coefficients all but finitely many zero — together with the convolution multiplication that comes from thinking of the sequence as a generating function. This is the "abstract before concrete" view: the formal expression $\sum a_k x^k$ is a notational device for the sequence $(a_0, a_1, a_2, \ldots)$; addition is sequence addition; multiplication is convolution. Cleansed of notation, $F[x]$ is the **monoid algebra** of the additive monoid $\mathbb{N}$ over $F$, written $F[\mathbb{N}]$. This perspective makes $F[x, y]$ obvious — it is the monoid algebra of $\mathbb{N}^2$ — and unifies polynomial rings with group algebras and convolution algebras.

The connections across fields are direct. The polynomial ring $F[x]$ is the **simplest** non-trivial commutative ring that is not a field, and it is **the** central example of a Euclidean domain in algebra. The other canonical Euclidean domain — the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ — is the closest analogue: degree on $F[x]$ is absolute value on $\mathbb{Z}$, the division algorithm has the same shape ("quotient and remainder of smaller absolute value/degree"), and the proofs of "Euclidean $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD" go through verbatim. The pair $(\mathbb{Z}, F[x])$ is the standard mental model for arithmetic in any Euclidean domain.

In **algebraic geometry**, the polynomial ring $F[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ — the multivariable analogue — is the coordinate ring of affine $n$-space over $F$. Solutions of polynomial equations are points of the corresponding affine variety, and the polynomial ring itself is the "ring of functions" on the variety. The whole subject of algebraic geometry takes the polynomial ring as its starting object.

In **representation theory**, an $F[x]$-module is exactly a vector space equipped with a single chosen operator — see [[Def - The Module of a Linear Operator]]. The dictionary
$$(V, T \in \mathcal{L}(V)) \;\longleftrightarrow\; F[x]\text{-module}$$
is the bridge that makes the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID specialise to the [[Thm - Jordan Normal Form|Jordan]] and [[Thm - Rational Canonical Form|rational canonical]] forms.

# Examples / Corollaries

**The constant polynomials are exactly the field elements.** A polynomial of degree $0$ has the form $p(x) = a_0$ with $a_0 \in F$, $a_0 \neq 0$. Together with the zero polynomial, the constants form the subspace $\mathcal{P}_0(F) \cong F$. So $F \hookrightarrow F[x]$ as a [[Def - Subring|subring]], identifying scalars with constant polynomials.

**The degree of a product is additive: $\deg(pq) = \deg p + \deg q$.** If $p = \sum_{j \leq m} a_j x^j$ with $a_m \neq 0$ and $q = \sum_{k \leq n} b_k x^k$ with $b_n \neq 0$, the coefficient of $x^{m+n}$ in $pq$ is $a_m b_n$, which is nonzero in a field. So $\deg(pq) = m + n$. This is the place where the field hypothesis enters most directly — in a ring with zero divisors, the leading coefficient of a product could vanish, and the degree formula would fail.

**A nonzero polynomial of degree $m$ has at most $m$ zeros.** Each zero $\lambda$ produces a factor $(x - \lambda)$ in $p$, and degrees add under multiplication, so the number of factors (counted with multiplicity) is at most $m$. This is the cornerstone uniqueness statement: it implies that a polynomial is determined by its values on any set of $\deg p + 1$ distinct points, and that two polynomials agreeing on infinitely many points are equal as formal expressions. *(Calibration check.)*

**A non-example: $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a polynomial ring but is not over a field.** The ring $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a polynomial ring in our broader sense, but $\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field. Consequently $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is not a PID — the ideal $(2, x)$ generated by $2$ and $x$ is not principal. This is the structural feature that distinguishes "polynomial ring over a field" from "polynomial ring over a ring", and is the reason the minimal polynomial of an operator over a *ring* (rather than a field) is a more delicate notion.

**A non-example: a formal power series is not a polynomial.** The expression $1 + x + x^2 + x^3 + \cdots$ has infinitely many nonzero coefficients and is therefore an element of the formal power series ring $F[[x]]$, not of $F[x]$. The two [[Def - Ring|rings]] differ in essential ways: $F[[x]]$ has $x$ as a non-unit but is a local ring (its only maximal ideal is $(x)$); $F[x]$ has $(x)$ as one of infinitely many prime [[Def - Ideal|ideals]], all of the form $(p)$ for monic irreducible $p$. *(Calibration check: every element of $F[x]$ has finite degree; ask yourself whether the same is true in $F[[x]]$.)*

**Calibration check.** If you have absorbed the definition, you should be able to verify quickly that (a) the multiplication of polynomials is associative and commutative — write out $(pq)r = p(qr)$ in terms of coefficients and check both sides equal $\sum_n \big(\sum_{i+j+k=n} a_i b_j c_k\big) x^n$; (b) the polynomial $x^2 + 1$ has no root in $\mathbb{R}$ but two roots in $\mathbb{C}$; (c) the polynomial $x^2 + 1$ in $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$ equals $(x+1)^2$ — over a field of characteristic $2$, signs collapse and the factorisation is different.
