# Notation

Throughout, $F$ is a field, $F[x]$ is the polynomial ring, and $\deg p$ is the degree of $p \in F[x]$ (with $\deg 0 = -\infty$). The full registry is on the parent page [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Division Algorithm for Polynomials).** Let $p, s \in F[x]$ with $s \neq 0$. There exist unique polynomials $q, r \in F[x]$ such that
> $$p = sq + r \qquad \text{and} \qquad \deg r < \deg s.$$
> The polynomial $q$ is the **quotient** and $r$ is the **remainder** in dividing $p$ by $s$.

This says concretely that $F[x]$ admits division with remainder analogous to the integers, with degree playing the role of absolute value. Structurally, the theorem is the statement that $F[x]$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] with Euclidean function $\deg$.

---

# Motivation

The division algorithm is the **engine** of polynomial algebra over a field. It is what makes every other structural result of [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]] possible:

- It is the proof that $F[x]$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] (since every Euclidean domain is a PID).
- It is the proof that **the [[Def - Minimal Polynomial|minimal polynomial]] of an operator exists and is unique**: the set of polynomials annihilating $T$ is an ideal, principal because of the division algorithm, with a unique monic generator.
- It is the source of unique factorization in $F[x]$ (via PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD).
- It is the algebraic content of the **factor theorem** ("$\lambda$ is a zero of $p$ iff $(x - \lambda)$ divides $p$"): apply the algorithm with $s = x - \lambda$ to get $p = (x - \lambda) q + r$ with $\deg r < 1$, so $r$ is constant, and $p(\lambda) = r$.
- It is the rigorous form of "polynomial long division" learned in school: the iterative procedure for subtracting off multiples of $s$ until the remainder has degree less than $\deg s$.

The Axler proof is unusually elegant. Instead of running the iterative subtraction algorithm, it observes that the list $1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^{m-1}, s, xs, x^2 s, \ldots, x^{n-m}s$ — where $m = \deg s$ and $n = \deg p$ — has length $n + 1$, all entries of distinct degrees, hence is a basis of $\mathcal{P}_n(F)$. Expressing $p$ in this basis gives the decomposition $p = r + s q$ for free, with $r$ being the part of degree less than $m$ and $sq$ being the part using the $s$-multiples.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The theorem's input is just two polynomials $p, s \in F[x]$ with $s \neq 0$. The interesting sources are the *contexts* in which one wants to invoke the algorithm.

The first disguised source is **any ideal of $F[x]$**. Given any nontrivial ideal $I \subseteq F[x]$, the division algorithm produces a unique monic generator: take the monic polynomial $g$ in $I$ of smallest positive degree (which exists since $I$ is nonzero and degrees are non-negative integers), and for any other $p \in I$, divide $p = gq + r$; then $r = p - gq \in I$ and $\deg r < \deg g$, forcing $r = 0$ by minimality of $g$. So $p = gq$, i.e. $g$ divides $p$, proving $I = (g)$. *Example problem:* "Show that the kernel of the evaluation map $F[x] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$, $p \mapsto p(T)$, is generated by a unique monic polynomial." The disguised source is that the kernel is an ideal.

The second disguised source is **the desire to verify polynomial divisibility**. Given two polynomials $f, g$, deciding whether $f \mid g$ is equivalent to computing the remainder of $g$ divided by $f$ — if the remainder is zero, $f \mid g$; if not, $f \nmid g$. *Example problem:* "Decide whether $x^2 + 1$ divides $x^4 + x^2 + 1$ in $\mathbb{R}[x]$." Compute: $x^4 + x^2 + 1 = (x^2 + 1)(x^2) + 1$, so the remainder is $1$ and $x^2 + 1 \nmid x^4 + x^2 + 1$.

The third disguised source is **the relationship between polynomial zeros and linear factors**, namely the factor theorem. Whenever you know a polynomial $p$ has a specific zero $\lambda \in F$, you can divide $p$ by $x - \lambda$ — the result has remainder zero (by evaluating at $\lambda$) and quotient one degree lower than $p$. *Example problem:* "Find all real zeros of $p(x) = x^3 - 6x^2 + 11x - 6$." Guess $\lambda = 1$ (verify: $1 - 6 + 11 - 6 = 0$); divide $p$ by $x - 1$ to get $p = (x - 1)(x^2 - 5x + 6) = (x-1)(x-2)(x-3)$. The division algorithm is the engine of this factor extraction.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusion is $p = sq + r$ with $\deg r < \deg s$. Combinations:

Combined with **finite-dimensionality of $V$**, the algorithm amplifies to the existence and uniqueness of the [[Def - Minimal Polynomial|minimal polynomial]] of an operator. The kernel of the evaluation map is an ideal of $F[x]$, and the algorithm reduces it to a principal ideal $(m_T)$.

Combined with **the factor theorem**, the algorithm gives the **zero-count bound**: a polynomial of degree $m$ has at most $m$ zeros (each producing a linear factor, and degrees of factors sum to $m$).

Combined with **the FTA over $\mathbb{C}$**, the algorithm gives **complete factorisation over $\mathbb{C}$**: every nonconstant polynomial in $\mathbb{C}[x]$ factors as $c(x - \lambda_1) \cdots (x - \lambda_m)$ for $\lambda_k \in \mathbb{C}$. This is the input to [[Thm - Existence of Eigenvalues on Complex Vector Spaces|the existence-of-eigenvalues theorem]].

# Why Is It True

The theorem rests on a single structural fact about polynomials over a field: **a list of polynomials of strictly distinct degrees is linearly independent**. From this fact, the proof writes itself.

Why are polynomials of distinct degrees linearly independent? If $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_k$ have strictly different degrees, then a linear relation $a_1 p_1 + \cdots + a_k p_k = 0$ must have all $a_i = 0$: otherwise, take the polynomial $p_i$ with the largest degree among those with $a_i \neq 0$ — its leading coefficient survives in the sum (no cancellation from polynomials of smaller degree), so the sum has degree equal to $\deg p_i$ and is not zero. This requires only that the leading coefficient of $p_i$, multiplied by the nonzero scalar $a_i$, remain nonzero — which uses the field hypothesis ($F$ has no zero divisors).

> **The mechanism: polynomials of distinct degrees form a "staircase" — and in any polynomial decomposition, only one term lives on each step.**

Now apply this. The list
$$1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^{m-1}, \;\; s, xs, x^2 s, \ldots, x^{n-m} s$$
has $n + 1$ elements (with $m = \deg s$, $n = \deg p \geq m$). The first $m$ are $1, x, \ldots, x^{m-1}$ of degrees $0, 1, \ldots, m-1$; the next $n - m + 1$ are $s, xs, \ldots, x^{n-m} s$ of degrees $m, m+1, \ldots, n$ (since $\deg(x^k s) = k + \deg s$). All $n+1$ degrees are distinct (they cover $\{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$ exactly once), so by the lemma the list is linearly independent. It has length $n + 1 = \dim \mathcal{P}_n(F)$, so it is a *basis* of $\mathcal{P}_n(F)$.

The polynomial $p \in \mathcal{P}_n(F)$ has unique coordinates in this basis: $p = a_0 \cdot 1 + a_1 \cdot x + \cdots + a_{m-1} \cdot x^{m-1} + b_0 \cdot s + b_1 \cdot xs + \cdots + b_{n-m} \cdot x^{n-m} s$. Grouping the first $m$ terms as $r$ and the last $n - m + 1$ terms as $sq$ (where $q = b_0 + b_1 x + \cdots + b_{n-m} x^{n-m}$):
$$p = r + sq, \qquad \deg r \leq m - 1 < m = \deg s.$$
This is the decomposition $p = sq + r$. **Uniqueness** is automatic: any other decomposition would yield different coordinates in the basis, contradicting uniqueness of coordinates.

# What Makes This Hard

The conceptual content of the proof is clear once one sees the staircase argument; the non-obvious step is the **observation that polynomials of distinct degrees are linearly independent** — without this insight, the proof is forced to use the iterative subtraction algorithm, which is correct but less illuminating. The other common slip is to forget that $\deg(pq) = \deg p + \deg q$ uses the field hypothesis (in a ring with zero divisors, the formula can fail).

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy.** Build a basis of $\mathcal{P}_n(F)$ using "low-degree monomials" together with "multiples of $s$"; express $p$ in this basis; read off $q$ and $r$.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Polynomials of distinct degrees are linearly independent.** Show that any list of nonzero polynomials with pairwise distinct degrees is linearly independent.
   - *Hint:* a nonzero linear combination has degree equal to the maximum degree of its nonzero summands; if there is only one summand of that degree, the leading coefficient is nonzero.
   - *Why needed:* this is the underlying fact that makes the staircase argument work.

2. **The list $1, x, \ldots, x^{m-1}, s, xs, \ldots, x^{n-m}s$ is a basis of $\mathcal{P}_n(F)$.** With $m = \deg s$ and $n = \deg p$, this list has $n+1$ elements of distinct degrees $0, 1, \ldots, n$.
   - *Hint:* count the elements ($m + (n - m + 1) = n + 1$); verify the degrees are $\{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$; apply Subgoal 1 to get linear independence; compare to the dimension $n + 1$ of $\mathcal{P}_n(F)$.
   - *Why needed:* it is the basis in which $p$ has the right form.

3. **Decompose $p$ in this basis to read off $q$ and $r$.** Since the basis splits as "low degrees" + "multiples of $s$", write $p = (\text{low-degree part}) + s \cdot (\text{remaining polynomial})$.
   - *Hint:* the low-degree part is $r$, the polynomial multiplying $s$ is $q$.
   - *Why needed:* this is the decomposition demanded by the theorem.

4. **Uniqueness from basis uniqueness.** Two decompositions $p = sq_1 + r_1 = sq_2 + r_2$ would correspond to two different coordinate vectors in the basis above, contradicting uniqueness of basis representation.
   - *Hint:* take the difference: $s(q_1 - q_2) = r_2 - r_1$; the right side has degree $< \deg s$ and the left has degree $\geq \deg s$ unless $q_1 = q_2$.
   - *Why needed:* completes the proof.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Polynomials of distinct degrees are linearly independent
> **Statement:** If $p_1, \ldots, p_k \in F[x]$ are nonzero and have pairwise distinct degrees, then $p_1, \ldots, p_k$ are linearly independent.
>
> **Hint:** Take a linear combination $\sum a_i p_i = 0$ with not all $a_i = 0$; look at the term $a_j p_j$ with the largest degree (among nonzero contributions), and observe its leading coefficient survives.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the engine of the basis construction.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Suppose $a_1 p_1 + \cdots + a_k p_k = 0$ with not all $a_i = 0$. Among the indices $i$ with $a_i \neq 0$, pick the one with the largest $\deg p_i$; call it $i_0$. The polynomial $a_{i_0} p_{i_0}$ has degree $\deg p_{i_0}$ and nonzero leading coefficient $a_{i_0} \cdot (\text{leading coeff of } p_{i_0})$ — nonzero because $F$ is a field. All other $a_i p_i$ have strictly smaller degree, so they contribute nothing to the coefficient of $x^{\deg p_{i_0}}$ in the sum. So the sum has nonzero coefficient of $x^{\deg p_{i_0}}$, contradicting that it equals $0$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The "monomial-plus-shifts-of-$s$" list is a basis
> **Statement:** Let $s \in F[x]$ with $\deg s = m \geq 1$ and let $n \geq m$. The list
> $$1, x, x^2, \ldots, x^{m-1}, s, xs, x^2 s, \ldots, x^{n-m} s$$
> is a basis of $\mathcal{P}_n(F)$.
>
> **Hint:** count to get $n + 1$ elements; observe the degrees are $\{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$; apply Lemma 1.
>
> **Why needed:** $p$ has a unique decomposition in this basis, which is exactly the division decomposition.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > The first $m$ entries $1, x, \ldots, x^{m-1}$ have degrees $0, 1, \ldots, m-1$. The next $n - m + 1$ entries $s, xs, \ldots, x^{n-m} s$ have degrees $m, m+1, \ldots, n$ (since $\deg(x^k s) = k + m$). Together the $n + 1$ entries have degrees exactly $\{0, 1, \ldots, n\}$ — all distinct. By Lemma 1, the list is linearly independent. Since $\dim \mathcal{P}_n(F) = n + 1$, the list is a basis of $\mathcal{P}_n(F)$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $p, s \in F[x]$ with $s \neq 0$.
>
> **Step 0 — handle the easy case.** If $\deg p < \deg s$, set $q = 0$ and $r = p$. Then $p = sq + r$ trivially and $\deg r = \deg p < \deg s$. Done. From now on, assume $\deg p \geq \deg s$; let $n = \deg p$ and $m = \deg s$, so $n \geq m \geq 0$.
>
> **Step 1 — apply the basis decomposition.** By Lemma 2, the list
> $$1, x, \ldots, x^{m-1}, s, xs, \ldots, x^{n-m} s$$
> is a basis of $\mathcal{P}_n(F)$. Since $p \in \mathcal{P}_n(F)$, there exist unique scalars $a_0, a_1, \ldots, a_{m-1}, b_0, b_1, \ldots, b_{n-m} \in F$ such that
> $$p = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_{m-1} x^{m-1} + b_0 s + b_1 xs + \cdots + b_{n-m} x^{n-m} s.$$
> Group the terms:
> $$p = \underbrace{(a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_{m-1} x^{m-1})}_{r} + s \cdot \underbrace{(b_0 + b_1 x + \cdots + b_{n-m} x^{n-m})}_{q}.$$
> Define $r = a_0 + a_1 x + \cdots + a_{m-1} x^{m-1}$ and $q = b_0 + b_1 x + \cdots + b_{n-m} x^{n-m}$.
>
> **Step 2 — verify the degree condition.** $\deg r \leq m - 1 < m = \deg s$, so the degree condition is satisfied.
>
> **Step 3 — uniqueness.** Suppose $p = sq_1 + r_1 = sq_2 + r_2$ with $\deg r_1, \deg r_2 < \deg s$. Subtracting: $s(q_1 - q_2) = r_2 - r_1$. The right side has $\deg(r_2 - r_1) < \deg s$. The left side has $\deg s(q_1 - q_2) = \deg s + \deg(q_1 - q_2)$ (if $q_1 \neq q_2$), which is $\geq \deg s$. The only way both can hold is $q_1 = q_2$ (making the left side $0$, hence $\deg = -\infty < \deg s$), which forces $r_1 = r_2$. So the decomposition is unique. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Euclidean algorithm for GCDs in $F[x]$ (algebra).** The division algorithm gives the GCD of two polynomials: repeatedly divide and take remainders, just as for integers. *Application:* compute $\gcd(x^4 - 1, x^6 - 1)$ in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$ — the answer is $x^2 - 1$.

**Hensel lifting in $p$-adic analysis.** The division algorithm in $\mathbb{Z}_p[x]$ (where $\mathbb{Z}_p$ is the $p$-adic integers) allows lifting roots modulo $p$ to roots modulo $p^k$, the foundation of $p$-adic analysis and the local-global principle in number theory.

**Polynomial interpolation and the Chinese remainder theorem.** Given values $y_i = p(\lambda_i)$ for distinct $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$, the division algorithm is the engine of polynomial interpolation: $p$ is uniquely determined modulo the product $\prod (x - \lambda_i)$, and the explicit interpolation formula is Lagrange. The same algorithm gives the CRT decomposition $F[x]/\prod(x - \lambda_i) \cong \prod F$, a useful tool in coding theory.

# Bridges

- **[[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean Domain]]** — the abstract framing. The division algorithm is exactly the statement that $F[x]$ is a Euclidean domain with Euclidean function $\deg$. The pair $(F[x], \deg)$ is the canonical example of a Euclidean domain, alongside $(\mathbb{Z}, |\cdot|)$.

- **[[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains|Euclidean Domains are PIDs]]** — the upgrade. The division algorithm immediately implies $F[x]$ is a PID: any ideal $I \subseteq F[x]$ is generated by any element of $I$ of smallest positive degree.

- **[[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]]** — the structural property. PIDness of $F[x]$ is what guarantees the existence and uniqueness of the [[Def - Minimal Polynomial|minimal polynomial]] of an operator (it is the monic generator of a principal ideal of $F[x]$).

- **The factor theorem** — the immediate corollary. Dividing $p$ by $x - \lambda$ gives $p = (x - \lambda) q + r$ with $\deg r < 1$, so $r$ is constant; evaluating at $\lambda$ gives $p(\lambda) = r$. Hence $\lambda$ is a zero of $p$ iff $r = 0$ iff $(x - \lambda) \mid p$.

- **[[Thm - Existence and Uniqueness of Minimal Polynomial|Existence of the Minimal Polynomial]]** — the operator analogue. The set $\operatorname{Ann}(T)$ is an ideal of $F[x]$, hence principal by the PID property, hence has a unique monic generator.
