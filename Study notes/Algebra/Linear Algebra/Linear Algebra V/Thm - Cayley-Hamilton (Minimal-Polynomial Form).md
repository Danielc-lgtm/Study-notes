# Notation

Throughout, $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$, $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ an operator with minimal polynomial $m_T$ and (when defined) characteristic polynomial $\chi_T$. The full registry is on the parent page [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Cayley–Hamilton, Minimal-Polynomial Form).** For any polynomial $q \in F[x]$:
> $$q(T) = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad m_T \mid q.$$
> Equivalently, the kernel of the evaluation map $\Phi_T : F[x] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$, $p \mapsto p(T)$, is exactly $(m_T)$.

> **Theorem (Classical Cayley–Hamilton, for reference).** When the characteristic polynomial $\chi_T$ is defined (via [[Def - Determinant|determinants]], in [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants]]), $\chi_T(T) = 0$. Equivalently, $m_T \mid \chi_T$, and the multiplicity of each eigenvalue in $\chi_T$ is at least its multiplicity in $m_T$.

> **Note on the LADR framework.** In the determinant-free approach of LADR, the *classical* Cayley–Hamilton theorem is essentially **tautological** once one has the minimal polynomial and (later) the characteristic polynomial: $m_T$ annihilates $T$ by definition, $\chi_T$ is then defined as a polynomial sharing the same eigenvalues with appropriate multiplicities, and the divisibility $m_T \mid \chi_T$ is immediate from comparing roots with multiplicities. The deep content of LADR's chapter 5 is the **minimal polynomial form** above — which is *the* non-trivial annihilation statement, the one from which everything else flows.

# Motivation

This is the **divisibility characterisation of annihilating polynomials**. The minimal polynomial $m_T$ is defined as the smallest-degree monic polynomial with $m_T(T) = 0$; this theorem says that *every* polynomial annihilating $T$ is a multiple of $m_T$ — the minimal polynomial is the *universal* [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]].

In the classical (determinant-based) treatments of linear algebra, the **Cayley–Hamilton theorem** is the statement $\chi_T(T) = 0$, where $\chi_T(z) = \det(zI - T)$ is the characteristic polynomial. This is presented as a deep and surprising result: "every matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation." LADR's approach inverts the perspective: the *minimal* polynomial is the universal [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]], and once you have it, the characteristic polynomial is the *secondary* invariant of $T$ (built from the determinant) which happens to have $m_T$ as a divisor. The classical Cayley–Hamilton then becomes the statement "the characteristic polynomial is also annihilating, by the divisibility property of $m_T$." This is much less mysterious — once you accept that there is *some* annihilating polynomial (the minimal one), additional annihilating polynomials are just its multiples.

The minimal polynomial form is the *operational* statement: when you have a polynomial relation in $T$ (e.g. $T^k = I$, or $T^2 = T$, or $T^3 = T^2 + T$), the relation tells you something about $m_T$ — specifically, that $m_T$ divides the corresponding polynomial. So polynomial relations in $T$ are constraints on $m_T$, not (immediately) on $T$ itself. The structural picture is:

$$\text{polynomial relations on } T \;\longleftrightarrow\; \text{multiples of } m_T.$$

A consequence: the set of polynomials annihilating $T$ is the principal [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $(m_T)$ in $F[x]$, equivalently the kernel of the evaluation homomorphism $\Phi_T : F[x] \to \mathcal{L}(V)$. The image of $\Phi_T$ is the subalgebra $F[T]$ of $\mathcal{L}(V)$ generated by $T$, and the first isomorphism theorem gives
$$F[T] \cong F[x] / (m_T) \cong F[x] / \operatorname{Ann}(T).$$

The [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] of $F[T]$ as an $F$-vector space is therefore $\deg m_T$. This is the substantial content of the minimal polynomial: it controls the *size* of the subalgebra generated by $T$, and hence the [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] of the space of polynomials in $T$.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The first disguised source is **any explicit polynomial relation in $T$**. Whenever $T$ satisfies a polynomial equation $p(T) = 0$, the minimal polynomial form gives $m_T \mid p$, immediately constraining $m_T$ to be one of finitely many monic divisors of $p$.

The second disguised source is **a polynomial $p$ known to annihilate $T$ for structural reasons**. For example, if $T$ has been shown to be a finite-order operator (i.e., $T^k = I$ for some $k$), then $m_T \mid z^k - 1$. If $T$ has been shown to be idempotent ($T^2 = T$), then $m_T \mid z(z - 1)$. The minimal polynomial form converts these structural statements into finite searches.

The third disguised source is **the eventual characteristic polynomial $\chi_T$**. When determinants are available (later), one can define $\chi_T = \det(zI - T)$ and prove (this is the classical Cayley–Hamilton theorem) that $\chi_T(T) = 0$. The minimal polynomial form then gives $m_T \mid \chi_T$ — the universal divisibility relation between these two invariants.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

Combined with **the dimension count $\deg m_T \leq \dim V$**, the theorem amplifies to a **finite description of all polynomial relations**: every polynomial relation in $T$ is determined by the (finitely many) coefficients of $m_T$. The ring $F[T]$ has dimension exactly $\deg m_T$, with basis $I, T, T^2, \ldots, T^{\deg m_T - 1}$.

Combined with **the factorisation of $m_T$**, the theorem amplifies to **specific algebraic identities for $T$**. For instance, $T^k = I$ over $\mathbb{C}$ implies $m_T \mid z^k - 1$, which factors into distinct linear factors, so $T$ is diagonalisable with eigenvalues that are $k$-th roots of unity.

Combined with **the spectral characterisation of $m_T$** (its roots are the eigenvalues), the theorem amplifies to: **the minimal polynomial detects the spectrum and its "Jordan structure"** — its degree equals the sum of the maximum Jordan block sizes at each eigenvalue, and its roots are the eigenvalues.

# Why Is It True

The mechanism is the [[Thm - Division Algorithm for Polynomials (LA)|division algorithm]] applied to the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] of annihilating polynomials.

**Forward ($m_T \mid q \Rightarrow q(T) = 0$):** If $q = m_T \cdot s$ for some $s \in F[x]$, then $q(T) = m_T(T) \cdot s(T) = 0 \cdot s(T) = 0$.

**Backward ($q(T) = 0 \Rightarrow m_T \mid q$):** Suppose $q(T) = 0$. Divide $q$ by $m_T$ using the division algorithm: $q = m_T \cdot s + r$ with $\deg r < \deg m_T$. Apply to $T$:
$$0 = q(T) = m_T(T) s(T) + r(T) = 0 \cdot s(T) + r(T) = r(T).$$
So $r(T) = 0$. But $\deg r < \deg m_T$, and $m_T$ is the smallest-degree polynomial annihilating $T$ — so the only such $r$ is $r = 0$. Hence $q = m_T \cdot s$, i.e. $m_T \mid q$.

> **The mechanism in one sentence: divide $q$ by $m_T$; the remainder must annihilate $T$ but have smaller degree than $m_T$, contradicting the minimality unless the remainder is zero.**

---

# What Makes This Hard

There is nothing non-trivial in the proof — it is a direct application of the division algorithm and the minimality of $m_T$. The conceptual content lies in *interpretation*: recognising that "the kernel of the evaluation map is principal" combined with the choice of monic generator $m_T$ as the smallest-degree element of the kernel is exactly the divisibility characterisation. Beginners often state $m_T$ as "the smallest-degree annihilator" without realising that this immediately implies the divisibility property by the division algorithm.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy.** Forward direction is direct (multiplication of polynomials commutes with evaluation). Backward direction uses the division algorithm to extract a remainder of smaller degree than $m_T$, which forces the remainder to be zero by minimality.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Forward direction: $m_T \mid q \Rightarrow q(T) = 0$.** Write $q = m_T s$; evaluate at $T$.
   - *Hint:* $(m_T s)(T) = m_T(T) s(T)$ because $\Phi_T$ is a [[Def - Ring|ring]] homomorphism.
   - *Why needed:* completes the trivial direction.

2. **Backward setup: divide $q$ by $m_T$.** Use the [[Thm - Division Algorithm for Polynomials (LA)|division algorithm]]: $q = m_T s + r$ with $\deg r < \deg m_T$.
   - *Hint:* division algorithm always applies when the divisor $m_T \neq 0$.
   - *Why needed:* extracts the remainder for the next step.

3. **Backward conclusion: $r(T) = 0$ and $\deg r < \deg m_T$ force $r = 0$.** Apply the division equation at $T$: $0 = q(T) = m_T(T) s(T) + r(T) = r(T)$. So $r$ is an annihilator of $T$ with $\deg r < \deg m_T$. By minimality of $m_T$, $r = 0$.
   - *Hint:* the minimal polynomial is the smallest-degree monic annihilator; a smaller-degree annihilator must be zero.
   - *Why needed:* completes the backward direction.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Evaluation is a [[Def - Ring|ring]] homomorphism
> **Statement:** For $p, q \in F[x]$ and $c \in F$: $(p + q)(T) = p(T) + q(T)$, $(cp)(T) = c \cdot p(T)$, and $(pq)(T) = p(T) q(T)$.
>
> **Hint:** all three follow from direct expansion of the operator definitions; the multiplicative property uses that polynomials in the same operator commute (consequence of $F[x]$ being commutative and the evaluation being a ring homomorphism).
>
> **Why needed:** lets us evaluate the division identity $q = m_T s + r$ at $T$ to get $q(T) = m_T(T) s(T) + r(T)$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > For $(pq)(T) = p(T) q(T)$: write $p = \sum a_j x^j$, $q = \sum b_k x^k$, then $pq = \sum_n (\sum_{j+k=n} a_j b_k) x^n$. Evaluating, $(pq)(T) = \sum_n (\sum_{j+k=n} a_j b_k) T^n$. Direct expansion of $p(T) q(T) = (\sum_j a_j T^j)(\sum_k b_k T^k) = \sum_{j,k} a_j b_k T^{j+k}$, regrouping by $n = j + k$, gives the same thing. The additive identities are immediate.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $V$ be finite-dimensional, $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$, $m_T$ the minimal polynomial of $T$, and $q \in F[x]$.
>
> **($\Leftarrow$) $m_T \mid q \Rightarrow q(T) = 0$.** If $m_T \mid q$, write $q = m_T s$ for some $s \in F[x]$. By Lemma 1, $q(T) = m_T(T) s(T) = 0 \cdot s(T) = 0$.
>
> **($\Rightarrow$) $q(T) = 0 \Rightarrow m_T \mid q$.** Suppose $q(T) = 0$.
>
> By the [[Thm - Division Algorithm for Polynomials (LA)|division algorithm]] applied to $q$ and $m_T$ (since $m_T \neq 0$), there exist unique $s, r \in F[x]$ with
> $$q = m_T s + r, \qquad \deg r < \deg m_T.$$
> By Lemma 1, evaluating at $T$:
> $$0 = q(T) = m_T(T) s(T) + r(T) = 0 \cdot s(T) + r(T) = r(T).$$
> So $r(T) = 0$ with $\deg r < \deg m_T$. By the minimality of $m_T$ ([[Thm - Existence and Uniqueness of Minimal Polynomial]]), no nonzero polynomial of degree less than $\deg m_T$ annihilates $T$. So $r = 0$, hence $q = m_T s$, i.e. $m_T \mid q$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Discussion: the classical Cayley–Hamilton theorem
> When the determinant is available (developed in [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants]]), the **characteristic polynomial** of $T$ is defined as $\chi_T(z) = \det(zI - T)$. Properties of the determinant give $\deg \chi_T = \dim V$ and that the roots of $\chi_T$ in $F$ are the eigenvalues of $T$ (with **algebraic multiplicities** — the multiplicities in $\chi_T$, which equal the sum of Jordan block sizes at each eigenvalue over $\mathbb{C}$).
>
> The classical Cayley–Hamilton theorem states $\chi_T(T) = 0$. In the LADR framework, this is *not* proved by a determinant-trick — it is proved (later, in chapter 9 of LADR) by combining the existence of an upper-triangular form (over $\mathbb{C}$) with the easy fact that an operator's product of "(T - diagonal entry)" annihilates it.
>
> Once $\chi_T(T) = 0$ is known, the minimal polynomial form immediately gives $m_T \mid \chi_T$. Comparing degrees, the ratio $\chi_T / m_T$ is a polynomial whose factors are the **excess Jordan blocks** beyond the largest one at each eigenvalue — see [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]].

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Evaluating polynomials in matrices (computational linear algebra).** For an explicit matrix $A \in M_n(F)$, the minimal polynomial form converts the question "compute $p(A)$ for a large-degree polynomial $p$" into "compute $p$ modulo $m_A$ and evaluate" — only powers up to $\deg m_A - 1$ are needed. *Application:* for $A$ with $m_A = z^2 - z$ (a projection), $A^k = A$ for all $k \geq 1$; so $e^A = I + (e - 1) A$ via the polynomial expansion.

**Square roots of operators (operator theory).** When does $T$ have a square root, i.e. $S$ with $S^2 = T$? The minimal polynomial form is part of the analysis: if $T$ is invertible and $m_T$ factors with no repeated roots over $\mathbb{C}$, then $T$ has a square root by extracting square roots of each eigenvalue. The general analysis requires the Jordan form, but the minimal-polynomial form sets up the framework.

**Differential equations and the matrix exponential (analysis).** For solving $\dot x = Ax$, the matrix exponential $e^{At}$ is a function of $A$ that, by the minimal polynomial form, is computable as a polynomial in $A$ of degree $< \deg m_A$. Specifically, $e^{At} = \sum_k a_k(t) A^k$ where the coefficients $a_k(t)$ are determined by the eigenvalue structure of $A$. This is the **resolvent method** for computing matrix exponentials.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Existence and Uniqueness of Minimal Polynomial|Existence and Uniqueness of mₜ]]** — the foundation. The minimal polynomial form is the *consequence* of $m_T$ being the smallest-degree monic annihilator.

- **[[Thm - Division Algorithm for Polynomials (LA)|Division Algorithm]]** — the engine. The proof of the divisibility direction is a direct application of the division algorithm.

- **The Characteristic Polynomial** — the related invariant. The classical Cayley–Hamilton theorem $\chi_T(T) = 0$ is the statement that the characteristic polynomial is also an annihilator. By the minimal polynomial form, $m_T \mid \chi_T$, with the difference being the "excess" Jordan structure. See [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants]] for the characteristic polynomial; in Modules II this is made precise via [[Thm - Jordan Normal Form]].

- **The Annihilator Ideal in Module Theory** — the abstract framing. In the language of [[Def - The Module of a Linear Operator|$F[x]$-modules]], the minimal polynomial form is the statement that the **annihilator** of the module $V_T$ is the principal ideal $(m_T)$ — the universal property of the annihilator.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Classical Cayley–Hamilton Theorem *(from Linear Algebra IX)*
> Once the characteristic polynomial $\chi_T$ is defined via determinants, the classical Cayley–Hamilton theorem $\chi_T(T) = 0$ is proved, and the minimal polynomial form gives $m_T \mid \chi_T$ for free.

> [!tip] Matrix Exponentials and Resolvent Calculus *(from Differential Equations / Functional Analysis)*
> The minimal polynomial form is the basis of the resolvent calculus: $f(T)$ — for any reasonable function $f$ — is computable as a polynomial in $T$ of degree $< \deg m_T$. This is the finite-dimensional version of the **holomorphic functional calculus**.

> [!tip] Structure Theorem for $F[x]$-Modules *(from Modules II)*
> The minimal polynomial form is the statement that the annihilator of $V_T$ as an $F[x]$-module is the principal ideal $(m_T)$. The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID then decomposes $V_T$ as $\bigoplus_k F[x]/(f_k)$ with $f_k \mid f_{k+1}$, where $f_s = m_T$ is the last invariant factor. See [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain]].
