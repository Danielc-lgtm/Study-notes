# Notation Registry

Throughout this topic, $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional vector spaces over a field $\mathbb{F}$, almost always $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. When the symmetric-bilinear-form classification is at stake we restrict to $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{R}$, so that the signs of diagonal entries are intrinsic; when the existence of eigenvalues is invoked (for example in the proof that the determinant equals the product of eigenvalues with multiplicity) we restrict to $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{C}$. The standing convention is that "finite-dimensional" is the default, and $n = \dim V$ unless otherwise stated.

- $\beta : V \times V \to \mathbb{F}$ — a **bilinear form** on $V$; the space of bilinear forms is $V^{(2)}$.
- $V^{(2)}_{\mathrm{sym}}$, $V^{(2)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$ — the subspaces of symmetric and alternating bilinear forms; $V^{(2)} = V^{(2)}_{\mathrm{sym}} \oplus V^{(2)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$ in characteristic $\neq 2$.
- $q : V \to \mathbb{F}$ — a **quadratic form**; $q_\beta(v) = \beta(v,v)$ is the quadratic form associated with the bilinear form $\beta$.
- $\mathcal{M}(\beta, (e_1, \dots, e_n))$ — the matrix of a bilinear form in a basis; entry $(i,j)$ is $\beta(e_i, e_j)$.
- Signature $(p, q, r)$ of a real symmetric bilinear form — the number of positive, negative, and zero diagonal entries in any diagonalization; by Sylvester's law, this is intrinsic.
- $V^m = \underbrace{V \times \cdots \times V}_{m \text{ factors}}$; $V^{(m)}$ — the space of $m$-linear forms $V^m \to \mathbb{F}$; $V^{(m)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$ — the alternating ones.
- $\operatorname{perm}(m)$ — the set of permutations of $\{1, \dots, m\}$; $\operatorname{sign}(\sigma) \in \{\pm 1\}$ is the sign of a permutation.
- $\det T$ — the **determinant of an operator** $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$, defined as the unique scalar with $\alpha_T = (\det T)\alpha$ for every $\alpha \in V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$, where $\alpha_T(v_1, \dots, v_n) := \alpha(Tv_1, \dots, Tv_n)$.
- $\det A$ — the determinant of a square matrix $A$, equal to $\det T$ for the operator $T$ whose matrix is $A$ in the standard basis. Equivalently, the explicit Leibniz formula
$$\det A = \sum_{\sigma \in \operatorname{perm}(n)} \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, A_{\sigma(1), 1} \cdots A_{\sigma(n), n}.$$
- $V \otimes W$ — the **tensor product** of $V$ and $W$; $v \otimes w \in V \otimes W$ for $v \in V$, $w \in W$.
- $\mathcal{B}(V, W; U)$ — the space of bilinear maps $V \times W \to U$; the universal property says $\mathcal{B}(V, W; U) \cong \mathcal{L}(V \otimes W, U)$ naturally.
- $\Lambda^k V$ — the $k$-th exterior power; alternating $k$-linear forms on $V^*$ (or equivalently, the quotient of $V^{\otimes k}$ by the antisymmetrisation relations). The wedge product $v_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge v_k$ is the canonical alternating tensor.

The whole chapter is held together by **alternating multilinearity**, so we keep one font convention: lower-case Greek $\alpha, \beta, \rho, \tau$ for forms, lower-case Roman $u, v, w$ for vectors, and upper-case $T, S$ for operators.

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **the determinant is the unique-up-to-scalar alternating multilinear form on $n$-tuples of vectors in an $n$-dimensional space**, and once you accept that statement everything else is corollaries. Multilinear algebra is the language you need to *say* that sentence; the determinant is what falls out at the end; bilinear and quadratic forms are the warm-up that teaches you the relevant kind of two-input linearity; tensor products are the abstract object that produces all multilinear maps from linear ones.

Chapter 9 of LADR is structured to make this conceptual ordering visible, and it is worth pausing on. In a first linear algebra course [[Def - Determinant|determinants]] come early, defined by the Leibniz sum $\sum \operatorname{sign}(\sigma) A_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots A_{\sigma(n),n}$ or by cofactor expansion, and their multilinearity and antisymmetry are derived as properties. LADR inverts this: the determinant is *defined* by the multilinear-alternating-uniqueness property and the explicit formula is *derived* from it. The reason this matters is that the explicit formula is computationally useless beyond $n = 4$ or so (the sum has $n!$ terms) and has no conceptual content; what makes the determinant useful — its multiplicativity, the change-of-variables formula, the eigenvalue-product identity, its geometric meaning as a signed volume scaling factor — all flow directly from the alternating-multilinear definition and would be miracles from the Leibniz sum. The whole point of the chapter is to take the determinant away from being a number you compute and turn it into the *unique* alternating multilinear gadget in $n$ variables, of which everything else is a manifestation.

The chapter splits naturally into four pieces, organised by the structural backbone

$$\text{bilinear forms} \;\subset\; \text{multilinear forms} \;\subset\; \text{tensor products,}\qquad \det \;=\; \text{the canonical alternating } n\text{-linear form.}$$

§9A studies the two-variable case: **bilinear and quadratic forms**, with the central theorem being that every symmetric bilinear form is diagonalizable, and the central invariant being the signature $(p, q, r)$ classified by [[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia|Sylvester's law of inertia]]. This is where the Minkowski metric of [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|special relativity]] comes from and where the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]] is defined as the [[Def - Isometry|isometry]] group of a non-degenerate symmetric form of signature $(1, 3)$. §9B generalises to **alternating multilinear forms**, proves the one-dimensionality $\dim V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}} = 1$ that makes the determinant unique, and develops the permutation calculus that powers all proofs. §9C is **[[Def - Determinant|determinants]]**: define them as the unique scalar attached to an operator, derive multiplicativity in a single line, derive the eigenvalue-product formula, derive cofactor expansion and Cramer's rule, and reinterpret everything as a volume scaling factor. §9D introduces **tensor products** via Axler's clever finite-dimensional construction $V \otimes W := \mathcal{B}(V', W')$ (the bilinear forms on the dual product), proves the universal property, and shows it is the right notion for converting multilinearity into linearity once and for all.

The chapter assumes fluency with the preceding material: bases and [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] from [[Linear Algebra II — §2 Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces]], linear maps and matrices from [[Linear Algebra III — §3A–D Linear Maps]], the dual space and dual map from [[Linear Algebra IV — §3E–F Products, Quotients, Duality]], eigenvalues and the minimal polynomial from [[Linear Algebra V — §4–5 Polynomials and Eigenvalues]], and ideally the real spectral theorem from [[Linear Algebra VII — §7 Operators on Inner Product Spaces]] for the proof of Sylvester's law. Familiarity with the [[Def - Group|group]]-theoretic notion of the sign of a permutation (from [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]] or its symmetric-group treatment) helps but is not strictly needed.

# Concept Map

## §9A Bilinear and Quadratic Forms

- **[[Def - Bilinear Form]]**
	- A **bilinear form** on $V$ is a function $\beta : V \times V \to \mathbb{F}$ that is linear in each slot when the other is held fixed: $\beta(au + a'u', w) = a\beta(u, w) + a'\beta(u', w)$ and likewise in the second slot. The matrix of $\beta$ in a basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ is $A_{ij} = \beta(e_i, e_j)$, and the change-of-basis rule is $A = C^t B C$ — the transpose appears, not the inverse, distinguishing it from the operator change-of-basis. Canonical examples are inner products on real spaces, $\beta(u, v) = u^t M v$ for a fixed matrix $M$, and $\beta(p, q) = \int_0^1 pq'$ on polynomial spaces.

- **[[Def - Symmetric and Alternating Bilinear Form]]**
	- A bilinear form $\rho$ is **symmetric** if $\rho(u, w) = \rho(w, u)$ for all $u, w$, and **alternating** if $\alpha(v, v) = 0$ for all $v$ — equivalently $\alpha(u, w) = -\alpha(w, u)$ in characteristic $\neq 2$. The space of bilinear forms decomposes as $V^{(2)} = V^{(2)}_{\mathrm{sym}} \oplus V^{(2)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$ via the symmetrisation $\rho = \tfrac{1}{2}(\beta + \beta^t)$ and antisymmetrisation $\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}(\beta - \beta^t)$. A form is symmetric iff its matrix is symmetric in *every* (equivalently, *some*) basis; the two characterisations agree because the transpose appears in the change-of-basis formula.

- **[[Def - Quadratic Form]]**
	- A **quadratic form** is a function $q : V \to \mathbb{F}$ such that $q(v) = \beta(v, v)$ for some bilinear form $\beta$. In characteristic $\neq 2$ there is a unique *symmetric* bilinear form $\rho$ realising $q$, recovered via the **polarisation identity** $\rho(u, w) = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(q(u + w) - q(u) - q(w)\big)$. Quadratic forms on $\mathbb{F}^n$ are exactly the functions $q(x) = \sum_{j,k} A_{jk} x_j x_k$ that are quadratic and homogeneous; the diagonalisation theorem says every quadratic form can be brought into the form $\lambda_1 x_1^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n x_n^2$ by a change of basis.

- **[[Ex - The bilinear form determined by a symmetric matrix]]** (⭐)
	- Show that for a symmetric matrix $M$, the form $\beta(u, v) = u^t M v$ is bilinear and symmetric, compute its matrix in the standard basis, and verify that it is positive definite iff $M$ has all positive eigenvalues.

- **[[Thm - Diagonalization of a Symmetric Bilinear Form]]**
	- Every symmetric bilinear form on a finite-dimensional space admits a basis in which its matrix is diagonal; over the reals, if $V$ is an inner product space, the basis can be chosen orthonormal (using the real spectral theorem applied to the self-adjoint operator $T$ with $\beta(u, v) = \langle u, Tv\rangle$). The proof is by induction on [[Def - Dimension|dimension]]: find $v$ with $\rho(v, v) \neq 0$, take the $\rho$-orthogonal complement $\{u : \rho(u, v) = 0\}$, and use the inductive hypothesis there. This is the foundation on which Sylvester's law is built.

- **[[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia]]**
	- For a real symmetric bilinear form $\rho$ on an $n$-dimensional space, the number $p$ of positive diagonal entries, the number $q$ of negative diagonal entries, and the number $r = n - p - q$ of zero entries are **the same in every diagonalising basis**. The triple $(p, q, r)$ is the **signature** of $\rho$, an intrinsic invariant. The proof is a dimension-counting argument: a $(p+r)$-dimensional positive-semidefinite [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] and a $q$-dimensional negative-definite subspace can intersect only in $\{0\}$, so $(p+r) + q \leq n$. This is the theorem that makes "the Minkowski metric has signature $(1, 3)$" a coordinate-free statement.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Quadratic Form|Quadratic Form]] on a [[Def - Riemannian Manifold|Riemannian Manifold]] *(from Differential Geometry)*
> The Riemannian metric $g_p$ on a manifold $M$ at a point $p$ is a positive-definite symmetric bilinear form on $T_p M$; pseudo-Riemannian generalisations (Lorentzian, etc.) allow signature $(1, n-1)$. The fact that "positive definite" and "signature $(1, n-1)$" are basis-free statements is Sylvester's law.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Minkowski Metric *(from Special Relativity)*
> The Minkowski metric is a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on $\mathbb{R}^4$ of signature $(1, 3)$ (or $(3, 1)$, depending on convention). See [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric]]. The fact that the signature is an invariant — not an artefact of coordinates — is exactly Sylvester's law of inertia.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Hessian *(from Multivariate Analysis)*
> The Hessian of a smooth function $f$ at a critical point is a symmetric bilinear form on the tangent space, classifying the critical point as a minimum, maximum, or saddle according to its signature.

## §9B Alternating Multilinear Forms

- **[[Def - Multilinear Form]]**
	- An **$m$-linear form** on $V$ is a function $\beta : V^m \to \mathbb{F}$ that is linear in each slot when the others are held fixed. The space $V^{(m)}$ of $m$-linear forms is a vector space, with $V^{(1)} = V^*$ (linear functionals) and $V^{(2)}$ the bilinear forms of §9A. The dimension is $(\dim V)^m$, because an $m$-linear form is determined by its values on $m$-tuples of basis vectors.

- **[[Def - Alternating Multilinear Form]]**
	- An $m$-linear form $\alpha$ is **alternating** if $\alpha(v_1, \dots, v_m) = 0$ whenever the list has a repeated vector, $v_j = v_k$ for some $j \neq k$. Three equivalent operational characterisations: (i) swapping any two arguments multiplies the value by $-1$; (ii) the form vanishes on every linearly dependent list; (iii) for a permutation $\sigma$, $\alpha(v_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, v_{\sigma(m)}) = \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, \alpha(v_1, \dots, v_m)$. The first is the most useful trigger; the second is the input-broadening that hooks alternating forms onto linear independence; the third is the calculational tool.

- **The one-dimensionality theorem** ($\dim V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}} = 1$)
	- The space of alternating $n$-linear forms on an $n$-dimensional space is **one-dimensional**: any two nonzero such forms differ by a scalar multiple. This is the structural fact that makes the determinant uniquely defined. The proof has two halves: (i) an alternating form is determined by its value $\alpha(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ on a basis (by multilinearity plus the permutation formula $\alpha(v_1, \dots, v_n) = \alpha(e_1, \dots, e_n) \sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, b_{\sigma(1),1}\cdots b_{\sigma(n),n}$), so $\dim \leq 1$; (ii) the explicit Leibniz construction $\alpha(v_1, \dots, v_n) = \sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, \varphi_{\sigma(1)}(v_1)\cdots \varphi_{\sigma(n)}(v_n)$ produces a nonzero form, so $\dim \geq 1$. For $m > n$ there are no nonzero alternating $m$-linear forms — any $m$-tuple in an $n$-dimensional space is linearly dependent.

- **[[Ex - The wedge product as an alternating multilinear form]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For $\varphi_1, \dots, \varphi_m \in V^*$, the wedge product $\varphi_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_m$ is defined by $(\varphi_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \varphi_m)(v_1, \dots, v_m) = \det[\varphi_i(v_j)]$. Show this is alternating and multilinear, and that wedge products of basis dual vectors give a basis for $V^{(m)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Differential Form|Differential Forms]] *(from Differential Geometry)*
> A differential $k$-form on a manifold $M$ is a smooth section of the alternating tensor bundle $\Lambda^k T^*M$ — at each point it is an alternating $k$-linear form on the tangent space. The whole machinery of exterior calculus, Stokes' theorem, and de Rham cohomology runs on this construction. The fact that $\dim \Lambda^k T^*M = \binom{n}{k}$ at each point — peaking at $\binom{n}{n/2}$ in the middle and dropping to $\binom{n}{n} = 1$ at the top — is what gives the top form (the volume form) its uniqueness.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Exterior Algebra *(from Algebra)*
> The exterior algebra $\Lambda^* V = \bigoplus_k \Lambda^k V$ is the universal graded algebra in which $v \wedge v = 0$, equivalently the quotient of the tensor algebra by the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] generated by $v \otimes v$. It packages all alternating multilinear constructions into a single algebraic object and is the home of differential forms, the wedge product, and the Plücker embedding of Grassmannians.

## §9C Determinants

- **[[Def - Determinant]]**
	- The **determinant of an operator** $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ is the unique scalar $\det T$ such that $\alpha(Tv_1, \dots, Tv_n) = (\det T)\, \alpha(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ for every alternating $n$-linear form $\alpha$ on $V$. This is well-defined because the action $\alpha \mapsto \alpha \circ T$ is a linear map of the one-dimensional space $V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$ to itself, and every such map is multiplication by a scalar. The determinant of a matrix is the determinant of the corresponding operator on $\mathbb{F}^n$, and equals the Leibniz formula $\det A = \sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, A_{\sigma(1), 1}\cdots A_{\sigma(n), n}$. Geometrically, $|\det T|$ is the factor by which $T$ scales $n$-dimensional volume; the sign records orientation.

- **[[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative]]**
	- $\det(ST) = (\det S)(\det T)$ for any operators $S, T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$; equivalently $\det(AB) = (\det A)(\det B)$ for square matrices of the same size. The proof is one line from the definition: $\alpha_{ST}(v_1, \dots, v_n) = \alpha(STv_1, \dots, STv_n) = (\det S)\alpha(Tv_1, \dots, Tv_n) = (\det S)(\det T)\alpha(v_1, \dots, v_n)$. Corollary: $T$ is invertible iff $\det T \neq 0$, and then $\det T^{-1} = 1/\det T$. The multiplicativity is what makes the determinant a *homomorphism* from $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{F})$ to $\mathbb{F}^\times$, whose kernel is $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb{F})$.

- **[[Ex - Determinant of an upper-triangular matrix is the product of diagonal entries]]** (⭐)
	- Show that $\det$ of an upper-triangular matrix equals the product of its diagonal entries, by examining which permutations contribute to the Leibniz sum.

- **[[Thm - Determinant Equals Product of Eigenvalues with Multiplicity]]**
	- For an operator $T \in \mathcal{L}(V)$ on a complex (or any algebraically closed) vector space, $\det T = \lambda_1 \lambda_2 \cdots \lambda_n$, where the $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of $T$ listed with algebraic multiplicity. Equivalently, the characteristic polynomial is $p_T(z) = \det(zI - T) = \prod (z - \lambda_i)$. Two proofs: (i) reduce to upper-triangular form via Schur's theorem and use the upper-triangular formula; (ii) use the multiplicativity of $\det$ on the Jordan decomposition. This bridges the two worlds: $\det$ in the abstract sense and "eigenvalue arithmetic". For real spaces, $\det$ equals the product of complex eigenvalues, which is real because non-real ones come in conjugate pairs.

- **[[Thm - Cofactor Expansion and Cramer's Rule]]**
	- The determinant of an $n \times n$ matrix can be computed by **cofactor expansion** along any row or column: $\det A = \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{i+j} A_{ij} M_{ij}$, where $M_{ij}$ is the determinant of the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ minor obtained by deleting row $i$ and column $j$. **Cramer's rule** then gives an explicit formula for the inverse: $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\, \operatorname{adj}(A)$, where the adjugate $\operatorname{adj}(A)$ has entries $(\operatorname{adj} A)_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ji}$. This is the formula that solves $Ax = b$ as $x_i = \det A_i / \det A$, where $A_i$ replaces the $i$th column of $A$ by $b$. Computationally useless for $n > 4$ (Gaussian elimination wins by a wide margin), it is the formula that makes the determinant *visibly* a polynomial in the matrix entries — used in proofs about smoothness of the determinant, continuity of eigenvalues, and the [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton (Minimal-Polynomial Form)|Cayley-Hamilton]] theorem.

- **[[Ex - Determinant of a block matrix]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For a block upper-triangular matrix $\begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ 0 & D \end{pmatrix}$, show $\det = \det A \cdot \det D$. For a general $2 \times 2$ block matrix with $D$ invertible and the blocks commuting, $\det = \det(AD - BC)$.

- **[[Ex - Cayley-Hamilton via determinants and via the minimal polynomial agree]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- The [[Thm - Cayley-Hamilton (Minimal-Polynomial Form)|Cayley-Hamilton]] theorem says every operator $T$ satisfies its own characteristic polynomial $p_T(T) = 0$. Show that the determinantal characteristic polynomial $\det(zI - T)$ agrees with the [[Def - Minimal Polynomial|minimal polynomial]] up to multiplicity factors, and use this to compare the LADR §8 proof of Cayley-Hamilton (via minimal polynomials) with the §9 proof (via the adjugate identity $(zI - T)\operatorname{adj}(zI - T) = \det(zI - T) I$).

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Volume Form|Volume Form]] *(from Differential Geometry)*
> On an oriented Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$, the **volume form** is the unique alternating top-form $\omega \in \Lambda^n T^*M$ with $\omega(e_1, \dots, e_n) = 1$ for every positively oriented orthonormal frame. It lets you integrate functions over the manifold, and in local coordinates it reads $\omega = \sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\, dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n$. The factor $\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}$ is what carries the determinant's role as "volume scaling factor" into curved geometry.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Lie Group|Lie Group]] SL(n) *(from Lie Theory)*
> The **special linear group** $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{F}) = \ker(\det)$ is the kernel of the determinant homomorphism $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{F}) \to \mathbb{F}^\times$, hence a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] of $\mathrm{GL}(n)$. It is the volume-preserving (and orientation-preserving, over $\mathbb{R}$) linear transformations, and one of the most studied non-compact Lie [[Def - Group|groups]]. Its Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{sl}(n) = \{X : \operatorname{tr} X = 0\}$, the trace-zero matrices, because the trace is the linearisation of the determinant at the identity: $\det(I + \epsilon X) = 1 + \epsilon \operatorname{tr} X + O(\epsilon^2)$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Jacobian Change of Variables *(from Multivariate Analysis)*
> For a $C^1$-[[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]] $\Phi : U \to \Phi(U) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, the change-of-variables formula is $\int_{\Phi(U)} f\, dx = \int_U f(\Phi(y)) |\det D\Phi(y)|\, dy$. The Jacobian determinant $|\det D\Phi|$ is the local volume scaling factor — exactly the geometric content of $\det$ in linear algebra, applied infinitesimally. See [[Def - Partial Derivatives and the Jacobian Matrix|the Jacobian]] and [[Def - The Total Derivative and Differentiability|the total derivative]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Reidemeister Torsion *(from Topology — for the more curious)*
> Reidemeister torsion is a topological invariant defined using determinants of chain-complex maps. It distinguishes lens spaces $L(7, 1)$ and $L(7, 2)$, which have the same [[Def - Homotopy|homotopy]] type, the same homology, and the same simple invariants, but different torsion. The technical content is that a chain complex of finite-dimensional vector spaces with a chosen basis at each level has a torsion element in $\mathbb{F}^\times / (\pm 1)$, defined as an alternating product of determinants of basis-change matrices.

## §9D Tensor Products

- **[[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]]**
	- The **tensor product** $V \otimes W$ is the vector space that converts bilinear maps from $V \times W$ into linear maps from $V \otimes W$. Concretely (LADR's construction): set $V \otimes W := \mathcal{B}(V', W')$, the space of bilinear forms on the dual product, and define $v \otimes w \in V \otimes W$ by $(v \otimes w)(\varphi, \tau) := \varphi(v)\tau(w)$. Then $\dim(V \otimes W) = \dim V \cdot \dim W$, the tensor product $\otimes : V \times W \to V \otimes W$ is bilinear, and the elements $e_i \otimes f_j$ for bases $(e_i)$ of $V$ and $(f_j)$ of $W$ form a basis of $V \otimes W$. Elementary tensors $v \otimes w$ span but do not exhaust $V \otimes W$: most elements are *sums* of elementary tensors.

- **[[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product]]**
	- For every bilinear map $\Gamma : V \times W \to U$ into any vector space $U$, there is a unique linear map $\hat\Gamma : V \otimes W \to U$ with $\hat\Gamma(v \otimes w) = \Gamma(v, w)$. Equivalently: the bilinear map $\otimes : V \times W \to V \otimes W$ is **initial** among bilinear maps out of $V \times W$ — every other bilinear map factors through it uniquely. This is the categorical characterisation that pins $V \otimes W$ down up to a unique isomorphism, independent of any construction: any two solutions to "factor every bilinear map through one fixed linear-map source" are canonically isomorphic. The construction $\mathcal{B}(V', W')$ is one way to write down a solution; the universal property is what the solution *means*.

- **[[Ex - Tensor product of two copies of F^2 has dimension 4]]** (⭐)
	- Compute $\mathbb{F}^2 \otimes \mathbb{F}^2$ and verify $\dim = 4$. Exhibit an explicit basis $e_1 \otimes e_1, e_1 \otimes e_2, e_2 \otimes e_1, e_2 \otimes e_2$, and identify $V \otimes V \cong M_2(\mathbb{F})$ via $u \otimes v \mapsto uv^t$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Tensors of Type (p,q) *(from Differential Geometry)*
> A tensor of type $(p, q)$ on a vector space $V$ is an element of $V^{\otimes p} \otimes (V^*)^{\otimes q}$, equivalently a multilinear map taking $q$ vectors and $p$ covectors to a scalar. On a manifold, tensor fields of type $(p, q)$ are sections of the associated bundle, and the index notation $T^{i_1 \cdots i_p}_{j_1 \cdots j_q}$ (which physicists use everywhere) is just a coordinate expression of an element of this tensor space. The metric is type $(0, 2)$; the Riemann curvature is $(1, 3)$; a vector field is $(1, 0)$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Hilbert Space Tensor Products *(from Quantum Mechanics)*
> The state space of a multi-particle quantum system is the **tensor product** of the single-particle Hilbert spaces. Two distinguishable spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ particles live in $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 \cong \mathbb{C}^4$; an entangled state like $|\!\uparrow\downarrow\rangle - |\!\downarrow\uparrow\rangle$ is precisely a tensor that is *not* an elementary product $v \otimes w$ — entanglement is the failure of the wavefunction to be a pure product, and that failure is a feature of the tensor product, not a pathology.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The chapter has five recurring proof targets, and they hang together because each is a way of pinning down a multilinear gadget. The first is **computing a determinant** — given a matrix, find the number; given an operator, find the scalar — and the workhorse is *not* the Leibniz formula but cofactor expansion when the matrix has structure (zeros, blocks), or the product-of-eigenvalues formula when the operator has known spectrum, or the upper-triangular reduction via Schur. The second is **classifying a symmetric bilinear form by signature**, where the target is the triple $(p, q, r)$ in some standard diagonal form; [[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia|Sylvester's law]] is what guarantees the answer does not depend on the choice of diagonalisation. The third is **proving an isomorphism between tensor-product spaces**, where the route is always to invoke the universal property: rather than checking a map by hand on elementary tensors, you construct it from a bilinear map and let universal property do the work. The fourth is **identifying an alternating multilinear form as a determinant**, exploiting $\dim V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}} = 1$ — any nonzero alternating form on $n$-tuples is determined by its value on one basis, so two such forms agreeing on one basis are equal (up to scalar). The fifth is **proving multilinearity-or-alternation of a given construction**, which is the gateway to letting any of the above theorems apply.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The recurring source patterns are equally stereotyped. **A bilinear form is given** — perhaps as a matrix, perhaps as an integral $\int p q'$ on a function space, perhaps as $\langle u, Tv\rangle$ for a self-adjoint $T$; the move is to ask whether it is symmetric or alternating, because then the structure theorems of §9A apply. **An alternating multilinear form is given** — the determinant, the wedge product, the volume form — and the move is to apply the one-dimensionality theorem to identify it with a known form (typically $\det$). **A bilinear map between vector spaces is given** — composition, evaluation, the cross product, multiplication of matrices — and the universal property of the tensor product is the source-to-target router: convert it to a linear map out of $V \otimes W$. **An eigenvalue decomposition is available** — via diagonalisability, Schur upper-triangularisation, or the Jordan form of [[Linear Algebra VIII — §8 Operators on Complex Vector Spaces]] — and the multiplicative structure of the determinant means $\det T = \prod \lambda_i$ reduces a determinant computation to spectral data. **A block decomposition is available** — the matrix factors as a block-triangular form — and the determinant of the whole is the product of the determinants of the diagonal blocks. The five sources route to the five targets in expected ways: bilinear-form sources route to signature targets via Sylvester; bilinear-map sources route to tensor isomorphisms via the universal property; eigenvalue and block-structure sources route to determinant computations; alternating-form sources route to "this is the determinant" identifications. The routes are summarised in the [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves out of which almost every exercise in the chapter is assembled. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything here is self-contained.

1. **Polarise a quadratic form to recover the symmetric bilinear form.** Given a quadratic form $q$, define $\rho(u, w) := \tfrac{1}{2}(q(u + w) - q(u) - q(w))$. This is a symmetric bilinear form with $q_\rho = q$, and it is the *unique* symmetric bilinear form with that property (in characteristic $\neq 2$). *Trigger:* you have a quadratic form and want to apply symmetric-bilinear theorems (Sylvester, diagonalisation). *Pattern:* "polarise to get the symmetric form, then diagonalise."

2. **Split a bilinear form into symmetric and alternating parts.** Write $\beta = \rho + \alpha$ with $\rho = \tfrac{1}{2}(\beta + \beta^t)$ and $\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}(\beta - \beta^t)$. *Trigger:* you have a general bilinear form and need a tool that requires either symmetry or alternation. *Pattern:* "the symmetric and alternating parts each behave nicely; combine the conclusions."

3. **Diagonalise a symmetric bilinear form by an orthogonal change of basis.** For a real symmetric bilinear form $\rho$ on an inner product space, the operator $T$ with $\rho(u, v) = \langle u, Tv\rangle$ is self-adjoint, so the [[Thm - Real Spectral Theorem|real spectral theorem]] gives an orthonormal eigenbasis in which $\rho$ is diagonal. *Trigger:* you need to bring a real symmetric matrix into diagonal form via congruence and you have an inner product available. *Pattern:* "spectral theorem on the associated self-adjoint operator."

4. **Apply Sylvester's law to read off the signature.** Once a real symmetric bilinear form is diagonalised, the triple $(p, q, r)$ of positive/negative/zero diagonal entries is intrinsic — it does not depend on the diagonalisation chosen. *Trigger:* you want to know whether two real symmetric matrices are congruent, or you want to detect the kind of pseudo-metric a form induces (Riemannian: $(n, 0)$; Lorentzian: $(1, n-1)$; degenerate: $r > 0$). *Pattern:* "compute the signature; congruence equivalent to same signature."

5. **Identify a candidate alternating $n$-linear form by evaluating on one basis.** If $\alpha, \alpha'$ are two alternating $n$-linear forms on $V$ ($n = \dim V$) and $\alpha(e_1, \dots, e_n) = \alpha'(e_1, \dots, e_n) \neq 0$ for one basis, then $\alpha = \alpha'$ — by the one-dimensionality of $V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$. *Trigger:* you want to show two alternating multilinear constructions agree. *Pattern:* "evaluate both on $e_1, \dots, e_n$; if they agree there, they agree everywhere."

6. **Compute a determinant via cofactor expansion when the matrix has zeros or block structure.** Expand along the row or column with the most zeros, so that most cofactors vanish from the sum. *Trigger:* the matrix has at least one row or column with $\leq 2$ nonzero entries. *Pattern:* "expand along the sparse row; recurse."

7. **Compute a determinant via eigenvalues when the operator has known spectrum.** $\det T = \prod \lambda_i$ (with multiplicity). *Trigger:* you know the eigenvalues or the operator is given as a diagonal/triangular form. *Pattern:* "$\det = $ product of eigenvalues — no further computation needed."

8. **Apply multiplicativity $\det(ST) = \det S \cdot \det T$.** Either to factorise a hard determinant into easier ones, or to derive constraints (e.g., $\det T \cdot \det T^{-1} = 1$). *Trigger:* the matrix factors naturally (LU, QR, change-of-basis $C^t M C$, etc.). *Pattern:* "factor and multiply the determinants."

9. **Use the universal property to construct a linear map out of $V \otimes W$.** To define $\hat\Gamma : V \otimes W \to U$, construct a bilinear $\Gamma : V \times W \to U$ and invoke the universal property. *Trigger:* you need to define a linear map on a tensor product but cannot define it on every element of $V \otimes W$ directly. *Pattern:* "define on elementary tensors via a bilinear formula; universal property extends."

10. **Use the universal property to prove that an alleged map on $V \otimes W$ is well-defined.** An attempted formula like "$\hat\Gamma(v \otimes w) := \dots$" is *not* automatically well-defined, because elements of $V \otimes W$ do not have unique representations as $v \otimes w$. Instead, define a bilinear map $\Gamma : V \times W \to U$ and let universal property produce $\hat\Gamma$. *Trigger:* you have a tentative formula on simple tensors and want to extend to all of $V \otimes W$.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Defining a linear map $V \otimes W \to U$ by the formula on $v \otimes w$ alone
> Tempting: "define $\hat\Gamma : V \otimes W \to U$ by $\hat\Gamma(v \otimes w) = \Gamma(v, w)$ and extend linearly". Wrong: elements of $V \otimes W$ are **sums of elementary tensors**, and these representations are *not unique* — $(v_1 + v_2) \otimes w = v_1 \otimes w + v_2 \otimes w$, so the same element has multiple expressions. A formula given on elementary tensors must be checked to give the same answer regardless of how the element is decomposed. The correct route is to define a bilinear $\Gamma : V \times W \to U$ and invoke the universal property of [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|the tensor product]]; well-definedness is then automatic. The counterexample to "extend linearly from elementary tensors": try to define $f(v \otimes w) := \|v\|\|w\|$. This is *not* well-defined as a function on $V \otimes W$, because the same element $v \otimes w$ can be written as $\tfrac{1}{2}((v + w) \otimes (v + w) - v \otimes v - w \otimes w)$ in certain product algebras, giving different "values" of $f$.

> [!warning] 2. Treating "the determinant" as the Leibniz sum formula
> Tempting: define $\det A := \sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, A_{\sigma(1), 1}\cdots A_{\sigma(n), n}$, then prove its properties from there. This is logically possible but conceptually wrong-way-round: the multiplicativity $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$ becomes a miraculous identity that has to be checked by hand-grinding through $n!$-term sums, and the geometric meaning as a volume scaling factor is obscured. The counterexample to "this is a good way to think": try proving $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$ from the Leibniz formula. You will reach for the alternating-multilinearity of the column map after about a paragraph; that is the actual content of the proof. The "becomes legal" repair: take alternating multilinearity as the definition and derive the Leibniz sum as a *consequence* — exactly LADR's path.

> [!warning] 3. Forgetting that "alternating" is stronger than "antisymmetric" in characteristic 2
> Tempting in characteristic 2: equate "$\alpha(v, v) = 0$" with "$\alpha(u, w) = -\alpha(w, u)$". The latter says $\alpha(u, w) = \alpha(w, u)$ in characteristic 2 (because $-1 = 1$), so it does *not* force $\alpha(v, v) = 0$. The correct definition of alternating is always the "vanishes on diagonals" formulation; the antisymmetric form is a *consequence* in characteristic $\neq 2$. We assume characteristic $\neq 2$ throughout LADR Ch 9, but the distinction matters in algebraic geometry over $\mathbb{F}_2$ and in modular representation theory.

> [!warning] 4. Concluding $\det(A + B) = \det A + \det B$
> Tempting because the determinant *is* multilinear in the rows (or columns) — so additivity holds *one row at a time*, not in all rows at once. Counterexample: $\det(I + I) = \det(2I) = 2^n$, but $\det I + \det I = 2$. The determinant is linear in each row separately, with the others fixed — that is the meaning of multilinearity. It is *not* linear in the matrix entries jointly. The repair: $\det(A + B)$ expands as a sum of $2^n$ determinants, one for each way to choose either $A$'s or $B$'s row at each level (multilinearity), and there is no simpler general formula.

> [!warning] 5. Identifying $V \otimes W$ with the Cartesian product $V \times W$
> Tempting because elementary tensors $v \otimes w$ are indexed by pairs $(v, w)$. Wrong: $V \times W$ has dimension $\dim V + \dim W$, while $V \otimes W$ has dimension $\dim V \cdot \dim W$. The product is "additive", the tensor product is "multiplicative". The correct identification of $V \times W$ in this context is as the *domain* of bilinear maps, and the universal property of $V \otimes W$ is precisely the statement that the tensor product *replaces* the Cartesian product as a target for linear maps. The conceptual error is to confuse "ordered pair" with "product"; the tensor product is the latter.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this chapter cluster around five recognisable targets, and the route to each is sufficiently formulaic that knowing the routine is most of the work. Here they are, in the order of frequency they appear.

If the problem **asks you to compute a determinant**, classify the matrix first. Is it triangular or block-triangular? Then $\det$ is the product of diagonal (or block-diagonal) entries — done in a line, no further work. Is it small ($n \leq 4$) with mostly nonzero entries? Use the Leibniz formula directly. Does it have one row or column with many zeros? Cofactor-expand along that row, recursing on the cofactors. Is the operator given via a spectral structure (diagonal, normal, self-adjoint, given Jordan form)? Then $\det = \prod \lambda_i$ — read it off the spectrum. Is the matrix the product of factors with known determinants (LU decomposition, QR factorisation)? Use [[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative|multiplicativity]] $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$. The reason to classify before computing is that each of these routines is much faster than blindly applying the Leibniz formula, which is exponentially expensive in $n$.

If the problem **asks for the signature of a real symmetric bilinear form**, the route is diagonalisation followed by reading off signs. The fastest way to diagonalise depends on what is available: if the form is given as $\langle u, Tv\rangle$ for a self-adjoint $T$ on an inner product space, the real spectral theorem hands you an orthonormal eigenbasis in which the form is diagonal with the eigenvalues of $T$ as the diagonal entries — and the signature is then $(p, q, r) = (\text{positive eigenvalues}, \text{negative eigenvalues}, \text{zero eigenvalues})$. If the form is given by an arbitrary matrix $M$, the right move is *not* to compute eigenvalues but to perform simultaneous row-and-column operations (the "congruence" operations $M \mapsto C^t M C$) bringing $M$ to diagonal form. Either way, [[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia|Sylvester]] guarantees the answer does not depend on the diagonalisation chosen.

If the problem **asks you to identify two alternating multilinear forms as equal** — or to identify a construction as "the" determinant — invoke the one-dimensionality of $V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$. Two alternating $n$-linear forms on an $n$-dimensional space are scalar multiples of each other, and the scalar can be detected by evaluating both on a single basis. So the route is always: (i) verify the candidate is alternating and multilinear, (ii) compute its value on the standard basis, (iii) divide by the value of the reference form. This collapses what looks like a long calculational identity ("the wedge product equals this determinant of inner products") into a one-line argument: both sides are alternating multilinear, they agree on the basis, therefore they are equal everywhere.

If the problem **asks you to construct a map out of a tensor product**, never try to define it on the tensor $v \otimes w$ directly. Define instead a bilinear map $\Gamma : V \times W \to U$ — that is, a function $V \times W \to U$ linear in each slot separately — and invoke the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|universal property]] to produce the unique linear $\hat\Gamma : V \otimes W \to U$ with $\hat\Gamma(v \otimes w) = \Gamma(v, w)$. The reason this is the only sane approach is that elements of $V \otimes W$ do not have unique representations as $v \otimes w$ — the same element can be written multiple ways as sums of elementary tensors — so a formula given on elementary tensors needs separately verifying. The universal property is the theorem that says "if your formula is bilinear, the well-definedness check is for free". This pattern recurs throughout linear algebra, differential geometry, and representation theory: anywhere a tensor product appears, the universal property is the right tool.

If the problem **asks you to prove an identity involving determinants and matrix factorisations** — for instance $\det(I + AB) = \det(I + BA)$ for $A : V \to W, B : W \to V$ — the route is multiplicativity plus a block-triangular trick. Set up an auxiliary block matrix in which the desired identity becomes the equality of two ways of computing $\det$ via block-triangular factorisations. This is one of the most common qualifying-exam patterns; it teaches that $\det$ is most often *not* computed but *manipulated algebraically* via multiplicativity and the determinant-similarity invariance.

The meta-strategy threading through all of these is: **every question in this chapter is either "what is the determinant or signature of this thing?" or "how do I move this construction through a multilinear gadget without losing track of variance?"**. The first kind is solved by classifying the matrix and reading the appropriate formula. The second kind is solved by writing down a bilinear or multilinear map and using the universal property (for tensor products) or the one-dimensionality theorem (for alternating forms) to convert it into the linear data you wanted.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative|Determinant is multiplicative]]**: $\det(ST) = (\det S)(\det T)$. The most-used single fact about determinants — it is the reason $\det$ is a homomorphism $\mathrm{GL}_n \to \mathbb{F}^\times$, the reason $\det$ is a similarity invariant ($\det(S^{-1}TS) = \det T$), and the engine of every block-determinant identity. **Typical use:** factor the matrix into pieces with known determinants and multiply.

- **The one-dimensionality theorem $\dim V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}} = 1$**. The reason "the determinant" exists at all — without it, there would be many incompatible alternating $n$-linear forms and no single one would deserve the name. **Typical use:** prove two alternating $n$-linear constructions agree by checking them on one basis.

- **[[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|Universal property of the tensor product]]**: linear maps out of $V \otimes W$ correspond bijectively to bilinear maps out of $V \times W$. Every tensor-product computation routes through this. **Typical use:** construct a linear map on $V \otimes W$ by constructing a bilinear map on $V \times W$.

- **[[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia|Sylvester's law of inertia]]**: the signature of a real symmetric bilinear form is basis-independent. The theorem that makes "$(1, 3)$ signature" a coordinate-free statement about Minkowski space and the equivalence principle. **Typical use:** classify symmetric matrices up to congruence; identify pseudo-Riemannian metric types.

- **The polarisation identity**: a symmetric bilinear form is determined by its diagonal — that is, by its associated quadratic form — via $\rho(u, w) = \tfrac{1}{2}(q(u+w) - q(u) - q(w))$. Reusable across analysis (inner product spaces, parallelogram law), quantum mechanics (recovering observables from expectation values), and algebra (recovering [[Def - Ring|ring]] multiplication from norms). **Typical use:** convert a problem stated in terms of quadratic data into one about a symmetric bilinear form, then apply the symmetric-form theorems.

# Bridges

**[[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|Minkowski space]] and the signature classification.** The Minkowski metric on $\mathbb{R}^4$ is a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form $\eta$ of signature $(1, 3)$ (or $(3, 1)$, depending on convention) — that is, in any diagonalising basis, the matrix has one positive and three negative eigenvalues (or the reverse). [[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia|Sylvester's law]] is exactly the theorem that makes this signature statement basis-independent: if it were not, the distinction between timelike and spacelike directions would depend on a choice of coordinates, and special relativity would have no coordinate-free content. The Lorentz group $O(1, 3)$ is by definition the group of [[Def - Linear Map|linear maps]] preserving $\eta$, which makes it the "isometry group" of this symmetric bilinear form — see the next bridge for how this construction generalises.

**[[Def - The Lorentz Group|The Lorentz group]] as a pseudo-orthogonal group.** For any non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form $\rho$ on a real vector space of signature $(p, q)$, the **pseudo-orthogonal group** $O(p, q)$ is the group of linear maps $T$ preserving $\rho$, that is, $\rho(Tu, Tv) = \rho(u, v)$ for all $u, v$. When $(p, q) = (n, 0)$ this is the standard orthogonal group $O(n)$; when $(p, q) = (1, 3)$ it is the Lorentz group. The structure theory — that $O(p, q)$ is a non-compact Lie group of dimension $\binom{n}{2}$, with four connected components when $p, q \geq 1$ corresponding to orientation and time orientation — is determined entirely by the signature, which is why Sylvester's law makes the construction well-defined. The Lie algebra of $O(p, q)$ is the space of matrices $X$ satisfying $X^t \eta + \eta X = 0$ — the "antisymmetric in the $\eta$-pairing" matrices — which gives a concrete description of infinitesimal Lorentz transformations.

**[[Def - The Total Derivative and Differentiability|The total derivative]], [[Def - Partial Derivatives and the Jacobian Matrix|the Jacobian]], and the change-of-variables formula.** For a $C^1$ map $\Phi : U \to V$ between open sets in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the total derivative $D\Phi_x : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ at each point is a linear map, and its determinant $\det D\Phi_x$ is the **Jacobian** — a scalar function on $U$. The geometric content of $\det$ as "linear volume scaling factor" extends infinitesimally to: $|\det D\Phi_x|$ is the factor by which $\Phi$ scales $n$-dimensional volume in an infinitesimal neighbourhood of $x$. This is the conceptual core of the multivariate change-of-variables formula $\int_{\Phi(U)} f\, dx = \int_U f(\Phi(y)) |\det D\Phi(y)|\, dy$, which is essentially the statement "$d(\text{volume after } \Phi) = |\det D\Phi| \cdot d(\text{volume before } \Phi)$" written as an integral identity. The sign $\det D\Phi$ (without absolute value) tracks orientation: an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism has positive Jacobian, an orientation-reversing one has negative Jacobian.

**[[Def - Module|Modules]] over a ring and the tensor product of modules.** The construction $V \otimes_\mathbb{F} W$ for vector spaces generalises to $M \otimes_R N$ for two modules over a commutative ring $R$. The universal property is identical: bilinear maps from $M \times N$ to a third module $L$ correspond to $R$-linear maps from $M \otimes_R N$ to $L$. The concrete construction is different — one cannot use $\mathcal{B}(M', N')$ because $M'$ may behave badly (a module's dual need not have the right dimension), so one builds $M \otimes_R N$ as a quotient of a free module by the bilinearity relations. The vector-space case is a clean special case, and learning the universal property in the vector-space setting prepares you for the ring-theoretic version where the universal property is the only sensible *definition*. Over a non-commutative ring the construction needs additional bimodule structure; this is where the theory becomes genuinely subtle.

**[[Def - Signed Measure|Signed measures]] as a vector space and the determinant pushforward.** The space of signed measures of bounded variation on a measurable space is a vector space. For a smooth map $\Phi$, the pushforward of a measure transforms via $|\det D\Phi|$ — this is the measure-theoretic version of the Jacobian formula. In probability theory, the change-of-variables formula for densities is $p_Y(y) = p_X(\Phi^{-1}(y)) \cdot |\det D\Phi^{-1}(y)|$, which is the same identity dressed up as a density transformation. The determinant's role as the "scaling factor of $n$-dimensional measure" is what makes it the right object here.

**Differential Forms (Differential Geometry).** A differential $k$-form on a manifold is at each point an alternating $k$-linear form on the tangent space. So the entire theory of differential forms — including Stokes' theorem, de Rham cohomology, and the wedge product algebra — is built on the alternating-multilinear machinery of §9B applied pointwise to tangent spaces. The fact that $\dim \Lambda^k T^*M = \binom{n}{k}$ at each point comes directly from the dimension count of alternating $k$-linear forms on an $n$-dimensional space.

**Volume Form (Differential Geometry).** On an oriented $n$-dimensional manifold, a **volume form** is a smooth non-vanishing alternating $n$-form. The space of volume forms is a one-dimensional real vector space at each point (a manifestation of $\dim V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}} = 1$), and a Riemannian metric picks out a canonical volume form $\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\, dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n$ via its determinant. This is how the "determinant as volume scaling" idea is globalised: the metric tensor's determinant becomes a local volume density.

**Quadratic Variation (Stochastic Analysis).** The quadratic variation of a continuous martingale $M$ is the limit (in probability) of $\sum (M_{t_{i+1}} - M_{t_i})^2$ over partitions — a quadratic functional of the increment process. For multidimensional martingales, the quadratic covariation $[M, N]_t$ is a *symmetric bilinear form* on the increment pairs, and its diagonalisation (via Sylvester's law) classifies the possible local dispersion structures. The Itô formula's correction term $\tfrac{1}{2} \operatorname{tr}(\sigma^t H_f \sigma)$ is the trace of a quadratic form on the noise structure $\sigma$, weighted by the Hessian $H_f$ — a direct manifestation of §9A in stochastic analysis.

# Insights

**The unifying frame of Chapter 9: every multilinear gadget is determined by its alternating part, and every alternating gadget is one-dimensional in the top degree.** The chapter is structured around a single conceptual triangle. At one corner sit the bilinear forms of §9A, where the split $V^{(2)} = V^{(2)}_{\mathrm{sym}} \oplus V^{(2)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$ is the first hint that "alternating part" is a meaningful operation. At the second corner sit the multilinear forms of §9B, where the antisymmetrisation $\operatorname{Alt}(\beta) := \tfrac{1}{m!}\sum_\sigma \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, \beta \circ \sigma$ extracts the alternating piece of an arbitrary $m$-linear form. At the third corner sits the determinant: the *unique* (up to scalar) alternating $n$-linear form on an $n$-dimensional space, and the device by which any alternating $n$-linear quantity becomes a single number $\det T$. The tensor product machinery of §9D is what makes "multilinear" and "alternating" precise as operations: the antisymmetrisation extracts the alternating part from $V^{\otimes m}$, giving the exterior power $\Lambda^m V$, and the determinant is the canonical isomorphism $\Lambda^n V \cong \mathbb{F}$ for $n = \dim V$. Every theorem in the chapter is a corollary of this triangle: the cofactor expansion is "expand along a chosen coordinate, keeping alternation in the remaining slots"; multiplicativity is "alternating-multilinear-of-product equals product-of-alternating-multilinears"; the eigenvalue product formula is "evaluate on an eigenbasis where the alternating form factors completely".

**The true name of the determinant is "volume scaling factor", and the sign records orientation.** The Leibniz formula $\sum \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, A_{\sigma(1), 1}\cdots A_{\sigma(n), n}$ is the right computational tool for $n \leq 3$ and the wrong conceptual definition for everything else. Geometrically, $|\det T|$ is the factor by which the linear map $T$ scales $n$-dimensional Euclidean volume: a unit cube under $T$ becomes a parallelepiped of volume $|\det T|$. The sign of $\det T$ is positive when $T$ preserves orientation (you can deform $T$ continuously to the identity through invertibles) and negative when it reverses orientation. This is why $\det$ appears in change-of-variables formulas (as the Jacobian), in the equations of motion of Hamiltonian systems (Liouville's theorem: volume in phase space is preserved, so the flow has Jacobian $1$), in Maxwell's equations (the orientation-dependent terms with cross products), and as the structure constants of $\mathfrak{sl}(n)$ (the trace-zero matrices are infinitesimal volume-preserving). The Leibniz formula is just the explicit calculation: if you take the standard basis $e_1, \dots, e_n$ as unit edges of the cube and apply $T$, the resulting parallelepiped's signed volume is exactly that sum. The conceptual ordering "volume scaling → multilinear-alternating → Leibniz formula" is the right one; the historical ordering (Leibniz → properties) is the wrong way round.

**The signature of a symmetric bilinear form is the analogue of the determinant for *non-square* operators.** This is a deeper unifying observation than it seems. The determinant assigns to a square operator a single scalar invariant. The signature assigns to a real symmetric bilinear form a *triple* $(p, q, r)$ of integers — a coarser, discrete invariant. What both have in common: they are basis-independent (Sylvester for signature; similarity invariance for $\det$), they classify their objects up to a natural equivalence (congruence for symmetric forms; similarity for operators), and they are computable by reducing to a normal form (diagonal for symmetric forms via Sylvester; Jordan form for operators). The signature is what the "spectrum" becomes when there is no canonical sign to the diagonal entries — that is, when there is no inner product to anchor them. In an inner product space the operator $T$ with $\rho(u, v) = \langle u, Tv \rangle$ is self-adjoint with real eigenvalues, and the signature equals (positive eigenvalues, negative eigenvalues, zero eigenvalues). In a non-inner-product setting the signs of diagonal entries depend on the diagonalising basis — but their *count* doesn't, which is exactly Sylvester. This is why the Minkowski metric has well-defined signature $(1, 3)$ without invoking an inner product: the signs are not signs of "eigenvalues", they are signs of "self-pairings", and only the count is intrinsic.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: see a bilinear or multilinear construction → look for a universal property formulation.** This is the most reliable working insight of the chapter. Anywhere in mathematics where a multilinear gadget appears — the cross product of vectors, the commutator bracket, evaluation of operators on vectors, multiplication of polynomials, the curvature tensor — there is a tensor-product-like universal property lurking, and if you write it down, the linear algebra falls out. Multiplicative structures (rings, algebras) are bilinear maps $A \times A \to A$, hence linear maps $A \otimes A \to A$. The exterior algebra is the universal algebra in which $v \wedge v = 0$, hence the universal alternating multilinear gadget. The symmetric algebra is the universal commutative algebra, hence the universal symmetric multilinear gadget. The recognition heuristic is: if you have an operation that takes multiple inputs of vector-space type and produces a single output, *and* the operation is linear in each input separately, then the operation factors through a tensor product (or a symmetrised/antisymmetrised quotient of one), and the universal property is the theorem that does the factoring for you.

**An inheritance observation: the alternating property is inherited from one-dimensional invariance.** The reason $\dim V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}} = 1$ — the central fact that makes "the determinant" well-defined — is that the dimension of $V^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}}$ counts the ways to choose an alternating $n$-linear form on $V$, which is the same as choosing a value for $\alpha(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ on one fixed basis, which is one free parameter, hence dimension $1$. This one-dimensionality propagates: it gives $\dim \Lambda^n V = 1$ for $\dim V = n$, which gives a one-dimensional bundle (the determinant line bundle) on any vector bundle of rank $n$, which gives the notion of an orientation (a global section of the unit subbundle), which gives the volume form on a Riemannian manifold, which gives the change-of-variables formula in integration. Every "uniqueness up to scalar" in this whole tower is the same one-dimensionality. When you see a uniqueness-up-to-scalar statement involving alternating forms, it is almost always a manifestation of this single fact.
