# Notation Registry

Throughout this topic, vectors live in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and matrices have real entries. The single unifying object is a **vector residual map** $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ whose squared norm is to be minimized; everything else is built around it.

- $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ — the optimization variable
- $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ — the **residual map** (nonlinear function); we minimize $\|f(x)\|^2$
- $g : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^p$ — the **equality-constraint map**; the constraint is $g(x) = 0$
- $Df(x)$ — the $m \times n$ **Jacobian** (derivative matrix) of $f$ at $x$, with entries $(Df(x))_{ij} = \partial f_i / \partial x_j$
- $\hat f(x; x^{(k)}) = f(x^{(k)}) + Df(x^{(k)})(x - x^{(k)})$ — the **affine approximation** of $f$ around $x^{(k)}$
- $x^{(k)}$ — the $k$th **iterate** of an iterative algorithm; $x^{(1)}$ is the **starting point**
- $\lambda^{(k)} > 0$ — the Levenberg–Marquardt **trust parameter** at iteration $k$ (do not confuse with $\lambda$ used elsewhere for regularization or eigenvalues; the symbol is overloaded by convention)
- $\mu^{(k)} > 0$ — the penalty / augmented-Lagrangian **penalty parameter** at iteration $k$
- $z^{(k)} \in \mathbb{R}^p$ — the augmented-Lagrangian estimate of the **Lagrange multiplier**
- $\|\cdot\|$ — the Euclidean norm
- $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^p$ — the **model-parameter** vector in nonlinear model fitting; the data are $(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})$ for $i = 1, \ldots, N$ and the model is $y \approx \hat f(x; \theta)$
- $\phi(u) = (e^u - e^{-u})/(e^u + e^{-u})$ — the **sigmoid** (hyperbolic-tangent) function used in classification; its smooth values in $(-1, 1)$ replace the non-differentiable $\operatorname{sign}$
- $L(x, z) = \|f(x)\|^2 + g(x)^T z$ — the **Lagrangian** for the constrained problem
- $L_\mu(x, z) = \|f(x)\|^2 + g(x)^T z + \mu \|g(x)\|^2$ — the **augmented Lagrangian** with penalty $\mu$

**Standing-convention preamble.** Throughout, $f$ is at least once continuously differentiable on the relevant domain — every algorithm here needs to form the Jacobian $Df(x^{(k)})$ at the current iterate. In Boyd's notation, the Jacobian is written $Df(x)$ rather than the more common $J(x)$ or $f'(x)$; we follow Boyd. The norm is always the Euclidean $\ell^2$ norm — the topic is about *least squares*, and every other norm in sight (operator, $\ell^1$, infinity) is irrelevant here. The trust parameter $\lambda^{(k)}$ in Levenberg–Marquardt is *not* a regularization parameter on the model; it is a *trust-region* parameter on the step, updated dynamically as the algorithm proceeds. This distinction matters when reading nonlinear model fitting examples in §XII.1, where both a regularization $\lambda$ and a Levenberg–Marquardt $\lambda^{(k)}$ may coexist; the two are unrelated.

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: when the residual is nonlinear, linearize and iterate. Everything else — Gauss–Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt, the penalty algorithm, the augmented Lagrangian — is a particular implementation of this single idea, decorated with safety mechanisms that keep the linearization honest.

The setup is the same one that ran through [[Linear Algebra XI — Applied II — Least Squares|Topic XI]]: we have a residual we wish to make small, and we measure smallness in the sum-of-squares sense. The difference is structural. In Topic XI the residual $r(x) = Ax - b$ was **affine** in $x$, so the gradient was constant and the global minimizer was given by a single closed-form solve, the **normal equations** $A^T A x = A^T b$. The geometry was a paraboloid; the answer was at the bottom. In Topic XII the residual $f(x)$ is *nonlinear* in $x$. There is no closed form, the geometry can have multiple local minima, and there is no guarantee of finding a global minimizer at all. We must iterate, and at each iterate the only honest thing we can do is what Newton and Gauss between them taught: replace $f$ by its tangent affine approximation, solve the resulting *linear* least-squares subproblem, and call the answer our next iterate. Then linearize again.

That single move organizes the chapter. The basic **Gauss–Newton algorithm** does exactly this. It is correct in spirit but unsafe: when the linearization is poor — when the current iterate is far from a residual minimum — the linear subproblem's minimizer can be wildly inappropriate, and the iteration can diverge. The **Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm** is a single, beautiful, modification: do not blindly solve the linear subproblem, but solve a *regularized* version of it that penalizes large steps. This bridges Gauss–Newton (when the linearization is trustworthy and the step is small) and gradient descent (when the linearization is untrustworthy and we should not stray far). The regularization parameter $\lambda^{(k)}$ is updated adaptively: if a step decreased the true objective, $\lambda^{(k)}$ is shrunk to take a bolder step next time; if a step *failed* to decrease the objective, $\lambda^{(k)}$ is grown to take a more cautious step. This is the **trust-region** interpretation: $\lambda^{(k)}$ controls how far we trust the linearization, hence how far we step.

The second half of the topic adds **equality constraints** $g(x) = 0$. The same linearize-and-iterate idea persists, but now we must also enforce the constraint, which is generally nonlinear and likewise has no closed-form treatment. The **penalty algorithm** is the most direct attempt: add $\mu \|g(x)\|^2$ to the objective and minimize the composite using Levenberg–Marquardt; as $\mu \to \infty$ the constraint is enforced ever more strictly. This works in principle but ill-conditions the subproblem catastrophically as $\mu$ grows. The **augmented Lagrangian algorithm** is the fix: add not just the penalty $\mu \|g(x)\|^2$ but also a Lagrange-multiplier term $z^T g(x)$, and update the multiplier $z$ using the gradient-of-the-dual rule $z^{(k+1)} = z^{(k)} + 2\mu^{(k)} g(x^{(k+1)})$. This converges with $\mu$ kept moderate, so the subproblem stays well-conditioned. The pattern of "Lagrange multiplier estimate + adaptive penalty + Newton-like inner solver" is the workhorse of practical constrained nonlinear optimization, generalizing far beyond the least-squares setting to sequential quadratic programming and interior-point methods.

The chapter ends with a flagship application: **nonlinear control**. A nonlinear dynamical system $x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k)$ with a prescribed initial and final state, together with a cost on the input trajectory, becomes a large equality-constrained nonlinear least-squares problem with the dynamics as the equality constraints. The augmented Lagrangian algorithm then numerically computes optimal control inputs, demonstrating that the unified frame of this chapter solves problems that look very far removed from "fitting a curve to data."

Audience assumption. The reader is assumed to have working knowledge of [[Linear Algebra XI — Applied II — Least Squares|linear least squares]] (the [[Def - Least Squares Problem|least-squares problem]], the [[Def - Normal Equations|normal equations]], and the [[Def - Constrained Least Squares|equality-constrained]] case via the KKT system), of the [[Def - Partial Derivatives and the Jacobian Matrix|Jacobian]] and [[Def - The Total Derivative and Differentiability|total derivative]] from multivariate analysis, and of Taylor approximation $f(x) \approx f(x_0) + Df(x_0)(x - x_0)$. Familiarity with the language of unconstrained convex optimization is helpful but not strictly required — the chapter develops what it needs.

# Concept Map

## §XII.1 Nonlinear Least Squares

- **[[Def - Nonlinear Least Squares Problem]]**
	- The **nonlinear least squares problem** is to minimize $\|f(x)\|^2 = \sum_i f_i(x)^2$ over $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, where $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is a differentiable but otherwise arbitrary nonlinear residual map. When $m = n$ and the minimum value is zero, this reduces to solving the **nonlinear equations** $f(x) = 0$. The first-order optimality condition is $2 Df(x)^T f(x) = 0$, a system of $n$ nonlinear equations in $n$ unknowns. Unlike its linear counterpart in [[Linear Algebra XI — Applied II — Least Squares|Topic XI]], the problem has no closed-form solution and may have many local minima; the algorithms below are heuristics that find a local minimum near the chosen starting point.

- **[[Def - Gauss-Newton Algorithm]]**
	- The **Gauss–Newton algorithm** is the iterative procedure that, at each iterate $x^{(k)}$, replaces $f$ by its Taylor affine approximation $\hat f(x; x^{(k)}) = f(x^{(k)}) + Df(x^{(k)})(x - x^{(k)})$ and sets $x^{(k+1)}$ as the minimizer of $\|\hat f(x; x^{(k)})\|^2$, a *linear* least squares problem. Explicitly, $x^{(k+1)} = x^{(k)} - (Df(x^{(k)})^T Df(x^{(k)}))^{-1} Df(x^{(k)})^T f(x^{(k)})$. The matrix $Df^T Df$ plays the role of Hessian, with the second-derivative correction dropped — an approximation that is exact when $f(x) = 0$ at the minimum and degrades as residuals grow. When $m = n$, Gauss–Newton specializes to **Newton's method** for solving $f(x) = 0$. It can diverge; it can also fail outright if $Df(x^{(k)})$ has linearly dependent columns.

- **[[Def - Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm]]**
	- The **Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm** modifies Gauss–Newton to be safe. At each iterate it minimizes the *regularized* subproblem $\|\hat f(x; x^{(k)})\|^2 + \lambda^{(k)} \|x - x^{(k)}\|^2$, which has the closed form $x^{(k+1)} = x^{(k)} - (Df^T Df + \lambda^{(k)} I)^{-1} Df^T f(x^{(k)})$. The trust parameter $\lambda^{(k)}$ blends Gauss–Newton (small $\lambda$) with gradient descent (large $\lambda$) and is updated adaptively: shrunk by a factor like $0.8$ when a step succeeds (decreases $\|f\|^2$), grown by a factor like $2$ when it fails. The matrix $Df^T Df + \lambda^{(k)} I$ is *always* invertible for $\lambda^{(k)} > 0$, repairing Gauss–Newton's second failure mode.

- **[[Thm - Local Convergence of Gauss-Newton]]**
	- If $\hat x$ is a *zero-residual* local minimum, i.e., $f(\hat x) = 0$ and $Df(\hat x)$ has full column rank, then Gauss–Newton started sufficiently close to $\hat x$ converges to $\hat x$ at a quadratic rate — the same rate as Newton's method on the original equations $f(x) = 0$. Away from zero-residual minima the rate degrades to linear, with a contraction factor governed by the curvature term that Gauss–Newton drops; for large-residual problems Gauss–Newton may even diverge. This is the precise sense in which Gauss–Newton is "Newton's method with the Hessian approximated by $Df^T Df$": the approximation is correct at $r = 0$ and only there.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Newton's Method *(from Optimization)*
> The full **Newton's Method** for minimizing $\phi : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ takes the update $x^{(k+1)} = x^{(k)} - (\nabla^2 \phi(x^{(k)}))^{-1} \nabla \phi(x^{(k)})$, using the *true* Hessian $\nabla^2 \phi$. For $\phi(x) = \|f(x)\|^2$ the gradient is $2 Df(x)^T f(x)$ and the Hessian is $2 Df(x)^T Df(x) + 2 \sum_i f_i(x) \nabla^2 f_i(x)$. Gauss–Newton drops the second sum — the **curvature term** weighted by the residuals — replacing the true Hessian by $2 Df^T Df$. Once you have Newton's method you have access to its enormous downstream theory: quadratic local convergence, Wolfe line searches, BFGS and L-BFGS quasi-Newton variants, trust-region globalizations, and the general theory of self-concordant functions underpinning interior-point methods.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Stochastic Gradient Descent *(from Optimization / Machine Learning)*
> When the objective is $\sum_{i=1}^N f_i(x)^2$ with $N$ enormous (millions of data points), evaluating $Df$ in full is prohibitive. **Stochastic gradient descent** sidesteps this by using, at each iteration, a small mini-batch of indices and taking a (sub)gradient step on the partial sum. The unifying frame of "linearize and iterate" persists, but now the linearization is itself stochastic; the convergence theory (Robbins–Monro) gives almost-sure convergence under decaying step sizes. SGD is the algorithm of modern deep learning, and the bridge from Gauss–Newton to SGD is the recognition that the sum-of-squares structure is precisely the setting where stochastic *gradient* methods become natural.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Backpropagation *(from Deep Learning)*
> A deep neural network's residual is a composition $f(x) = f_L(f_{L-1}(\cdots f_1(x)\cdots))$, and its Jacobian $Df$ factors by the chain rule as a product of layer-wise Jacobians. **Backpropagation** is the efficient algorithm for computing $Df(x)^T v$ — and hence the gradient $Df(x)^T f(x)$ — by traversing the composition from right to left, reusing intermediate quantities. Once you see the deep network as a structured nonlinear residual, training it by SGD on a least-squares loss is Levenberg–Marquardt with the Jacobian computed by backpropagation. The "Adam" optimizer popular in practice is a quasi-second-order method that estimates a diagonal approximation of $Df^T Df$ from past gradients — a direct descendent of Gauss–Newton.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Logistic Regression *(from Statistics / Machine Learning)*
> When the outcomes $y^{(i)}$ are Boolean ($\pm 1$), replacing the non-differentiable $\operatorname{sign}$ with the smooth sigmoid $\phi$ converts classification into the nonlinear least squares problem of fitting $\phi(\beta^T x + v) \approx y$. This is **logistic regression** in its least-squares formulation (the more standard formulation uses cross-entropy loss, but the parameter estimates are similar and the optimization landscape is closely related). The sigmoid links the classification problem to the toolkit of nonlinear least squares, and Levenberg–Marquardt solves it. Logistic regression is the workhorse of probabilistic binary classification and the building block of every neural network's final layer.

- **[[Ex - Solving a system of nonlinear equations with Gauss-Newton]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Set up and run Gauss–Newton on the small two-dimensional equilibrium-prices problem $f(p) = S(p) - D(p) = 0$, observing the quadratic convergence and the Jacobian inversion at each step.

- **[[Ex - Levenberg-Marquardt outperforms Gauss-Newton on a hard problem]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Apply both algorithms to the sigmoid $f(x) = (e^x - e^{-x})/(e^x + e^{-x})$ from starting point $x^{(1)} = 1.15$, where Newton/Gauss–Newton diverges but Levenberg–Marquardt converges; tracking $\lambda^{(k)}$ exposes the trust-region mechanism.

- **[[Ex - Logistic regression as nonlinear least squares classification]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Fit a sigmoid-link classifier $\phi(\beta^T x + v) \approx y$ to MNIST data by minimizing the nonlinear least squares objective $\sum_i (\phi(\beta^T x^{(i)} + v) - y^{(i)})^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|^2$ using Levenberg–Marquardt.

## §XII.2 Constrained Nonlinear Least Squares

- **[[Def - Constrained Nonlinear Least Squares]]**
	- The **equality-constrained nonlinear least squares problem** is $\min \|f(x)\|^2$ subject to $g(x) = 0$, where both $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ and $g : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^p$ are nonlinear. The **Lagrangian** is $L(x, z) = \|f(x)\|^2 + g(x)^T z$, and the first-order optimality conditions (KKT for equality constraints) are $2 Df(\hat x)^T f(\hat x) + Dg(\hat x)^T \hat z = 0$ together with $g(\hat x) = 0$, where $\hat z$ is the **Lagrange multiplier** vector. When $f, g$ are affine the problem reduces to the linear [[Def - Constrained Least Squares|equality-constrained least squares]] of [[Linear Algebra XI — Applied II — Least Squares|Topic XI]], solved in closed form by the [[Def - KKT System|KKT system]]. The nonlinear case admits no closed form; the algorithms below are heuristics.

- **[[Def - Penalty Algorithm]]**
	- The **penalty algorithm** replaces the constrained problem by an unconstrained problem with a quadratic constraint penalty: minimize $\|f(x)\|^2 + \mu \|g(x)\|^2$ using Levenberg–Marquardt, then increase $\mu$ and repeat with the previous solution as the new starting point. As $\mu \to \infty$ the penalized minimum approaches the constrained minimum. The algorithm is conceptually clean but numerically unstable: for the constraint to be enforced one must drive $\mu$ to very large values, and the conditioning of the Levenberg–Marquardt subproblem degrades like $\mu$, so the inner solves become inaccurate just when the algorithm needs them most.

- **[[Def - Augmented Lagrangian Algorithm]]**
	- The **augmented Lagrangian algorithm** repairs the penalty method by adding a Lagrange-multiplier term: minimize $L_\mu(x, z) = \|f(x)\|^2 + g(x)^T z + \mu \|g(x)\|^2$ over $x$ for fixed $z, \mu$ (using Levenberg–Marquardt), update $z^{(k+1)} = z^{(k)} + 2\mu^{(k)} g(x^{(k+1)})$, and grow $\mu$ only when the constraint residual fails to shrink fast enough. The multiplier update is gradient ascent on the Lagrangian dual; the algorithm converges with $\mu$ kept *moderate*, avoiding the ill-conditioning of the penalty method. In practice it is the standard tool for medium-scale constrained nonlinear least squares.

- **[[Thm - Convergence of Penalty Algorithm]]**
	- Under mild regularity (the constraint Jacobian $Dg(\hat x)$ has full row rank at the constrained minimum $\hat x$, and the inner Levenberg–Marquardt finds the global minimum of each subproblem), the penalty algorithm's iterates $x^{(k)}$ converge to $\hat x$ as $\mu^{(k)} \to \infty$, and the implicit multiplier $z^{(k+1)} = 2\mu^{(k)} g(x^{(k+1)})$ converges to the optimal Lagrange multiplier $\hat z$. The convergence rate is governed by how fast $\mu$ grows; in practice $\mu^{(k)}$ doubles each iteration. The price of asymptotic correctness is the well-known ill-conditioning.

- **[[Thm - Augmented Lagrangian Recovers Lagrange Multipliers]]**
	- For the augmented Lagrangian algorithm, if $\tilde x$ minimizes $L_\mu(x, z)$ for some $z$ and $\mu$, then $\tilde x$ together with $\tilde z = z + 2\mu g(\tilde x)$ satisfies the KKT stationarity condition $2 Df(\tilde x)^T f(\tilde x) + Dg(\tilde x)^T \tilde z = 0$. Therefore: each pair $(x^{(k+1)}, z^{(k+1)})$ produced by the algorithm satisfies the KKT *stationarity* condition exactly; the only obstacle to optimality is the *feasibility* condition $g(x) = 0$, which the algorithm drives to zero by alternating multiplier updates with (occasional) penalty growth. This is the structural reason the algorithm beats the penalty method.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Sequential Quadratic Programming *(from Optimization)*
> **Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP)** is the generalization of augmented Lagrangian / Newton's method to constrained optimization with both equality *and inequality* constraints. At each iterate, SQP forms a *quadratic program* — minimize a quadratic approximation of the Lagrangian subject to linearized constraints — and the solution gives the next iterate. For equality-only constrained least squares, SQP reduces to Gauss–Newton applied jointly to $(x, z)$ via the KKT system; for inequality constraints, SQP uses an active-set strategy or an interior-point inner solver. SQP and augmented Lagrangian are the two main families of practical nonlinear-programming codes (e.g., SNOPT is SQP, IPOPT is interior-point).

> [!tip] Unlocked: Interior Point Methods *(from Convex Optimization)*
> **Interior point methods** transform an inequality-constrained problem $\min \phi(x)$ s.t. $h_i(x) \leq 0$ into a sequence of *unconstrained* problems $\min \phi(x) - t \sum_i \log(-h_i(x))$ by adding a logarithmic barrier; as $t \to 0$ the solutions trace a central path to the constrained optimum. The inner unconstrained problems are solved by Newton's method, which connects directly to Gauss–Newton when $\phi$ is a sum of squares. For *equality* constraints, interior-point and augmented Lagrangian methods are closely related: both build a sequence of unconstrained subproblems whose minimizers converge to the constrained optimum, with Lagrange multipliers emerging in the limit.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Optimal Control with Constraints *(from Control Theory)*
> The **nonlinear control problem** $\min \sum \|u_k\|^2$ subject to the dynamics $x_{k+1} = f(x_k, u_k)$ and boundary conditions $x_1 = x_{\text{init}}$, $x_N = x_{\text{final}}$ is exactly a large constrained nonlinear least squares problem with the dynamics as the equality constraints. The augmented Lagrangian algorithm solves it. This is the **direct transcription** (or "direct multiple shooting") approach to optimal control: discretize the dynamics, collect everything into a finite-dimensional optimization, and solve numerically. The alternative **indirect method** writes down the Pontryagin maximum principle and solves the resulting two-point boundary value problem; the two approaches converge to the same answer but trade off problem size against analytical content.

- **[[Ex - Penalty method on a quadratic with linear constraint]]** (⭐)
	- Apply the penalty algorithm to a problem where the answer is known in closed form via the linear KKT system, observing the convergence of $x^{(k)}$ and the implicit multiplier $2\mu^{(k)} g(x^{(k+1)})$ to the exact values.

- **[[Ex - Nonlinear control with augmented Lagrangian]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Solve the nonlinear-car steering problem of Boyd §19.4 using the augmented Lagrangian algorithm, setting up $u_1, \ldots, u_N$ and $x_2, \ldots, x_N$ as joint variables with the dynamics $x_{k+1} - f(x_k, u_k) = 0$ as equality constraints.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove or compute?**

In the applied-mathematical practice of this chapter, the targets are computational rather than theorem-like. The most common is a **local minimizer of a sum of squares**: an iterate $\hat x$ at which $\|f(\hat x)\|^2$ is small, ideally zero, and from which no nearby step decreases the objective. A second target is a **solution of a system of nonlinear equations** $f(x) = 0$, which is the special case where the minimum value is zero. A third is **fitted model parameters** $\theta$ minimizing $\sum_i (\hat f(x^{(i)}; \theta) - y^{(i)})^2$ — the nonlinear analogue of [[Linear Algebra XI — Applied II — Least Squares|linear data fitting]] — covering exponential models, neural networks, and orthogonal-distance regression. A fourth is **classification parameters** for a smooth (sigmoid) replacement of the sign function, yielding the least-squares formulation of logistic regression. The fifth recurring target is an **input trajectory** $u_1, \ldots, u_{N-1}$ that steers a nonlinear dynamical system from a given initial state to a specified final state while minimizing a smoothness or energy cost — the **optimal control** target. These five — minimize a sum of squares, solve nonlinear equations, fit nonlinear-model parameters, fit classification parameters, compute an optimal control trajectory — are the recurring goals, and recognizing which one a problem is really asking for determines which of the four algorithms (Gauss–Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt, penalty, augmented Lagrangian) is the right tool.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The sources are likewise stereotyped. **A differentiable residual map** $f$ is the bare minimum; without it nothing in this chapter applies. **An initial point** $x^{(1)}$ near a presumed local minimum is essential — every algorithm in the chapter is local, and a bad starting point can converge to a wrong local minimum or fail outright. **Knowledge that the problem is well-posed**, in the sense that the Jacobian $Df(x)$ has full column rank near the minimizer (for Gauss–Newton) or that the constraint Jacobian $Dg(\hat x)$ has full row rank (for the constrained algorithms), is what makes the inner linear-algebra solves correct; without these conditions the unmodified Gauss–Newton stops with an error, and Levenberg–Marquardt is the safe fallback. **A residual structure** — that the objective is a *sum of squares* rather than a general scalar function — is the source that distinguishes this chapter's methods from general optimization: it gives the Jacobian a natural meaning and the approximate Hessian $Df^T Df$ a clean form. **Equality constraints** $g(x) = 0$ activate the second half of the chapter; if the constraints are linear they should be handled inside Levenberg–Marquardt directly (add them to each linear subproblem), and only nonlinear constraints require the penalty or augmented Lagrangian outer loops. The recurring routing is: differentiable residual $+$ good initialization $\to$ Gauss–Newton (if confident) or Levenberg–Marquardt (default); add nonlinear constraints $\to$ wrap Levenberg–Marquardt inside the augmented Lagrangian outer loop; add a model-fitting setup $\to$ instantiate $\theta$ as the variable and the data as the source of the residuals.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves every problem in the chapter is assembled from. Everything here is self-contained — a reader who has just learned linear least squares should be able to follow the descriptions and apply each operation from the text alone.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Linearize the residual at the current iterate, then solve a linear least squares subproblem.** Given the current iterate $x^{(k)}$, form the affine approximation $\hat f(x; x^{(k)}) = f(x^{(k)}) + Df(x^{(k)})(x - x^{(k)})$ — a [[Def - The Total Derivative and Differentiability|first-order Taylor expansion]] — and set $x^{(k+1)}$ as the linear-least-squares minimizer of $\|\hat f(x; x^{(k)})\|^2$. This is the entire content of [[Def - Gauss-Newton Algorithm|Gauss–Newton]]. The closed form is $x^{(k+1)} = x^{(k)} - (Df^T Df)^{-1} Df^T f$, evaluated at $x^{(k)}$. *Trigger:* you need to make progress on $\|f(x)\|^2$ and you have a differentiable residual map. *Pattern:* compute the Jacobian, solve the linear subproblem via the [[Def - Normal Equations|normal equations]] (or [[Thm - Least Squares via QR Factorization|QR factorization]] for stability), update the iterate.

2. **Regularize the step by adding a trust-region penalty.** Instead of the bare Gauss–Newton subproblem, minimize $\|\hat f(x; x^{(k)})\|^2 + \lambda^{(k)} \|x - x^{(k)}\|^2$. This is the [[Def - Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm|Levenberg–Marquardt]] modification, and the closed form is $x^{(k+1)} = x^{(k)} - (Df^T Df + \lambda^{(k)} I)^{-1} Df^T f$. The penalty makes the inner matrix invertible (so the algorithm cannot stop with an error) and limits the step size (so the algorithm cannot diverge). *Trigger:* Gauss–Newton is failing, or you cannot guarantee $Df$ has linearly independent columns. *Pattern:* this is now the default — use Levenberg–Marquardt unless you have a specific reason not to.

3. **Adapt the trust parameter based on whether each step succeeds.** After a Levenberg–Marquardt step, compare $\|f(x^{(k+1)})\|^2$ to $\|f(x^{(k)})\|^2$. If smaller, accept the step and shrink $\lambda^{(k+1)} = 0.8\,\lambda^{(k)}$ (be bolder next time). If larger or equal, reject the step (revert $x^{(k+1)} \leftarrow x^{(k)}$) and grow $\lambda^{(k+1)} = 2\,\lambda^{(k)}$ (be cautious). *Trigger:* a Levenberg–Marquardt iteration has just been computed. *Pattern:* this single adaptation is what makes Levenberg–Marquardt robust across a wide range of problems without manual tuning.

4. **Warm-start from a related problem's solution.** When solving a sequence of related nonlinear least squares problems (e.g., a regularization parameter sweep, a frame-by-frame video tracking task, or successive iterations of an outer constrained-optimization loop), use the previous problem's solution as the starting point for the next. This is **warm starting**, and it can drop the iteration count from dozens to one or two. *Trigger:* you are about to solve a nonlinear least squares problem that is similar to one you have just solved. *Pattern:* always do this — it costs nothing and accelerates convergence dramatically.

5. **Run from multiple starting points and take the best.** Because the algorithms are local, different $x^{(1)}$ can yield different local minima. Running the algorithm from several diverse starting points and keeping the best result (smallest $\|f(\hat x)\|^2$) is a cheap and effective way to avoid being trapped in a poor local minimum. *Trigger:* the objective is known or suspected to have multiple local minima. *Pattern:* generate a handful of random or grid-based starting points; run Levenberg–Marquardt from each; pick the iterate with the smallest residual norm.

6. **Replace a non-differentiable component with a smooth surrogate.** The classifier example replaces $\operatorname{sign}(u)$ — the function we really want — with the sigmoid $\phi(u) = (e^u - e^{-u})/(e^u + e^{-u})$, which is differentiable and approximates the sign function. This converts a problem outside the chapter's reach into a nonlinear least squares problem inside it. *Trigger:* the desired objective involves a non-differentiable function ($\operatorname{sign}$, $\max$, indicator). *Pattern:* find a smooth surrogate (sigmoid, softmax, smoothed-max, log-sum-exp), substitute, and apply Levenberg–Marquardt.

7. **Convert an equality constraint into a quadratic penalty.** Replace $\min \|f(x)\|^2$ s.t. $g(x) = 0$ by the unconstrained $\min \|f(x)\|^2 + \mu \|g(x)\|^2$ for a sequence of increasing $\mu$. Each subproblem is in turn a nonlinear least squares problem (concatenate $f$ and $\sqrt{\mu}\,g$ as a single residual map), solvable by Levenberg–Marquardt. *Trigger:* the problem has nonlinear equality constraints. *Pattern:* this is the [[Def - Penalty Algorithm|penalty algorithm]] — conceptually simplest but prone to ill-conditioning at large $\mu$, so usually superseded by augmented Lagrangian.

8. **Estimate a Lagrange multiplier by the augmented Lagrangian update.** When solving a constrained problem, maintain a running estimate $z^{(k)}$ of the Lagrange multiplier and update it after each inner Levenberg–Marquardt solve by $z^{(k+1)} = z^{(k)} + 2 \mu^{(k)} g(x^{(k+1)})$. This update is gradient ascent on the dual; it accelerates convergence dramatically over pure penalty and keeps $\mu$ moderate. *Trigger:* you are doing a penalty-type algorithm and want better conditioning. *Pattern:* augment the objective to $\|f\|^2 + z^T g + \mu \|g\|^2$, solve, update $z$, repeat — this is the [[Def - Augmented Lagrangian Algorithm|augmented Lagrangian algorithm]].

9. **Group state and control variables together as a single block.** In a nonlinear control problem, write the state trajectory $x_2, \ldots, x_N$ and the input trajectory $u_1, \ldots, u_N$ as a single tall vector of unknowns, with the dynamics $x_{k+1} - f(x_k, u_k) = 0$ as a single tall block of equality constraints. The augmented Lagrangian algorithm then sees the entire trajectory at once. *Trigger:* a nonlinear control or dynamic-programming problem. *Pattern:* this is **direct transcription** (or "direct multiple shooting"); it scales to long horizons by virtue of the sparsity of $Df, Dg$.

10. **Add a regularization term to nonlinear model fitting.** When fitting $\hat f(x; \theta) \approx y$ over many data points, add $\lambda \|\theta\|^2$ to the objective and treat the regularizer as one more block of residuals: stack the data residuals with $\sqrt{\lambda}\,\theta$ as additional rows. This is **Tikhonov regularization** for nonlinear models. *Trigger:* the model has many parameters relative to data, or you observe overfitting. *Pattern:* tune $\lambda$ by validation on a held-out test set — the [[Def - Validation (Training and Test Error)|validation framework]] from Topic XI carries over unchanged.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Trusting Gauss–Newton in the large-residual regime
> When $f(\hat x) \neq 0$ at the minimum — meaning the model cannot fit the data exactly — the curvature term $\sum_i f_i \nabla^2 f_i$ that Gauss–Newton drops is *not* small, so the approximate Hessian $Df^T Df$ is no longer close to the true Hessian. The temptation is to use Gauss–Newton anyway, since it gave quadratic convergence on zero-residual test problems. The standard counterexample is fitting a slightly mis-specified nonlinear model: residuals do not go to zero, and Gauss–Newton's convergence factor depends on the eigenvalues of the dropped curvature term — it can be slow or even fail to converge. *The repair:* use Levenberg–Marquardt unconditionally. The trust parameter compensates for the missing curvature, and the per-iteration cost is the same.

> [!warning] 2. Driving the penalty parameter $\mu$ to a huge value in one shot
> The penalty method requires $\mu \to \infty$ to enforce the constraint. The temptation is to skip the gradual increase and just set $\mu = 10^{12}$ from the start. This fails badly: the Levenberg–Marquardt subproblem becomes hopelessly ill-conditioned (its Jacobian has rows with very different scales — the original residual rows of size $\sim 1$ and the constraint rows of size $\sim \sqrt{\mu}$), and the inner solves return garbage. Even with double-precision arithmetic, $\mu \sim 10^{16}$ is the practical ceiling and even $10^{10}$ is suspect. *The repair:* grow $\mu$ gradually, doubling per outer iteration, or — better — use the [[Def - Augmented Lagrangian Algorithm|augmented Lagrangian]], which keeps $\mu$ moderate by absorbing the constraint into the Lagrange multiplier.

> [!warning] 3. Declaring convergence at a stationary point that is not a minimum
> Every algorithm in the chapter stops at a point satisfying the first-order condition $2 Df^T f = 0$. This condition is also satisfied by **saddle points** and **local maxima** of $\|f\|^2$; the algorithms cannot distinguish. The temptation is to trust the algorithm's termination unconditionally. The standard counterexample is a problem with a small basin around a saddle, where Levenberg–Marquardt with a small $\lambda$ behaves like Gauss–Newton and converges to the saddle. *The repair:* always run from multiple starting points (operation 5 above) and compare $\|f\|^2$ values at the resulting stationary points; a saddle exposes itself because its $\|f\|^2$ is larger than nearby points'.

> [!warning] 4. Using a non-differentiable residual without a smooth surrogate
> One might wish to fit a model whose residual involves $|\cdot|$, $\max$, or $\operatorname{sign}$, and try to push it through Levenberg–Marquardt anyway by ignoring the kinks. This fails: the Jacobian is undefined at the kinks, and the algorithm typically oscillates between two configurations on either side of a kink, never converging. *The repair:* smooth the offending function (replace $|u|$ by $\sqrt{u^2 + \varepsilon^2}$, $\max(u, 0)$ by the softplus $\log(1 + e^u)$, $\operatorname{sign}(u)$ by the sigmoid $\phi$), or switch to a problem-class designed for non-smooth objectives (e.g., proximal-gradient methods or the simplex-based approaches of LP).

# Problem-Solving Strategy

Problems in this topic split sharply along two axes: *constrained vs unconstrained*, and *zero-residual vs general*. Recognizing where a problem sits determines the algorithm and the expected behaviour.

If the problem is **unconstrained and looks zero-residual** — solving $f(x) = 0$ with $m = n$, or fitting a model that can in principle fit the data exactly — then [[Def - Gauss-Newton Algorithm|Gauss–Newton]] (which here reduces to Newton's method on $f(x) = 0$) is the natural tool. The expected behaviour is quadratic convergence in a neighborhood of the solution. Choose a starting point informed by the problem geometry — for a location-from-range problem, the centroid of the anchors; for a curve fit, a reasonable parameter guess. Iterate, watching $\|f(x^{(k)})\|^2$ shrink. If you observe slowdown or divergence, this is a signal that the starting point is too far from the solution, or that the problem is *not* truly zero-residual after all — pivot immediately to Levenberg–Marquardt.

If the problem is **unconstrained and might be large-residual** — model fitting where the model is approximate, nonlinear regression with noisy data, any case where you have no reason to believe $\|f\|^2$ can be driven to zero — go straight to [[Def - Levenberg-Marquardt Algorithm|Levenberg–Marquardt]] with $\lambda^{(1)} = 1$. The adaptive trust parameter handles both the early phase (where the linearization is poor, so large $\lambda$ keeps steps short and the algorithm behaves like gradient descent) and the late phase (where the linearization is excellent, so small $\lambda$ allows Gauss–Newton's fast local convergence). The single design decision is the starting point; for this run several from random or domain-informed initializations and take the best result. Watch $\lambda^{(k)}$ — if it stays large, the algorithm is consistently struggling and your starting point may be too far away; if it stays small, you are in fast-convergence regime.

If the problem is **constrained**, the question is whether the constraints are *linear* or *nonlinear*. Linear equality constraints can be folded directly into the inner Levenberg–Marquardt subproblem by adjoining the constraint as additional rows of the linear system — the [[Def - KKT System|KKT system]] from [[Linear Algebra XI — Applied II — Least Squares|Topic XI]] handles it in one solve. Nonlinear equality constraints demand an outer loop. The principled choice is the [[Def - Augmented Lagrangian Algorithm|augmented Lagrangian]] algorithm — it converges faster, is numerically better-conditioned, and produces accurate Lagrange-multiplier estimates as a byproduct. The [[Def - Penalty Algorithm|pure penalty]] algorithm is pedagogically simpler but rarely the best practical choice; treat it as the "wrong but instructive" baseline that the augmented Lagrangian refines.

A meta-strategy threads through all of this: **trust the linear least squares subproblem, and let the outer loop fix everything else.** Every algorithm in the chapter is built on a single primitive — solving one linear least squares problem — that you already know how to do from [[Linear Algebra XI — Applied II — Least Squares|Topic XI]]. Each iteration: form the Jacobian, set up the linear subproblem, solve it (via [[Def - Normal Equations|normal equations]] or [[Thm - QR Factorization|QR]]). The outer mechanisms — trust-region regularization, multi-start, penalty, multiplier updates — are layers of safety surrounding the same linear solve. When stuck on a problem in this chapter, the question is rarely "how do I solve the linear subproblem" but rather "which outer mechanism do I need around it." The unifying question of the chapter is, in fact, exactly that: *which outer mechanism turns the linear subproblem into the answer I need?* For unconstrained zero-residual, none — Gauss–Newton suffices. For unconstrained general, trust-region regularization — Levenberg–Marquardt. For constrained, multiplier-and-penalty updates — augmented Lagrangian.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **The Jacobian as approximate Hessian: $\nabla^2 \|f\|^2 \approx 2 Df^T Df$.** This single approximation is what makes Gauss–Newton tick. The full Hessian of $\|f(x)\|^2$ is $2 Df(x)^T Df(x) + 2 \sum_i f_i(x) \nabla^2 f_i(x)$; the first term is computed from first derivatives alone, while the second requires second derivatives of every component. Dropping the second term gives an approximate Hessian that costs nothing extra (you already need $Df$ for the gradient $2 Df^T f$) and is accurate to leading order at residual minima. **Typical use:** whenever you would reach for Newton's method on a sum of squares — model fitting, nonlinear inverse problems, neural-network training in its least-squares formulation — Gauss–Newton or Levenberg–Marquardt should be the default; full Newton is rarely needed and rarely worth its second-derivative price.

- **Trust-region regularization makes the inner solve unconditionally well-posed.** The matrix $Df^T Df + \lambda I$ with $\lambda > 0$ is symmetric positive definite *regardless* of whether $Df$ has full column rank. **Typical use:** when in doubt about rank, use Levenberg–Marquardt; the same is true for any sum-of-squares problem where you cannot guarantee that the Jacobian's columns stay linearly independent during the iteration. This single repair handles rank-deficient model fitting (e.g., overparameterized neural networks), nonlinear-equation systems where the Jacobian degenerates at some iterate, and equilibrium computations where the equations are themselves nearly degenerate.

- **Augmented Lagrangian: penalty + multiplier estimate = well-conditioned constraint enforcement.** The penalty method's $\mu \to \infty$ regime kills numerical conditioning. Adding the Lagrange-multiplier term $z^T g(x)$ to the penalized objective, with $z$ updated by the rule $z \leftarrow z + 2\mu g(x)$, removes the need for huge $\mu$ — the constraint is enforced through the multiplier, not through the penalty. **Typical use:** any nonlinear-equality-constrained optimization where the constraint is genuinely nonlinear and matters (nonlinear control, equilibrium computation with conservation laws, model fitting under nonlinear parameter constraints). The same mechanism, generalized to handle inequality constraints, becomes [[Def - Constrained Nonlinear Least Squares|sequential quadratic programming]] and the augmented-Lagrangian interior-point hybrids that power modern nonlinear-programming codes.

- **The "linearize and solve a linear LS subproblem" loop generalizes to any problem with a smooth residual.** This is the master pattern of the chapter, transcending the specific algorithms. **Typical use:** if you can write your problem as "minimize $\|f(x)\|^2$ for some smooth $f$," everything in this chapter applies. The smoothness can be hidden — neural network training, parameter estimation in PDEs, image registration, bundle adjustment in computer vision — but once the residual is identified the toolchain is the same. The non-obvious instance is **the extended Kalman filter**, which is exactly Gauss–Newton applied iteratively to a nonlinear state-space residual at each measurement update.

- **Warm starting drops iteration counts by orders of magnitude.** When a sequence of related nonlinear-LS problems is to be solved (regularization sweep, time-series tracking, continuation methods), starting each from the previous solution typically requires 1–3 iterations instead of 10–30. **Typical use:** any application that solves the same parametric family repeatedly — animation rigging, model predictive control, parameter sweeps in scientific computing — should warm-start. The savings compound: a $20\times$ speedup over a 1000-step trajectory is the difference between real-time control and offline post-processing.

# Bridges

1. **Linear least squares — the inner subproblem.** Every algorithm in this chapter solves, at each iterate, a linear least squares problem of the form $\min_x \|A x - b\|^2$ or its regularized variant $\min_x \|Ax - b\|^2 + \lambda \|x\|^2$. The matrix $A$ is the Jacobian $Df(x^{(k)})$; the vector $b$ is built from $f(x^{(k)})$ and $Df(x^{(k)}) x^{(k)}$; the regularization arises from the trust-region $\lambda^{(k)} \|x - x^{(k)}\|^2$ term. This is the [[Def - Least Squares Problem|least squares problem]] of [[Linear Algebra XI — Applied II — Least Squares|Topic XI]], solved exactly as before — by the [[Def - Normal Equations|normal equations]] for small problems or by [[Thm - QR Factorization|QR factorization]] for stability. The contribution of this chapter is the outer structure that decides *which* linear least squares problem to solve next; the inner machinery is unchanged. So nonlinear least squares is, computationally, linear least squares applied inside an outer loop that updates the linearization point.

2. **Multivariate analysis — the Jacobian is the central object.** The [[Def - Partial Derivatives and the Jacobian Matrix|Jacobian]] $Df(x)$ and the [[Def - The Total Derivative and Differentiability|total derivative]] are imported wholesale from multivariate analysis. The affine approximation $\hat f(x; x^{(k)}) = f(x^{(k)}) + Df(x^{(k)})(x - x^{(k)})$ is the first-order Taylor expansion of $f$ at $x^{(k)}$. The chain-rule machinery from multivariate analysis is what makes the Jacobian of composite residuals (as in a neural network) tractable — efficient evaluation of $Df^T v$ via backpropagation is just the chain rule applied right-to-left. The convergence theorems below depend on $f$ being at least $C^1$ (continuously differentiable), and quadratic convergence of Newton/Gauss–Newton near a zero-residual minimum requires $f \in C^2$ with a Lipschitz continuous Hessian — these regularity hypotheses come from the standard error analysis of Taylor approximation.

3. **Newton's method — Gauss–Newton with the true Hessian.** **Newton's method** for minimizing a scalar $\phi(x)$ is $x^{(k+1)} = x^{(k)} - (\nabla^2 \phi(x^{(k)}))^{-1} \nabla \phi(x^{(k)})$. For $\phi(x) = \|f(x)\|^2$ the gradient is $2 Df^T f$ and the Hessian is $2 Df^T Df + 2 \sum_i f_i \nabla^2 f_i$. Gauss–Newton drops the curvature term and uses $Df^T Df$ as approximate Hessian — the approximation is exact when all $f_i = 0$ and degrades smoothly as residuals grow. The bridge is precise: Gauss–Newton **is** Newton's method on the *Gauss–Newton approximate Hessian*. Knowing this clarifies when Gauss–Newton is fast (zero-residual, where the approximation is exact) and when it can be slow (large residuals, where the dropped curvature term contributes substantially to the true Hessian).

4. **Logistic regression — sigmoid replacement of the sign function.** **Logistic regression** is conventionally formulated with the log-likelihood loss $\sum_i \log(1 + e^{-y^{(i)} (\beta^T x^{(i)} + v)})$, which has its own optimization theory (Newton's method, IRLS — iteratively reweighted least squares). Boyd's formulation in §18.5 is the **least-squares logistic regression**: replace $\operatorname{sign}$ in the classification error $(\operatorname{sign}(u) - y)^2$ by the sigmoid $\phi(u) = \tanh(u)$, giving the smooth objective $\sum_i (\phi(\beta^T x^{(i)} + v) - y^{(i)})^2 + \lambda \|\beta\|^2$, then minimize via Levenberg–Marquardt. The two formulations give similar classifiers in practice. The bridge to the standard logistic regression is the recognition that *any* smooth invertible squashing function — sigmoid, tanh, smoothed Heaviside — converts a Boolean classification problem into a least-squares one that the chapter's algorithms can solve.

5. **Trust region methods — Levenberg–Marquardt is the prototype.** The general theory of **trust region methods** for unconstrained optimization is built on the same principle as Levenberg–Marquardt: at each iterate, build a model $m^{(k)}(p)$ of the objective near $x^{(k)}$ (typically a quadratic), and minimize the model subject to a constraint $\|p\| \leq \Delta^{(k)}$ (the trust region). The radius $\Delta^{(k)}$ is updated based on the ratio of actual to predicted decrease. Levenberg–Marquardt is precisely the trust-region method whose model is the Gauss–Newton quadratic and whose trust constraint is enforced via a Lagrange-multiplier-style $\lambda^{(k)}$ that is dual to a radius constraint. The general trust-region machinery (Steihaug's truncated CG, dogleg method, Powell's algorithm) extends Levenberg–Marquardt to non-least-squares objectives.

6. **Sequential Quadratic Programming — augmented Lagrangian for inequality constraints.** **Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP)** solves general nonlinear programs $\min \phi(x)$ s.t. $h(x) \leq 0, g(x) = 0$ by repeatedly forming a *quadratic program* — minimize a quadratic approximation of the Lagrangian subject to linearized constraints — and taking a step. For equality-only constrained least squares, SQP coincides with applying Newton's method to the KKT system, which is closely related to the augmented Lagrangian when the Lagrangian is well-conditioned. The augmented Lagrangian algorithm is, structurally, an outer-loop method that solves a sequence of *unconstrained* subproblems; SQP is the parallel approach that solves a sequence of *constrained-but-linearized* subproblems. The two families are complementary and frequently combined in production solvers (e.g., SNOPT, IPOPT, LANCELOT).

# Insights

**The unifying frame: linearize, solve linear LS, repeat.** Every algorithm in this chapter is built from a single move — replace the nonlinear residual $f$ by its tangent affine approximation $\hat f$ at the current iterate, then solve the resulting *linear* least-squares problem. Gauss–Newton, Levenberg–Marquardt, the penalty algorithm, and the augmented Lagrangian are not four separate algorithms; they are four ways of choosing *which* linear least squares problem to solve at iteration $k$. Gauss–Newton solves the bare $\min \|\hat f\|^2$. Levenberg–Marquardt adds a trust-region regularization $\lambda \|x - x^{(k)}\|^2$. The penalty algorithm changes the residual map itself by appending $\sqrt{\mu} g(x)$. The augmented Lagrangian further offsets the constraint penalty by the multiplier estimate. The chapter is unified by recognizing this: once you can solve linear least squares (Topic XI), you can solve every problem in this chapter — you just need to choose the right outer loop.

**The true name of Gauss–Newton is "Newton's method with the curvature term dropped."** The textbook presentation of Gauss–Newton — "approximate the residual by its tangent and solve linear LS" — is correct but obscures the deeper structure. The operational characterization is: the Hessian of $\|f\|^2$ is $2 Df^T Df + 2 \sum_i f_i \nabla^2 f_i$, and Gauss–Newton uses only the first term as approximate Hessian. This is what makes Gauss–Newton fast (the first term is free, given $Df$) and what makes it sometimes wrong (the second term is missing, and matters when residuals are large). Once this is seen, the position of Gauss–Newton in the broader landscape of optimization algorithms becomes clear — it sits between gradient descent (uses $\nabla \phi$ only, no Hessian) and Newton's method (uses the full Hessian), favoring computational economy over Hessian accuracy. Levenberg–Marquardt's $\lambda I$ correction can be read as a crude estimate of the missing curvature term — when the residual is large, $\lambda$ grows, and the algorithm shrinks toward gradient descent precisely because the Gauss–Newton Hessian approximation has degraded.

**Trigger-reaction patterns scattered across the chapter.** *Want to minimize a sum of squares of nonlinear functions →* Levenberg–Marquardt, unconditionally; use Gauss–Newton only if you have evidence the residual minimum is zero and the starting point is excellent. *Want to fit a logistic-style model →* write it as nonlinear LS classification with a sigmoid link, then apply the algorithms of this chapter. *Have nonlinear equality constraints →* penalty method if pedagogically illustrative, augmented Lagrangian if practically needed. *Encountered a divergence or stagnation →* the trust parameter is the diagnostic: a $\lambda^{(k)}$ that grows persistently means the linearization is poor (try a different starting point); a $\lambda^{(k)}$ that stays small but the residual does not shrink means you are at a saddle (multi-start). *Faced with a non-differentiable objective →* find a smooth surrogate. These triggers are dense; learning them is much of the chapter's content.

**The augmented Lagrangian's multiplier update is dual gradient ascent.** This is the deepest single fact of the constrained half of the chapter. The augmented Lagrangian $L_\mu(x, z) = \|f(x)\|^2 + z^T g(x) + \mu \|g(x)\|^2$ has the structure: minimize over $x$ for fixed $z$, then update $z$ to push the constraint $g(x)$ toward zero. The update rule $z \leftarrow z + 2\mu g(x)$ is *precisely* a gradient ascent step on the dual function $\phi(z) = \min_x L_\mu(x, z)$, because $\partial \phi/\partial z = g(\hat x(z))$ where $\hat x(z)$ is the minimizer. So the augmented Lagrangian is alternating primal-dual updates: minimize the primal exactly (or approximately, with Levenberg–Marquardt), take a gradient step on the dual. This is the structure of **method of multipliers** in convex optimization and the conceptual ancestor of [[Linear Algebra XI — Applied II — Least Squares|ADMM]] (alternating direction method of multipliers). The $\mu$ in the augmented Lagrangian sets the *step size* of the dual gradient ascent; a moderate $\mu$ is optimal because too small a step is slow and too large a step is unstable — exactly as in any gradient method.

**Why optimization heuristics work in practice even though they are local.** Every algorithm in this chapter is a heuristic — none provides a global-optimality guarantee, and all converge only to stationary points of $\|f\|^2$ (which might be saddles or local minima, not global). The practical success of these methods in modern data fitting, neural network training, and control is at first puzzling: how can such weak guarantees lead to such reliable practice? The answer is that the geometry of *most useful* nonlinear least squares problems is benign — the landscapes have either a unique global minimum (low-residual model fits with full Jacobian rank near the minimum), or many local minima of comparable quality (overparameterized neural networks, where every reasonable starting point converges to a model that fits the data well). The pathological cases where multiple isolated local minima differ substantially in value are surprisingly rare in practice. This is an empirical observation, not a theorem, and it is one of the open frontiers of modern optimization theory — the **landscape analysis** of overparameterized models, currently being mapped by techniques from random matrix theory and statistical physics. For the working applied mathematician: trust the algorithms, but always run from multiple starting points to catch the rare bad case.
