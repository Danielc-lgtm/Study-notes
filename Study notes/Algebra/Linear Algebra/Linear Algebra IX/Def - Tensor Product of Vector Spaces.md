# Notation

Throughout, $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional vector spaces over a field $\mathbb{F}$, with dimensions $m = \dim V$ and $n = \dim W$. The dual spaces are $V' = V^*$ (linear functionals on $V$) and $W' = W^*$. The space of [[Def - Bilinear Form|bilinear forms]] $V \times W \to \mathbb{F}$ is denoted $\mathcal{B}(V, W)$, and the space of bilinear maps $V \times W \to U$ for a third vector space $U$ is $\mathcal{B}(V, W; U)$. The tensor product is $V \otimes W$, and an elementary (or simple, or decomposable) tensor is denoted $v \otimes w$ for $v \in V$, $w \in W$.

---

# Axiom Motivation

The tensor product is the vector space that **converts bilinear maps into linear maps**. The conceptual problem it solves: given a bilinear map $\Gamma : V \times W \to U$, we have two inputs $v \in V$ and $w \in W$ and one output $\Gamma(v, w) \in U$, with the linearity holding *separately* in each input. We would like to package this data as a *single* linear map, because linear maps are the basic morphisms of linear algebra and theorems about linear maps (the rank-nullity theorem, isomorphism theorems, dual maps) all want to apply. But the domain of $\Gamma$ is $V \times W$ as a Cartesian product of sets, and a *linear* map $V \times W \to U$ (joint linearity in $V \oplus W$) is a strictly weaker notion than a bilinear map — bilinear maps $V \times W \to U$ have $mn$ degrees of freedom (a value for each pair of basis vectors), while linear maps $V \oplus W \to U$ have only $m + n$.

The tensor product $V \otimes W$ is the space designed so that bilinear maps out of $V \times W$ correspond to linear maps out of $V \otimes W$. The defining feature is the **universal property** (see [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product]]): there is a canonical bilinear map $\otimes : V \times W \to V \otimes W$, $(v, w) \mapsto v \otimes w$, such that every bilinear map $\Gamma : V \times W \to U$ factors uniquely as $\Gamma = \hat\Gamma \circ \otimes$ for a unique linear $\hat\Gamma : V \otimes W \to U$.

The universal property *characterises* $V \otimes W$ up to a unique isomorphism, but it does not by itself construct one. Multiple constructions exist:

1. **Quotient of a free space (the standard construction).** Take the free vector space $\mathbb{F}^{V \times W}$ on the set of pairs $(v, w)$, and quotient by the [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] generated by the bilinearity relations $(v_1 + v_2, w) - (v_1, w) - (v_2, w)$, $(v, w_1 + w_2) - (v, w) - (v, w_2)$, $(\lambda v, w) - \lambda(v, w)$, $(v, \lambda w) - \lambda(v, w)$. The result is $V \otimes W$, and the image of $(v, w)$ is $v \otimes w$.

2. **Bilinear forms on dual product (LADR's construction).** Define $V \otimes W := \mathcal{B}(V', W')$, the bilinear forms on the product of duals. For $v \in V$ and $w \in W$, define $v \otimes w \in V \otimes W$ by $(v \otimes w)(\varphi, \tau) := \varphi(v) \tau(w)$ for $\varphi \in V', \tau \in W'$. This is more concrete and has the advantage that one *immediately* sees $\dim(V \otimes W) = \dim V \cdot \dim W$.

3. **Universal property treated as a definition.** Declare $V \otimes W$ to be "any vector space equipped with a bilinear map $\otimes : V \times W \to V \otimes W$ satisfying the universal property". By the universal property itself, any two such spaces are canonically isomorphic, so the definition is unique up to a canonical isomorphism.

LADR uses construction (2). Its merit is that, for *finite-dimensional* vector spaces, it gives a concrete model whose properties are easy to check. Its limitation is that it does not generalise to infinite-dimensional spaces (where $V'$ is much larger than $V$) or to [[Def - Module|modules]] over a [[Def - Ring|ring]] (where the dual can behave pathologically). For those generalisations, construction (1) is standard.

**Why the universal property is the *right* characterisation, even though we use a concrete construction.** The construction $\mathcal{B}(V', W')$ is *a* model of the tensor product, but it is not "the" tensor product — any other model is canonically isomorphic to it. The conceptual content of the tensor product is the universal property: linear maps out of $V \otimes W$ are bilinear maps out of $V \times W$. Every theorem about tensor products should be provable from this universal property without reference to the concrete model. In particular: the existence of bases for $V \otimes W$, the dimension formula, the associativity $(U \otimes V) \otimes W \cong U \otimes (V \otimes W)$, the distributivity over direct sums, the natural isomorphism $V \otimes \mathbb{F} \cong V$ — all of these are consequences of the universal property, with the concrete model just being a convenient one-time construction to prove the universal property has a solution.

**A trigger-reaction pattern.** When you encounter a bilinear (or multilinear) construction in linear algebra, the *first* question to ask is: "what is the universal-property formulation?" The benefits are that (i) it abstracts away the choice of basis or coordinate system, (ii) it makes statements transparently functorial, and (iii) it lets you import tensor-product machinery from algebra, topology, and category theory.

# The Definition

**LADR's construction (9.71).** Let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces over $\mathbb{F}$, with duals $V' = V^*$ and $W' = W^*$. Define

$$V \otimes W := \mathcal{B}(V', W'),$$

the vector space of [[Def - Bilinear Form|bilinear forms]] $V' \times W' \to \mathbb{F}$. For $v \in V$ and $w \in W$, define the **elementary tensor** $v \otimes w \in V \otimes W$ as the bilinear form

$$(v \otimes w)(\varphi, \tau) := \varphi(v) \cdot \tau(w) \qquad \text{for } \varphi \in V',\, \tau \in W'.$$

**[[Def - Dimension|Dimension]] (LADR 9.72).** With the construction above,

$$\dim(V \otimes W) = \dim V \cdot \dim W = mn.$$

The reason: $\mathcal{B}(V', W')$ has dimension $\dim V' \cdot \dim W' = mn$, since a bilinear form on a product is determined by its values on a basis-pair.

**Basis (LADR 9.74(b)).** If $(e_1, \dots, e_m)$ is a basis of $V$ and $(f_1, \dots, f_n)$ is a basis of $W$, then the family

$$\{e_i \otimes f_j\}_{1 \leq i \leq m,\, 1 \leq j \leq n}$$

is a basis of $V \otimes W$. Every element of $V \otimes W$ can be written uniquely as $\sum_{i, j} c_{ij}\, e_i \otimes f_j$ for scalars $c_{ij} \in \mathbb{F}$.

**Bilinearity (LADR 9.73).** The map $V \times W \to V \otimes W$, $(v, w) \mapsto v \otimes w$, is bilinear:

$$(v_1 + v_2) \otimes w = v_1 \otimes w + v_2 \otimes w, \quad v \otimes (w_1 + w_2) = v \otimes w_1 + v \otimes w_2,$$
$$(\lambda v) \otimes w = \lambda (v \otimes w) = v \otimes (\lambda w).$$

These are direct from the definition: each side is a bilinear form on $V' \times W'$, and they agree at every $(\varphi, \tau)$ by linearity of $\varphi$ and $\tau$.

**Universal property (LADR 9.79 — see [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product]]).** For every vector space $U$ and every bilinear map $\Gamma : V \times W \to U$, there exists a **unique linear map** $\hat\Gamma : V \otimes W \to U$ with

$$\hat\Gamma(v \otimes w) = \Gamma(v, w) \quad \text{for all } v \in V, w \in W.$$

This is the defining characterisation of the tensor product up to canonical isomorphism.

**Elementary tensors are spanning but not exhaustive.** Every element of $V \otimes W$ is a *sum* of elementary tensors: $z = \sum_k v_k \otimes w_k$ for some $v_k \in V, w_k \in W$. However, not every element is itself an elementary tensor. For instance, in $\mathbb{F}^2 \otimes \mathbb{F}^2$, the element $e_1 \otimes e_1 + e_2 \otimes e_2$ cannot be written as a single $v \otimes w$ (a fact related to the rank of the corresponding matrix; see [[Ex - Tensor product of two copies of F^2 has dimension 4]]).

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The categorical formulation of the tensor product is its defining property and the only one that generalises cleanly.

**The universal property as a definition.** A **tensor product** of $V$ and $W$ is a pair $(T, \otimes)$ consisting of a vector space $T$ and a bilinear map $\otimes : V \times W \to T$ satisfying:

> For every vector space $U$ and every bilinear map $\Gamma : V \times W \to U$, there exists a unique linear map $\hat\Gamma : T \to U$ such that $\Gamma = \hat\Gamma \circ \otimes$.

This characterises $(T, \otimes)$ up to a unique isomorphism. The notation $V \otimes W$ refers to any such $T$, and any two solutions are canonically isomorphic via the unique linear map produced by the universal property applied to the other.

**The categorical statement: the tensor product represents the bifunctor of bilinear maps.** Define the functor $\mathcal{B}(V, W; -) : \mathbf{Vect}_\mathbb{F} \to \mathbf{Set}$, sending a vector space $U$ to the set $\mathcal{B}(V, W; U)$ of bilinear maps $V \times W \to U$. The universal property says this functor is *representable* by $V \otimes W$:

$$\mathcal{B}(V, W; U) \;\cong\; \mathcal{L}(V \otimes W, U) \qquad \text{naturally in } U.$$

The natural isomorphism sends a bilinear map $\Gamma$ to its linear factorisation $\hat\Gamma$, and conversely sends a linear $\Lambda : V \otimes W \to U$ to the bilinear $(v, w) \mapsto \Lambda(v \otimes w)$. This is an instance of the Yoneda perspective on universal constructions: $V \otimes W$ is the representing object for the bilinear-map functor.

**Tensor-hom adjunction.** Equivalently, there is a natural isomorphism

$$\mathcal{L}(V \otimes W, U) \;\cong\; \mathcal{L}(V, \mathcal{L}(W, U)).$$

This is "currying" for linear maps: a linear map on the tensor product is the same as a linear map taking $V$ to the space of linear maps from $W$ to $U$. Both sides have dimension $mn \cdot \dim U$. This adjunction is one of the foundational facts of category theory and recurs across mathematics.

**The tensor product as a *bifunctor*.** $\otimes : \mathbf{Vect}_\mathbb{F} \times \mathbf{Vect}_\mathbb{F} \to \mathbf{Vect}_\mathbb{F}$ is a functor in both arguments: given linear maps $S : V \to V'$ and $T : W \to W'$, there is an induced linear map $S \otimes T : V \otimes W \to V' \otimes W'$ defined by $(S \otimes T)(v \otimes w) := Sv \otimes Tw$ on elementary tensors and extended by linearity (well-defined by universal property). Composition is preserved: $(S' \otimes T')(S \otimes T) = (S'S) \otimes (T'T)$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The tensor product is **the canonical operation for multiplying vector spaces multiplicatively**, in the sense that $\dim(V \otimes W) = \dim V \cdot \dim W$ (whereas $\dim(V \oplus W) = \dim V + \dim W$ is the additive operation). It is the universal recipient of bilinear maps, the foundation of multilinear algebra, and the home of "tensors of multiple variance" in differential geometry.

From the algebra side, the construction generalises: $M \otimes_R N$ for two modules over a commutative ring $R$ has the same universal property (linear-over-$R$ maps out of $M \otimes_R N$ correspond to bilinear-over-$R$ maps out of $M \times N$), but its concrete construction must be the free-quotient one — the dual-product construction does not generalise to modules. The base ring $R$ enters by demanding all maps be $R$-linear/bilinear; tensor products over $\mathbb{Z}$ give the connecting bridges in homological algebra.

From the geometry side, the tensor product appears at every level:

- The **tangent space** $T_p M$ at a point of a manifold is a vector space, and tensor products of tangent spaces $(T_p M)^{\otimes k} \otimes (T_p^* M)^{\otimes \ell}$ define tensors of type $(k, \ell)$ at $p$.
- The **metric** is a section of $T^* M \otimes T^* M$ (a covariant 2-tensor).
- The **stress-energy tensor** in physics is a section of $T^* M \otimes T^* M$ on the spacetime manifold.
- **Multi-particle quantum systems** live in tensor products of single-particle Hilbert spaces — this is one of the deepest physical applications.

**True name:** $V \otimes W$ is the universal recipient of bilinear maps out of $V \times W$ — the vector space that converts bilinearity into linearity. The construction $\mathcal{B}(V', W')$ is one model; the universal property is the meaning.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: $V \otimes \mathbb{F} \cong V$.** Take $W = \mathbb{F}$, so $W' = \mathbb{F}^* \cong \mathbb{F}$. A bilinear form on $V' \times \mathbb{F}$ is determined by its value at $(\varphi, 1)$, since $(\varphi, \lambda) = \lambda(\varphi, 1)$ in each slot. So $\mathcal{B}(V', \mathbb{F}) \cong V'^*$ (the linear functionals on $V'$), which is canonically isomorphic to $V$ in finite [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]] (the double-dual isomorphism). The isomorphism is $v \leftrightarrow v \otimes 1$.

**Is an instance: $\mathbb{F}^m \otimes \mathbb{F}^n \cong M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{F})$.** A bilinear form on $(\mathbb{F}^m)' \times (\mathbb{F}^n)'$ — that is, on $\mathbb{F}^m \times \mathbb{F}^n$ via the natural identification — is given by a matrix $M$ with $M(\varphi, \tau) = \varphi^t M \tau$. So $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{F}^m, \mathbb{F}^n) = M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{F})$. Under this identification, the elementary tensor $v \otimes w$ corresponds to the **rank-one matrix** $v w^t$ (an outer product). Sums of $k$ elementary tensors correspond to matrices of rank $\leq k$. See [[Ex - Tensor product of two copies of F^2 has dimension 4]].

**Is an instance: $V^* \otimes V \cong \mathcal{L}(V, V)$ as vector spaces.** The elementary tensor $\varphi \otimes v$ corresponds to the rank-one operator $w \mapsto \varphi(w) v$. The full isomorphism (well-defined by universal property) makes $V^* \otimes V$ a model for "linear maps from $V$ to $V$". The trace is the canonical map $V^* \otimes V \to \mathbb{F}$, $\varphi \otimes v \mapsto \varphi(v)$ — a universal-property construction from the bilinear evaluation pairing.

**Is an instance: $\mathbb{F}[X] \otimes_\mathbb{F} \mathbb{F}[Y] \cong \mathbb{F}[X, Y]$.** The tensor product of two polynomial [[Def - Ring|rings]] (over $\mathbb{F}$) is the polynomial ring in two variables, with $X^i \otimes Y^j \leftrightarrow X^i Y^j$. This is *not* finite-dimensional, but the universal property still characterises the tensor product cleanly.

**Is an instance: $\mathbb{C} \otimes_\mathbb{R} \mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{C} \oplus \mathbb{C}$.** A classic example of a tensor product of fields that produces a direct sum. The dimension over $\mathbb{R}$ is $2 \cdot 2 = 4$, matching $\dim_\mathbb{R}(\mathbb{C} \oplus \mathbb{C}) = 4$. This shows that the tensor product of two copies of a field over a smaller subfield can break into a sum of pieces.

**Is NOT an instance: $V \otimes W \neq V \oplus W$ in general.** The direct sum $V \oplus W$ has dimension $\dim V + \dim W$, while $V \otimes W$ has dimension $\dim V \cdot \dim W$. For $\dim V = \dim W = 2$: $\dim(V \oplus W) = 4$, $\dim(V \otimes W) = 4$ — coincidentally equal, which can mislead. For $\dim V = \dim W = 3$: $\dim(V \oplus W) = 6$, $\dim(V \otimes W) = 9$ — different.

**Is NOT an instance: every element of $V \otimes W$ is *not* an elementary tensor.** Counterexample: $e_1 \otimes e_1 + e_2 \otimes e_2 \in \mathbb{F}^2 \otimes \mathbb{F}^2$ is *not* of the form $v \otimes w$. If it were, we would have $v \otimes w = e_1 \otimes e_1 + e_2 \otimes e_2$, so writing $v = ae_1 + be_2$ and $w = ce_1 + de_2$, expanding gives $ac\, e_1 \otimes e_1 + ad\, e_1 \otimes e_2 + bc\, e_2 \otimes e_1 + bd\, e_2 \otimes e_2$. Matching the basis expansion: $ac = 1$, $bd = 1$, $ad = 0$, $bc = 0$. From $ad = 0$ and $ac = 1$ (so $a \neq 0$), we get $d = 0$, contradicting $bd = 1$. So the sum is *not* a simple tensor. This is the algebraic origin of **quantum entanglement**: states that cannot be written as a product are "entangled".

**Corollary (functoriality).** For linear maps $S : V \to V'$ and $T : W \to W'$, the induced map $S \otimes T : V \otimes W \to V' \otimes W'$ is well-defined and linear. It is the universal-property factorisation of the bilinear map $(v, w) \mapsto Sv \otimes Tw$.

**Corollary (associativity).** $(U \otimes V) \otimes W \cong U \otimes (V \otimes W)$ canonically, via the unique linear isomorphism sending $(u \otimes v) \otimes w$ to $u \otimes (v \otimes w)$. Both sides have the universal property for trilinear maps out of $U \times V \times W$. This lets us speak unambiguously of $U \otimes V \otimes W$ — the iterated tensor product.

**Corollary (distributivity over direct sum).** $V \otimes (W_1 \oplus W_2) \cong (V \otimes W_1) \oplus (V \otimes W_2)$. The proof is by universal property: bilinear maps out of $V \times (W_1 \oplus W_2)$ correspond to *pairs* of bilinear maps out of $V \times W_1$ and $V \times W_2$.

**Calibration check.** If you have understood the definition, you should be able to: (i) verify $\dim(\mathbb{R}^3 \otimes \mathbb{R}^2) = 6$ and find a basis; (ii) verify the bilinear-form construction: $(v \otimes w)(\varphi, \tau) = \varphi(v)\tau(w)$ is bilinear in $(\varphi, \tau)$, hence is in $\mathcal{B}(V', W') = V \otimes W$; (iii) verify the bilinearity of $\otimes : V \times W \to V \otimes W$: $(v_1 + v_2) \otimes w = v_1 \otimes w + v_2 \otimes w$ as elements of $\mathcal{B}(V', W')$, by checking at every $(\varphi, \tau)$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Tensor Algebra and Exterior Algebra *(LADR §9D and beyond)*
> The infinite direct sum $T(V) = \bigoplus_{m \geq 0} V^{\otimes m}$ is the **tensor algebra**, with concatenation as multiplication. Quotienting by the antisymmetrisation relations gives the **exterior algebra** $\Lambda^* V$, the home of the wedge product and differential forms.

> [!tip] Tensors of Type (p, q) *(from Differential Geometry)*
> A tensor of type $(p, q)$ on $V$ is an element of $V^{\otimes p} \otimes (V^*)^{\otimes q}$, equivalently a multilinear map taking $q$ vectors and $p$ covectors to a scalar. The metric $g_{ij}$ is type $(0, 2)$; the Riemann curvature is type $(1, 3)$. The index notation $T^{i_1 \cdots i_p}_{j_1 \cdots j_q}$ that physicists use everywhere is just a coordinate expression of an element of this tensor space.

> [!tip] Hilbert Space Tensor Products *(from Quantum Mechanics)*
> The state space of a multi-particle quantum system is the **tensor product** of the single-particle Hilbert spaces. Two distinguishable spin-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ particles live in $\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^2 \cong \mathbb{C}^4$; an entangled state like $|\!\uparrow\downarrow\rangle - |\!\downarrow\uparrow\rangle$ is precisely a tensor that is *not* an elementary product $v \otimes w$. Entanglement is the failure of the state to be a simple tensor.

> [!tip] Tensor Product of Modules and Extension of Scalars *(from Algebra)*
> For a ring homomorphism $R \to S$ and an $R$-module $M$, the **extension of scalars** $S \otimes_R M$ is an $S$-module — a way to convert an $R$-module into an $S$-module canonically. This is foundational in representation theory and Galois theory: representations over a small field extend canonically to representations over a larger field.

> [!tip] Categorical Products in Monoidal Categories *(from Category Theory)*
> The tensor product gives the category $\mathbf{Vect}_\mathbb{F}$ the structure of a **symmetric monoidal category**, with $\mathbb{F}$ as the unit. This abstract structure is shared by many categories: $\mathbf{Set}$ with Cartesian product, the category of topological spaces with smash product, the category of chain complexes with the homological tensor product. Monoidal categories are the natural setting for "duality", "trace", and the algebraic structures arising in topological field theories.
