# Notation

Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ be twice continuously differentiable, with Jacobian $Df(x)$ and component Hessians $\nabla^2 f_i(x)$. The **Gauss–Newton iteration** is $x^{(k+1)} = G(x^{(k)})$ where
$$G(x) = x - (Df(x)^T Df(x))^{-1} Df(x)^T f(x),$$
defined wherever $Df(x)$ has full column rank. We use the operator norm $\|\cdot\|$ for matrices and the Euclidean norm $\|\cdot\|$ for vectors. The full registry is on [[Linear Algebra XII — Applied III — Nonlinear Least Squares]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Local convergence of Gauss–Newton).** Let $\hat x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a stationary point of $\|f(\cdot)\|^2$, that is, $Df(\hat x)^T f(\hat x) = 0$, and assume:
> 1. $Df(\hat x)$ has full column rank (so $Df(\hat x)^T Df(\hat x)$ is invertible);
> 2. $f$ is $C^2$ in a neighborhood of $\hat x$, with $\nabla^2 f_i$ Lipschitz continuous near $\hat x$.
>
> Then there exists $\delta > 0$ and a constant $\alpha$ such that, for any starting point $x^{(1)}$ with $\|x^{(1)} - \hat x\| < \delta$, the Gauss–Newton iterates $x^{(k+1)} = G(x^{(k)})$ are well-defined and satisfy
> $$\|x^{(k+1)} - \hat x\| \leq \alpha \, \big\| (Df(\hat x)^T Df(\hat x))^{-1} \textstyle\sum_i f_i(\hat x) \nabla^2 f_i(\hat x) \big\| \cdot \|x^{(k)} - \hat x\| \; + \; O(\|x^{(k)} - \hat x\|^2).$$
>
> **Corollary (zero-residual case).** If $f(\hat x) = 0$ in addition, then the linear coefficient vanishes and the convergence is **quadratic**:
> $$\|x^{(k+1)} - \hat x\| \leq C \, \|x^{(k)} - \hat x\|^2$$
> for some constant $C$ depending only on $Df(\hat x)$ and the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla^2 f$.
>
> **Corollary (divergence in the large-residual regime).** If the contraction factor
> $$\rho := \alpha \, \big\| (Df(\hat x)^T Df(\hat x))^{-1} \textstyle\sum_i f_i(\hat x) \nabla^2 f_i(\hat x) \big\|$$
> exceeds $1$, the iteration is *not* a contraction near $\hat x$, and Gauss–Newton may fail to converge even from arbitrarily close starting points.

---

# Motivation

The theorem precisely answers when and how fast Gauss–Newton converges. The answer is not "Gauss–Newton always works" — it does not — but rather "Gauss–Newton is a contraction with a specific factor, and the factor depends on how large the residuals are at the solution." For zero-residual problems the factor is zero (to leading order) and convergence is quadratic — just as for Newton's method on $f(x) = 0$. For large-residual problems the factor can be large, and Gauss–Newton may fail to converge.

This is the crucial *operational* fact about Gauss–Newton: its speed and reliability depend on the residual size at the minimum, not just on the smoothness of $f$. The theorem makes this dependence quantitative.

The theorem also explains *why* Levenberg–Marquardt is the recommended algorithm: by adding $\lambda I$ to the inner matrix, Levenberg–Marquardt reduces the "contraction factor" $\rho$ above (and, importantly, makes the algorithm a contraction even when the unmodified Gauss–Newton is not). The Levenberg–Marquardt analogue of this theorem is similar in spirit but with the contraction factor reduced by the regularization.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening).** The theorem applies whenever you can verify that you are near a stationary point of a sum of squares with a full-rank Jacobian. The disguised inputs:

The first disguised source is **a model-fitting problem with consistent data**. If you are fitting a model $\hat f(x; \theta) \approx y$ and the data are generated by the model with small noise (the model is "approximately correct"), then the residuals $f_i(\theta) = \hat f(x^{(i)}; \theta) - y^{(i)}$ are small at the true parameter, the problem is approximately zero-residual, and Gauss–Newton's quadratic convergence applies. The non-obvious step is recognizing that "the model approximately fits" implies "the problem is approximately zero-residual" — this gives quadratic convergence even when $f \neq 0$ exactly.

The second disguised source is **a system of nonlinear equations with $m = n$**. Here we are solving $f(x) = 0$ and "zero residual" is the *goal*; if a solution exists with $Df$ nonsingular, Gauss–Newton (= Newton's method) converges quadratically. The non-obviousness is that the convergence statement of Newton's method for root-finding is precisely the zero-residual specialization of this Gauss–Newton theorem.

The third disguised source is **an iterative algorithm that uses Gauss–Newton-like updates as a subroutine**. Sequential quadratic programming, the extended Kalman filter, and Newton-step damping in interior-point methods all involve Gauss–Newton-like local solves, and the theorem governs their inner-loop convergence rates. The non-obvious step is to recognize the LS-like structure in a problem that does not look like nonlinear LS — for example, the EKF's update step is *exactly* Gauss–Newton on a nonlinear measurement residual at each measurement step.

**Targets (Output Amplification).**

Combine the conclusion with **the algorithm's failure modes**. If Gauss–Newton diverges in practice, the theorem identifies two possible causes: (i) the starting point is outside the basin of attraction (start closer, or use Levenberg–Marquardt to enlarge the basin); (ii) the residual at the minimum is large enough that the contraction factor $\rho \geq 1$ (the problem is not really a "least squares with small residuals," and Gauss–Newton is fundamentally the wrong algorithm — use Newton with the full Hessian, or use Levenberg–Marquardt). The non-obvious combination is using the theorem *diagnostically*: a convergence failure narrows down the cause to one of these two, and the appropriate fix differs accordingly.

Combine the conclusion with **Lipschitz constants of the second derivatives**. The constant $C$ in the quadratic-convergence bound depends on the Lipschitz constant of $\nabla^2 f_i$ near $\hat x$. For practical problems this constant can be estimated from the spectrum of the Hessian over a small ball, giving *quantitative* convergence bounds rather than just rates. The non-obvious combination is using the bound to *predict iteration counts*: knowing $C$ and the initial error $\|x^{(1)} - \hat x\|$ tells you how many iterations are needed to reach a desired tolerance.

Combine the conclusion with **the linear-residual special case**. If $f$ is affine, the second derivatives $\nabla^2 f_i$ vanish identically, the linear coefficient is zero, and the higher-order term also vanishes (because the Gauss–Newton step is exact for affine $f$). Combined: Gauss–Newton converges in *one step* for any affine $f$. This is the trivial-but-important consistency check: the theorem subsumes the linear case as an extreme of the nonlinear convergence rate.

# Why Is It True

**The mechanism in one sentence: Gauss–Newton uses $Df^T Df$ as approximate Hessian, which equals the true Hessian only when residuals vanish; the convergence rate degrades smoothly with the magnitude of the missing curvature term.**

The Gauss–Newton iteration is a particular instance of Newton's method, applied to a *wrong* Hessian. Newton's method on a generic equation $F(x) = 0$ converges quadratically near a non-degenerate root, with the contraction factor governed by $DF(\hat x)$. Gauss–Newton is Newton's method on the gradient equation
$$F(x) := Df(x)^T f(x) = 0,$$
using the *approximate* Jacobian of $F$:
$$\widetilde{DF}(x) := Df(x)^T Df(x),$$
which omits the second-derivative term
$$\Sigma(x) := \sum_i f_i(x) \nabla^2 f_i(x).$$
The *true* Jacobian of $F$ is $DF(x) = Df^T Df + \Sigma$.

So Gauss–Newton is using $\widetilde{DF} = DF - \Sigma$ in place of $DF$. The error in this approximation is $\Sigma$, the curvature term weighted by the residuals. The convergence factor is the operator norm of the matrix $\widetilde{DF}^{-1} \Sigma$ — the relative error in inverting the wrong Hessian. When $f(\hat x) = 0$, $\Sigma(\hat x) = 0$, the error vanishes, the wrong Hessian is asymptotically right, and quadratic convergence is recovered. When $f(\hat x) \neq 0$, $\Sigma(\hat x) \neq 0$, the wrong Hessian remains wrong, and convergence is at best linear with rate $\|\widetilde{DF}^{-1} \Sigma\|$.

This explains every qualitative feature of the theorem. **Quadratic convergence at zero-residual minima**: the inversion error vanishes. **Linear convergence at large-residual minima**: the inversion error is constant. **Possible divergence**: if $\|\widetilde{DF}^{-1} \Sigma\| > 1$, Gauss–Newton is not even a contraction.

A more elementary view. Near $\hat x$, write $x^{(k)} = \hat x + e^{(k)}$. Expanding the Gauss–Newton iteration to leading order in $e^{(k)}$ gives
$$e^{(k+1)} = \widetilde{DF}(\hat x)^{-1} \Sigma(\hat x) e^{(k)} + O(\|e^{(k)}\|^2).$$
The leading coefficient is exactly $\widetilde{DF}^{-1} \Sigma$. The expansion is a calculation: differentiate the iteration $G(x)$ at $\hat x$, use the chain rule, and recognize that $f(\hat x)$ appears in the linear-in-$e^{(k)}$ term only through $\Sigma$.

# What Makes This Hard

The proof has two non-obvious steps. **First**, identifying the precise form of the contraction factor — recognizing that the leading error coefficient is $(Df^T Df)^{-1} \sum_i f_i \nabla^2 f_i$, not something simpler — requires careful Taylor expansion of the iteration map $G(x)$ at $\hat x$ and tracking which terms vanish at $f(\hat x) = 0$. **Second**, the zero-residual quadratic-convergence proof requires bounding the higher-order term using the Lipschitz continuity of $\nabla^2 f_i$, not just $C^2$ regularity; the standard reference proofs handle this with second-order Taylor remainder estimates. The common error is to assume zero-residual convergence is "automatic" without verifying the rank condition $Df(\hat x)^T Df(\hat x)$ invertible — the rank condition is a substantive hypothesis without which the iteration is undefined.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** Expand the Gauss–Newton iteration $G(x^{(k)})$ in Taylor series about $\hat x$, using the stationarity $Df(\hat x)^T f(\hat x) = 0$ to cancel the leading constant term. The linear coefficient in $e^{(k)} = x^{(k)} - \hat x$ is the contraction factor; when $f(\hat x) = 0$ it vanishes, giving quadratic convergence.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Iteration map is differentiable at $\hat x$.** Show $G(x) = x - (Df^T Df)^{-1} Df^T f$ is $C^1$ near $\hat x$ given $f \in C^2$ and $Df(\hat x)^T Df(\hat x)$ invertible.
   - *Hint:* The matrix $Df^T Df$ is a $C^1$ function of $x$; its inverse is $C^1$ wherever invertible; products of $C^1$ functions are $C^1$.
   - *Why needed:* To Taylor-expand $G$ at $\hat x$ we need it to be differentiable.

2. **$\hat x$ is a fixed point of $G$.** Show $G(\hat x) = \hat x$.
   - *Hint:* $G(\hat x) = \hat x - (Df(\hat x)^T Df(\hat x))^{-1} Df(\hat x)^T f(\hat x)$, and the second term is zero by the stationarity hypothesis.
   - *Why needed:* The expansion is around a fixed point, so the constant term in $G(x) - \hat x$ vanishes.

3. **Compute the Jacobian $DG(\hat x)$.** Differentiate $G$ and evaluate at $\hat x$.
   - *Hint:* Use the product rule on $-(Df^T Df)^{-1} Df^T f$. The derivative of $(Df^T Df)^{-1}$ contributes a term proportional to $Df^T f$, which vanishes at $\hat x$. The remaining terms simplify to $-(Df^T Df)^{-1} \big[ \sum_i \nabla^2 f_i \cdot f_i + Df^T Df \big]$, where the second derivatives come from differentiating $Df^T$, and the $\sum_i \nabla^2 f_i \cdot f_i$ term is $\Sigma$.
   - *Why needed:* The Jacobian $DG(\hat x)$ controls local convergence by the contraction-mapping principle.

4. **Simplify $DG(\hat x) = -(Df^T Df)^{-1} \Sigma$.** Combine the previous step's expression.
   - *Hint:* The "identity-cancellation" — the $+ Df^T Df$ inside the bracket cancels with the implicit $-I$ from the $x$ part of $G(x) = x - \cdots$, leaving $DG(\hat x) = -\widetilde{DF}^{-1} \Sigma$.
   - *Why needed:* This identifies the contraction factor with the missing-curvature operator.

5. **Apply the contraction-mapping theorem.** If $\|DG(\hat x)\| < 1$, then $G$ is locally a contraction near $\hat x$, and the iterates converge to $\hat x$.
   - *Hint:* Standard fixed-point argument; for any $\rho < 1$ with $\|DG(\hat x)\| < \rho$, there is $\delta > 0$ such that $\|x - \hat x\| < \delta$ implies $\|G(x) - \hat x\| \leq \rho \|x - \hat x\|$, giving linear convergence with factor $\rho$.
   - *Why needed:* This is the existence of convergence.

6. **Zero-residual case: improve to quadratic convergence.** If $f(\hat x) = 0$, then $\Sigma(\hat x) = 0$, $DG(\hat x) = 0$, and the linear term vanishes; the next-order term is quadratic.
   - *Hint:* When $DG(\hat x) = 0$, the expansion $G(x) - \hat x = DG(\hat x)(x - \hat x) + \tfrac12 D^2 G(\hat x)(x - \hat x, x - \hat x) + O(\|x - \hat x\|^3)$ has the quadratic term dominant. Bounding $D^2 G$ uses the Lipschitz continuity of $\nabla^2 f_i$.
   - *Why needed:* This is the zero-residual quadratic-convergence statement of the theorem.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: $\hat x$ is a fixed point of the Gauss–Newton map
> **Statement:** Under the rank condition $Df(\hat x)^T Df(\hat x)$ invertible and the stationarity $Df(\hat x)^T f(\hat x) = 0$, we have $G(\hat x) = \hat x$.
>
> **Hint:** Substitute $x = \hat x$ into the explicit formula for $G$ and use stationarity.
>
> **Why needed:** Allows Taylor expansion of $G$ at the fixed point $\hat x$; without this, the iteration would have a nonzero "constant term" and the contraction analysis would not apply.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > By definition, $G(\hat x) = \hat x - (Df(\hat x)^T Df(\hat x))^{-1} Df(\hat x)^T f(\hat x)$. By the stationarity hypothesis, $Df(\hat x)^T f(\hat x) = 0$, so the second term vanishes. Hence $G(\hat x) = \hat x$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The Jacobian $DG(\hat x) = -\widetilde{DF}^{-1} \Sigma$
> **Statement:** At the fixed point $\hat x$,
> $$DG(\hat x) = -\big(Df(\hat x)^T Df(\hat x)\big)^{-1} \sum_{i=1}^m f_i(\hat x) \nabla^2 f_i(\hat x).$$
>
> **Hint:** Differentiate $G(x) = x - (Df^T Df)^{-1} Df^T f$ using the product rule, then evaluate at $\hat x$ where the terms containing $Df^T f$ vanish.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the linear coefficient of the iteration; its norm is the contraction factor.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Write $H(x) = Df(x)^T Df(x)$ and $b(x) = Df(x)^T f(x)$, so $G(x) = x - H(x)^{-1} b(x)$. Then
> > $$DG(x) = I - DH(x)^{-1} b(x) - H(x)^{-1} Db(x),$$
> > where $DH^{-1}(x)$ denotes the derivative of the matrix-valued function $H^{-1}$, applied to $b(x)$ in a particular linear way. The first term simplifies: $DH^{-1}(x) b(x)$ is a linear operator in the perturbation direction. *At $x = \hat x$, $b(\hat x) = 0$*, so $DH^{-1}(\hat x) \cdot 0 = 0$ — this entire term drops.
> >
> > So $DG(\hat x) = I - H(\hat x)^{-1} Db(\hat x)$.
> >
> > Compute $Db(x) = D(Df^T f)(x)$. By the product rule applied component-wise, this is
> > $$Db(x) = (Df(x))^T Df(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m f_i(x) \nabla^2 f_i(x) = H(x) + \Sigma(x).$$
> > Substituting: $DG(\hat x) = I - H(\hat x)^{-1} (H(\hat x) + \Sigma(\hat x)) = I - I - H(\hat x)^{-1} \Sigma(\hat x) = -H(\hat x)^{-1} \Sigma(\hat x)$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Contraction-mapping convergence
> **Statement:** Let $\rho := \|DG(\hat x)\|$ in any operator norm. If $\rho < 1$, then for any $\rho' \in (\rho, 1)$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $\|x - \hat x\| < \delta$ implies $\|G(x) - \hat x\| \leq \rho' \|x - \hat x\|$. Hence the iterates converge to $\hat x$ at linear rate $\rho'$.
>
> **Hint:** Continuity of $DG$ at $\hat x$ gives $\|DG(x)\| < \rho'$ near $\hat x$; integrate to bound $\|G(x) - \hat x\|$ in terms of $\|x - \hat x\|$.
>
> **Why needed:** Converts the Jacobian formula of Lemma 2 into an actual convergence statement.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > By continuity of $DG$ (Lemma 1 and $C^2$ regularity), choose $\delta > 0$ small enough that $\|DG(y)\| < \rho'$ for all $\|y - \hat x\| < \delta$. For any $x$ with $\|x - \hat x\| < \delta$, by the mean value theorem in vector form,
> > $$G(x) - G(\hat x) = \int_0^1 DG(\hat x + t(x - \hat x)) (x - \hat x) \, dt,$$
> > so $\|G(x) - G(\hat x)\| \leq \rho' \|x - \hat x\|$. Since $G(\hat x) = \hat x$ by Lemma 1, this gives $\|G(x) - \hat x\| \leq \rho' \|x - \hat x\|$. Iterating, $\|x^{(k)} - \hat x\| \leq \rho'^{k-1} \|x^{(1)} - \hat x\| \to 0$.

> [!note]- Lemma 4: Zero-residual quadratic convergence
> **Statement:** If $f(\hat x) = 0$ (in addition to the stationarity and rank hypotheses), then there exists $\delta > 0$ and a constant $C$ such that $\|x^{(1)} - \hat x\| < \delta$ implies $\|x^{(k+1)} - \hat x\| \leq C \|x^{(k)} - \hat x\|^2$.
>
> **Hint:** When $f(\hat x) = 0$, $\Sigma(\hat x) = 0$, so $DG(\hat x) = 0$ by Lemma 2. Then $G(x) - \hat x$ has no linear term in $x - \hat x$; the leading term is quadratic, with coefficient bounded by the second derivative of $G$.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the central "fast-convergence" claim for zero-residual minima — and the foundation of every quadratic-convergence theorem for Newton's method.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > By Lemma 2, $f(\hat x) = 0$ implies $DG(\hat x) = 0$. Taylor expand $G$ to second order at $\hat x$:
> > $$G(x) - G(\hat x) = DG(\hat x)(x - \hat x) + \tfrac12 D^2 G(\xi)(x - \hat x, x - \hat x) = \tfrac12 D^2 G(\xi)(x - \hat x, x - \hat x),$$
> > for some $\xi$ on the line segment from $\hat x$ to $x$. The hypothesis that $\nabla^2 f_i$ is Lipschitz near $\hat x$ implies that $D^2 G$ is bounded near $\hat x$ (it is built out of $f$, $Df$, $\nabla^2 f$ and their products through smooth operations). Choose $\delta > 0$ small enough that $\|D^2 G(\xi)\| \leq 2C$ for all $\xi$ in the ball $\|\xi - \hat x\| < \delta$. Then
> > $$\|G(x) - \hat x\| = \|\tfrac12 D^2 G(\xi)(x - \hat x, x - \hat x)\| \leq C \|x - \hat x\|^2.$$
> > For $\|x^{(1)} - \hat x\| < \min(\delta, 1/(2C))$, the iteration contracts ($\|x^{(2)} - \hat x\| \leq C \|x^{(1)} - \hat x\|^2 < \|x^{(1)} - \hat x\|/2$), staying within the ball, and quadratic convergence follows by induction.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Assume the hypotheses: $\hat x$ is stationary ($Df(\hat x)^T f(\hat x) = 0$), $Df(\hat x)$ has full column rank, and $f$ is $C^2$ with Lipschitz $\nabla^2 f_i$ near $\hat x$.
>
> **Step 0 — Gauss–Newton map is defined near $\hat x$.** By continuity, $Df(x)^T Df(x)$ is invertible in a neighborhood of $\hat x$, so $G(x) = x - (Df^T Df)^{-1} Df^T f$ is well-defined and continuously differentiable there.
>
> **Step 1 — $\hat x$ is a fixed point** (Lemma 1). $G(\hat x) = \hat x$ by direct substitution and stationarity.
>
> **Step 2 — Compute $DG(\hat x)$** (Lemma 2). Differentiating $G$ at $\hat x$ and using that the $b(\hat x) = 0$ kills the term involving $DH^{-1}$,
> $$DG(\hat x) = -\big(Df(\hat x)^T Df(\hat x)\big)^{-1} \sum_{i} f_i(\hat x) \nabla^2 f_i(\hat x).$$
> Call this matrix $-M$ for brevity. Note that $\|M\|$ is *small* when $f(\hat x)$ is small (each term in the sum is weighted by $f_i(\hat x)$).
>
> **Step 3 — Linear convergence in the general case** (Lemma 3). If $\|DG(\hat x)\| = \|M\| < 1$, the iteration is a contraction near $\hat x$, with rate $\|M\| + o(1)$. So the iterates converge to $\hat x$ at linear rate determined by $\|M\|$.
>
> **Step 4 — Quadratic convergence in the zero-residual case** (Lemma 4). If $f(\hat x) = 0$, then $M = 0$, the linear term vanishes, and second-order Taylor expansion of $G$ gives
> $$\|G(x) - \hat x\| \leq C \|x - \hat x\|^2$$
> for some constant $C$ bounded by the supremum of $\|D^2 G\|$ near $\hat x$, which is finite by the $C^2$ + Lipschitz $\nabla^2 f$ hypothesis. Iterating, $\|x^{(k+1)} - \hat x\| \leq C \|x^{(k)} - \hat x\|^2$.
>
> **Step 5 — Possible divergence if $\|M\| \geq 1$.** When the residual at $\hat x$ is so large that $\|(Df^T Df)^{-1} \Sigma\| \geq 1$, the iteration is *not* a contraction near $\hat x$, and convergence is not guaranteed from any neighborhood. This is the structural failure mode of Gauss–Newton — and the reason Levenberg–Marquardt regularization is needed in general.
>
> Combining Steps 1–5 gives the theorem and its two corollaries. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Newton's method root-finding rate (numerical analysis).** Specialize this theorem to $m = n$ with the goal $f(x) = 0$. The "zero-residual" hypothesis becomes the goal of the problem, so quadratic convergence is the standard Newton's method convergence theorem. Verify by hand: starting from $x^{(1)}$ near $\hat x$ where $f(\hat x) = 0$ and $Df(\hat x)$ is invertible, the iteration $x^{(k+1)} = x^{(k)} - Df^{-1} f$ converges quadratically. This is the canonical numerical-analysis statement that nonlinear LS subsumes.

**Convergence of the Extended Kalman Filter (control / signal processing).** The EKF updates a state estimate $\hat x$ by, at each measurement, performing one step of Gauss–Newton on the nonlinear residual $h(\hat x) - y_\text{meas}$. The convergence of the EKF *across many measurement steps* is governed by the per-step Gauss–Newton contraction factor — directly the $\|M\|$ of this theorem with $m =$ measurement [[Def - Dimension|dimension]]. When measurements are accurate (small residuals) the EKF tracks the true state quadratically; when measurements are noisy the contraction is only linear and the filter accuracy degrades. This is a non-obvious application: a *filter* in control theory has its accuracy governed by a theorem about *optimization*.

**Iterative methods for nonlinear PDEs (numerical analysis).** Discretizing a nonlinear PDE on a mesh and solving the resulting algebraic system by Newton's method is a finite-dimensional Newton (= Gauss–Newton with $m = n$). The convergence rate is governed by this theorem at the discrete level, with the constant $C$ depending on the discretization step $h$ and the smoothness of the continuous problem. Practical PDE solvers (FEniCS, deal.II) implement damped Newton with line search to enlarge the basin of attraction when the bare theorem's $\delta$ is too small.

**Bundle adjustment in 3D reconstruction (computer vision).** Bundle adjustment minimizes reprojection errors over hundreds of thousands of camera/point parameters via Gauss–Newton (with Schur complement for sparsity). The residuals are nearly zero at the optimum (a well-calibrated system fits the images well), so the theorem predicts quadratic local convergence — and indeed practical bundle adjusters converge in ~10 iterations once near the optimum. The starting point usually comes from a closed-form algorithm (essential matrix, P3P), bringing the iterate into the basin of attraction.

# Bridges

- **Newton's method for root-finding** — the special case $m = n$ with the zero-residual hypothesis becomes the classical Newton's method convergence theorem. Quadratic convergence near a non-degenerate root, the basin of attraction problem, the requirement of a good starting point — all are inherited. The Gauss–Newton theorem is, structurally, "Newton's theorem for $f(x) = 0$" lifted to the overdetermined setting $m > n$.

- **Newton's method on the gradient $\nabla \phi = 0$** — for minimizing a scalar $\phi$, full Newton uses the true Hessian $\nabla^2 \phi$ as inner matrix. Gauss–Newton for $\phi = \|f\|^2$ uses the approximate Hessian $Df^T Df$ instead. The Gauss–Newton theorem is the precise quantification of the *error introduced by this approximation*: the convergence factor degrades by exactly the missing curvature term $\Sigma$.

- **Levenberg–Marquardt convergence analysis** — Levenberg–Marquardt replaces $Df^T Df$ by $Df^T Df + \lambda I$. The Levenberg–Marquardt analogue of this theorem has the same structure but with $\Sigma$ replaced by something involving $\lambda I$ in the denominator, *guaranteeing* a contraction even when bare Gauss–Newton fails. This is the formal way of saying "Levenberg–Marquardt enlarges the basin of attraction."

- **Stochastic gradient theory** — for objectives $\sum_i f_i^2$ with very large $i$, replacing Gauss–Newton by SGD changes the convergence theorem: deterministic *quadratic* convergence becomes *sublinear* (specifically $O(1/k)$ for strongly convex objectives, $O(1/\sqrt k)$ for nonconvex), as the price of stochasticity. The convergence analysis of SGD on least-squares problems is one of the most active areas of modern optimization theory and builds directly on the Gauss–Newton baseline this theorem provides.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Globalization Strategies *(from Numerical Optimization)*
> The theorem requires the starting point $x^{(1)}$ to be within distance $\delta$ of $\hat x$. **Globalization strategies** enlarge the basin of attraction so that Gauss–Newton-like methods converge from any reasonable starting point. The two main strategies are **line search** (take a step in the Gauss–Newton direction but shrink the step size to ensure objective decrease) and **trust region** (constrain the step to lie within a ball where the model is trustworthy — this is exactly what Levenberg–Marquardt does). The theory of globalization is built directly on local-convergence results like this one, with the global theorem replacing $\delta$ by "any starting point in a connected sub-level set."

> [!tip] Self-Concordant Analysis *(from Convex Optimization)*
> Nesterov and Nemirovski's theory of **self-concordant functions** is a refinement of Newton-method convergence analysis that bounds higher-order derivatives in terms of lower-order ones, yielding *quantitative* (not just asymptotic) bounds on the basin of attraction and the iteration count to a given accuracy. The Gauss–Newton theorem above can be sharpened for self-concordant LS objectives, giving the polynomial-time iteration bounds that drive modern interior point methods for convex programming.
