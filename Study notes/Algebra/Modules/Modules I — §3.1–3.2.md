# Notation Registry

- $R, S, T$ — rings; in this course always commutative with a multiplicative identity $1_R$
- $M, N, L$ — modules; always $R$-modules unless a different ring is named
- $(M, +, 0_M, \cdot)$ — the full data of an $R$-module: the underlying set, its addition, its zero, and the action $R \times M \to M$
- $0_M$, or just $0$ — the additive identity (zero element) of the module $M$
- $r \cdot m$, or just $rm$ — the action of the ring element $r \in R$ on the module element $m \in M$
- $+_R$ versus $+_M$ — addition in the ring versus addition in the module, written explicitly only when confusion threatens
- $N \leq M$ — $N$ is an $R$-submodule of $M$
- $M/N$ — the quotient module (the set of additive cosets $m + N$)
- $m + N$ — the coset of $m$ in $M/N$; the quotient map sends $m \mapsto m + N$
- $f : M \to N$ — an $R$-module homomorphism
- $\ker f$, $\operatorname{im} f$ — the kernel and image of $f$, both submodules
- $M \cong N$ — $M$ and $N$ are isomorphic $R$-modules
- $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$, $\operatorname{Ann}(S)$, $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$ — the annihilator of an element, a subset, the whole module: the ring elements that act as zero
- $Rm$ — the **cyclic submodule** generated by $m$, the set $\{r \cdot m : r \in R\}$
- $Rm_1 + \cdots + Rm_k$ — the submodule generated by $m_1, \dots, m_k$, the set $\{\sum r_i m_i : r_i \in R\}$
- $R^n$ or $R^k$ — the **free module of rank $n$**, the set of $n$-tuples $R \times \cdots \times R$ with coordinatewise operations
- $M_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus M_k$ — the **direct sum** of modules, the product set with coordinatewise addition and action
- $F$ — a field; $F[X]$ — the polynomial ring in one indeterminate over $F$
- $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ — the usual number systems, regarded as rings; $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — the integers modulo $n$

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: **a module is a vector space over a ring.** Recall how a vector space was built. You first fixed a field $F$ of scalars, then took an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]] $V$ and equipped it with a scalar multiplication $F \times V \to V$ compatible with the arithmetic of $F$ — distributivity each way, associativity of scalars, and the rule that $1$ acts trivially. Now read that definition again and notice something: **division in $F$ was never used.** Not in the axioms, not anywhere. The definition of a vector space never asks to divide by a scalar. So the definition still makes complete sense if $F$ is only a [[Def - Ring|ring]] rather than a field. The object you get is called a **module**, and this topic is the study of what happens when you make that one substitution.

The reason this is worth a whole topic — rather than a one-line remark — is that almost every *theorem* about vector spaces, unlike the definition, does secretly use division. The proof that every vector space has a basis, the proof that you can extend any linearly independent set to a basis or shrink any spanning set to one, the proof that dimension is well-defined: trace through each of them and you will find a moment where some scalar gets inverted. Strip away the field and those proofs collapse. So modules have a far richer and more unruly structure than vector spaces, and the single phenomenon responsible for all of it is the **possible non-existence of a basis**. Over a field every module is a vector space and therefore has a basis — it is "free". Over a general ring, most modules have no basis at all. The loss of bases is not a minor technicality; it *is* the new content of the subject.

A concrete failure makes this vivid. Consider $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ as a module over $\mathbb{Z}$ — and one should: a module over $\mathbb{Z}$ is *exactly* an abelian group, with the only possible action being repeated addition, $n \cdot a = a + \cdots + a$. The group $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has a generating set, namely $\{1\}$, but it cannot be a basis, because $1$ is not linearly independent: the relation $2 \cdot 1 = 0$ holds with a non-zero scalar $2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. And you cannot fix this by choosing a smaller generating set, because the empty set generates only $\{0\}$. So $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has *no* basis whatsoever. The obstruction is the element $1$ which is annihilated by a non-zero ring element — this is the phenomenon of **torsion**, and over a field it cannot happen, because you could divide the relation $2 \cdot 1 = 0$ by $2$. Torsion is what a field forbids and a ring permits, and torsion is why bases disappear.

Two further examples show that modules are not an exotic curiosity but a frame that *unifies things you already know*. A module over $\mathbb{Z}$ is an abelian group, as just noted — so all of abelian group theory is the special case $R = \mathbb{Z}$. And a module over the polynomial ring $F[X]$ turns out to be exactly a vector space $V$ over $F$ together with a chosen linear endomorphism $\alpha : V \to V$, where the indeterminate $X$ acts as $\alpha$: knowing how to multiply by $X$ is knowing one linear map, and the rest of the action is forced. This is not a coincidence to be memorized; it is the doorway to the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, the next topic, which simultaneously classifies finitely generated abelian groups and produces the Jordan and rational canonical forms of a matrix. Those two famous theorems are *one theorem* once you see them as statements about modules. Meanwhile the bookkeeping — submodules, quotient modules, the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|isomorphism theorems]], the submodule correspondence — carries over from rings and groups verbatim, with "ideal" or "normal subgroup" replaced by "submodule" and *no special closure condition required*. That part costs almost nothing to learn. The investment of this topic, §3.1–3.2, is entirely in understanding free generation, bases, and the universal property — the apparatus for measuring exactly how a module fails to be a vector space.

# Concept Map

## §3.1 Modules, Submodules and Quotients

- **[[Def - Module]]**
	- An $R$-**module** is a quadruple $(M, +, 0_M, \cdot)$ in which $(M, +, 0_M)$ is an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]] and the action $\cdot : R \times M \to M$ satisfies, for all $r, r_1, r_2 \in R$ and $m, m_1, m_2 \in M$, the four laws $(r_1 + r_2)m = r_1 m + r_2 m$, $r(m_1 + m_2) = rm_1 + rm_2$, $r_1(r_2 m) = (r_1 r_2) m$, and $1_R \cdot m = m$. These are *verbatim* the vector space axioms with the field of scalars relaxed to a [[Def - Ring|ring]]; an $F$-module for a field $F$ is precisely a vector space over $F$. A module over $\mathbb{Z}$ is exactly an abelian group, and a module over $F[X]$ is a vector space carrying a chosen endomorphism. The slogan is "rings act on abelian groups as groups act on sets".

- **[[Def - Submodule]]**
	- A subset $N \subseteq M$ is an $R$-**submodule**, written $N \leq M$, if it is a subgroup of $(M, +, 0_M)$ and is closed under the action: $n \in N$ and $r \in R$ imply $rn \in N$. Crucially there is **only one** kind of substructure here — unlike rings, which had subrings *and* the stronger notion of ideal, and unlike groups, which had subgroups *and* the stronger notion of normal subgroup. Two specializations pin down the meaning: a submodule of $R$ regarded as a module over itself is exactly an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]], and a submodule of a vector space is exactly a vector subspace. There is no extra closure axiom to remember.

- **[[Def - Quotient Module]]**
	- For a submodule $N \leq M$, the **quotient module** $M/N$ is the set of additive cosets $m + N$ with addition $(m_1 + N) + (m_2 + N) = (m_1 + m_2) + N$ and action $r \cdot (m + N) = (rm) + N$. Both operations are automatically well-defined: addition because $N$ is an additive subgroup, and the action because $N$ absorbs the ring — perturbing the representative by $n \in N$ changes $rm$ by $rn \in N$. The headline contrast with rings and groups: **you may quotient by an arbitrary submodule**, with no normality or ideal-style condition, because the single submodule axiom already provides everything well-definedness needs.

- **[[Def - Module Homomorphism]]**
	- An $R$-**module homomorphism** $f : M \to N$ is a homomorphism of the underlying abelian groups that also respects the action: $f(r \cdot m) = r \cdot f(m)$ for all $r \in R$, $m \in M$ (the action on the left is in $M$, on the right in $N$). An **isomorphism** is a bijective homomorphism, and its set-inverse is automatically a homomorphism. For a field $F$, an $F$-module homomorphism is exactly an $F$-linear map, so module homomorphisms are the honest generalization of linear maps. The kernel $\ker f = \{m : f(m) = 0_N\}$ and image $\operatorname{im} f$ are submodules, and $f$ is injective if and only if $\ker f = \{0_M\}$.

- **[[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules]]**
	- For any $R$-module homomorphism $f : M \to N$, the kernel and image are submodules and $M/\ker f \cong \operatorname{im} f$ (first); for submodules $A, B \leq M$, $A + B$ and $A \cap B$ are submodules and $(A + B)/A \cong B/(A \cap B)$ (second); for $N \leq L \leq M$, $(M/N)/(L/N) \cong M/L$ (third); and there is an inclusion-preserving bijection between submodules of $M/N$ and submodules of $M$ containing $N$ (correspondence). These are the *same theorems proved a third time*, after groups and rings, with "ideal"/"normal subgroup" replaced by "submodule" — and the proofs are word-for-word identical, because the additive and bijective content was always group theory and only the action needs a one-line check.

- **[[Def - Annihilator]]**
	- The **annihilator** of an element $m \in M$ is $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = \{r \in R : r \cdot m = 0\}$; of a subset $S$, the intersection $\operatorname{Ann}(S) = \bigcap_{m \in S} \operatorname{Ann}(m)$; of the whole module, $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$. Each annihilator is an **ideal** of $R$: if $r$ and $s$ kill $m$ then so does $r + s$, and if $r$ kills $m$ then so does $sr$ for any $s$. This concept has *no analogue in rings or groups* — it measures torsion, the failure of elements to be free. For the cyclic submodule one has $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$, so the annihilator records exactly which scalars are "wasted" on $m$.

- **[[Def - Finitely Generated Module]]**
	- An $R$-module $M$ is **finitely generated** if there is a finite list $m_1, \dots, m_k$ with $M = Rm_1 + \cdots + Rm_k = \{\sum r_i m_i : r_i \in R\}$, the analogue of a vector space being finite-dimensional. But it behaves differently: a submodule of a finitely generated module **need not** be finitely generated — in $R = \mathbb{C}[X_1, X_2, \dots]$ the module $R$ is generated by $1$, yet its submodule (ideal) $(X_1, X_2, \dots)$ is not finitely generated. Quotients are better behaved: any quotient of a finitely generated module is finitely generated, since a surjection composed with the quotient map stays surjective.

- **[[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module]]**
	- An $R$-module $M$ is finitely generated **if and only if** there is a surjective $R$-module homomorphism $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ for some finite $k$. Given generators $m_1, \dots, m_k$ one defines $f(r_1, \dots, r_k) = \sum r_i m_i$; conversely the images $m_i = f(e_i)$ of the standard tuples generate $M$. This is the *preferred* characterization of finite generation because it is structural rather than element-by-element: it converts the property into the existence of one map from a known free module, immediately yielding for example that quotients of finitely generated modules are finitely generated.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Noetherian Modules and Rings *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> A module is **Noetherian** when *every* submodule is finitely generated — the condition that repairs the defect above, that a submodule of a finitely generated module can fail to be finitely generated. A ring is Noetherian when it is Noetherian as a module over itself, equivalently when every ideal is finitely generated; Hilbert's basis theorem says $R[X]$ inherits this from $R$. Noetherian modules are the natural setting in which finite-generation arguments run without obstruction. See [[Rings III — §2.5–2.6]].

## §3.2 Direct Sums and Free Modules

- **[[Def - Direct Sum of Modules]]**
	- For $R$-modules $M_1, \dots, M_k$, the **direct sum** $M_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus M_k$ is the product set $M_1 \times \cdots \times M_k$ with coordinatewise addition $(m_i) + (m_i') = (m_i + m_i')$ and coordinatewise action $r \cdot (m_i) = (r m_i)$. It is the module-theoretic way to *build* larger modules from smaller ones, and it is the construction underlying the free module: the rank-$n$ free module is $R^n = R \oplus \cdots \oplus R$, the $n$-fold direct sum of the ring with itself. For finitely many summands the direct sum coincides with the direct product; the distinction between sum and product matters only for infinite families.

- **[[Def - Free Module]]**
	- A subset $S \subseteq M$ **generates $M$ freely** if $S$ generates $M$ and every set function $\psi : S \to N$ into any $R$-module $N$ extends to an $R$-module homomorphism $M \to N$ (necessarily unique); $M$ is a **free module** if it is freely generated by some subset $S$, and such an $S$ is a **basis**. This is the precise sense in which a free module is "a vector space over a ring" — it has a basis. Over a field *every* module is free; over a general ring **non-free modules exist**, the prototype being $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, which has no basis because its generator $1$ satisfies $2 \cdot 1 = 0$.

- **[[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation]]**
	- For a finite subset $S = \{m_1, \dots, m_k\} \subseteq M$, three conditions are equivalent: (i) $S$ generates $M$ freely; (ii) $S$ generates $M$ and $S$ is linearly independent (meaning $\sum r_i m_i = 0$ forces every $r_i = 0$); (iii) every element of $M$ is *uniquely* expressible as $\sum r_i m_i$. So a basis is exactly an independent generating set, exactly a set giving unique coordinates, exactly the data needed to define maps freely — and a free module is one with such a set. The equivalence of (ii) and (iii) is inherited from linear algebra; the new content is that free generation (i), defined by a mapping property, is the *same* thing.

- **[[Def - Finitely Presented Module]]**
	- For a finitely generated $M$ with chosen surjection $\varphi : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$, the submodule $\ker \varphi \leq R^k$ is the **relation module** of those generators; $M$ is **finitely presented** if some such $\ker \varphi$ is itself finitely generated. Concretely $M$ is then specified by finitely many generators $m_1, \dots, m_k$ together with finitely many relations $\sum r_{ij} m_j = 0$, so the entire module can be written down "with finitely much paper". Finite presentation is strictly stronger than finite generation, the two coinciding over a Noetherian ring, where every submodule of $R^k$ is automatically finitely generated.

- **[[Thm - Invariance of Rank]]**
	- For a non-zero ring $R$, if $R^n \cong R^m$ as $R$-modules then $n = m$ — so the rank of a free module is a genuine, well-defined invariant, exactly as dimension is for vector spaces. The proof reduces the ring case to the field case: choose a maximal ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$ (one exists by Zorn's lemma), and the construction $M \mapsto M/IM$ turns the isomorphism $R^n \cong R^m$ into an isomorphism $(R/I)^n \cong (R/I)^m$ of vector spaces over the field $R/I$, where invariance of dimension is already known. This legitimizes speaking of *the* rank.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Structure Theorem for Modules over a PID *(from Module Theory)*
> Once free modules, ranks, and relations are in hand, the central theorem of the subject becomes statable: every finitely generated module over a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] is a direct sum of a free module $R^r$ and finitely many cyclic torsion modules $R/(d_i)$. Applied to $R = \mathbb{Z}$ it classifies all finitely generated abelian groups; applied to $R = F[X]$, via the $F[X]$-module of a linear map, it produces the Jordan and rational canonical forms. See [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]].

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of an introductory module theory section pursue a recognizable handful of goals, and they sort neatly by the field-versus-ring distinction. The most characteristic target is **deciding whether a module is free, and if so producing a basis or the rank** — equivalently, detecting torsion, the obstruction to freeness. Because over a field every module is free, this target is *vacuous* for vector spaces and is therefore the genuinely new question of the subject. A close relative is **identifying a module up to isomorphism**, typically recognizing a concretely built module as a familiar one: a quotient $R^k/N$, a cyclic module $R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$, a direct sum $R^r \oplus (\text{torsion})$, or — when $R = \mathbb{Z}$ — a known abelian group. A third target is **computing an annihilator**, which is asking precisely how much torsion an element or a module carries. A fourth is **deciding whether a module is finitely generated, or finitely presented**, and locating the gap between the two. A fifth is the routine but essential **verifying that a subset is a submodule**, or that a map is a homomorphism — a single closure check, made easy here by the absence of any ideal-style strong-closure subtlety. These targets recur because they exhaust the ways a module can differ from a vector space: a module is understood once you know whether it is free, what its torsion (annihilators) is, and how it decomposes as a sum of cyclic pieces.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The assumptions are correspondingly stereotyped, and each switches on a specific tool. **The ground ring is a field** is the most powerful hypothesis available: it instantly makes every module free, restores all of linear algebra, and is the assumption to *look for* or to *manufacture* — the proof of [[Thm - Invariance of Rank|invariance of rank]] is the model, manufacturing a field by quotienting $R$ by a maximal ideal. **The ground ring is a principal ideal domain** ($\mathbb{Z}$ or $F[X]$, in practice) is the weaker hypothesis under which the structure theorem of the next topic holds. **A module is given by generators**, finitely many, so that the [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|surjection-from-a-free-module]] characterization applies and the module appears as $R^k/N$. **A module is presented by generators and relations**, so $N = \ker(R^k \to M)$ is itself given by generators and every structural question becomes linear algebra over $R$. **A module homomorphism is given or can be built**, which — exactly as for rings — immediately hands over a submodule $\ker f$ and a quotient $\operatorname{im} f$, with the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] standing ready. **An element has a non-trivial annihilator**, i.e. there is torsion, which is the certificate that the module is *not* free. The recurring discipline is to route a source to a target: a field ground ring routes through "every module is free" to a basis; generators route through a free-module surjection to a presentation; a non-trivial annihilator routes directly to non-freeness; a presentation routes through the relation module to an isomorphism identification. The [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything here is self-contained: a reader with no module theory background should be able to follow each operation from the description alone.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Transport intuition and theorems from vector spaces — but only the division-free ones.** A [[Def - Module]] is the vector space definition with the field relaxed to a ring, so every fact whose proof never inverts a scalar carries over unchanged: submodules, quotient modules, homomorphisms, the isomorphism theorems, linear combinations, the notion of a generating set. The trigger is any "show this basic structural fact" request — first ask whether the analogous vector space fact is true *and proved without division*; if so, the same proof works. The discipline is the second half of the sentence: facts that *do* use division (existence of a basis, every spanning set contains a basis) must **not** be transported, and forgetting this is the chief error of the subject.

2. **Build a surjection from a free module $R^k$.** A module $M$ is [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] precisely when there is a surjection $R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$, by [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|the surjection characterization]]: send the standard tuple $e_i$ to the $i$-th generator $m_i$ and extend linearly. The trigger is *any* appearance of a finitely generated module — this is the standard first move, because it converts an awkward element-by-element property into a single map out of a fully understood module, and the free module $R^k$ is the most transparent module there is. From the surjection you immediately get $M \cong R^k/\ker$, a presentation of $M$.

3. **Apply the first isomorphism theorem to identify a module.** If you can write a surjective homomorphism $f : M \twoheadrightarrow T$, then $M/\ker f \cong T$; run with $M = R^k$ and you have identified an arbitrary finitely generated module as $R^k$ modulo an explicit submodule. This is the verbatim analogue of the ring-theory move, and the trigger is the same: the problem asks "what is this module" or "show $M \cong \ldots$". See [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules]]. The art, again, is guessing the target $T$ and the map — usually read off the generators and relations.

4. **Quotient by any submodule you like.** Forming $M/N$ requires *nothing* of $N$ beyond its being a submodule — no normality, no ideal-style strong closure — because the single submodule axiom already makes both the addition and the action well-defined on cosets. See [[Def - Quotient Module]]. The trigger is wanting to *simplify* a module by collapsing a chosen part, or wanting to *impose a relation* (set the elements of $N$ to zero); the freedom here is greater than in groups or rings, and one should exploit it without the hesitation those subjects trained in.

5. **Compute the annihilator to detect torsion and identify cyclic modules.** For $m \in M$, the [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]] $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = \{r : rm = 0\}$ is an ideal of $R$, and the cyclic submodule satisfies $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ by the first isomorphism theorem applied to $r \mapsto rm$. The trigger is any question about whether a module or element is free (a non-zero annihilator forbids freeness) or any request to identify a cyclic module: compute $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ and read off $Rm$ as a quotient ring. This is the module-theoretic counterpart of the orbit–stabilizer theorem, with $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ playing the stabilizer.

6. **Test for a basis via the three equivalent characterizations.** By [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|the characterizations of free generation]], a generating set $S = \{m_1, \dots, m_k\}$ is a basis if and only if it is linearly independent, if and only if every element has *unique* coordinates, if and only if it generates freely (the universal mapping property). The trigger is "is $S$ a basis" or "is $M$ free": pick whichever of the three forms is easiest to check — independence is usually a direct computation, the universal property is best when you must *construct* a map. Use linear independence over a ring with care: $\sum r_i m_i = 0$ must force every $r_i = 0$, and over a ring you may *not* divide to clear coefficients.

7. **Define a homomorphism out of a free module by choosing images of the basis.** This is the operational payoff of free generation: to specify an $R$-module homomorphism $R^k \to N$, it suffices — and is necessary — to name the image of each basis element; any function on the basis extends uniquely. See [[Def - Free Module]]. The trigger is "construct a homomorphism with such-and-such properties": if the source is free, the construction reduces to a finite list of arbitrary choices in $N$. This is exactly how the surjection in operation 2 is built, and it is why free modules are the modules you map *out of* with ease.

8. **Pass to a quotient by a maximal ideal to manufacture a vector space.** If $R$ is not a field, choose a maximal ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$ (one exists by Zorn's lemma) so that $R/I$ *is* a field; for any module $M$, the construction $M \mapsto M/IM$ produces an $R/I$-module, i.e. a vector space, and it is functorial enough to carry isomorphisms of $R$-modules to isomorphisms of $R/I$-vector spaces. The trigger is any statement you know for vector spaces and want over a general ring — this is precisely the engine of [[Thm - Invariance of Rank|invariance of rank]]. The move trades a hard ring problem for a known field problem.

9. **Split a module as a direct sum.** Building $M_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus M_k$ from given modules, or *recognizing* a given module as such a sum, is the basic assembly and disassembly operation; see [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules]]. The trigger is a module that visibly decomposes into independent parts, or the goal of reducing a structural question to its summands — most cleanly, $R^k$ is the direct sum of $k$ copies of $R$, which is why questions about free modules reduce to questions about $R$ itself. The full power of this operation is realized in the structure theorem of the next topic, which states that *every* finitely generated module over a PID is such a sum.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming every module has a basis (is free)
> Over a field, every vector space has a basis, and after years of linear algebra this feels like a law of nature. It is not — it is a fact *about fields*, and its proof inverts scalars. Over a general ring, modules with no basis are everywhere. The cleanest counterexample is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-[[Def - Module|module]]: its only possible generating set is $\{1\}$, which fails to be a basis because $2 \cdot 1 = 0$ is a non-trivial relation, and no other set generates. So $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ has *no* basis at all. The additional condition that restores a basis is exactly "$R$ is a field", or for the module itself "no torsion *and* projective". The non-existence of bases is the entire new content of module theory — see [[Def - Free Module]] — so assuming freeness assumes the subject away.

> [!warning] 2. Shrinking a spanning set to a basis by discarding "redundant" elements
> In a vector space, if a spanning set is not independent you simply throw away the dependent vectors until what remains is a basis. The instinct to do the same in a module is strong and wrong. Consider $\{2, 3\}$ as a generating set of the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}$: it does generate, since $3 \cdot 2 + (-2) \cdot 3$ and integer combinations reach $1$, and it is *not* independent, since $3 \cdot 2 + (-2) \cdot 3 = 0$. The vector space reflex says discard one element. But neither $\{2\}$ nor $\{3\}$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$ — each generates only the proper submodule of its multiples. So a dependent generating set need not contain any basis, and "redundant" is a field-theoretic illusion. See [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation]]. There is no condition short of working over a field that makes this move legal in general.

> [!warning] 3. Cancelling or dividing scalars in a linear relation
> Faced with $r m_1 = r m_2$, or with $\sum r_i m_i = 0$ and a non-zero coefficient $r_1$, the trained instinct is to cancel $r$ or to divide by $r_1$. In a ring there is no division, and worse, cancellation can fail outright in the presence of zero-divisors: in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ one has $2 \cdot 3 = 0 = 2 \cdot 0$ with $2 \neq 0$, yet $3 \neq 0$. So a non-zero scalar coefficient in a relation may *not* be removed, and a non-zero scalar annihilating a non-zero element is precisely [[Def - Annihilator|torsion]] — a feature, not a glitch. The conditions that license cancellation are "$R$ is an integral domain" (cancellation of non-zero-divisors) or "$R$ is a field" (genuine division); absent those, every relation must be handled as-is.

> [!warning] 4. Believing a submodule of a finitely generated module is finitely generated
> Finite generation feels hereditary — surely a "part" of a finitely generated module inherits the property. For *quotients* this is true, but for *submodules* it is false, which is jarring because the vector space analogue (a subspace of a finite-dimensional space is finite-dimensional) holds without exception. The counterexample: let $R = \mathbb{C}[X_1, X_2, \dots]$ and view $R$ as a module over itself, [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] by the single element $1$. Its submodule $(X_1, X_2, \dots)$ — an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]], hence a submodule — needs infinitely many generators. The condition that repairs this is that $R$ be a **Noetherian** ring, under which every submodule of a finitely generated module is again finitely generated; the polynomial ring in finitely many variables qualifies, the one in infinitely many does not.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic are won the instant you classify what you are being asked, and the master classification is a single question asked first of every exercise: **does the ground ring's being a field do the work, or is the difficulty exactly that it is not a field?** Because a module is a vector space over a ring, every problem is either a piece of linear algebra in disguise — solvable by transporting a division-free vector space argument — or a problem about the failure of linear algebra, which is the genuine subject. Sorting the exercise into one of these two camps is more than half the battle.

If the problem **lives over a field** $F$ — the modules are $F$-modules, or you have manufactured a field — then it *is* linear algebra. Every module is free, every generating set contains a basis, dimension is well-defined and the only invariant, every short exact sequence splits. The strategy is to forget the word "module", recall the vector space theorem, and apply it. The one subtlety is recognizing when you are *secretly* over a field: the proof of [[Thm - Invariance of Rank|invariance of rank]] is the template, where the ring $R$ is not a field but quotienting by a maximal ideal $I$ produces the field $R/I$ and the construction $M \mapsto M/IM$ transports the whole problem there. Whenever a problem over a general ring resists, ask whether quotienting by a maximal (or merely prime) ideal converts it into a vector space statement you already know.

If the problem **asks whether a module is free, or to find a basis or the rank**, you are looking at the central new question of the subject, and the decisive sub-question is: *is there torsion?* A module with a non-zero element $m$ killed by a non-zero scalar — non-trivial [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]] — cannot be free, because in a free module the basis elements, and hence (by [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|the characterizations]]) all coordinates, are linearly independent and admit no such relation. So the first move is to hunt for torsion: the prototype is $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, non-free because $2 \cdot 1 = 0$. If you suspect the module *is* free, exhibit a basis and verify it through whichever of the three equivalent characterizations is cheapest — independence by direct computation, or the universal property if you must build maps. Do not attempt to shrink a spanning set to a basis: over a ring that move is invalid, as $\{2, 3\}$ generating $\mathbb{Z}$ shows.

If the problem **hands you a finitely generated module**, the reflex is immediate and almost mechanical: build a surjection $R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ from a free module, by [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|the surjection characterization]], sending standard tuples to generators. This converts the module into the form $R^k/N$ and routes you straight to the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] for any identification question. If in addition the *relations* are given — the module is finitely *presented* — then $N$ too has explicit generators, and every structural question about $M$ has become a question of linear algebra over $R$: about the matrix of relations. This is the setup in which the structure theorem of the next topic operates, and recognizing that an unfamiliar module is secretly a presentation $R^k/N$ is frequently the move that unlocks the problem.

If the problem **asks to identify a cyclic module** — one generated by a single element $m$ — the route is a one-liner: $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$, the first isomorphism theorem applied to the homomorphism $r \mapsto rm$ from $R$ onto $Rm$. Compute the annihilator, an ideal of $R$, and you have named the module as a quotient ring. This is the module analogue of the orbit–stabilizer theorem, the annihilator playing the role of the stabilizer, and it is the atom from which the structure theorem's classification of all finitely generated PID-modules into cyclic pieces is built.

Finally, a meta-strategy threads through all of the above. **A module problem is a question about the gap between $M$ and a free module, and the isomorphism theorems plus annihilators are the instruments that measure the gap.** Every finitely generated module sits in a surjection $R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ from a free module; the kernel — the relation module — is the precise record of how $M$ falls short of being free, the annihilators are the precise record of its torsion, and the first isomorphism theorem is the bridge that turns those records into an identification. The reason this viewpoint always repays adoption is that it converts every structural question about an abstract module into a concrete question about a free module $R^k$ and an explicit submodule of relations — and concrete questions about $R^k$ are the ones linear algebra over $R$ can actually answer.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|First Isomorphism Theorem]]**: $M/\ker f \cong \operatorname{im} f$. This is the workhorse for *identification*, exactly as in ring and group theory, and the effort spent internalizing it earlier pays off here for free, since the proof is word-for-word the same. The recognizable setup is "what module is this" or "show $M \cong N$": build a surjective homomorphism whose kernel is the submodule in question and read off the quotient. Its single most reused special case is $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ for cyclic modules — apply the theorem to $r \mapsto rm$ — which converts every cyclic module into a quotient ring and is the seed of the structure theorem. Recognize its applicability whenever a module is presented as $R^k/N$, since that presentation *is* the conclusion of the theorem applied to a free-module surjection.

- **[[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|Surjection from a free module]]**: $M$ is finitely generated if and only if there is a surjection $R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$. The reusable move is the equivalence itself: to *use* finite generation, replace the element-by-element definition with the single structural map out of a free module — the most transparent module available — and to *establish* finite generation, exhibit such a surjection. It is the standard first step on encountering any finitely generated module, because it produces the presentation $M \cong R^k/N$ on which every later argument runs, and it makes hereditary properties (like "quotients of finitely generated modules are finitely generated") fall out by composing surjections. Reach for it the moment the words "finitely generated" appear.

- **[[Def - Annihilator|The annihilator]]** $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$: the ideal of scalars killing $m$, with the companion fact $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. Its reusability is as the *torsion meter* of the subject. As a detector of non-freeness it is decisive — a non-zero annihilator on a non-zero element rules out freeness instantly, which settles a large class of "is this module free" problems with a single computation. As an identifier it converts any cyclic module into an explicit quotient ring. Whenever a problem concerns whether a module or element is free, or asks to recognize a cyclic module, the annihilator is the first thing to compute; it has no analogue in groups or rings, so it is the genuinely module-specific tool to keep sharp.

- **[[Def - Free Module|Free modules and the universal property]]**: a free module has a basis, and a homomorphism out of it is determined by — and may be defined by — arbitrary choices of the images of the basis. The reusable consequences are twofold. Free modules are the modules you *map out of* effortlessly: any required homomorphism $R^k \to N$ is just a finite list of elements of $N$, which is how every free-module surjection is constructed. And free modules are the *reference objects* against which all modules are measured — every finitely generated module is a quotient of one, and the structure theorem expresses every PID-module as a free part plus torsion. Recognize that "$M$ is free" is the hypothesis that restores all of linear algebra, and that constructing maps becomes trivial the moment the source is free.

- **[[Thm - Invariance of Rank|Invariance of rank]]**: $R^n \cong R^m$ forces $n = m$ over any non-zero ring, so rank is a well-defined invariant. It is reused as a *rigidity* statement, the module analogue of "dimension is well-defined": it guarantees that the rank appearing in the structure theorem is unambiguous, that two free modules of different ranks are never isomorphic, and that counting basis elements is a legitimate invariant rather than an artifact of a choice. Its proof is itself a reusable template — the trick of pushing a ring-level statement down to a field-level statement by quotienting by a maximal ideal and forming $M/IM$ is a move worth keeping, applicable far beyond this one theorem whenever a known vector space fact must be upgraded to a general ring.

# Bridges

1. **Linear algebra — modules are vector spaces with the field weakened to a ring, and $F[X]$-modules are linear maps.** This is not an analogy but the literal definitional relationship: the [[Def - Module]] axioms are the vector space axioms verbatim, and an $F$-module for a field $F$ *is* a vector space over $F$, with module homomorphisms being exactly $F$-linear maps and submodules exactly subspaces. The whole of linear algebra is the special case "$R$ is a field" of module theory. The deeper bridge runs the other way: a module over the polynomial ring $F[X]$ is precisely a finite-dimensional $F$-vector space $V$ equipped with a chosen linear endomorphism $\alpha$, the indeterminate $X$ acting as $\alpha$ and the action $(f, v) \mapsto f(\alpha)(v)$ being forced by the module axioms. Different choices of $\alpha$ give different $F[X]$-module structures on the same $V$. This is the bridge by which the structure theorem of [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]] — finitely generated modules over a PID — yields the Jordan and rational canonical forms: classifying $F[X]$-modules up to isomorphism is classifying matrices up to similarity.

2. **Group theory and ring theory — modules generalize abelian groups, and ideals are submodules.** A module over $\mathbb{Z}$ is exactly an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]]: the module axioms force the only possible action, repeated addition $n \cdot a = a + \cdots + a$, so abelian group theory is the special case $R = \mathbb{Z}$ of module theory, and the structure theorem applied to $R = \mathbb{Z}$ classifies all finitely generated abelian groups. The link to rings is equally tight — an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] of $R$ is *precisely* a submodule of $R$ regarded as a module over itself, so the ideal theory of [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2]] is a fragment of module theory. And the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|isomorphism theorems]] of this topic are the *same theorems* proved in group theory and again in ring theory, with the substructure ("normal subgroup", "ideal") now simply "submodule" — the proofs are word-for-word identical because the additive and bijective content was always abelian group theory.

3. **Homological algebra — free modules are the first projectives, and presentations are the start of resolutions.** A finitely generated module written as $R^k/N$ is the beginning of a process homological algebra makes systematic: resolving $N$ in turn by another free module yields an exact sequence $\cdots \to R^{k_1} \to R^{k_0} \to M \to 0$, a **free resolution**, and the entire derived-functor machinery (Tor, Ext) is built from such resolutions. Free modules are the simplest **projective** modules — modules over which every surjection onto them splits — and projectivity is exactly the property of "behaving like a free module for the purpose of lifting maps". The non-free modules whose existence is the new content of this topic are what *force* homological algebra to exist: if every module were free, every short exact sequence would split and there would be nothing to derive. The relation module $\ker(R^k \to M)$ defined here is literally the first syzygy of homological algebra.

4. **Representation theory — a representation of a group is a module over its group ring.** Given a finite group $G$ and a field $F$, the **group ring** $F[G]$ has the elements of $G$ as a basis and multiplication extending that of $G$; an $F[G]$-module is then exactly a vector space $V$ together with a linear action of $G$ — that is, a **representation** of $G$. Submodules are subrepresentations, module homomorphisms are intertwiners, irreducible representations are simple modules, and Maschke's theorem becomes the statement that $F[G]$-modules are all direct sums of simple ones when $|G|$ is invertible in $F$. So the representation theory of finite groups is the module theory of group rings, and the direct-sum and free-module language of this topic is the language in which that theory is written.

5. **Commutative algebra and algebraic geometry — modules are the vector bundles and coherent sheaves of geometry.** Under the dictionary that studies a space through its ring of functions, a [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^n$ corresponds to a *trivial* vector bundle of rank $n$, and a finitely generated **projective** module — locally free, though not globally free — corresponds to a genuine, possibly twisted, vector bundle (the Serre–Swan theorem makes this an equivalence for nice spaces). General finitely generated modules correspond to **coherent sheaves**, the basic objects of modern algebraic geometry, which allow for the singular behaviour that torsion encodes. The failure of freeness — the central phenomenon of this topic — is geometrically the failure of a bundle to be trivial, and the rank of [[Thm - Invariance of Rank|invariance of rank]] is the rank of the corresponding bundle.

# Insights

**A module is a vector space over a ring, and the loss of bases is the entire new content.** This is the single frame that organizes the whole subject, and it is worth stating with precision rather than as a slogan. The *definition* of a vector space — abelian group plus a compatible scalar action — never mentions division, so it transplants without change to a ring of scalars; that transplant is the definition of a module. What does not transplant is the *theory*, because nearly every theorem of linear algebra has a proof that, somewhere, inverts a scalar. The proof that a basis exists, that any independent set extends to a basis, that any spanning set contains one, that dimension is well-defined, that every subspace has a complement — open each of these and you find division being used. Remove the field and exactly these theorems fail, and they fail *together*, because they are all aspects of one fact: over a field every module has a basis. Over a ring, most do not. So when you study modules you are studying precisely the residue of linear algebra after bases are taken away — and the surprise, exactly as in ring theory after division was taken away, is how much remains. The bookkeeping layer (submodules, quotients, homomorphisms, the isomorphism theorems) survives untouched, because it never used division in the first place. All the genuine difficulty, and all the genuine novelty, is concentrated in the one phenomenon: non-free modules.

**Torsion is the name of the obstruction, and the annihilator is how you measure it.** It is worth being able to say *exactly* what goes wrong when a module fails to be free, and the answer is torsion: a non-zero element $m$ for which some non-zero scalar $r$ satisfies $rm = 0$. In a free module this is impossible — a basis element, and by the unique-coordinates characterization every element, admits no such non-trivial relation — so any torsion at all rules out freeness. Over a field, torsion cannot occur, because the relation $rm = 0$ with $r \neq 0$ could be divided by $r$ to give $m = 0$; this is the precise sense in which "field" and "free" are linked. The [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]] $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ collects all the scalars that kill $m$ into an ideal of $R$, and so it is a quantitative torsion meter: $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = \{0\}$ says $m$ is torsion-free, a larger annihilator says $m$ is "more torsion". The companion isomorphism $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ then says that a single torsion element is, as a module, nothing but a quotient of the ring — and this is the atom out of which the structure theorem of the next topic builds *every* finitely generated module over a PID, as a free part $R^r$ plus a finite sum of cyclic torsion pieces $R/(d_i)$. The annihilator has no counterpart in group or ring theory; it exists because modules, and only modules, can fail to be free, and it is the instrument purpose-built to detect and grade that failure.

**Modules quotient more freely than groups or rings, because all the special-case anxiety has been absorbed into the action axioms.** There is a pattern across the three algebraic structures studied in this course, and seeing it removes a recurring source of confusion. In group theory you may not quotient by an arbitrary subgroup — only by a *normal* one — and the normality condition is a genuine extra hypothesis that must be checked. In ring theory you may not quotient by an arbitrary subring — only by an *ideal* — and an ideal is not even a special kind of subring but a separate notion with its own strong-closure axiom. A reasonable expectation, arriving at modules, is that the pattern continues: that submodules come in an ordinary flavour and a stronger "normal" flavour, and only the strong ones may be quotiented. **They do not.** There is exactly one notion of submodule, and you may quotient by *any* of them. The reason is structural and worth internalizing: the obstruction to quotienting is always whether the operations are well-defined on cosets, and the single submodule axiom — closure under the ring action, $n \in N, r \in R \Rightarrow rn \in N$ — already supplies precisely what well-definedness of the coset action needs. There is no residual condition because the action axiom has already done the work that normality did for groups and strong closure did for rings. So the apparent simplification is real, and it should be used without the hesitation those earlier subjects trained in: when you want to quotient a module, you simply may.

**When $R$ is a field, all of linear algebra is recovered — and modules are exactly the setting where the next topic's structure theorem can live.** Two boundary observations frame the topic. At one extreme, set $R = F$ a field: every module becomes a vector space, every module is free, rank is the unique invariant under the name dimension, and module theory degenerates into the linear algebra you already know. This is not a curiosity but a calibration — it tells you that any phenomenon genuinely belonging to module theory must be invisible over a field, which is a sharp test for whether a difficulty you are facing is real. At the other extreme, the *point* of building the apparatus of §3.1–3.2 — finite generation, free modules, ranks, relations, presentations — is that it is exactly the apparatus needed to *state and prove* the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain. That theorem is the summit of the subject, and it is remarkable for its reach: a single statement about $R$-modules, instantiated at $R = \mathbb{Z}$, classifies all finitely generated abelian groups, and instantiated at $R = F[X]$, via the bridge that an $F[X]$-module is a vector space with a chosen endomorphism, produces the Jordan and rational canonical forms of a matrix. The classification of finite abelian groups and the canonical form of a linear operator are, from a height, the *same theorem* — and modules are the vantage point from which that unity becomes visible. Everything on this page is the scaffolding for that view.
