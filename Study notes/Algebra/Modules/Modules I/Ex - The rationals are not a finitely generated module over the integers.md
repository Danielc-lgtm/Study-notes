# Problem Statement

Regard the field of rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$ as a module over the ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}$, with the action $n\cdot q = nq$ (ordinary multiplication). Prove that $\mathbb{Q}$ is **not a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module**: there is no finite subset $S = \{q_1,\dots,q_k\}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}$ such that every rational number is a $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination $\sum_{i=1}^k n_i q_i$ with $n_i\in\mathbb{Z}$.

The idea is a *denominator bound*. Finitely many fractions $q_1,\dots,q_k$ can be written over a single common denominator $d$. Every integer combination $\sum n_i q_i$ then also has denominator dividing $d$. But the rational $\tfrac{1}{2d}$ has denominator $2d$, which does not divide $d$, so $\tfrac{1}{2d}$ escapes the submodule generated by $S$.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the ring $\mathbb{Z}$, the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Q}$, and the notion of a *finitely generated* module.

![[Def - Finitely Generated Module#The Definition]]

For the ring $R = \mathbb{Z}$ acting on $M = \mathbb{Q}$, a **$\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination** of a subset $S$ is a finite sum $\sum_i n_i s_i$ with integer coefficients $n_i$ and $s_i\in S$. The subset $S$ **generates** $M$ when every element of $M$ is such a combination; the **submodule generated by $S$**, written $\langle S\rangle$, is the [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] consisting of all $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combinations of $S$ — the smallest submodule containing $S$. A module is **finitely generated** when it has a *finite* generating set.

A standard fact about fractions is used: any finite list of rationals admits a **common denominator**. If $q_i = a_i/b_i$ in lowest terms with $b_i\ge 1$, then setting $d = b_1 b_2\cdots b_k$ (or $\operatorname{lcm}(b_1,\dots,b_k)$) we may rewrite each $q_i = c_i/d$ with $c_i\in\mathbb{Z}$. We will also use that the rationals with denominator dividing a fixed $d$, namely $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z} = \{\,m/d : m\in\mathbb{Z}\,\}$, form a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Q},+)$ closed under multiplication by integers — that is, a $\mathbb{Z}$-submodule of $\mathbb{Q}$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *prove a module is not finitely generated* problem — a negative finiteness claim. As the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] explains, a negative finiteness statement is proved by *assuming* a finite generating set exists, attaching to it a numerical bound that constrains everything the set can reach, and then exhibiting an element that violates the bound. The bound must be an invariant of *any* finite set.

**Assumption pattern.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $S = \{q_1,\dots,q_k\}$ is a finite generating set. The exploitable feature of a *finite* set of rationals is that finitely many denominators have a common multiple $d$. A single common denominator is the bound; it would not exist for an infinite set, which is exactly why the argument is special to *finite* generation.

**Theorem routing.** No named theorem is required; the route is a containment of submodules. Step one: every $q_i$, hence every integer combination of the $q_i$, lies in the submodule $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ of fractions with denominator dividing $d$ — so $\langle S\rangle\subseteq\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$. Step two: exhibit a rational *not* in $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$, namely $\tfrac{1}{2d}$, whose reduced denominator $2d$ does not divide $d$. Then $\mathbb{Q}\neq\langle S\rangle$, so $S$ does not generate $\mathbb{Q}$ — contradicting the assumption.

**Key decision point.** The crux is choosing the right *invariant* to bound. The naive invariants — how many elements $S$ has, how large they are — are useless, because $\mathbb{Q}$ has elements of every size and a small set can have complicated members. The productive invariant is the **common denominator** $d$: it is finite precisely because $S$ is finite, and it caps the "fineness" of every fraction $\langle S\rangle$ can produce. Once $d$ is named, the escaping element practically writes itself: take any denominator that does not divide $d$, the simplest being $2d$. Recognising that *denominator*, not size or count, is the conserved quantity is the entire insight.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Assume a finite generating set and extract a finiteness invariant** (the bound-the-generators operation). From a hypothetical finite $S$, read off a single numerical quantity — here the common denominator $d$ — that every element of $\langle S\rangle$ must respect.

2. **Place the generated submodule inside a concrete larger submodule.** Show $\langle S\rangle\subseteq\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ by checking that the candidate overgroup $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ is itself a submodule containing every $q_i$; since $\langle S\rangle$ is the *smallest* submodule containing $S$, the containment follows.

3. **Verify a subset is a submodule by closure checks.** Confirm $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ is a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]]: it contains $0$, is closed under addition and negation, and absorbs multiplication by integers.

4. **Exhibit an escaping element to defeat generation.** Produce a specific element of $M$ — here $\tfrac{1}{2d}$ — provably outside the overgroup $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$, hence outside $\langle S\rangle$.

5. **Detect non-membership via the reduced-fraction invariant.** Show $\tfrac{1}{2d}\notin\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ by the divisibility test: a fraction in lowest terms with denominator $D$ lies in $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ iff $D\mid d$, and $2d\nmid d$.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Argue by contradiction: suppose a *finite* set $S = \{q_1,\dots,q_k\}$ generates $\mathbb{Q}$. The word "finite" must be the lever — what can you do with finitely many fractions that you could not do with infinitely many? Think about their denominators.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Finitely many fractions $q_1,\dots,q_k$ share a common denominator: there is a single positive integer $d$ with every $q_i$ expressible as $c_i/d$ for some integer $c_i$ (take $d$ to be the product, or least common multiple, of the individual denominators). Now ask: if you form an integer combination $\sum n_i q_i$, what can its denominator be?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Every integer combination $\sum n_i q_i = \sum n_i (c_i/d) = \big(\sum n_i c_i\big)/d$ is again a fraction *over the denominator $d$*. So the submodule generated by $S$ is contained in $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z} = \{m/d : m\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ — the rationals whose denominator (in lowest terms) divides $d$. Check that $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ really is a submodule, and that it is a *proper* subset of $\mathbb{Q}$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> To finish, find one rational that is *not* of the form $m/d$. The fraction $\tfrac{1}{2d}$ works: if $\tfrac{1}{2d} = m/d$ then $d = 2dm$, forcing $1 = 2m$ — impossible for an integer $m$. So $\tfrac{1}{2d}\notin\langle S\rangle$, yet $\tfrac{1}{2d}\in\mathbb{Q}$. Thus $S$ fails to generate $\mathbb{Q}$, contradicting the assumption.

# Solution

The strategy is a denominator bound: a finite generating set has a common denominator $d$, every element it generates is a fraction over $d$, and the rational $\tfrac{1}{2d}$ — needing the finer denominator $2d$ — escapes.

**Step 1: Set up the contradiction and extract a common denominator.**

Assume $S = \{q_1,\dots,q_k\}$ is a finite generating set for $\mathbb{Q}$. Then there is a positive integer $d$ such that each $q_i = c_i/d$ with $c_i\in\mathbb{Z}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Suppose, for contradiction, that $\mathbb{Q}$ *is* finitely generated as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, with finite generating set $S = \{q_1,\dots,q_k\}$. (If $S$ is empty, $\langle S\rangle = \{0\}\neq\mathbb{Q}$ and we are already done, so $k\ge 1$.)
>
> Write each generator in lowest terms, $q_i = a_i/b_i$ with $a_i\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $b_i\ge 1$. Set
> $$d = b_1\,b_2\cdots b_k\ \ (\ge 1).$$
> This $d$ is a finite positive integer *because there are finitely many $b_i$* — the one place the finiteness of $S$ is used. Each $b_i$ divides $d$, so writing $d = b_i e_i$ we have
> $$q_i = \frac{a_i}{b_i} = \frac{a_i e_i}{b_i e_i} = \frac{a_i e_i}{d} = \frac{c_i}{d},\qquad c_i := a_i e_i\in\mathbb{Z}.$$
> So every generator is a fraction with the *single common denominator* $d$.

**Step 2: The generated submodule lies inside the fractions over $d$.**

The set $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z} = \{m/d : m\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-submodule of $\mathbb{Q}$ containing every $q_i$, hence $\langle S\rangle\subseteq\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> First, $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ is a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] of $\mathbb{Q}$. It contains $0 = 0/d$. It is closed under addition, $m/d + m'/d = (m+m')/d$, and under negation, $-(m/d) = (-m)/d$. And it absorbs the $\mathbb{Z}$-action: for $n\in\mathbb{Z}$, $n\cdot(m/d) = (nm)/d\in\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$. So $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ satisfies the submodule axioms.
>
> Second, every generator lies in it: by Step 1, $q_i = c_i/d\in\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> Now the submodule $\langle S\rangle$ generated by $S$ is by definition the *smallest* submodule of $\mathbb{Q}$ containing $S$. Since $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ is *a* submodule containing $S$, it contains the smallest one:
> $$\langle S\rangle\subseteq\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}.$$
> Concretely, this just says every integer combination of the generators stays over the denominator $d$: $\sum_i n_i q_i = \sum_i n_i(c_i/d) = \big(\sum_i n_i c_i\big)\big/d\in\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$.

**Step 3: The rational $\tfrac{1}{2d}$ escapes — it is not a fraction over $d$.**

The number $\tfrac{1}{2d}\in\mathbb{Q}$ does not lie in $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$, because its denominator $2d$ does not divide $d$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Consider $\tfrac{1}{2d}$, a perfectly good rational number (here $d\ge 1$, so $2d\ge 2$). Suppose for contradiction that $\tfrac{1}{2d}\in\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$, i.e.
> $$\frac{1}{2d} = \frac{m}{d}\quad\text{for some } m\in\mathbb{Z}.$$
> Cross-multiplying (both denominators are non-zero) gives $d = 2dm$, and cancelling the non-zero factor $d$ gives
> $$1 = 2m.$$
> But $2m$ is even for every integer $m$, while $1$ is odd — no integer $m$ satisfies this. Contradiction. Hence $\tfrac{1}{2d}\notin\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> The conceptual reason: $\tfrac{1}{2d}$ is already in lowest terms with denominator $2d$, and a fraction in lowest terms with denominator $D$ belongs to $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $D\mid d$. Since $2d\nmid d$ (a number strictly larger than $d$ cannot divide $d$), the fraction $\tfrac{1}{2d}$ is excluded.

**Step 4: Conclude.**

The escaping element contradicts the assumption that $S$ generates $\mathbb{Q}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By Step 2, $\langle S\rangle\subseteq\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$. By Step 3, $\tfrac{1}{2d}\notin\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$, hence $\tfrac{1}{2d}\notin\langle S\rangle$. But $\tfrac{1}{2d}$ *is* an element of $\mathbb{Q}$. So $\langle S\rangle\neq\mathbb{Q}$ — the set $S$ does **not** generate $\mathbb{Q}$.
>
> This contradicts the assumption of Step 1 that $S$ is a generating set. Since $S$ was an arbitrary *finite* subset of $\mathbb{Q}$, no finite subset generates $\mathbb{Q}$. Therefore $\mathbb{Q}$ is not a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is not a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module.
>
> Suppose for contradiction that $S = \{q_1,\dots,q_k\}$ is a finite generating set (if $S = \emptyset$ then $\langle S\rangle = \{0\}\neq\mathbb{Q}$, so $k\ge 1$). Write $q_i = a_i/b_i$ in lowest terms with $b_i\ge 1$, and set $d = b_1\cdots b_k\ge 1$. Each $b_i\mid d$, so $q_i = c_i/d$ for suitable $c_i\in\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> The set $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z} = \{m/d : m\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ is a submodule of $\mathbb{Q}$: it contains $0$, is closed under addition and negation, and satisfies $n\cdot(m/d) = (nm)/d$ for $n\in\mathbb{Z}$. It contains every $q_i = c_i/d$. As $\langle S\rangle$ is the smallest submodule containing $S$, we get $\langle S\rangle\subseteq\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> Now $\tfrac{1}{2d}\in\mathbb{Q}$. If $\tfrac{1}{2d} = m/d$ for some $m\in\mathbb{Z}$, then $d = 2dm$, so $1 = 2m$ — impossible. Hence $\tfrac{1}{2d}\notin\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}\supseteq\langle S\rangle$, so $\tfrac{1}{2d}\notin\langle S\rangle$.
>
> Thus $\langle S\rangle\neq\mathbb{Q}$, contradicting that $S$ generates $\mathbb{Q}$. No finite subset generates $\mathbb{Q}$, so $\mathbb{Q}$ is not finitely generated over $\mathbb{Z}$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**To refute finite generation, attach a finiteness invariant to a hypothetical generating set and exhibit an escaping element.** The universal shape of a "not finitely generated" proof is: assume a finite generating set $S$ exists; from its *finiteness* extract a single numerical quantity that bounds everything $S$ can reach; then produce an element of the module that violates the bound. Here the quantity is the **common denominator** $d$ — finite precisely because $S$ is finite — and the escaping element is $\tfrac{1}{2d}$. The trigger is the phrase "is $M$ finitely generated?" together with a *no* suspicion; the reaction is "what finite list of numbers would a finite generating set produce, and what does combining them conserve?". The same template, with the appropriate invariant, shows that the polynomial ring $k[X]$ is not finitely generated as a $k$-module (invariant: maximal degree — a finite set has a degree cap, but $X^{N+1}$ exceeds it), that an infinite-dimensional vector space is not finitely generated, and that the ring $k[X_1,X_2,\dots]$ in infinitely many variables is not a finitely generated algebra.

**Choose the *right* invariant: it must be both bounded by finiteness and violable.** The art is selecting the conserved quantity. Size and count are tempting but inert here — $\mathbb{Q}$ has rationals of every magnitude, and a two-element set can contain wild fractions, so neither "how many" nor "how big" constrains $\langle S\rangle$. The denominator works because it has two properties simultaneously: (i) it is *bounded by finiteness* — finitely many fractions have a common denominator, an infinite set need not; and (ii) it is *violable* — for any bound $d$ there is a rational $\tfrac{1}{2d}$ requiring a strictly finer denominator. An invariant lacking either property is useless. When you set up such an argument, explicitly check both: does the invariant become finite *because* the set is finite, and can it always be exceeded? This is also why $\mathbb{Q}$ is the canonical example of a *non-finitely-generated, yet torsion-free* $\mathbb{Z}$-module — it is "infinitely divisible", admitting denominators without bound.

**"Smallest submodule containing $S$" is proved $\subseteq$ a target by exhibiting *any* containing submodule.** Step 2 used the defining property of $\langle S\rangle$: it is the *smallest* submodule containing $S$, so to show $\langle S\rangle\subseteq T$ it suffices to verify that $T$ is *a* submodule and that $T\supseteq S$. You never have to enumerate the elements of $\langle S\rangle$. This "smallest object is below any competitor" move is ubiquitous and worth internalising as a reflex: it proves a generated subgroup lies in a given subgroup, a generated ideal lies in a given ideal, a generated subspace lies in a given subspace, a topology generated by a subbasis is coarser than any topology containing the subbasis. The dual move — to show $T\subseteq\langle S\rangle$ — instead requires writing each element of $T$ explicitly as a combination of $S$. Knowing which direction is "free" (the one using minimality) and which is "work" (the one requiring explicit combinations) organises a great many containment proofs.

**$\mathbb{Q}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ is a reservoir of counterexamples — finitely generated is strictly stronger than its weaker cousins.** This exercise places $\mathbb{Q}$ on the map of module-theoretic finiteness conditions, and the placement is instructive. $\mathbb{Q}$ is *not* finitely generated; yet it *is* torsion-free, *is* a submodule of nothing smaller than itself, and every *finitely generated* submodule of it is free of rank one (each is contained in some $\tfrac{1}{d}\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}$). So $\mathbb{Q}$ is a module all of whose finitely generated pieces are tame, but which is not tame globally — the canonical example showing that finite generation is *not* a local-to-global property. It is also the standard counterexample separating finite generation from being *finitely presented*, and a building block in non-Noetherian phenomena. Keep $\mathbb{Q}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ in mind as the first thing to test any conjectured implication "(weaker finiteness) $\Rightarrow$ (finitely generated)" against. The companion exercises [[Ex - Generating sets that are not bases]] and [[Ex - A submodule of a free module that is not free]] play the analogous role of separating *generating* from *freely generating* and *submodule-of-free* from *free*; together they delineate the gap between modules and vector spaces.
