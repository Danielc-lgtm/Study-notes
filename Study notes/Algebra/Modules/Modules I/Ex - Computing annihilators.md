# Problem Statement

Regard $M=\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ as a module over $R=\mathbb{Z}$ (the scalar action is $n\cdot\bar a=\overline{na}$, the only $\mathbb{Z}$-module structure on the abelian group $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$). Write $\bar a$ for the residue class of $a$.

1. Compute the **annihilator of the whole module**, $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=\{n\in\mathbb{Z}:n\cdot\bar a=\bar 0\text{ for all }\bar a\in M\}$.

2. Compute the **annihilators of the individual elements** $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3)$ and $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 4)$ in $M$.

3. Prove the **general formula**: in $M=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, for any $a\in\mathbb{Z}$,
$$\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)=\Big(\tfrac{n}{\gcd(a,n)}\Big),$$
the ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ generated by $n/\gcd(a,n)$. Confirm that parts 1–2 are special cases.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the annihilator of an element, and the annihilator of a module.

![[Def - Annihilator#The Definition]]

For a module $M$ over a commutative ring $R$ and an element $m\in M$, the **annihilator** of $m$ is $\operatorname{Ann}(m)=\{r\in R:r\cdot m=0\}$, the set of scalars that kill $m$. For the whole module, $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=\{r\in R:r\cdot m=0\text{ for all }m\in M\}=\bigcap_{m\in M}\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. The annihilator is always an **ideal** of $R$: if $r,s$ kill $m$ then so does $r+s$ (since $(r+s)m=rm+sm=0$) and so does $tr$ for any $t\in R$ (since $(tr)m=t(rm)=0$).

![[Def - Ideal#The Definition]]

The module here is $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the integers modulo $n$: residue classes $\bar a$ with $\bar a=\bar b$ if and only if $n\mid(a-b)$, and the $\mathbb{Z}$-action $k\cdot\bar a=\overline{ka}$. Two facts about $\mathbb{Z}$ are used. The **ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ are principal** — every ideal is $(d)=d\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique $d\geq 0$ (see [[Ex - Every ideal of the integers is principal]]). And the **greatest common divisor**: $\gcd(a,n)$ is the largest positive integer dividing both $a$ and $n$; equivalently $(a,n)=(\gcd(a,n))$ — the ideal generated by $a$ and $n$ is principal, generated by their gcd.

A basic divisibility fact, used repeatedly: for positive integers, $\;n\mid ka\iff \dfrac{n}{\gcd(a,n)}\;\Big|\;k$. Writing $g=\gcd(a,n)$, $a=ga'$, $n=gn'$ with $\gcd(a',n')=1$, the condition $n\mid ka$ reads $gn'\mid kga'$, i.e. $n'\mid ka'$; and since $n'$ is coprime to $a'$, this holds if and only if $n'\mid k$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *compute an invariant* problem — evaluate the annihilator ideal of given module elements. As the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] notes, computing $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ in a concrete module means *unwinding the definition* "$r\cdot m=0$" into the native language of the module, then *recognising the resulting set as a known ideal*. Because the answer is guaranteed to be an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$, and ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ are principal, the entire task reduces to finding the *single generator*.

**Assumption pattern.** The module is $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and the ring is $\mathbb{Z}$ — a setting where "$=0$" means "divisible by $n$" and where every ideal is a single $d\mathbb{Z}$. The pattern is "cyclic module over $\mathbb{Z}$": such a module is a quotient of $\mathbb{Z}$, the action is multiplication, and the condition $k\cdot\bar a=\bar 0$ is the divisibility $n\mid ka$. The presence of a *principal ideal domain* as the scalar ring is what guarantees the answer is one generator, not a list.

**Theorem routing.** No named theorem; the route is: (i) translate $k\cdot\bar a=\bar 0$ into the divisibility $n\mid ka$; (ii) apply the gcd divisibility lemma — $n\mid ka\iff(n/\gcd(a,n))\mid k$ — to rewrite the kill-set as the multiples of a single integer; (iii) read off that this set is the principal ideal $(n/\gcd(a,n))$. For $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$, intersect over all $\bar a$: $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=\bigcap_a(n/\gcd(a,n))$, and the intersection is largest-generator $=$ least common multiple, which works out to $(n)$.

**Key decision point.** The crux is the gcd lemma: the naive condition "$n\mid ka$" is *not* directly "the multiples of something," but dividing through by $\gcd(a,n)$ turns it into the *coprime* divisibility $n'\mid k$, which *is*. The non-obvious recognition is that the right generator is $n/\gcd(a,n)$ — equivalently the *order* of the element $\bar a$ in the additive group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. Annihilator of $\bar a$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ $=$ ideal generated by the order of $\bar a$.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Unwind "$r\cdot m=0$" into the module's native equality.** In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the equation $k\cdot\bar a=\bar 0$ is the congruence $ka\equiv 0\pmod n$, i.e. the divisibility $n\mid ka$. This is the operation that converts an abstract annihilator condition into concrete arithmetic.

2. **Reduce a divisibility to a coprime divisibility by dividing out a gcd.** From $n\mid ka$, factor $g=\gcd(a,n)$ out of both $a$ and $n$ to obtain the equivalent $n'\mid k$ with $\gcd(a',n')=1$ — the operation that exposes the principal generator.

3. **Recognise a set of integers as a principal ideal.** The set $\{k:n'\mid k\}$ is literally $n'\mathbb{Z}=(n')$; identifying it as such (rather than leaving it as a divisibility condition) is the operation that produces the answer in the demanded form.

4. **Compute an annihilator of a module as an intersection of element-annihilators.** $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=\bigcap_{m}\operatorname{Ann}(m)$; for $M=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ this intersection of principal ideals is taken via least common multiple of generators.

5. **Use that an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ has a unique non-negative generator.** Since [[Ex - Every ideal of the integers is principal|every ideal of ℤ is principal]], the annihilator — known abstractly to be an ideal — is fully determined once one generator is found; this licenses "find the generator and stop."

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Translate first. The element $\bar a\in\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is annihilated by the integer $k$ exactly when $k\cdot\bar a=\bar 0$, and "$=\bar 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$" means "divisible by $n$." So $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:n\mid ka\}$. The whole problem is now: which integers $k$ satisfy $n\mid ka$?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Do the concrete cases by hand. For $\bar 3\in\mathbb{Z}/12$: which $k$ have $12\mid 3k$? That says $4\mid k$. For $\bar 4\in\mathbb{Z}/12$: which $k$ have $12\mid 4k$? That says $3\mid k$. So $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3)=(4)$ and $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 4)=(3)$. Notice the generator is $12$ divided by something — what?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> The "something" is the gcd. To prove $n\mid ka\iff(n/g)\mid k$ with $g=\gcd(a,n)$: write $a=ga'$ and $n=gn'$, so $\gcd(a',n')=1$. Then $n\mid ka$ becomes $gn'\mid kga'$, cancel the $g$, giving $n'\mid ka'$. Since $n'$ and $a'$ share no factor, $n'$ must divide $k$. That is the generator: $n'=n/\gcd(a,n)$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$ with $M=\mathbb{Z}/12$: a scalar must kill *every* element, so $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=\bigcap_{\bar a}\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)$. An integer in every $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)$ must in particular kill $\bar 1$, and $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 1)=(12)$ since $\gcd(1,12)=1$. Conversely $12$ kills everything. So $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=(12)=(n)$.

# Solution

The strategy is to translate the annihilator condition into a divisibility, divide out the gcd to expose a coprime divisibility, and read off the principal generator. The whole-module annihilator is then the intersection, controlled by the element $\bar 1$.

**Step 1: Translate — the annihilator of $\bar a$ is the set of $k$ with $n\mid ka$.**

In $M=\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, $\;\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:n\mid ka\}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By definition of the $\mathbb{Z}$-action on $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the scalar $k$ acts on $\bar a$ by $k\cdot\bar a=\overline{ka}$. The element $\overline{ka}$ is the zero of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $ka\equiv 0\pmod n$, that is, when $n\mid ka$. Hence
> $$\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:k\cdot\bar a=\bar 0\}=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:n\mid ka\}.$$
> We already know abstractly (from the [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]] definition) that this set is an *ideal* of $\mathbb{Z}$; since every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is principal, the remaining task is purely to find its generator.

**Step 2: The concrete cases — $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3)=(4)$ and $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 4)=(3)$ in $\mathbb{Z}/12$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Element $\bar 3$, $n=12$.* By Step 1, $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3)=\{k:12\mid 3k\}$. Now $12\mid 3k\iff 4\mid k$ (cancel the common factor $3$: $12=3\cdot 4$, $3k=3\cdot k$, so $12\mid 3k$ is $4\mid k$). Hence $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3)=\{k:4\mid k\}=4\mathbb{Z}=(4)$.
>
> *Element $\bar 4$, $n=12$.* By Step 1, $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 4)=\{k:12\mid 4k\}$. Cancel $4$: $12=4\cdot 3$, so $12\mid 4k\iff 3\mid k$. Hence $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 4)=3\mathbb{Z}=(3)$.
>
> Sanity check against the orders: $\bar 3$ has additive order $4$ in $\mathbb{Z}/12$ (since $4\cdot\bar 3=\overline{12}=\bar 0$ and no smaller positive multiple vanishes), and $\bar 4$ has order $3$. The annihilator generator equals the order — anticipated by the general formula, since $12/\gcd(3,12)=12/3=4$ and $12/\gcd(4,12)=12/4=3$.

**Step 3: The whole-module annihilator — $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=(12)$ for $M=\mathbb{Z}/12$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> By definition $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=\bigcap_{\bar a\in M}\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)$: a scalar kills the whole module if and only if it kills every element.
>
> *Lower bound — $(12)\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}(M)$.* For any $\bar a$, $12\cdot\bar a=\overline{12a}=\bar 0$ since $12\mid 12a$. So $12\in\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)$ for every $\bar a$, hence $12\in\operatorname{Ann}(M)$, and being an ideal, $\operatorname{Ann}(M)\supseteq(12)$.
>
> *Upper bound — $\operatorname{Ann}(M)\subseteq(12)$.* If $k\in\operatorname{Ann}(M)$ then in particular $k$ kills $\bar 1$, so $k\in\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 1)$. By Step 1, $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 1)=\{k:12\mid k\cdot 1\}=\{k:12\mid k\}=(12)$. Hence $k\in(12)$.
>
> The two inclusions give $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=(12)=(n)$. Conceptually: $\bar 1$ is a *generator* of the cyclic module $\mathbb{Z}/12$, so killing $\bar 1$ already forces killing everything — the module annihilator is the annihilator of any generator.

**Step 4: The general formula — $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)=(n/\gcd(a,n))$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.**

For every $a\in\mathbb{Z}$, the annihilator of $\bar a$ is principal, generated by $n$ divided by $\gcd(a,n)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By Step 1, $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:n\mid ka\}$. We prove $\{k:n\mid ka\}=(n')$ where $g:=\gcd(a,n)$ and $n':=n/g$.
>
> Write $a=g\,a'$ and $n=g\,n'$ with $a',n'\in\mathbb{Z}$. Dividing out the gcd makes the cofactors coprime: $\gcd(a',n')=1$ (any common divisor of $a'$ and $n'$ would, multiplied by $g$, be a common divisor of $a$ and $n$ exceeding $g$ — impossible since $g$ is the *greatest*).
>
> Now transform the divisibility condition:
> $$n\mid ka\;\iff\;g\,n'\mid k\,g\,a'\;\iff\;n'\mid k\,a',$$
> the last step cancelling the nonzero common factor $g$ (cancellation is legitimate in $\mathbb{Z}$, an integral domain). It remains to show $n'\mid ka'\iff n'\mid k$. The direction ($\Leftarrow$) is clear. For ($\Rightarrow$): $n'\mid ka'$ and $\gcd(n',a')=1$; by Euclid's lemma (a divisor of a product coprime to one factor divides the other factor) $n'\mid k$.
>
> Therefore $\{k:n\mid ka\}=\{k:n'\mid k\}=n'\mathbb{Z}=(n')$, i.e.
> $$\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)=\Big(\tfrac{n}{\gcd(a,n)}\Big).$$
>
> *Special cases.* With $n=12$: $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3)=(12/\gcd(3,12))=(12/3)=(4)$ and $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 4)=(12/\gcd(4,12))=(12/4)=(3)$ — matching Step 2. And $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 1)=(12/\gcd(1,12))=(12/1)=(12)$, the generator-element computation behind Step 3, so $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=(12)=(n)$. In general $\operatorname{Ann}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})=\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 1)=(n)$.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** In the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, $\;\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)=(n/\gcd(a,n))$; in particular $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3)=(4)$, $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 4)=(3)$ in $\mathbb{Z}/12$, and $\operatorname{Ann}(\mathbb{Z}/12)=(12)$.
>
> *Translation.* The $\mathbb{Z}$-action is $k\cdot\bar a=\overline{ka}$, and $\overline{ka}=\bar 0$ if and only if $n\mid ka$. So $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}:n\mid ka\}$.
>
> *General formula.* Put $g=\gcd(a,n)$, $a=ga'$, $n=gn'$; then $\gcd(a',n')=1$. We have $n\mid ka\iff gn'\mid kga'\iff n'\mid ka'\iff n'\mid k$, the last equivalence by Euclid's lemma since $\gcd(n',a')=1$. Hence $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)=\{k:n'\mid k\}=(n')=(n/\gcd(a,n))$.
>
> *Specialisations.* For $n=12$: $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3)=(12/3)=(4)$; $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 4)=(12/4)=(3)$. For the module: $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=\bigcap_{\bar a}\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 1)=(12/\gcd(1,12))=(12)$, and conversely $12\cdot\bar a=\bar 0$ for all $\bar a$ gives $(12)\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}(M)$; so $\operatorname{Ann}(\mathbb{Z}/12)=(12)$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**An annihilator is computed by unwinding "$r\cdot m=0$" into the module's native equality, then recognising a known ideal.** The annihilator is *defined* abstractly as a set of scalars, but it is *computed* by translating the defining condition into whatever "$=0$" means concretely in the module at hand. In $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ that means "$n\mid\,$"; in a module of functions it might mean "vanishes everywhere"; in $F[X]$ acting on a vector space via an endomorphism $\alpha$ it means "the polynomial in $\alpha$ is the zero map." The two-step rhythm — *unwind the condition, then identify the resulting set as a recognisable ideal* — is the universal method for annihilator computations. The reason the second step always succeeds is structural: the annihilator is *guaranteed* to be an ideal of the scalar ring, so the answer must be expressible in the ideal vocabulary of that ring; over a principal ideal domain like $\mathbb{Z}$ or $F[X]$, "expressible as an ideal" collapses to "find a single generator."

**Dividing out a gcd turns an unruly divisibility into a clean coprime one — and coprime divisibility *is* a principal ideal.** The condition "$n\mid ka$" is not, as it stands, the set of multiples of anything obvious — the factor $a$ obstructs it. The decisive manoeuvre is to cancel $g=\gcd(a,n)$ from $a$ and $n$ simultaneously, producing coprime cofactors $a',n'$; then "$n'\mid ka'$" with $\gcd(n',a')=1$ collapses, by Euclid's lemma, to the clean "$n'\mid k$," which is visibly the principal ideal $(n')$. This gcd-cancellation trick — *strip the common factor to reveal coprimality, then invoke Euclid's lemma* — is one of the most reused moves in elementary number theory and ring theory: it is how one solves linear congruences $ka\equiv b\pmod n$, how one computes orders of elements in $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and how one finds the structure of cyclic groups. The trigger is any divisibility statement with a "spectator" factor; the reaction is "divide by the gcd to make the spectator coprime."

**The annihilator generator of $\bar a$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ is exactly the additive order of $\bar a$ — annihilators generalise the notion of order.** The number $n/\gcd(a,n)$ that emerged is not a new quantity: it is precisely the *order* of $\bar a$ in the additive group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — the least positive integer $k$ with $k\cdot\bar a=\bar 0$. This is the conceptual heart of the exercise. For a $\mathbb{Z}$-module — i.e. an abelian group — $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is the ideal generated by the order of $m$, so the annihilator is the *module-theoretic refinement of order*. Where the order is a single number, the annihilator is an ideal, and the ideal carries strictly more information once the scalar ring is larger than $\mathbb{Z}$: over $F[X]$, $\operatorname{Ann}(v)$ for a vector $v$ is generated by the *minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ at $v$*, the polynomial analogue of order. Recognising "annihilator $=$ ideal-generated-by-order" lets one import the entire intuition for orders of elements into module theory.

**For a cyclic module, the module annihilator is the annihilator of any single generator.** Computing $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$ looks like an infinite task — intersect $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar a)$ over *all* elements — but Step 3 collapsed it to a single computation, $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 1)$, because $\bar 1$ *generates* $\mathbb{Z}/12$. The principle is general: if $M=Rm_1+\cdots+Rm_k$ is generated by finitely many elements, then a scalar kills $M$ if and only if it kills each generator, so $\operatorname{Ann}(M)=\bigcap_i\operatorname{Ann}(m_i)$ — an intersection over the *generators only*, not the whole module. For a *cyclic* module ($k=1$) this is a single annihilator; this is exactly why, in the isomorphism $Rm\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ of [[Ex - The submodule generated by an element]], the module $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ reappears as $\mathbb{Z}/\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 1)$. The lesson: whenever a module has a known generating set, restrict every "for all elements" condition to "for all generators" — generation exists precisely to make such reductions valid.
