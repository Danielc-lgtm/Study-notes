# Problem Statement

Let $R$ be a commutative ring, let $M$ be an $R$-module, and fix an element $m\in M$. The **submodule generated by $m$** is
$$Rm=\{r\cdot m:r\in R\}\subseteq M,$$
the set of all scalar multiples of $m$.

1. Prove the **cyclic submodule isomorphism**
$$Rm\;\cong\;R/\operatorname{Ann}(m),$$
where $\operatorname{Ann}(m)=\{r\in R:r\cdot m=0\}$ is the annihilator of $m$. Do this by applying the first isomorphism theorem to the map $\varphi:R\to M$, $\;\varphi(r)=r\cdot m$.

2. **Deduce the classification of simple modules over a commutative ring.** A module $S\neq 0$ is *simple* if its only submodules are $0$ and $S$. Show that a simple $R$-module $S$ is isomorphic to $R/\mathfrak{m}$ for some **maximal ideal** $\mathfrak{m}\trianglelefteq R$ — and conversely that $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is simple for every maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are an $R$-module $M$, the submodule generated by one element, the annihilator ideal, the quotient module $R/I$, and a maximal ideal.

![[Def - Submodule#The Definition]]

The submodule generated by $m$, written $Rm$, is the smallest submodule of $M$ containing $m$; concretely it is $\{rm:r\in R\}$. A module is **cyclic** if it equals $Rm$ for some single $m$.

![[Def - Quotient Module#The Definition]]

For an ideal $I\trianglelefteq R$, the ring $R$ is itself an $R$-module (scalars act by ring multiplication), $I$ is a submodule of it, and the **quotient module** $R/I$ has elements $r+I$ with action $s\cdot(r+I)=(sr)+I$. As an *abelian group* this is the quotient ring, but here it is viewed as an $R$-module.

![[Def - Annihilator#The Definition]]

![[Def - Module Homomorphism#The Definition]]

The engine is the first isomorphism theorem for modules; the page below states all four isomorphism theorems together with the submodule correspondence, and we use the first one and the correspondence.

![[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules#Statement]]

A **maximal ideal** $\mathfrak{m}\trianglelefteq R$ is a proper ideal ($\mathfrak{m}\neq R$) not contained in any strictly larger proper ideal — equivalently, $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field.

![[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal#The Definition]]

The bridge between these notions is the **submodule correspondence**, the final clause of the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|isomorphism theorems]] above: for an ideal $I\trianglelefteq R$, the $R$-submodules of the quotient module $R/I$ correspond bijectively, inclusion-preservingly, to the ideals of $R$ containing $I$. This is the module-theoretic form of the ideal correspondence theorem, and it holds because $R$-submodules of $R/I$ are exactly the additive subgroups closed under ring multiplication — i.e. the ideals of $R/I$ — which pull back to ideals of $R$ between $I$ and $R$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** Part 1 is an *identify a module up to isomorphism* problem — recognise the concrete module $Rm$ as a quotient. As the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] records, the canonical route for "this module *is* that quotient" is the **first isomorphism theorem**: build a surjection onto the target, compute its kernel, conclude domain/kernel $\cong$ target. Part 2 is a *classify all objects of a kind* problem (all simple modules), solved by *reducing the general object to a cyclic one* and then invoking Part 1.

**Assumption pattern.** Part 1 has essentially no hypotheses — $R$ commutative, $M$ any module, $m$ any element — which signals that the result is a *structural identity*, true in total generality, and the proof must therefore be a pure naturality argument with no special cases. Part 2 adds the hypothesis "$S$ is simple"; simplicity is a *smallness* condition, and the strategic reading of "no proper non-zero submodule" is "every non-zero element generates everything" — which is exactly the trigger to apply Part 1 with $Rm=S$.

**Theorem routing.** Part 1: define $\varphi:R\to M$, $r\mapsto rm$; check it is an $R$-module homomorphism; observe $\ker\varphi=\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ and $\operatorname{im}\varphi=Rm$; the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] then yields $R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)\cong Rm$. Part 2: pick any $0\neq s\in S$; simplicity forces $Rs=S$ (the submodule $Rs$ is non-zero, so it is all of $S$); Part 1 gives $S\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(s)$; finally simplicity of $S$ translates, *via the submodule correspondence*, into "$\operatorname{Ann}(s)$ is maximal."

**Key decision point.** Part 1's crux is recognising that "multiply $m$ by a varying scalar" *is a homomorphism out of $R$* — viewing $R$ as a module over itself and $\varphi(r)=rm$ as $R$-linear is the entire idea; it is the precise module-theory analogue of the orbit map in the orbit–stabiliser theorem. Part 2's crux is the final translation: a simple module has *exactly two* submodules, and under the correspondence "submodules of $R/\operatorname{Ann}(s)$" $\leftrightarrow$ "ideals of $R$ containing $\operatorname{Ann}(s)$," having exactly two such ideals is *precisely* the definition of $\operatorname{Ann}(s)$ being maximal.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **View the ring as a module over itself.** $R$ is an $R$-module with scalars acting by ring multiplication; this is what makes $\varphi:R\to M$ a map *of modules* rather than merely of sets, and lets the first isomorphism theorem apply.

2. **Build an "orbit map" from an element.** Given $m\in M$, the map $\varphi:r\mapsto rm$ has image $Rm$; constructing this evaluation-style homomorphism is the operation that exhibits $Rm$ as a homomorphic image of $R$.

3. **Apply the first isomorphism theorem to identify a quotient.** With $\varphi$ in hand, [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|the first isomorphism theorem]] converts the pair $(\ker\varphi,\operatorname{im}\varphi)$ into the isomorphism $R/\ker\varphi\cong\operatorname{im}\varphi$.

4. **Read a kernel as an annihilator.** The kernel of $\varphi(r)=rm$ is $\{r:rm=0\}=\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ by inspection — the operation of recognising one defined object as another.

5. **Turn a smallness hypothesis into a generation statement.** "$S$ simple" $\Rightarrow$ "$Rs=S$ for every $0\neq s$": the non-zero submodule $Rs$ is forced to be all of $S$. This converts an abstract hypothesis into the concrete "$S$ is cyclic."

6. **Translate submodules of a quotient into ideals of the ring via the correspondence.** The submodule correspondence sends submodules of $R/I$ to ideals of $R$ containing $I$; applying it converts "$S$ has exactly two submodules" into "$\operatorname{Ann}(s)$ has exactly two ideals above it," i.e. is maximal.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> The phrase "$Rm\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$" with $R/(\text{something})$ on the right is the unmistakable signature of the **first isomorphism theorem**. To use it you need a homomorphism *out of $R$*. What is the most obvious $R$-module map $R\to M$ that involves the chosen element $m$?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Take $\varphi:R\to M$, $\varphi(r)=r\cdot m$. Treat $R$ as a module over *itself*. Check $\varphi$ is $R$-linear: $\varphi(r+r')=\varphi(r)+\varphi(r')$ and $\varphi(sr)=s\,\varphi(r)$ — both follow straight from the module axioms. Now identify $\ker\varphi$ and $\operatorname{im}\varphi$. What set is $\{r:rm=0\}$? What set is $\{rm:r\in R\}$?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> $\ker\varphi=\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ and $\operatorname{im}\varphi=Rm$, so the first isomorphism theorem gives $R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)\cong Rm$ directly. For Part 2: if $S$ is simple and $s\neq 0$, look at the submodule $Rs$. It contains $s\neq 0$, so it is *not* the zero submodule. What does simplicity then force $Rs$ to be?

> [!note]- Hint 4
> $Rs=S$, so by Part 1, $S\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(s)$. Now the submodules of $R/\operatorname{Ann}(s)$ correspond to the ideals of $R$ *containing* $\operatorname{Ann}(s)$. The module $S$ is simple — it has exactly the two submodules $0$ and $S$. So $R/\operatorname{Ann}(s)$ has exactly two submodules, hence $\operatorname{Ann}(s)$ has exactly two ideals above it: itself and $R$. An ideal $I\neq R$ whose only over-ideals are $I$ and $R$ is *maximal*.

# Solution

The strategy is to recognise $Rm$ as the image of the "multiply by $m$" homomorphism $R\to M$ and apply the first isomorphism theorem; then to deduce the classification of simple modules by noting a simple module is cyclic, hence a quotient $R/\mathfrak{m}$, with maximality of $\mathfrak{m}$ forced by simplicity through the submodule correspondence.

**Step 1: The map $\varphi:R\to M$, $\;\varphi(r)=r\cdot m$, is an $R$-module homomorphism.**

Regarding $R$ as a module over itself, $\varphi$ respects addition and the scalar action.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The ring $R$ is an $R$-module: the underlying abelian group is $(R,+)$ and the scalar action of $s\in R$ on $r\in R$ is the ring product $sr$ (the module axioms for this are the ring axioms). With this in place we check $\varphi$ is a [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]].
>
> *Additive.* For $r,r'\in R$, using module axiom (a) ($(r_1+r_2)\cdot m=r_1\cdot m+r_2\cdot m$):
> $$\varphi(r+r')=(r+r')\cdot m=r\cdot m+r'\cdot m=\varphi(r)+\varphi(r').$$
> *Scalar-linear.* For $s\in R$ (a scalar) and $r\in R$ (a module element), the action of $s$ on $r$ in the module $R$ is the ring product $sr$; using module axiom (c) ($s\cdot(r\cdot m)=(sr)\cdot m$):
> $$\varphi(s\cdot r)=\varphi(sr)=(sr)\cdot m=s\cdot(r\cdot m)=s\cdot\varphi(r).$$
> Both conditions hold, so $\varphi$ is an $R$-module homomorphism. (This is the module analogue of the *orbit map* $g\mapsto g\cdot x$ in group actions — fixing one point and letting the operator vary.)

**Step 2: $\ker\varphi=\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ and $\operatorname{im}\varphi=Rm$.**

The kernel of $\varphi$ is exactly the annihilator of $m$; the image is exactly the submodule generated by $m$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Kernel.* By definition $\ker\varphi=\{r\in R:\varphi(r)=0\}=\{r\in R:r\cdot m=0\}$. The right-hand set is, verbatim, the [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]] $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. So $\ker\varphi=\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. (The first isomorphism theorem guarantees $\ker\varphi$ is a submodule of $R$; submodules of $R$ are ideals, recovering the known fact that $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is an ideal.)
>
> *Image.* By definition $\operatorname{im}\varphi=\{\varphi(r):r\in R\}=\{r\cdot m:r\in R\}$, which is exactly $Rm$, the [[Def - Submodule|submodule generated by]] $m$. So $\operatorname{im}\varphi=Rm$.

**Step 3: Conclude $Rm\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$.**

The first isomorphism theorem applied to $\varphi$ gives the claimed isomorphism.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem for modules]] states: for an $R$-module homomorphism $\varphi:A\to B$, the kernel is a submodule of $A$, the image is a submodule of $B$, and there is an $R$-module isomorphism $A/\ker\varphi\cong\operatorname{im}\varphi$.
>
> Apply it to $\varphi:R\to M$ (a module homomorphism by Step 1). By Step 2, $\ker\varphi=\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ and $\operatorname{im}\varphi=Rm$. Substituting:
> $$R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)\;\cong\;Rm.$$
> Concretely, the isomorphism sends the coset $r+\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ to the element $r\cdot m$; it is well-defined because two scalars differing by an element of $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ produce the same multiple of $m$, injective because the kernel has been quotiented out, and surjective onto $Rm$ by definition of the image. This completes Part 1.

**Step 4: A simple module is cyclic — generated by any one of its non-zero elements.**

If $S$ is a simple $R$-module and $0\neq s\in S$, then $Rs=S$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By the [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] axioms, $Rs=\{r\cdot s:r\in R\}$ is a submodule of $S$. It is *non-zero*: taking the scalar $r=1_R$, module axiom (d) gives $1_R\cdot s=s$, so $s\in Rs$, and $s\neq 0$ by choice.
>
> Now invoke simplicity. By definition, the only submodules of $S$ are $0$ and $S$. The submodule $Rs$ is not $0$, so it must be the other option:
> $$Rs=S.$$
> Thus every simple module is *cyclic*, and indeed *every* non-zero element is a generator.

**Step 5: Deduce $S\cong R/\mathfrak{m}$ with $\mathfrak{m}$ maximal, and the converse.**

A simple module is $R/\mathfrak{m}$ for a maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$; conversely $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is simple for every maximal $\mathfrak{m}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Simple $\Rightarrow R/\mathfrak{m}$, $\mathfrak{m}$ maximal.* Let $S$ be simple and pick $0\neq s\in S$. By Step 4, $S=Rs$, so by Part 1 (Step 3),
> $$S\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(s).$$
> Set $\mathfrak{m}=\operatorname{Ann}(s)$, an ideal of $R$. We must show $\mathfrak{m}$ is maximal. Apply the **submodule correspondence**: the $R$-submodules of the quotient module $R/\mathfrak{m}$ correspond bijectively and inclusion-preservingly to the ideals of $R$ containing $\mathfrak{m}$. Since $S\cong R/\mathfrak{m}$ and $S$ is simple, $R/\mathfrak{m}$ has *exactly two* submodules, $0$ and the whole thing. Under the correspondence, $0\subseteq R/\mathfrak{m}$ matches the ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ itself and the whole module matches $R$. So the ideals of $R$ containing $\mathfrak{m}$ are *exactly* $\mathfrak{m}$ and $R$ — and these are distinct, since a simple module is non-zero so $R/\mathfrak{m}\neq 0$, i.e. $\mathfrak{m}\neq R$. An ideal $\mathfrak{m}\neq R$ such that the only ideals between $\mathfrak{m}$ and $R$ are $\mathfrak{m}$ and $R$ is, by definition, a [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|maximal ideal]]. Hence $S\cong R/\mathfrak{m}$ with $\mathfrak{m}$ maximal.
>
> *Converse: $\mathfrak{m}$ maximal $\Rightarrow R/\mathfrak{m}$ simple.* Let $\mathfrak{m}$ be a maximal ideal. View $R/\mathfrak{m}$ as an $R$-module. By the submodule correspondence, its submodules correspond to ideals of $R$ containing $\mathfrak{m}$. Maximality of $\mathfrak{m}$ says there are exactly two such ideals, $\mathfrak{m}$ and $R$; correspondingly $R/\mathfrak{m}$ has exactly two submodules, $0$ and $R/\mathfrak{m}$. And $R/\mathfrak{m}\neq 0$ because $\mathfrak{m}\neq R$. A non-zero module with exactly two submodules is simple. Hence $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a simple $R$-module.
>
> Combining both directions: the simple $R$-modules are *precisely* the modules $R/\mathfrak{m}$ for $\mathfrak{m}$ a maximal ideal of $R$.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** (1) $Rm\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ for any $m$ in any $R$-module $M$. (2) The simple $R$-modules are exactly the $R/\mathfrak{m}$ for $\mathfrak{m}$ maximal.
>
> *(1)* Regard $R$ as an $R$-module via ring multiplication and define $\varphi:R\to M$ by $\varphi(r)=r\cdot m$. Then $\varphi(r+r')=(r+r')m=rm+r'm=\varphi(r)+\varphi(r')$ (axiom (a)) and $\varphi(sr)=(sr)m=s(rm)=s\varphi(r)$ (axiom (c)), so $\varphi$ is an $R$-module homomorphism. Its kernel is $\{r:rm=0\}=\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ and its image is $\{rm:r\in R\}=Rm$. The first isomorphism theorem for modules gives $R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)\cong\operatorname{im}\varphi=Rm$.
>
> *(2)* Let $S$ be simple and choose $0\neq s\in S$. The submodule $Rs$ contains $s=1_R\cdot s\neq 0$, so $Rs\neq 0$; by simplicity $Rs=S$. By (1), $S\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(s)$. Put $\mathfrak{m}=\operatorname{Ann}(s)$. By the submodule correspondence, submodules of $R/\mathfrak{m}$ correspond to ideals of $R$ containing $\mathfrak{m}$; simplicity of $S\cong R/\mathfrak{m}$ means there are exactly two, namely $\mathfrak{m}$ and $R$, and they are distinct since $R/\mathfrak{m}\neq 0$. Hence $\mathfrak{m}$ is maximal. Conversely, if $\mathfrak{m}$ is maximal then the ideals containing $\mathfrak{m}$ are exactly $\mathfrak{m}$ and $R$, so by the correspondence $R/\mathfrak{m}$ has exactly the two submodules $0$ and $R/\mathfrak{m}$, and $R/\mathfrak{m}\neq 0$; thus $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is simple. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**The first isomorphism theorem is the universal tool for "this module *is* that quotient" — and the homomorphism is almost always an evaluation/orbit map.** Whenever a problem asks to identify a concrete module $N$ with a quotient $R/I$ (or $A/B$), the route is fixed: find a *surjection* onto $N$, compute its *kernel*, and let [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|the first isomorphism theorem]] do the rest. The art is choosing the homomorphism, and in module theory the choice is stereotyped: when the target is built from a single element $m$, the map is $\varphi(r)=r\cdot m$ — *fix the element, vary the scalar*. This is literally the orbit map of a group action transcribed into module language, and $Rm\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is the exact analogue of the orbit–stabiliser theorem $G\cdot x\cong G/G_x$: the orbit $Rm$ is the module, the stabiliser $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is the annihilator, and "quotient by the stabiliser" is the same divide-out. The trigger "module generated by one element" should always fire the reaction "build $\varphi(r)=rm$ and quotient by its kernel."

**A kernel is rarely just a kernel — recognise it as a named object.** The mechanical step "$\ker\varphi=\operatorname{Ann}(m)$" is where the abstract first-isomorphism machinery meets the specific structure of the problem. Half the value of the first isomorphism theorem is realised only when the kernel is *identified* with something one already understands: here the annihilator, in [[Ex - Identifying a quotient ring with the first isomorphism theorem|evaluation-homomorphism problems]] an ideal of polynomials vanishing at a point, in linear algebra the null space. The discipline is: after writing down $\ker\varphi=\{r:\varphi(r)=0\}$, do not stop — translate the condition $\varphi(r)=0$ until it becomes a recognisable set. The quality of the final isomorphism statement is exactly the quality of this recognition; $Rm\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is useful precisely because $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is a thing with its own theory (see [[Ex - Computing annihilators]]).

**A "smallness" hypothesis on a structure should be re-read as a "largeness" statement about its elements.** Simplicity — "no submodules except $0$ and everything" — sounds like a constraint that *removes* objects. The productive reading inverts it: *every non-zero element generates the whole module*. This element-level reformulation is what makes simplicity *usable*, because it hands you a generator and lets you apply Part 1. The same inversion is ubiquitous: a *simple group* has every non-trivial normal-closure equal to the group; a *field* has every non-zero element a unit; an *irreducible* representation is generated by any non-zero vector; a *minimal* counterexample has every smaller instance well-behaved. The trigger is any hypothesis of the form "the only sub-objects are the trivial ones"; the reaction is "therefore any non-trivial element generates," after which a generation-based theorem (here, the cyclic isomorphism) applies.

**Classification theorems are assembled by reducing the general object to a cyclic one, then quoting the cyclic case.** The classification of simple modules has a two-layer architecture worth internalising as a *template*. The bottom layer is a structural identity that holds for *all* cyclic modules: $Rm\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. The top layer specialises: a simple module is cyclic (Step 4), so it is *some* $R/\operatorname{Ann}(s)$, and the extra hypothesis (simplicity) pins down *which* ideals can occur (the maximal ones). This "general $\to$ cyclic $\to$ quote the cyclic theorem $\to$ specialise the hypothesis" pattern recurs throughout algebra: the classification of finitely generated abelian groups reduces to cyclic groups $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$; the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID reduces to cyclic modules $R/(d)$; the classification of finite simple modules over $F[X]$ reduces to $F[X]/(p)$ for irreducible $p$. The conceptual punchline of this exercise — *simple modules over a commutative ring $R$ are exactly the residue fields $R/\mathfrak{m}$* — also re-expresses maximal ideals geometrically: in algebraic geometry the maximal ideals of a coordinate ring are the *points* of the variety, so "simple modules $\leftrightarrow$ maximal ideals" says the irreducible representations of a ring see exactly its points.
