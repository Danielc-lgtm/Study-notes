# Problem Statement

Consider the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ as a [[Def - Module|module]] over themselves (a $\mathbb{Z}$-module). Let $S = \{2,3\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}$. Prove the following three statements.

1. **$S$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$.** Every integer is a $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination $a\cdot 2 + b\cdot 3$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$.
2. **$S$ does not generate $\mathbb{Z}$ *freely*.** There is a non-trivial linear relation among the elements of $S$, namely $3\cdot 2 + (-2)\cdot 3 = 0$.
3. **You cannot pass to a basis by discarding an element of $S$.** Neither $\{2\}$ nor $\{3\}$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. Hence, unlike the situation for a spanning set of a vector space, a redundant generating set of a module need not contain a basis: there is no "exchange lemma" over a general ring.

The point of the exercise is to exhibit, in the simplest possible setting, the breakdown of two facts that hold for vector spaces — that a dependent spanning set can be thinned to a basis, and that any two bases have the same size.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}$ (the ring acting on itself by multiplication), the subset $S = \{2,3\}$, and the notions of *generating*, *generating freely*, and *basis*.

![[Def - Finitely Generated Module#The Definition]]

For the ring $R = \mathbb{Z}$ acting on the module $M = \mathbb{Z}$, the action is ordinary multiplication, and a **$\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination** of a subset $S$ is a finite sum $\sum_i r_i s_i$ with $r_i\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $s_i\in S$. The subset $S$ **generates** $M$ when every element of $M$ is such a combination. A subset of $M$ is a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] when it is a [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]] closed under the $R$-action; the **[[Def - Submodule|submodule]] generated by $S$**, written $\langle S\rangle$, is the set of all $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combinations of $S$ — equivalently the smallest submodule containing $S$.

A subset $S = \{m_1,\dots,m_k\}$ **generates $M$ freely** when it generates $M$ and additionally every set function $\psi : S\to N$ into any module $N$ extends to a module homomorphism $M\to N$; a freely generating subset is a **basis** and a module with a basis is **free**. By [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|the characterisation of free generation]], for a finite $S$ this is equivalent to "$S$ generates $M$ and is **linearly independent**", where linear independence means the only relation $\sum_i r_i m_i = 0$ is $r_1 = \cdots = r_k = 0$, and equivalent again to "every element of $M$ is *uniquely* a $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination of $S$".

One number-theoretic fact is used: **Bézout's identity**. For integers $a$ and $b$, not both zero, the set of integer combinations $\{xa + yb : x,y\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ is exactly $d\mathbb{Z}$, where $d = \gcd(a,b)$; in particular there exist $x,y$ with $xa + yb = d$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *probe a definition by separating its clauses* problem. "Generates freely" is a conjunction of two requirements — generating, and the lifting/independence property — and the exercise is engineered to display a set that satisfies the first and fails the second. As the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] notes, the way to understand a multi-clause definition is to find a witness for each possible pattern of satisfaction; here the instructive witness is "generates but not freely".

**Assumption pattern.** The hypotheses are entirely concrete: the module is $\mathbb{Z}$, and $S = \{2,3\}$. The relevant feature is that $2$ and $3$ are *coprime* but *neither is a unit*. Coprimality ($\gcd(2,3)=1$) is what makes $S$ generate all of $\mathbb{Z}$. The fact that neither $2$ nor $3$ is $\pm 1$ is what makes each singleton fail to generate. The interplay of these two facts is the whole content.

**Theorem routing.** Statement 1 routes through **Bézout's identity**: $\gcd(2,3)=1$, so $1$ — and hence every integer — is an integer combination of $2$ and $3$. Statement 2 needs no theorem: the relation $3\cdot 2 - 2\cdot 3 = 0$ is exhibited directly, and [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation]] then certifies that a dependent generating set is not a free one. Statement 3 again routes through Bézout in reverse: the singleton $\{n\}$ generates the submodule $n\mathbb{Z}$, which is all of $\mathbb{Z}$ only when $n = \pm 1$; since $2,3\neq\pm 1$, neither singleton generates.

**Key decision point.** The interesting move is recognising *why the vector-space intuition fails here*. Over a field, a dependent relation $\sum r_i s_i = 0$ with some $r_j\neq 0$ lets you solve for $s_j = -r_j^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j} r_i s_i$, expressing $s_j$ through the others — so $s_j$ is genuinely redundant and may be deleted. The relation here is $3\cdot 2 = 2\cdot 3$; to "solve for $2$" you would divide by $3$, and to "solve for $3$" you would divide by $2$ — and in $\mathbb{Z}$ neither division is allowed, because $2$ and $3$ are not units. The non-invertibility of ring elements is precisely the obstruction. Identifying *that* — rather than merely checking the three statements numerically — is the point.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Generate the whole module by producing the unit $1$ as a combination** (the generate-from-a-unit operation). Once $1\in\langle S\rangle$, every integer $n = n\cdot 1$ lies in $\langle S\rangle$ because a submodule is closed under the action. Producing $1$ is achieved by Bézout's identity.

2. **Exhibit a non-trivial relation to defeat linear independence.** To show a generating set is not free, display one $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination $\sum r_i s_i = 0$ with not all $r_i$ zero. Here $r_1 = 3,\,r_2 = -2$ and $(s_1,s_2) = (2,3)$.

3. **Replace freeness by its linear-independence characterisation** (the [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|free-generation equivalence]]). A finite generating set is free if and only if it is linearly independent; the explicit relation therefore certifies non-freeness without touching set functions.

4. **Identify the submodule generated by a singleton.** The submodule $\langle n\rangle$ generated by a single element $n$ of $\mathbb{Z}$ is the principal submodule $n\mathbb{Z} = \{nr : r\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ — the cyclic submodule it spans.

5. **Detect failure of generation by an invariant of the submodule.** To show $\{n\}$ does not generate $\mathbb{Z}$, observe $\langle n\rangle = n\mathbb{Z}$ is a *proper* subset whenever $n\neq\pm 1$ (it omits, for instance, $1$). The index $[\mathbb{Z} : n\mathbb{Z}] = |n|$ is the invariant separating $n\mathbb{Z}$ from $\mathbb{Z}$.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Treat the three statements separately; they need different ideas. For statement 1, you want every integer to be $a\cdot 2 + b\cdot 3$. It suffices to write the integer $1$ this way — why does getting $1$ give you everything? For finding such $a,b$, recall what the integer combinations of two numbers $a,b$ form: the multiples of their greatest common divisor.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> For statement 2, "generates freely" is equivalent (for a finite generating set) to "generates and is linearly independent". You already have "generates" from statement 1. So you only need to break independence: find integers $r_1, r_2$, not both zero, with $r_1\cdot 2 + r_2\cdot 3 = 0$. Look for the smallest such relation — what is the least common multiple of $2$ and $3$?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For statement 3, ask what a *single* element $n$ generates: the submodule $\langle n\rangle = n\mathbb{Z}$, all multiples of $n$. For this to be all of $\mathbb{Z}$ you would need $1\in n\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $n$ divides $1$. Which integers divide $1$? Is $2$ one of them? Is $3$?

> [!note]- Hint 4
> To see *why* you cannot thin $\{2,3\}$ to a basis the way you would for a vector space: the relation is $3\cdot 2 = 2\cdot 3$. In a vector space, a relation lets you solve for one vector in terms of the other — but solving for $2$ here means dividing by $3$, and solving for $3$ means dividing by $2$. Neither $2$ nor $3$ has a multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{Z}$. That missing inverse is exactly the exchange lemma's hidden hypothesis, and it is what fails over a ring that is not a field.

# Solution

The strategy is to verify the three statements and, in doing so, locate the exact reason the vector-space "thin a spanning set to a basis" move breaks: it silently uses that scalars are invertible.

**Step 1: $S = \{2,3\}$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$.**

Since $\gcd(2,3)=1$, Bézout's identity produces $1$ as an integer combination of $2$ and $3$; scaling that combination produces every integer.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **Bézout's identity** states that for integers $a,b$ not both zero, every integer combination $xa + yb$ is a multiple of $d = \gcd(a,b)$, and conversely $d$ itself is attained. Here $a = 2$, $b = 3$, and $d = \gcd(2,3) = 1$. An explicit Bézout combination is
> $$(-1)\cdot 2 + (1)\cdot 3 = 1.$$
> Now let $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ be arbitrary. Multiplying the displayed identity by $n$,
> $$n = (-n)\cdot 2 + (n)\cdot 3,$$
> which is a $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination of $2$ and $3$. Hence every integer lies in the submodule $\langle 2,3\rangle$ generated by $S$, so $\langle 2,3\rangle = \mathbb{Z}$ and $S$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> The structural reading: a submodule containing the *unit* $1$ contains $n\cdot 1 = n$ for every $n$, so it is the whole of $\mathbb{Z}$. Producing $1$ is therefore the entire job, and coprimality is exactly the condition under which Bézout produces it.

**Step 2: $S$ does not generate $\mathbb{Z}$ freely — there is a non-trivial relation.**

The identity $3\cdot 2 + (-2)\cdot 3 = 0$ is a non-trivial $\mathbb{Z}$-linear relation among the elements of $S$, so $S$ is linearly dependent and hence not a free generating set.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Compute directly in $\mathbb{Z}$:
> $$3\cdot 2 + (-2)\cdot 3 = 6 - 6 = 0.$$
> This has the form $r_1\cdot 2 + r_2\cdot 3 = 0$ with $r_1 = 3$ and $r_2 = -2$, and crucially **not both coefficients are zero** — in fact neither is. So the only-the-trivial-relation requirement of [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|linear independence]] fails: $S = \{2,3\}$ is linearly *dependent*.
>
> By [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|the characterisation of free generation]], for a finite generating set "generates freely" is equivalent to "generates **and** is linearly independent". From Step 1 the set $S$ generates, but it is not linearly independent, so it does not generate freely.
>
> The relation is not an accident: $3\cdot 2 = 6 = 2\cdot 3 = \operatorname{lcm}(2,3)$, so the relation says "$2$ and $3$ agree at their least common multiple". This also exhibits the failure of *unique* expressibility: the integer $6$ is both $3\cdot 2 + 0\cdot 3$ and $0\cdot 2 + 2\cdot 3$, two different combinations of the same element — exactly what condition (iii) of the characterisation forbids for a basis.

**Step 3: Neither singleton $\{2\}$ nor $\{3\}$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$.**

A single integer $n$ generates the submodule $n\mathbb{Z}$, which equals $\mathbb{Z}$ only when $n = \pm 1$. Since $2\neq\pm 1$ and $3\neq\pm 1$, neither singleton generates $\mathbb{Z}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The submodule of $\mathbb{Z}$ generated by a single element $n$ is
> $$\langle n\rangle = \{r\cdot n : r\in\mathbb{Z}\} = n\mathbb{Z},$$
> the set of all multiples of $n$. For $\langle n\rangle$ to be the whole module $\mathbb{Z}$ we would need $1\in n\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $1 = rn$ for some integer $r$ — that is, $n$ would have to *divide* $1$. The only integers dividing $1$ are $\pm 1$.
>
> - For $n = 2$: $\langle 2\rangle = 2\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots,-4,-2,0,2,4,\dots\}$, the even integers. This omits $1$, so $\langle 2\rangle\neq\mathbb{Z}$ and $\{2\}$ does not generate $\mathbb{Z}$.
> - For $n = 3$: $\langle 3\rangle = 3\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots,-6,-3,0,3,6,\dots\}$. This omits $1$ (and $2$), so $\langle 3\rangle\neq\mathbb{Z}$ and $\{3\}$ does not generate $\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> Therefore *neither* element of $S$ may be discarded while keeping a generating set: removing $3$ leaves $\{2\}$, removing $2$ leaves $\{3\}$, and both fail. The dependent generating set $\{2,3\}$ has no generating subset other than itself, so in particular it contains no basis (a basis would be a *linearly independent* generating subset, and $\{2,3\}$ is dependent while its proper subsets do not generate).

**Step 4: Why the exchange lemma fails — locating the missing hypothesis.**

The relation $3\cdot 2 = 2\cdot 3$ would, over a field, let us solve for one element through the other; over $\mathbb{Z}$ this requires dividing by $2$ or $3$, which are not units. Non-invertibility of ring elements is the precise obstruction.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Recall the vector-space fact the exercise is contrasting against. If a set $S$ *spans* a vector space $V$ and is linearly dependent, there is a relation $\sum_i r_i s_i = 0$ with some coefficient $r_j\neq 0$. Because the scalars form a *field*, $r_j$ is invertible, so we may solve:
> $$s_j = -r_j^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j} r_i s_i.$$
> This expresses $s_j$ as a combination of the other elements, proving $s_j$ is redundant: $S\setminus\{s_j\}$ still spans. Iterating thins any spanning set down to a basis.
>
> Apply this template to our relation $3\cdot 2 + (-2)\cdot 3 = 0$ over the *ring* $\mathbb{Z}$. To "solve for $2$" we would compute $2 = -3^{-1}\cdot(-2)\cdot 3 = (2/3)\cdot 3$, which is not an integer operation — $3$ has no inverse in $\mathbb{Z}$. To "solve for $3$" we would compute $3 = -2^{-1}\cdot 3\cdot 2 = (3/2)\cdot 2$, again not legal — $2$ has no inverse in $\mathbb{Z}$. The step "$r_j^{-1}$ exists" is the *hidden hypothesis* of the exchange lemma, and it is exactly what a general ring does not supply.
>
> So the dependence relation $3\cdot 2 = 2\cdot 3$ genuinely *fails* to make either element redundant. This is the precise sense in which **the exchange lemma fails over a ring**: a dependent generating set need not contain a basis, because "dependent" no longer means "some element is a combination of the others". It only means "some non-trivial relation holds", and over a non-field the two notions come apart. (Consistently with this, $\mathbb{Z}$ *is* free as a module over itself, with the basis $\{1\}$ — a basis of size one. The set $\{2,3\}$ has size two and is not a basis; this is also a glimpse of *invariance of rank*, [[Thm - Invariance of Rank]]: every basis of $\mathbb{Z}$ has exactly one element, so $\{2,3\}$ could not be a basis on cardinality grounds alone.) $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $S = \{2,3\}$ generates the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}$ but not freely, and no proper subset of $S$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> *Generation.* By Bézout, $(-1)\cdot 2 + 1\cdot 3 = 1$. For any $n\in\mathbb{Z}$, multiplying by $n$ gives $n = (-n)\cdot 2 + n\cdot 3\in\langle 2,3\rangle$. Hence $\langle 2,3\rangle = \mathbb{Z}$.
>
> *Not free.* The identity $3\cdot 2 + (-2)\cdot 3 = 6 - 6 = 0$ is a non-trivial relation (coefficients $3,-2$ not both zero). By the characterisation of free generation, a finite generating set generates freely if and only if it is linearly independent; $\{2,3\}$ is dependent, so it does not generate freely. Equivalently, $6 = 3\cdot 2 + 0\cdot 3 = 0\cdot 2 + 2\cdot 3$ shows expressibility is not unique.
>
> *No generating proper subset.* A single integer $n$ generates $\langle n\rangle = n\mathbb{Z}$, which equals $\mathbb{Z}$ if and only if $n\mid 1$ if and only if $n = \pm 1$. Since $2,3\neq\pm 1$, neither $\{2\}$ nor $\{3\}$ generates $\mathbb{Z}$. Hence $\{2,3\}$ contains no basis: it is itself dependent, and its proper subsets do not generate.
>
> *Exchange lemma fails.* Over a field, a dependence relation $\sum r_i s_i = 0$ with $r_j\neq 0$ yields $s_j = -r_j^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j} r_i s_i$, so $s_j$ is redundant. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the relation $3\cdot 2 = 2\cdot 3$ cannot be solved for either element, since $2$ and $3$ are not units. Thus a dependent generating module-set need not contain a basis. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**"Generates" and "generates freely" are independent clauses — and the gap between them is exactly non-uniqueness.** This exercise is the cleanest demonstration that the definition of a free generating set is a genuine *conjunction*: $\{2,3\}$ satisfies the first clause (generation) and flatly fails the second (independence / unique expressibility). The diagnostic for the second clause is always the same: a generating set fails to be free precisely when *some element of the module has two different representations* as a combination of the generators — here $6 = 3\cdot 2 = 2\cdot 3$. So when you must decide whether a generating set is a basis, do not re-verify generation; instead hunt for a single element with a redundant representation, equivalently a single non-trivial relation $\sum r_i s_i = 0$. The trigger "is this generating set a basis?" should fire the reaction "find two expressions for one element, or certify there is only ever one". This is the operational meaning of condition (iii) in [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation]].

**The exchange lemma has a hidden hypothesis: scalars are invertible.** Every linear-algebra course proves that a dependent spanning set can be thinned to a basis, and that all bases have equal size. Both proofs run on the *exchange* (Steinitz) lemma, whose engine is the step "from $\sum r_i s_i = 0$ with $r_j\neq 0$, solve $s_j = -r_j^{-1}\sum_{i\neq j} r_i s_i$". That single division by $r_j$ is invisible over a field and *fatal* over a ring. The relation $3\cdot 2 = 2\cdot 3$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ is dependent, yet makes *neither* generator redundant, because solving for either requires inverting $2$ or $3$. The general lesson for module theory: a relation $\sum r_i m_i = 0$ does **not**, over a general ring, mean "some $m_j$ is a combination of the others"; it only means "a non-trivial relation exists", and the implication "dependent $\Rightarrow$ some element redundant" needs the coefficients to be units. Whenever you find yourself wanting to "throw away a redundant generator", check whether the relation that justifies it actually has an invertible coefficient on the element you are deleting.

**A single element of $\mathbb{Z}$ generates only its multiples — generation by a unit is special.** Statement 3 turns on a tiny but reusable fact: the submodule $\langle n\rangle$ generated by one element of $\mathbb{Z}$ is the principal submodule $n\mathbb{Z}$, and this is all of $\mathbb{Z}$ exactly when $n$ is a *unit* ($n = \pm 1$). The same phenomenon recurs everywhere: in any ring $R$ viewed as a module over itself, the cyclic submodule $\langle r\rangle = rR$ is the whole ring if and only if $r$ is a unit. This is why "generate from a unit" is a legal operation worth naming — finding the unit $1$ inside a submodule instantly collapses it to the entire ring. It also explains *why* coprimality made $\{2,3\}$ generate: Bézout assembles the unit $1$ out of coprime elements, and once $1$ is in, everything is in. The contrast — neither $2$ nor $3$ alone is a unit, so neither alone generates — is the same fact read in the negative.

**Cardinality is an obstruction to being a basis, via invariance of rank.** Beyond the dependence argument, there is a second, *cardinality* reason $\{2,3\}$ cannot be a basis of $\mathbb{Z}$: the module $\mathbb{Z}$ is free of rank one, with basis $\{1\}$, and [[Thm - Invariance of Rank|invariance of rank]] guarantees that every basis of a free module over a non-zero ring has the *same* number of elements. A two-element set therefore cannot be a basis of a rank-one module, whatever its dependence properties. This is the module-theoretic shadow of "all bases of a vector space have the same [[Def - Dimension|dimension]]", and it survives the passage from fields to [[Def - Ring|rings]] even though the exchange lemma does not — invariance of rank holds over *every* non-zero commutative ring. The takeaway: when asked whether a specific set is a basis, you have two independent lines of attack — a *local* one (find a non-trivial relation) and a *global* one (count, and compare against the known rank). The companion exercise [[Ex - The integers mod two is not a free module]] uses the local/torsion line; this exercise illustrates that both are available and that they probe different aspects of "being a basis".
