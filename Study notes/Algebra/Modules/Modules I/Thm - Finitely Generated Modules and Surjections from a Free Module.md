# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a commutative ring with identity and modules are [[Def - Module|$R$-modules]]. For a finite $k$, the module $R^k = R \oplus \dots \oplus R$ ($k$ copies) is the [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules|direct sum]] of $k$ copies of $R$ regarded as a module over itself; its elements are tuples $(r_1, \dots, r_k)$ with $R$-action $r \cdot (r_1, \dots, r_k) = (rr_1, \dots, rr_k)$. Write $e_i = (0, \dots, 0, 1, 0, \dots, 0) \in R^k$ for the tuple with $1$ in position $i$ and $0$ elsewhere — the **standard generators** of $R^k$. A module $M$ is [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] if $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_k$ for some finite list $m_1, \dots, m_k \in M$, where $Rm = \{r \cdot m : r \in R\}$ is the **cyclic submodule generated by $m$**. The [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]] of $m \in M$ is $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = \{r \in R : r \cdot m = 0\}$, an ideal of $R$. A [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]] is a map of abelian groups commuting with the $R$-action; the double-headed arrow $\twoheadrightarrow$ denotes a *surjective* one. The symbol $\cong$ denotes module isomorphism. The full registry is on the parent page [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2]].

# Statement

> **Finitely Generated Modules as Quotients of Free Modules.** Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $M$ an $R$-module. Then
> $$M \text{ is finitely generated} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \text{there is a surjective module homomorphism } f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M \text{ for some finite } k.$$
>
> **Corollary 1 (Cyclic modules).** For any $m \in M$, the cyclic submodule $Rm$ satisfies
> $$Rm \;\cong\; R/\operatorname{Ann}(m).$$
>
> **Corollary 2 (Quotients stay finitely generated).** If $M$ is finitely generated and $N \leq M$ is any submodule, then the quotient $M/N$ is finitely generated.

---

# Motivation

The definition of a [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated module]] — "$M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_k$ for some finite list of elements" — is concrete and easy to state, but it is awkward to *compute with*. To use it directly you must, every time, write down a generating list and chase $R$-linear combinations $\sum r_i m_i$ by hand. What you would prefer is a way to package "finitely generated" as a *single object*, so that the property becomes a structural fact rather than a recipe to be re-run.

This theorem provides that package. It says: $M$ is finitely generated precisely when $M$ is a *quotient of a free module $R^k$*. The free module $R^k$ is the cleanest, most concrete module there is — tuples of ring elements, with coordinatewise operations — and it is universal: a homomorphism out of $R^k$ is determined by, and may be freely chosen by, where it sends the $k$ standard generators $e_1, \dots, e_k$. So "$M$ is finitely generated" is repackaged as "$M$ is a homomorphic image of something completely understood". This is the same move that the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] makes for quotients: trade an awkward intrinsic description for "image of a homomorphism out of a nice object".

Why is the repackaged form better? Because once you have a surjection $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$, the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] immediately gives $M \cong R^k/\ker f$. The submodule $\ker f$ is called the *relation module* — it records every relation $\sum r_i m_i = 0$ holding among the chosen generators. So a finitely generated module is *completely specified* by two pieces of data: a number $k$ (how many generators) and a submodule $\ker f \leq R^k$ (what relations they satisfy). This is the doorway to [[Def - Finitely Presented Module|finite presentations]] and, downstream, to the entire structure theory of finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain — which subsumes the classification of finitely generated abelian groups and the theory of canonical forms of matrices.

The two corollaries are the immediate dividends. **Corollary 1** handles the simplest case, $k = 1$: a *cyclic* module — one generated by a single element $m$ — is $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. This is the precise sense in which "the cyclic module generated by $m$" is determined by a single ideal, the annihilator. The source flags the analogy out loud: just as the orbit–stabiliser theorem says an orbit is the group modulo a stabiliser subgroup, here a cyclic submodule is the ring modulo an annihilator ideal — rings acting on modules is the analogue of groups acting on sets, and Corollary 1 is the orbit–stabiliser theorem of that analogy. **Corollary 2** records a basic closure property — quotients of finitely generated modules are finitely generated — and its one-line proof is the clearest possible advertisement for why the surjection-from-$R^k$ viewpoint is worth having: the proof is just "compose two surjections".

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The two directions of the theorem have opposite source profiles, so they broaden differently.

For the **"$\Leftarrow$" direction** (a surjection from $R^k$ implies finitely generated), the precondition is "a surjective homomorphism out of a free module". The disguised source is **any presentation or any quotient description of $M$**. If $M$ is given to you as $R^k/K$ for some submodule $K$, then the quotient map $R^k \to R^k/K = M$ is already a surjection from a free module, so $M$ is finitely generated *for free* — no generating set need be exhibited. The non-obvious recognition is that "$M$ is defined as a quotient of $R^k$" is *itself* the hypothesis of this direction; you do not have to do anything. *Example problem:* show that $\mathbb{Z}^3/K$ is a finitely generated abelian group for any subgroup $K$ — immediate, since the quotient map $\mathbb{Z}^3 \twoheadrightarrow \mathbb{Z}^3/K$ is the required surjection.

For the **"$\Rightarrow$" direction** (finitely generated implies a surjection exists), the precondition is "$M$ has a finite generating set". The disguised sources are properties that *force* finite generation without naming a generating set. One such source is **being a quotient of a finitely generated module** (this is Corollary 2 itself). Another is **being a finite module** — if $M$ has finitely many elements, the whole of $M$ is a finite generating set. Another is **being a submodule of $R^n$ when $R$ is Noetherian** — Noetherianity guarantees finite generation of submodules, though that requires machinery beyond this page. The non-obvious step in each case is to *produce a finite generating list* indirectly, then hand it to the construction. *Example problem:* the image of a finitely generated module under any homomorphism is finitely generated — apply $\Rightarrow$ to the source, push the generators forward, apply $\Leftarrow$.

For **Corollary 1**, the disguised source is **a single distinguished element of a module**. Any time a problem hands you one element $m$ and asks about the submodule it generates, or about its annihilator, Corollary 1 converts the question into ring theory: $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. The non-obvious recognition is that "the submodule generated by one element" is never something exotic — it is *always* a quotient of $R$ itself. *Example problem:* identify the cyclic subgroup of an abelian group generated by an element of additive order $n$ — by Corollary 1 with $R = \mathbb{Z}$, it is $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, because $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = n\mathbb{Z}$.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusion is the equivalence "finitely generated $\iff$ surjection from $R^k$". Combined with other facts it does more.

Combine the surjection $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ with **the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]]**. The first theorem upgrades the surjection to an isomorphism $M \cong R^k/\ker f$. The further result is that every finitely generated module is a quotient of a free module by an explicit relation submodule — and if that relation submodule is *itself* finitely generated, $M$ is [[Def - Finitely Presented Module|finitely presented]] and can be described with finitely much paper. This combination is non-obvious because it converts an existence statement (a surjection exists) into a *normal form* (a concrete quotient presentation).

Combine **Corollary 1** with **a hypothesis on the annihilator**. If $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = 0$ then $Rm \cong R$, so $m$ generates a *free* cyclic submodule — a copy of $R$ sitting inside $M$. If $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is a maximal ideal then $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is a *field*, hence a [[Def - Module|simple module]]. The further result is a dictionary: properties of the cyclic submodule $Rm$ are read off from the ideal-theoretic nature of $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. This is non-obvious because it routes a question about a submodule entirely into the ideal lattice of $R$.

Combine **Corollary 2** with **iteration along a chain**. Since every quotient of a finitely generated module is finitely generated, finite generation is inherited all the way down any tower of quotients $M \twoheadrightarrow M/N_1 \twoheadrightarrow (M/N_1)/N_2 \twoheadrightarrow \dots$. The further result is that finite generation is a robust property: it survives every quotient operation, which is exactly what makes it a good finiteness hypothesis to carry through an induction.

A warning belongs here, because it is a *target that does not exist*: finite generation does **not** pass to *submodules*. The source gives the explicit counterexample $R = \mathbb{C}[X_1, X_2, \dots]$, with $M = R$ finitely generated (by $1$) but the submodule $I = (X_1, X_2, \dots)$ not finitely generated. So the closure property is one-sided — quotients yes, submodules no — and Corollary 2 must not be mis-stated.

# Why Is It True

Both directions are, at heart, the single observation that a homomorphism out of $R^k$ *is the same data* as a choice of $k$ elements of the target. Let us see why.

Start with the **"$\Rightarrow$" direction**. Suppose $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_k$. Every element of $M$ is, by the definition of "finitely generated", some $R$-linear combination $r_1 m_1 + \dots + r_k m_k$. Now look at what a tuple $(r_1, \dots, r_k) \in R^k$ *is*: it is exactly a choice of coefficients for such a combination. So there is a completely natural map
$$f : R^k \to M, \qquad (r_1, \dots, r_k) \mapsto r_1 m_1 + \dots + r_k m_k,$$
"plug the coordinates in as coefficients". It is a homomorphism because addition and scaling of tuples is coordinatewise, and that is precisely how the linear combination depends on the coefficients. And it is *surjective* because the definition of "finitely generated" says every element of $M$ already *is* such a combination — the map hits everything by fiat. There is nothing to engineer; the surjection is the definition of finite generation, written as a map.

Now the **"$\Leftarrow$" direction**. Suppose conversely you are handed a surjection $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$. You want a finite generating set for $M$. Where could $k$ generators possibly come from? The free module $R^k$ has $k$ obvious distinguished elements — the standard generators $e_1, \dots, e_k$ — and *they* generate $R^k$, because $(r_1, \dots, r_k) = r_1 e_1 + \dots + r_k e_k$. A surjective homomorphism carries a generating set to a generating set: it carries $e_i$ to $m_i := f(e_i)$, and since $f$ is onto and respects the operations, the images $m_i$ generate the image $M$. Concretely, given $m \in M$, pull it back to some $(r_1, \dots, r_k)$ with $f(r_1, \dots, r_k) = m$, expand that tuple as $\sum r_i e_i$, and apply $f$:
$$m = f\Big(\sum_i r_i e_i\Big) = \sum_i r_i f(e_i) = \sum_i r_i m_i.$$
So $m$ is an $R$-linear combination of the $m_i$, and $M$ is finitely generated. The principle is: **surjections preserve generating sets, and $R^k$ comes with an obvious one of size $k$.**

For **Corollary 1** the picture is the $k = 1$ case made vivid. Fix one element $m \in M$. The "plug in as coefficient" map is now just scaling: $\varphi : R \to M$, $r \mapsto r \cdot m$. Its image is by definition $Rm$, the cyclic submodule. Its kernel is $\{r : r \cdot m = 0\}$ — which is *by definition* the annihilator $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. The first isomorphism theorem then matches the quotient to the image: $R/\operatorname{Ann}(m) \cong Rm$. The intuition is that scaling $m$ by ring elements sweeps out all of $Rm$, and two scalars $r, r'$ produce the same element exactly when $r - r'$ kills $m$ — so the cyclic submodule is the ring with "scalars that act trivially on $m$" folded together. This is genuinely the orbit–stabiliser theorem in disguise: the orbit of $m$ under the $R$-action is $Rm$, the "stabiliser" is $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$, and the orbit is the acting object modulo the stabiliser.

For **Corollary 2** the intuition is the one-line slogan **"a composite of surjections is a surjection"**. If $M$ is finitely generated, the $\Rightarrow$ direction gives a surjection $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$. The quotient map $q : M \twoheadrightarrow M/N$ is surjective for any submodule $N$. Composing,
$$R^k \xrightarrow{\ f\ } M \xrightarrow{\ q\ } M/N$$
is a surjection from a free module onto $M/N$, so the $\Leftarrow$ direction makes $M/N$ finitely generated. Conceptually: $M/N$ is a *more collapsed* version of $M$, and you cannot need more generators to describe something more collapsed — the images of $M$'s generators still generate.

# What Makes This Hard

Neither direction is hard once you see that "homomorphism out of $R^k$" $=$ "choice of $k$ elements of the target", so the genuine difficulty is conceptual rather than technical: recognising that the *definition* of finite generation already *is* a surjection from $R^k$ written in different notation, rather than something that must be cleverly constructed. The one place to slip in the "$\Leftarrow$" direction is the bookkeeping step — pulling $m$ back through $f$, expanding the preimage tuple in the standard generators $e_i$, and *then* applying $f$ and using its homomorphism property; people often try to expand $m$ itself in some basis of $M$, but $M$ need not have a basis at all. The most common error globally is to over-claim the closure property: Corollary 2 is about *quotients*, and the analogous statement for *submodules* is false — the source's counterexample $I = (X_1, X_2, \dots) \leq \mathbb{C}[X_1, X_2, \dots]$ is the canonical reminder.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
The theorem is the statement "a homomorphism $R^k \to M$ is the same as a choice of $k$ elements of $M$, and it is surjective exactly when those elements generate $M$". Prove the two directions by writing down the obvious map in each case. The corollaries then follow by specialising ($k = 1$ for Corollary 1) or composing surjections (Corollary 2), in both cases finishing with the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] where an isomorphism is wanted.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **"$\Rightarrow$": build the evaluation surjection.** Given $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_k$, define $f : R^k \to M$ by $f(r_1, \dots, r_k) = \sum r_i m_i$; show it is a homomorphism and surjective.
   - *Hint:* Operations on $R^k$ are coordinatewise, so $f$ respects them automatically; surjectivity *is* the definition of finitely generated.
   - *Why needed:* Produces the required surjection from a free module.

2. **"$\Leftarrow$": extract generators from a surjection.** Given $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$, set $m_i = f(e_i)$ and show $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_k$.
   - *Hint:* For $m \in M$, choose a preimage $(r_1, \dots, r_k)$, write it as $\sum r_i e_i$, apply $f$, use that $f$ is a homomorphism.
   - *Why needed:* Produces a finite generating set, completing the equivalence.

3. **Corollary 1: the cyclic case.** For $m \in M$ define $\varphi : R \to M$, $\varphi(r) = r \cdot m$; identify $\operatorname{im}\varphi = Rm$ and $\ker\varphi = \operatorname{Ann}(m)$, then apply the first isomorphism theorem.
   - *Hint:* $\operatorname{im}\varphi = Rm$ and $\ker\varphi = \operatorname{Ann}(m)$ are both *definitions*; the only theorem used is $R/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$.
   - *Why needed:* Gives the structural identification $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$.

4. **Corollary 2: quotients stay finitely generated.** Given $M$ finitely generated and $N \leq M$, compose a surjection $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ with the quotient map $q : M \twoheadrightarrow M/N$.
   - *Hint:* A composite of surjections is a surjection; then invoke the "$\Leftarrow$" direction on $q \circ f$.
   - *Why needed:* Establishes the (one-sided) closure of finite generation under quotients.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: The evaluation map out of a free module is a homomorphism
> **Statement:** For any list $m_1, \dots, m_k$ of elements of an $R$-module $M$, the map $f : R^k \to M$, $f(r_1, \dots, r_k) = r_1 m_1 + \dots + r_k m_k$, is a module homomorphism.
>
> **Hint:** Addition and the $R$-action on $R^k$ are coordinatewise; the linear combination depends on the coefficients in exactly the way that makes $f$ respect both.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the map used in the "$\Rightarrow$" direction; without knowing it is a homomorphism, surjectivity alone would not establish anything.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $\mathbf{r} = (r_1, \dots, r_k)$ and $\mathbf{s} = (s_1, \dots, s_k)$ in $R^k$, and $r \in R$. Addition in $R^k$ is coordinatewise, so
> > $$f(\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{s}) = \sum_{i} (r_i + s_i) m_i = \sum_i (r_i m_i + s_i m_i) = \sum_i r_i m_i + \sum_i s_i m_i = f(\mathbf{r}) + f(\mathbf{s}),$$
> > using the module distributive axiom $(r_i + s_i) m_i = r_i m_i + s_i m_i$. The $R$-action on $R^k$ is also coordinatewise, $r \cdot \mathbf{r} = (rr_1, \dots, rr_k)$, so
> > $$f(r \cdot \mathbf{r}) = \sum_i (r r_i) m_i = \sum_i r \cdot (r_i m_i) = r \cdot \sum_i r_i m_i = r \cdot f(\mathbf{r}),$$
> > using the axiom $(r r_i) m_i = r (r_i m_i)$ and then distributivity of the action over module addition. Hence $f$ is a module homomorphism.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: A surjective homomorphism carries generators to generators
> **Statement:** If $f : P \twoheadrightarrow M$ is a surjective module homomorphism and $P = R p_1 + \dots + R p_k$, then $M = R\,f(p_1) + \dots + R\,f(p_k)$.
>
> **Hint:** Pull an element of $M$ back into $P$, express the preimage in the generators of $P$, then push forward through $f$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the engine of the "$\Leftarrow$" direction, applied with $P = R^k$ and $p_i = e_i$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $m \in M$. Since $f$ is surjective, there is $p \in P$ with $f(p) = m$. Since $P = Rp_1 + \dots + Rp_k$, we may write $p = r_1 p_1 + \dots + r_k p_k$ for some $r_i \in R$. Applying $f$ and using that $f$ is a module homomorphism,
> > $$m = f(p) = f\Big(\sum_i r_i p_i\Big) = \sum_i r_i\, f(p_i).$$
> > So $m$ is an $R$-linear combination of $f(p_1), \dots, f(p_k)$. As $m$ was arbitrary, $M = R\,f(p_1) + \dots + R\,f(p_k)$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The standard generators generate the free module
> **Statement:** The standard generators $e_1, \dots, e_k$ generate $R^k$: every element satisfies $(r_1, \dots, r_k) = r_1 e_1 + \dots + r_k e_k$, so $R^k = Re_1 + \dots + Re_k$.
>
> **Hint:** Compute the right-hand side coordinatewise.
>
> **Why needed:** It supplies the size-$k$ generating set of $R^k$ that Lemma 2 then transports through a surjection.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > By definition $e_i$ has $1$ in position $i$ and $0$ elsewhere, so $r_i e_i$ has $r_i$ in position $i$ and $0$ elsewhere. The $R$-action and addition on $R^k$ are coordinatewise, so the sum $r_1 e_1 + \dots + r_k e_k$ has, in position $j$, the entry $\sum_i r_i (e_i)_j = r_j$ (only the $i = j$ term survives). Hence $r_1 e_1 + \dots + r_k e_k = (r_1, \dots, r_k)$, and every element of $R^k$ is such a combination.

> [!note]- Lemma 4: The scaling map of an element has image $Rm$ and kernel $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$
> **Statement:** For $m \in M$, the map $\varphi : R \to M$, $\varphi(r) = r \cdot m$, is a module homomorphism (where $R$ is viewed as a module over itself) with $\operatorname{im}\varphi = Rm$ and $\ker\varphi = \operatorname{Ann}(m)$.
>
> **Hint:** Each of $\operatorname{im}\varphi = Rm$ and $\ker\varphi = \operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is just an unwinding of definitions.
>
> **Why needed:** It sets up Corollary 1: feeding $\varphi$ to the first isomorphism theorem yields $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > $\varphi$ is a homomorphism: $\varphi(r + r') = (r + r')m = rm + r'm = \varphi(r) + \varphi(r')$ by the distributive axiom, and $\varphi(s \cdot r) = (sr)m = s(rm) = s \cdot \varphi(r)$ by the associativity axiom of the action — here $s \cdot r$ is the $R$-action on the module $R$, which is just ring multiplication.
> >
> > **Image.** $\operatorname{im}\varphi = \{\varphi(r) : r \in R\} = \{r \cdot m : r \in R\}$, which is exactly the definition of the cyclic submodule $Rm$.
> >
> > **Kernel.** $\ker\varphi = \{r \in R : \varphi(r) = 0\} = \{r \in R : r \cdot m = 0\}$, which is exactly the definition of $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $M$ an $R$-module.
>
> ---
> **The equivalence.**
>
> *($\Rightarrow$)* Suppose $M$ is finitely generated, say $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_k$. Define
> $$f : R^k \to M, \qquad f(r_1, \dots, r_k) = r_1 m_1 + \dots + r_k m_k.$$
> By Lemma 1, $f$ is a module homomorphism. It is surjective: an arbitrary element of $M$ is, by the definition of finitely generated, of the form $\sum r_i m_i = f(r_1, \dots, r_k)$ for some $r_i \in R$. So $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ is the required surjection from a free module.
>
> *($\Leftarrow$)* Conversely, suppose $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ is a surjective module homomorphism. Put $m_i = f(e_i)$ for $i = 1, \dots, k$, where $e_i$ is the $i$-th standard generator of $R^k$. By Lemma 3, $R^k = Re_1 + \dots + Re_k$. By Lemma 2 (applied with $P = R^k$, $p_i = e_i$), the images generate the image: $M = R m_1 + \dots + R m_k$. Hence $M$ is finitely generated. Explicitly, for $m \in M$ choose $(r_1, \dots, r_k)$ with $f(r_1, \dots, r_k) = m$; then
> $$m = f\Big(\sum_i r_i e_i\Big) = \sum_i r_i f(e_i) = \sum_i r_i m_i.$$
> This completes the equivalence. $\blacksquare$
>
> ---
> **Corollary 1.** Let $m \in M$ and define $\varphi : R \to M$, $\varphi(r) = r \cdot m$, viewing $R$ as a module over itself. By Lemma 4, $\varphi$ is a module homomorphism with $\operatorname{im}\varphi = Rm$ and $\ker\varphi = \operatorname{Ann}(m)$. The [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem for modules]] applied to $\varphi$ gives a module isomorphism
> $$R/\ker\varphi \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}\varphi \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R/\operatorname{Ann}(m) \;\cong\; Rm. \qquad \blacksquare$$
>
> ---
> **Corollary 2.** Let $M$ be finitely generated and $N \leq M$ a submodule. By the "$\Rightarrow$" direction there is a surjective module homomorphism $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$. The quotient map $q : M \to M/N$, $q(x) = x + N$, is a surjective module homomorphism. The composite
> $$q \circ f : R^k \longrightarrow M/N$$
> is a module homomorphism (composites of module homomorphisms are module homomorphisms) and is surjective, being a composite of two surjections: given $y \in M/N$, pick $x \in M$ with $q(x) = y$, then pick $\mathbf{r} \in R^k$ with $f(\mathbf{r}) = x$, so $(q \circ f)(\mathbf{r}) = y$. Thus $q \circ f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M/N$ is a surjection from a free module, and by the "$\Leftarrow$" direction $M/N$ is finitely generated. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Images of finitely generated modules.** Show that if $M$ is finitely generated and $g : M \to M'$ is any module homomorphism, then $\operatorname{im} g$ is finitely generated. The theorem applies because $\operatorname{im} g = M/\ker g$ up to isomorphism (first isomorphism theorem), so $\operatorname{im} g$ is a *quotient* of a finitely generated module — exactly the hypothesis of Corollary 2. The non-obvious recognition is that "the image of a homomorphism" is secretly "a quotient", so a statement that looks like it is about images is really Corollary 2.

**Finitely generated abelian groups.** With $R = \mathbb{Z}$, a finitely generated module is a finitely generated abelian group, and the theorem says every such group is a quotient $\mathbb{Z}^k/K$ of a free abelian group. This is the first step of the classification of finitely generated abelian groups: diagonalising the relation subgroup $K$ by integer row and column operations (Smith normal form) decomposes the group as $\mathbb{Z}^s \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_1\mathbb{Z} \oplus \dots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/d_r\mathbb{Z}$. The non-obvious application is recognising that an entirely group-theoretic classification rests on this module-theoretic packaging.

**The order of an element, via Corollary 1.** In an abelian group $A$ (a $\mathbb{Z}$-module), the cyclic subgroup generated by an element $a$ is $\mathbb{Z}a \cong \mathbb{Z}/\operatorname{Ann}(a)$. Since $\operatorname{Ann}(a) = \{n : na = 0\}$ is the ideal $n_0 \mathbb{Z}$ where $n_0$ is the additive order of $a$ (or $0$ if $a$ has infinite order), Corollary 1 *recovers* the classification of cyclic groups: $\mathbb{Z}a$ is $\mathbb{Z}/n_0\mathbb{Z}$ for finite order and $\mathbb{Z}$ for infinite order. The non-obvious point is that "order of an element" is annihilator data, and the cyclic-group classification is Corollary 1 read for $R = \mathbb{Z}$.

**Linear maps and the $F[X]$-module of a vector space.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$ and $\alpha : V \to V$ a linear map; recall $V$ becomes an $F[X]$-module via $X \cdot v = \alpha(v)$. Then $V$ is finitely generated as an $F[X]$-module (any $F$-basis is an $F[X]$-generating set, since $F \subseteq F[X]$). Corollary 1 applied to a single vector $v$ identifies the cyclic $F[X]$-submodule $F[X] \cdot v$ — the $\alpha$-cyclic subspace through $v$ — as $F[X]/\operatorname{Ann}(v)$, and $\operatorname{Ann}(v)$ is generated by the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ on that cyclic subspace. The non-obvious application is that the theory of minimal polynomials and cyclic subspaces, hence rational canonical form, is the cyclic-module corollary specialised to $R = F[X]$.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|First Isomorphism Theorem for Modules]]** — the constant partner. Every surjection $f : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ produced by this theorem is upgraded by the first isomorphism theorem to an isomorphism $M \cong R^k/\ker f$. Corollary 1 *is* the first isomorphism theorem applied to the scaling map $r \mapsto rm$; the theorem and its corollaries are barely separable from the first isomorphism theorem.

- **[[Def - Free Module|Free Module]]** — the universal object the theorem leans on. $R^k$ is free on the standard generators $e_1, \dots, e_k$; freeness is exactly the property that makes "a homomorphism out of $R^k$" the same as "a choice of $k$ elements of the target". The general statement is in [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation]].

- **[[Def - Finitely Presented Module|Finitely Presented Module]]** — the refinement. Writing $M \cong R^k/\ker f$ exhibits $M$ via generators (the $m_i$) and relations ($\ker f$); $M$ is *finitely presented* when the relation submodule $\ker f$ is itself finitely generated, so that $M$ is described by finitely many generators and finitely many relations.

- **Orbit–Stabiliser Theorem** — the structural analogue from group actions. A group acting on a set has orbits, and an orbit is the group modulo a stabiliser subgroup. A ring acting on a module is the analogue of a group acting on a set; Corollary 1, $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$, is the orbit–stabiliser theorem of that analogy — the "orbit" $Rm$ is the acting ring $R$ modulo the "stabiliser" $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$.

- **[[Thm - Invariance of Rank|Invariance of Rank]]** — a downstream user. Once finitely generated modules are presented as quotients of free modules $R^k$, the natural next question is whether the *number* $k$ for a free module is an invariant; invariance of rank answers this for free modules and underwrites the well-definedness of "rank".

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a PID *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> Packaging a finitely generated module as $M \cong R^k/K$ is step one of the structure theorem. When $R$ is a principal ideal domain, the relation submodule $K$ can be diagonalised (Smith normal form), decomposing $M$ as a direct sum of cyclic modules $R/(d_1) \oplus \dots \oplus R/(d_r) \oplus R^s$. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ this classifies finitely generated abelian groups; over $F[X]$ it produces rational and Jordan canonical forms of matrices.

> [!tip] Noetherian Modules *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> Corollary 2 — quotients of finitely generated modules are finitely generated — is one of the equivalent conditions packaged into the notion of a Noetherian module: a module all of whose submodules are finitely generated. The source's counterexample $(X_1, X_2, \dots) \leq \mathbb{C}[X_1, X_2, \dots]$ is precisely a *non-Noetherian* situation, where finite generation fails to descend to a submodule.
