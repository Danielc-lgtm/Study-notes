# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a commutative ring with identity, assumed **non-zero** (that is, $1_R \neq 0_R$), and modules are [[Def - Module|$R$-modules]]. For finite $n$, the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^n = R \oplus \dots \oplus R$ is the [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules|direct sum]] of $n$ copies of $R$, with elements the $n$-tuples $(r_1, \dots, r_n)$ and coordinatewise $R$-action; $n$ is its **rank**. The relation $\cong$ denotes module isomorphism. An [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $I \trianglelefteq R$ is a [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|maximal ideal]] if $I \neq R$ and no ideal lies strictly between $I$ and $R$; equivalently the quotient ring $R/I$ is a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]]. For an ideal $I$ and a module $M$, write
$$IM = \{a_1 m_1 + \dots + a_t m_t : t \geq 0,\ a_j \in I,\ m_j \in M\}$$
for the **submodule generated by all products $am$ with $a \in I$, $m \in M$** — the smallest submodule containing every such product. The quotient module $M/IM$ then carries an $R/I$-module structure via $(r + I) \cdot (m + IM) = r \cdot m + IM$. A module over a field is a **vector space**, and its **dimension** is the cardinality of any basis. The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2]].

# Statement

> **Invariance of Rank.** Let $R$ be a non-zero commutative ring. If
> $$R^n \;\cong\; R^m$$
> as $R$-modules, then $n = m$.
>
> Consequently the **rank** of a finite-rank free $R$-module — the number of elements in a basis — is a well-defined invariant, independent of the chosen basis.

---

# Motivation

The free module $R^n$ is the cleanest module there is, and the [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|characterisation of free generation]] has just told you that $R^n$ has a basis — the $n$ standard generators. From linear algebra you take for granted that the *number* of basis elements is intrinsic: a vector space does not have a basis of size $3$ and another of size $5$. The word "dimension" presupposes this. The whole language of finitely generated modules presupposes the analogous fact: when you write "the free module of rank $n$", the number $n$ had better be a property of the module, not of the basis you happened to pick.

But this is not obvious, and over general rings it is not automatic — it is a theorem that needs proof. The danger is concrete. An isomorphism $R^n \cong R^m$ is, after choosing bases, an invertible $m \times n$ matrix over $R$ with an $n \times m$ inverse. Over a *field* the rank of a matrix forbids a non-square invertible matrix, so $n = m$. Over a general commutative ring there is no immediately available rank theory for matrices — the determinant exists but its vanishing behaviour is subtler — so the argument that works for fields does not transcribe. One genuinely needs a reason that the number $n$ cannot lie. (The hypothesis $R \neq 0$ is essential and shows the result is not vacuous: over the zero ring *every* module is the zero module, so $R^n \cong R^m$ for *all* $n, m$, and rank is meaningless. Non-triviality of $R$ is exactly what makes rank an honest invariant.)

This theorem supplies the reason, and the strategy is the one worth remembering far beyond this result: **reduce the unknown case to the known case by quotienting**. We *know* the statement for fields — it is invariance of dimension for vector spaces. So manufacture a field out of $R$ and transport the isomorphism into vector spaces over that field. A non-zero commutative ring always has a maximal ideal $I$, and $R/I$ is a field. Reducing every module $M$ modulo $I$ — passing from $M$ to $M/IM$ — is a construction that turns $R$-modules into $R/I$-modules, i.e. into *vector spaces over the field $R/I$*, and it turns $R^n$ into the $n$-dimensional vector space $(R/I)^n$. An isomorphism $R^n \cong R^m$ survives the reduction to give $(R/I)^n \cong (R/I)^m$, and now invariance of dimension for vector spaces — already known — forces $n = m$. The proof is a single application of the "kill the ring down to a field" technique.

Why care that rank is well-defined? Because the entire theory of finitely generated modules is organised around free modules and their ranks. The [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|free-quotient theorem]] presents every finitely generated module as $R^k/K$; the structure theorem for modules over a principal ideal domain decomposes a module as a free part $R^s$ plus torsion, and the number $s$ is called the *free rank*; the classification of finitely generated abelian groups assigns each group a *Betti number*. Every one of these numerical invariants is meaningless unless rank is well-defined for free modules. Invariance of rank is the small theorem that licenses all of that numbering.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The literal precondition is a module isomorphism $R^n \cong R^m$ between two finite-rank free modules. The skill is recognising when a problem secretly presents one.

The first disguised source is **an invertible matrix over $R$**. An $m \times n$ matrix $A$ over $R$ with an $n \times m$ inverse $B$ ($AB = I_m$, $BA = I_n$) *is* a pair of mutually inverse module homomorphisms $R^n \rightleftarrows R^m$, hence an isomorphism $R^n \cong R^m$. The non-obvious recognition is that "invertible rectangular matrix over a ring" is the same data as "isomorphism of free modules", so invariance of rank says **a two-sided-invertible matrix over a non-zero commutative ring must be square**. *Example problem:* show there is no $2 \times 3$ matrix over $\mathbb{Z}$ with a $3 \times 2$ two-sided inverse — immediate, since that would give $\mathbb{Z}^3 \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$.

The second disguised source is **two finite bases of the same free module**. If a module $M$ is free and has a basis of size $n$ *and* a basis of size $m$, then (by the [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|universal property]]) $M \cong R^n$ and $M \cong R^m$, so $R^n \cong R^m$. The non-obvious recognition is that "this free module has two bases of different sizes" is itself the hypothesis $R^n \cong R^m$ in disguise — and the theorem forbids it. *Example problem:* prove a free module cannot simultaneously have a basis of $4$ elements and a basis of $7$.

The third disguised source is **a free module presented in two different ways inside a larger structure** — for instance a free module arising as a direct summand under two different decompositions, or as the underlying module of a ring expressed as a free module over a subring in two ways (the rank then being the *degree* of a ring or field extension). The non-obvious step is to extract from the structural data an explicit isomorphism between two $R^k$'s. *Example problem:* the degree of a finite field extension is well-defined because the larger field is a free module over the smaller and any two bases have the same size.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusion is the numerical equality $n = m$. Combined with other facts it does more.

Combine the conclusion with **the definition of rank as a function on free modules**. Once $R^n \cong R^m \Rightarrow n = m$, the assignment "free module $\mapsto$ size of a basis" is a well-defined function $\operatorname{rank}$, and isomorphic free modules have equal rank. The further result is that rank becomes a usable *invariant*: to prove two free modules are non-isomorphic it now suffices to compute two numbers and see they differ — a finite, mechanical certificate of non-isomorphism where otherwise one would need to rule out all possible isomorphisms.

Combine the conclusion with **a short exact sequence of free modules**. If $0 \to R^a \to R^b \to R^c \to 0$ is exact with all three terms free, then $R^b \cong R^a \oplus R^c \cong R^{a+c}$ (the sequence splits because $R^c$ is free), so invariance of rank gives $b = a + c$ — rank is *additive* on short exact sequences of free modules. The further result is a conservation law: rank behaves like a dimension, adding across extensions, and this additivity is what makes Euler-characteristic-style counting arguments work in homological algebra.

Combine the conclusion with **the structure theory over a principal ideal domain**. When $R$ is a PID, every finitely generated module decomposes as $R^s \oplus (\text{torsion})$. Invariance of rank guarantees the free part's rank $s$ is well-defined, so $s$ — the *free rank* or *Betti number* — is a genuine invariant of the module. The further result: the free rank becomes a complete-invariant ingredient in the classification of finitely generated modules over a PID, hence of finitely generated abelian groups.

# Why Is It True

The intuition is a single sentence: **fields are the case we understand, so push the problem into a field.**

Here is the obstacle, stated honestly. For vector spaces, "the number of basis vectors is well-defined" is invariance of dimension, and we have it. For modules over a general ring $R$ we do *not* have a ready-made dimension theory — there is no rank function for matrices over an arbitrary commutative ring that behaves as cleanly as over a field. So we cannot argue directly. The move is to *not* argue directly: instead, find inside $R$ a field, and use it as a measuring instrument.

Every non-zero commutative ring contains a maximal ideal $I$, and the defining virtue of a *maximal* ideal is that the quotient ring $R/I$ is a **field**. (Existence of a maximal ideal is a Zorn's-lemma argument: proper ideals are exactly those missing $1_R$, an increasing union of proper ideals still misses $1_R$ so is still proper, and Zorn produces a maximal one.) So $R$, however exotic, always comes equipped with at least one field $R/I$ that it surjects onto.

Now the key device: a way to turn $R$-modules into $R/I$-modules, i.e. into vector spaces over that field. Given an $R$-module $M$, form $IM$, the submodule of all $I$-multiples of elements of $M$, and pass to the quotient $M/IM$. The point of dividing by $IM$ is precisely that it makes the elements of $I$ act as zero: for $a \in I$ and $m \in M$, the product $am$ lies in $IM$, so $a \cdot (m + IM) = am + IM = 0$. Once $I$ acts as zero, the action of $R$ on $M/IM$ only depends on $R$ modulo $I$ — so $M/IM$ is naturally a module over $R/I$, that is, a **vector space over the field $R/I$**. We have built a machine: feed in an $R$-module, get out an $R/I$-vector space.

What does the machine do to $R^n$? It should — and does — return the $n$-dimensional vector space. Reducing $R^n$ modulo $I$ collapses each coordinate from $R$ to $R/I$, giving $R^n/IR^n \cong (R/I)^n$, the standard $n$-dimensional vector space over the field $R/I$. (Concretely $IR^n = I \oplus \dots \oplus I$, so the quotient is $(R/I) \oplus \dots \oplus (R/I)$.)

Finally, the machine is *functorial enough to carry isomorphisms*: an isomorphism of $R$-modules induces an isomorphism of the reduced $R/I$-modules, because the construction $M \mapsto M/IM$ is canonical and a module isomorphism $R^n \cong R^m$ maps $IR^n$ onto $IR^m$, descending to an isomorphism of quotients. So from $R^n \cong R^m$ we obtain $(R/I)^n \cong (R/I)^m$ — an isomorphism of *vector spaces* over the field $R/I$. And for vector spaces we already know rank is invariant: $(R/I)^n \cong (R/I)^m$ forces $n = m$ by invariance of dimension.

That is the whole proof, and the reason to expect the theorem is exactly this: the only obstruction to invariance of rank is the absence of a dimension theory over $R$, and a maximal ideal hands you a field — hence a dimension theory — for free. The non-triviality hypothesis $R \neq 0$ enters at precisely one spot: a maximal ideal is by definition *proper*, so it can only exist when $R$ has a proper ideal at all, which needs $1_R \neq 0_R$. Over the zero ring there is no field to reduce to, and indeed rank is not invariant there.

# What Makes This Hard

The hard part is strategic, not computational: the instinct is to attack $R^n \cong R^m$ head-on with matrices or determinants over $R$, which stalls because a general commutative ring has no clean matrix-rank theory — the unlock is the lateral move of *quotienting $R$ down to a field* rather than working over $R$. The single non-obvious construction is $M \mapsto M/IM$ and the recognition that dividing by $IM$ is exactly what forces the maximal ideal $I$ to act as zero, thereby converting an $R$-module into an $R/I$-vector space. The most common errors are forgetting the hypothesis $R \neq 0$ (without which no maximal ideal exists and the theorem is false), and asserting $R^n/IR^n \cong (R/I)^n$ without checking that reduction commutes with the direct sum.

---

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Do not work over $R$ directly. Choose a maximal ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$, so $R/I$ is a field. Build the reduction functor $M \mapsto M/IM$ that turns $R$-modules into $R/I$-vector spaces, check it sends $R^n$ to $(R/I)^n$ and carries the isomorphism $R^n \cong R^m$ to $(R/I)^n \cong (R/I)^m$, then finish with invariance of dimension for vector spaces.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Secure a field quotient.** Show $R$ has a maximal ideal $I$, so that $R/I$ is a field.
   - *Hint:* Proper ideals are those omitting $1_R$; an increasing union of proper ideals is proper; apply Zorn's lemma. Uses $R \neq 0$.
   - *Why needed:* The field $R/I$ is the measuring instrument; without it there is no dimension theory to invoke.

2. **Build the reduction construction.** For an ideal $I$ and $R$-module $M$, define $IM \leq M$ and show $M/IM$ is an $R/I$-module via $(r+I)(m+IM) = rm + IM$.
   - *Hint:* Well-definedness needs: if $r - r' \in I$ then $(r - r')m \in IM$. Elements of $I$ act as zero on $M/IM$.
   - *Why needed:* This is the machine converting $R$-modules to $R/I$-vector spaces.

3. **Reduce the free module.** Show $R^n/IR^n \cong (R/I)^n$ as $R/I$-modules.
   - *Hint:* $IR^n = I \oplus \dots \oplus I$, so the quotient is $(R/I) \oplus \dots \oplus (R/I)$ — reduction commutes with finite direct sums.
   - *Why needed:* Identifies the reduced free module as a standard $n$-dimensional vector space.

4. **Transport the isomorphism.** Show $R^n \cong R^m$ implies $(R/I)^n \cong (R/I)^m$ as $R/I$-modules.
   - *Hint:* A module isomorphism $\phi : R^n \to R^m$ satisfies $\phi(IR^n) = IR^m$, so it descends to an isomorphism of quotients; combine with step 3.
   - *Why needed:* Moves the problem from $R$-modules into vector spaces over the field $R/I$.

5. **Apply invariance of dimension.** Conclude $n = m$ from $(R/I)^n \cong (R/I)^m$.
   - *Hint:* $R/I$ is a field, so this is invariance of dimension for vector spaces — the known case.
   - *Why needed:* Delivers the numerical equality, completing the proof.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: A non-zero commutative ring has a maximal ideal
> **Statement:** Every non-zero commutative ring $R$ possesses a maximal ideal.
>
> **Hint:** Order the proper ideals by inclusion; a chain has the union as an upper bound, still proper; invoke Zorn's lemma.
>
> **Why needed:** It supplies the field $R/I$ into which the whole problem is reduced.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > An ideal $J \trianglelefteq R$ is *proper* exactly when $J \neq R$, and since $J = R$ holds if and only if $1_R \in J$ (an ideal containing a unit contains everything), $J$ is proper if and only if $1_R \notin J$.
> >
> > Let $\mathcal{P}$ be the set of proper ideals of $R$, partially ordered by inclusion. It is non-empty: the zero ideal $\{0\}$ is proper because $R \neq 0$ means $1_R \neq 0_R$, so $1_R \notin \{0\}$. Let $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{P}$ be a non-empty chain (totally ordered subset). Put $U = \bigcup_{J \in \mathcal{C}} J$. Then $U$ is an ideal: for $x, y \in U$ there are $J_1, J_2 \in \mathcal{C}$ with $x \in J_1$, $y \in J_2$, and since $\mathcal{C}$ is a chain one contains the other, say $J_1 \subseteq J_2$, so $x, y \in J_2$ and $x - y \in J_2 \subseteq U$; and for $r \in R$, $x \in J_1$ gives $rx \in J_1 \subseteq U$. Moreover $U$ is proper: if $1_R \in U$ then $1_R \in J$ for some $J \in \mathcal{C}$, contradicting properness of $J$. So $U \in \mathcal{P}$ is an upper bound for $\mathcal{C}$.
> >
> > Every chain in $\mathcal{P}$ thus has an upper bound in $\mathcal{P}$. By Zorn's lemma $\mathcal{P}$ has a maximal element $I$ — a proper ideal with no proper ideal strictly containing it, i.e. a maximal ideal of $R$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: $M/IM$ is a module over $R/I$
> **Statement:** Let $I \trianglelefteq R$ be an ideal and $M$ an $R$-module. Then $IM = \{\sum_j a_j m_j : a_j \in I,\ m_j \in M\}$ is a submodule of $M$, and the quotient module $M/IM$ becomes an $R/I$-module under $(r + I) \cdot (m + IM) = (r \cdot m) + IM$.
>
> **Hint:** Well-definedness of the action needs: changing $r$ by an element of $I$, or $m$ by an element of $IM$, changes $rm$ only by an element of $IM$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the reduction machine; when $I$ is maximal, $R/I$ is a field and $M/IM$ is a vector space.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **$IM$ is a submodule.** It contains $0$. It is closed under subtraction: a difference of two finite sums $\sum a_j m_j$ is again such a sum (with the coefficients of the second negated, and $-a_j \in I$). It absorbs the $R$-action: for $r \in R$, $r \cdot \sum_j a_j m_j = \sum_j (r a_j) m_j$, and $r a_j \in I$ because $I$ is an ideal. So $IM \leq M$ and the quotient module $M/IM$ exists.
> >
> > **The $R/I$-action is well-defined.** We must check $(r + I)(m + IM) := rm + IM$ does not depend on the representatives $r$ and $m$. Suppose $r + I = r' + I$ and $m + IM = m' + IM$, i.e. $r - r' \in I$ and $m - m' \in IM$. Then
> > $$rm - r'm' = r(m - m') + (r - r')m'.$$
> > The first term $r(m - m')$ lies in $IM$ because $IM$ is a submodule and $m - m' \in IM$. The second term $(r - r')m'$ is a single product of an element $r - r' \in I$ with an element of $M$, hence lies in $IM$ by definition. So $rm - r'm' \in IM$, giving $rm + IM = r'm' + IM$. The action is well-defined.
> >
> > **The module axioms** for $R/I$ on $M/IM$ are inherited directly from those for $R$ on $M$, each equation holding on representatives. Hence $M/IM$ is an $R/I$-module. In particular, for $a \in I$ the class $a + I = 0$ in $R/I$, so $a$ annihilates $M/IM$: $a \cdot (m + IM) = am + IM = 0 + IM$ since $am \in IM$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Reduction commutes with finite direct sums
> **Statement:** Let $I \trianglelefteq R$ be an ideal. Then there is an $R/I$-module isomorphism
> $$R^n / IR^n \;\cong\; (R/I)^n.$$
>
> **Hint:** Show $IR^n = I^n$ (the tuples with every entry in $I$); then the quotient is the direct sum of the coordinate quotients $R/I$.
>
> **Why needed:** It identifies the reduced free module as the standard $n$-dimensional $R/I$-vector space.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > First, $IR^n = I \oplus \dots \oplus I$ ($n$ copies), i.e. the set of tuples $(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ with every $a_i \in I$. Indeed any generator $a \cdot (r_1, \dots, r_n) = (ar_1, \dots, ar_n)$ with $a \in I$ has all coordinates in $I$, and finite sums of such tuples still have all coordinates in $I$; conversely a tuple $(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ with each $a_i \in I$ equals $\sum_i a_i \cdot e_i$, a sum of $I$-multiples of elements of $R^n$. So $IR^n = \{(a_1, \dots, a_n) : a_i \in I \text{ for all } i\}$.
> >
> > Now define $\pi : R^n \to (R/I)^n$ by $\pi(r_1, \dots, r_n) = (r_1 + I, \dots, r_n + I)$. This is a surjective $R$-module homomorphism (coordinatewise reduction), and
> > $$\ker\pi = \{(r_1, \dots, r_n) : r_i + I = 0 \text{ for all } i\} = \{(r_1, \dots, r_n) : r_i \in I\} = IR^n.$$
> > By the first isomorphism theorem for modules, $R^n/IR^n \cong (R/I)^n$ as $R$-modules; since $I$ annihilates both sides, this is equally an isomorphism of $R/I$-modules.

> [!note]- Lemma 4: A free-module isomorphism descends to the reductions
> **Statement:** Let $I \trianglelefteq R$ be an ideal. If $\phi : R^n \to R^m$ is an $R$-module isomorphism, then $\phi$ induces an $R/I$-module isomorphism $R^n/IR^n \cong R^m/IR^m$.
>
> **Hint:** Show $\phi(IR^n) = IR^m$, so $\phi$ maps the submodule defining one quotient onto the submodule defining the other and descends to the quotients.
>
> **Why needed:** It transports the hypothesis $R^n \cong R^m$ across the reduction, turning it into an isomorphism of $R/I$-vector spaces.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Because $\phi$ is a module homomorphism, $\phi(IR^n) \subseteq IR^m$: a generator $a \cdot x$ of $IR^n$ (with $a \in I$, $x \in R^n$) is sent to $\phi(a x) = a \phi(x) \in IR^m$, and $\phi$ of a finite sum is the finite sum of the images. Applying the same reasoning to the inverse isomorphism $\phi^{-1}$ gives $\phi^{-1}(IR^m) \subseteq IR^n$, hence $IR^m \subseteq \phi(IR^n)$. Therefore $\phi(IR^n) = IR^m$.
> >
> > Consequently $\phi$ descends to a well-defined map of quotients
> > $$\bar\phi : R^n/IR^n \to R^m/IR^m, \qquad \bar\phi(x + IR^n) = \phi(x) + IR^m,$$
> > well-defined because $x - x' \in IR^n \Rightarrow \phi(x) - \phi(x') = \phi(x - x') \in \phi(IR^n) = IR^m$. The map $\bar\phi$ is an $R$-module homomorphism (it is induced by one), and it is $R/I$-linear since $I$ annihilates both quotients. Its two-sided inverse is $\overline{\phi^{-1}}$, induced identically by $\phi^{-1}$ — one checks $\overline{\phi^{-1}} \circ \bar\phi$ and $\bar\phi \circ \overline{\phi^{-1}}$ are the identity on representatives. So $\bar\phi$ is an $R/I$-module isomorphism $R^n/IR^n \cong R^m/IR^m$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a non-zero commutative ring and suppose $R^n \cong R^m$ as $R$-modules; fix an $R$-module isomorphism $\phi : R^n \to R^m$.
>
> **Step 1 — choose a field quotient.** By Lemma 1, $R$ has a maximal ideal $I$. Since $I$ is maximal, the quotient ring $R/I$ is a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]]. (Maximality of $I$ means there is no ideal strictly between $I$ and $R$; under the ideal-correspondence this says $R/I$ has no proper non-zero ideal, which for a non-zero commutative ring is equivalent to being a field.)
>
> **Step 2 — reduce modulo $I$.** For any $R$-module $M$, Lemma 2 makes $M/IM$ into a module over the field $R/I$ — that is, an $R/I$-**vector space** — via $(r + I)(m + IM) = rm + IM$.
>
> **Step 3 — identify the reduced free modules.** By Lemma 3,
> $$R^n/IR^n \;\cong\; (R/I)^n \qquad\text{and}\qquad R^m/IR^m \;\cong\; (R/I)^m$$
> as $R/I$-vector spaces. So reduction sends the rank-$n$ free $R$-module to the $n$-dimensional $R/I$-vector space, and likewise for $m$.
>
> **Step 4 — transport the isomorphism.** By Lemma 4, the isomorphism $\phi : R^n \cong R^m$ descends to an $R/I$-module isomorphism $R^n/IR^n \cong R^m/IR^m$. Combining with Step 3,
> $$(R/I)^n \;\cong\; R^n/IR^n \;\cong\; R^m/IR^m \;\cong\; (R/I)^m,$$
> an isomorphism of vector spaces over the field $R/I$.
>
> **Step 5 — apply invariance of dimension.** The field $R/I$ being a field, $(R/I)^n$ is an $n$-dimensional vector space and $(R/I)^m$ an $m$-dimensional one. Invariance of dimension for vector spaces — the known case, where any two bases of a vector space have the same cardinality — states that isomorphic finite-dimensional vector spaces have equal dimension. Hence
> $$n = \dim_{R/I}(R/I)^n = \dim_{R/I}(R/I)^m = m.$$
>
> Therefore $n = m$, and the rank of a finite-rank free $R$-module is well-defined. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Invertible rectangular matrices over a ring must be square.** Show that if $A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix and $B$ an $n \times m$ matrix over a non-zero commutative ring $R$ with $AB = I_m$ and $BA = I_n$, then $m = n$. The theorem applies because $A$ and $B$ are mutually inverse $R$-module homomorphisms $R^n \rightleftarrows R^m$, so they constitute an isomorphism $R^n \cong R^m$. The non-obvious recognition is that a purely matrix-algebraic statement ("two-sided-invertible matrices are square") is invariance of rank in disguise — and that it needs $R \neq 0$, since over the zero ring every matrix is invertible of every shape.

**Well-definedness of the degree of a field extension.** Let $K \subseteq L$ be a field extension. Then $L$ is a vector space over $K$, hence a free $K$-module, and the degree $[L : K]$ is the size of a basis. That the degree is well-defined is invariance of rank for $R = K$ (here it is literally invariance of dimension, the field case the theorem reduces to). The non-obvious framing is that "the degree of a field extension does not depend on the basis chosen" is the field instance of the very same well-definedness statement; the general theorem extends this guarantee from fields to all non-zero commutative rings.

**The Betti number of a finitely generated abelian group.** A finitely generated abelian group $A$ — a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module — decomposes as $\mathbb{Z}^s \oplus T$ with $T$ finite (torsion). The integer $s$, the *Betti number* or *free rank*, is well-defined precisely because invariance of rank (with $R = \mathbb{Z}$) makes the rank of the free summand $\mathbb{Z}^s$ an invariant. The non-obvious application is that a numerical invariant central to the classification of finitely generated abelian groups rests on this theorem; without it, "the number of $\mathbb{Z}$-summands" would be ambiguous.

**Ranks of free modules in a split short exact sequence.** Suppose $0 \to R^a \to R^b \to R^c \to 0$ is a short exact sequence of free $R$-modules over a non-zero commutative ring. Because $R^c$ is free, the sequence splits, so $R^b \cong R^a \oplus R^c \cong R^{a+c}$; invariance of rank then forces $b = a + c$. The non-obvious recognition is that a homological constraint becomes an exact numerical identity once invariance of rank is available — rank is *additive on short exact sequences of free modules*, the prototype of every Euler-characteristic argument in homological algebra.

# Bridges

- **Invariance of Dimension for Vector Spaces** — the special case and the engine of the proof. For $R$ a field, invariance of rank *is* invariance of dimension; the general theorem is proved by *reducing to* this case via a maximal ideal. The relationship is not analogy but literal dependence: the field case is invoked verbatim in the last line.

- **[[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|Characterisations of Free Generation]]** — the prerequisite that makes the statement meaningful. That theorem establishes what a basis of a free module is and (via the universal property) that a free module with a basis of size $n$ is isomorphic to $R^n$; invariance of rank then certifies that the number $n$ attached to the basis is intrinsic. Together they make "rank" a well-defined function on free modules.

- **[[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|Finitely Generated Modules as Quotients of Free Modules]]** — the downstream beneficiary. That theorem presents every finitely generated module as $R^k/K$; invariance of rank is what guarantees the free modules appearing in such presentations carry a well-defined rank, so that numerical invariants extracted from presentations are meaningful.

- **[[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|First Isomorphism Theorem for Modules]]** — used inside the proof. The identification $R^n/IR^n \cong (R/I)^n$ is obtained by applying the first isomorphism theorem to the coordinatewise reduction map $R^n \to (R/I)^n$, whose kernel is exactly $IR^n$.

- **[[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|Maximal Ideals]] and the residue field** — the structural input. The construction $M \mapsto M/IM$ for $I$ maximal is *extension of scalars along the quotient $R \twoheadrightarrow R/I$*, sending $R$-modules to vector spaces over the residue field $R/I$. This passage to a residue field is a recurring device throughout commutative algebra and algebraic geometry, where it computes the "fibre" of a module at a point.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Rank of a Module over an Integral Domain *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> For a module over an integral domain $R$, the rank is defined as the dimension of the vector space obtained by extending scalars to the field of fractions $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$. Invariance of rank for free modules is the consistency check that makes this more general notion of rank agree with the naive "size of a basis" whenever a basis exists.

> [!tip] Euler Characteristic and Homology *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> When a chain complex consists of free modules of finite rank, the alternating sum of the ranks — the Euler characteristic — is a well-defined invariant precisely because rank is well-defined and additive on short exact sequences. Invariance of rank is the algebraic foundation under the topological fact that the Euler characteristic does not depend on the chosen cell decomposition.
