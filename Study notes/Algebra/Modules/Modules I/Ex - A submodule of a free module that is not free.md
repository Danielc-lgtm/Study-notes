# Problem Statement

Let $k$ be a field and let $R = k[X,Y]$ be the polynomial ring in two indeterminates. View $R$ as a module over itself — the **free** $R$-module of rank one, with basis $\{1\}$. Inside $R$ sits the ideal
$$I = (X,Y) = \{\,f X + g Y : f,g\in R\,\},$$
the set of polynomials with zero constant term. Being an ideal, $I$ is in particular an $R$-**submodule** of $R$.

Prove that $I$ is **not a free $R$-module**. Concretely, show that $I$ is generated by $\{X,Y\}$ but by no single element, and that the two generators satisfy the non-trivial relation
$$Y\cdot X - X\cdot Y = 0,$$
so that no subset of $I$ is linearly independent and generating. Consequently $I$ is a submodule of a free module that fails to be free — this cannot happen over a principal ideal domain, where *every* submodule of a free module is free, and pinpoints where $k[X,Y]$ differs from a PID.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the ring $R = k[X,Y]$, the free $R$-module $R$, the ideal-cum-submodule $I = (X,Y)$, and the notion of a *free* module.

![[Def - Free Module#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] $R = k[X,Y]$ consists of finite $k$-linear combinations of monomials $X^a Y^b$; it is a commutative ring, an integral domain, with the constant polynomials forming a copy of $k$. An [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $I\trianglelefteq R$ is an additive subgroup absorbing multiplication by all of $R$; the ideal $(X,Y)$ generated by $X$ and $Y$ is $\{fX + gY : f,g\in R\}$. Every ideal of $R$ is automatically an $R$-[[Def - Submodule|submodule]] of $R$, since the submodule axioms (subgroup, closed under the $R$-action) are exactly the ideal axioms.

A subset $S = \{m_1,\dots,m_k\}$ **generates the module $M$ freely**, and is a **basis**, when it generates $M$ and every set function $S\to N$ extends to a module homomorphism $M\to N$; a module with a basis is **free**. By [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|the characterisation of free generation]], for finite $S$ this is equivalent to "$S$ generates $M$ and is **linearly independent**" — the only relation $\sum r_i m_i = 0$ is the trivial one — and to "every element of $M$ is *uniquely* an $R$-linear combination of $S$". An element $m\in M$ with $r\cdot m = 0$ for some non-zero $r\in R$ is **torsion**.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *prove a module is not free* problem, sharpened: the module is *torsion-free* (it sits inside the domain $R$, which has no zero divisors), so the obstruction cannot be torsion. As the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] explains, when torsion is unavailable the way to defeat freeness is to show that *every* generating set carries a non-trivial relation — the module needs at least two generators, and any two generators of a *commutative*-ring ideal automatically relate.

**Assumption pattern.** The hypotheses: $R = k[X,Y]$ is a commutative integral domain, and $I = (X,Y)$ is the ideal of polynomials with zero constant term. Two features drive the proof. First, $I$ is *not principal* — it cannot be generated by one element — so any generating set has size at least two. Second, $R$ is *commutative*, so for any two elements $u,v$ of $I$ the relation $v\cdot u - u\cdot v = 0$ holds: a commutative ring manufactures a relation between any pair. The pairing "needs $\ge 2$ generators" $+$ "any $2$ generators relate" is the assumption pattern, and it is fatal to freeness.

**Theorem routing.** The route is the equivalence "free $\iff$ generating and linearly independent" of [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation]], used in the contrapositive. A basis would be a linearly independent generating set. A generating set of size $\ge 2$ is automatically dependent (the commutator relation), so a basis would have size $\le 1$ — but a single element generates only a principal ideal, and $I$ is not principal. Both possible basis sizes are excluded, so there is no basis.

**Key decision point.** The crux is realising that the *commutativity* of $R$ is what kills freeness, and that it does so for *any* two generators at once. A novice might try a specific generating set and exhibit a relation, then worry that a cleverer generating set might avoid it. The decisive observation closes that gap: for *any* $u,v\in I$ the commutator $vu - uv = 0$ is a relation, and it is non-trivial whenever $u,v$ are both non-zero — so *no* generating set of size $\ge 2$ can be independent. Combined with non-principality (no generating set of size $\le 1$), the impossibility is total. Spotting that the relation is *universal*, not incidental, is the insight.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Read off a generating set from the ideal's definition.** The ideal $(X,Y)$ is by construction generated by $\{X,Y\}$ as an $R$-module — $\{fX + gY\}$ is exactly the set of $R$-linear combinations of $X$ and $Y$.

2. **Defeat principality by a constant-term / degree argument.** To show no single element generates $I$, suppose $I = (h)$ and derive a contradiction: $h$ would have to divide both $X$ and $Y$, forcing $h$ to be a non-zero constant, whence $(h) = R\neq I$.

3. **Manufacture a relation from commutativity** (the commutator operation). In a commutative ring, any two elements $u,v$ satisfy $vu - uv = 0$. Applied to $u = X$, $v = Y$ this is the relation $Y\cdot X - X\cdot Y = 0$; applied to an arbitrary pair of generators it shows every generating set of size $\ge 2$ is dependent.

4. **Replace freeness by its linear-independence characterisation** (the [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|free-generation equivalence]]). Convert "is $I$ free?" into "does $I$ have a linearly independent generating set?", which the relation and non-principality jointly refute.

5. **Eliminate a class by ruling out every possible basis size.** A basis of $I$ would have some finite size $s$. Size $s\le 1$ is excluded by non-principality; size $s\ge 2$ is excluded by the commutator relation. No size survives, so no basis exists.

6. **Contrast against a structural theorem to locate the cause.** Compare with "over a PID, every submodule of a free module is free": the failure here isolates the property of $R$ that breaks — $k[X,Y]$ is not a principal ideal domain.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> $I$ lives inside the integral domain $R = k[X,Y]$, so $I$ is torsion-free — no non-zero element is killed by a non-zero scalar. The torsion argument that defeats $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is therefore unavailable. You need a different obstruction. Think about how *many* elements are needed to generate $I$, and what relations any generating set must satisfy.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> First show $I$ cannot be generated by a single element. If $I = (h)$ for one polynomial $h$, then $h$ divides every element of $I$, in particular $h\mid X$ and $h\mid Y$. What are the common divisors of the polynomials $X$ and $Y$ in $k[X,Y]$? What does that force $h$ to be — and what ideal does such an $h$ generate?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> So any generating set of $I$ has at least two elements. Now use that $R$ is *commutative*. For *any* two ring elements $u$ and $v$, the product commutes: $uv = vu$, equivalently $v\cdot u - u\cdot v = 0$. With $u = X$ and $v = Y$ this reads $Y\cdot X - X\cdot Y = 0$ — a relation among the generators $X,Y$ with coefficients $Y,-X\in R$, and these coefficients are *not zero*. Is $\{X,Y\}$ linearly independent?

> [!note]- Hint 4
> The relation is not special to $\{X,Y\}$. Take *any* generating set; pick two distinct non-zero members $u,v$ of it. Then $v\cdot u + (-u)\cdot v = 0$ is a non-trivial relation (coefficients $v,-u$, both non-zero, since $R$ is a domain). So *every* generating set of size $\ge 2$ is linearly dependent. Combined with Hint 2 (no generating set of size $1$), conclude via [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation]] that $I$ has no basis.

# Solution

The strategy: $I$ is torsion-free, so the obstruction to freeness is not torsion but *the impossibility of a small independent generating set*. Show $I$ needs at least two generators, then show any two generators of an ideal in a commutative ring satisfy a non-trivial relation, so no generating set is a basis.

**Step 1: $\{X,Y\}$ generates $I$ as an $R$-module.**

By definition of the ideal $(X,Y)$, every element of $I$ is an $R$-linear combination of $X$ and $Y$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The ideal generated by $X$ and $Y$ is
> $$I = (X,Y) = \{\,fX + gY : f,g\in R\,\}.$$
> An $R$-linear combination of the set $\{X,Y\}$ is precisely an expression $fX + gY$ with $f,g\in R$. So the set of $R$-linear combinations of $\{X,Y\}$ is exactly $I$, which is to say $\{X,Y\}$ generates $I$ as an $R$-module. (Equivalently: an ideal and the submodule it generates coincide, because the ideal axioms and the submodule axioms are the same conditions.) In passing, $I$ is *finitely generated* — by these two elements — so it is not the finiteness of generation that fails here.

**Step 2: $I$ is not principal — no single element generates it.**

There is no $h\in R$ with $I = (h)$: a generator of $I$ would have to divide both $X$ and $Y$, hence be a non-zero constant, but then $(h) = R\neq I$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Suppose, for contradiction, that $I = (h)$ for a single $h\in R$. Then every element of $I$ is a multiple of $h$. In particular $X\in I$ and $Y\in I$, so $h\mid X$ and $h\mid Y$ in $R = k[X,Y]$.
>
> Consider degrees. Total degree is additive on products in the integral domain $k[X,Y]$: $\deg(pq) = \deg p + \deg q$. From $h\mid X$, write $X = hp$; then $1 = \deg X = \deg h + \deg p$, so $\deg h\le 1$. If $\deg h = 1$, then $\deg p = 0$, so $p$ is a non-zero constant and $h$ is a non-zero scalar multiple of $X$; but then $h$ does *not* divide $Y$ (a scalar multiple of $X$ divides $Y$ only if $X\mid Y$, false, since $Y/X\notin k[X,Y]$). So $\deg h = 0$: $h$ is a constant. Since $h$ generates a non-zero ideal, $h\neq 0$, so $h$ is a *non-zero* constant, i.e. a unit of $k[X,Y]$ (its inverse is the scalar $h^{-1}\in k$).
>
> But a principal ideal generated by a unit is the whole ring: $(h) = R$, because $1 = h\cdot h^{-1}\in(h)$ and then $r = r\cdot 1\in(h)$ for all $r$. This contradicts $(h) = I\neq R$ — indeed $I\neq R$ because $1\notin I$ (every element of $I$ has zero constant term, while $1$ has constant term $1$).
>
> Hence no single $h$ generates $I$: the ideal $I$ is **not principal**. Every generating set of $I$ therefore has at least two elements.

**Step 3: Any two generators relate — the commutator gives a non-trivial relation.**

In the commutative ring $R$, the two generators satisfy $Y\cdot X - X\cdot Y = 0$, a non-trivial $R$-linear relation; so $\{X,Y\}$ is linearly dependent.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Because $R = k[X,Y]$ is a **commutative** ring, $XY = YX$. Rearranging,
> $$Y\cdot X + (-X)\cdot Y = 0.$$
> This is a relation $r_1\cdot X + r_2\cdot Y = 0$ among the generators $X,Y$, with coefficients $r_1 = Y$ and $r_2 = -X$ drawn from $R$. The relation is **non-trivial**: the coefficients $Y$ and $-X$ are *both non-zero* elements of $R$. Hence the requirement of [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|linear independence]] — that the only relation be the trivial one $r_1 = r_2 = 0$ — fails. The generating set $\{X,Y\}$ is linearly **dependent**.
>
> Equivalently, *unique* expressibility fails: the element $XY\in I$ is both $Y\cdot X + 0\cdot Y$ and $0\cdot X + X\cdot Y$, two distinct $R$-linear combinations of $\{X,Y\}$ — forbidden for a basis by condition (iii) of the characterisation.
>
> Crucially, the relation is *not* peculiar to $\{X,Y\}$. Let $T$ be **any** generating set of $I$ and pick two distinct elements $u,v\in T$; we may take them non-zero (the zero element is never in a basis). Commutativity gives
> $$v\cdot u + (-u)\cdot v = vu - uv = 0,$$
> a relation with coefficients $v$ and $-u$. Since $R$ is an integral *domain* and $u,v\neq 0$, the coefficients $v,-u$ are non-zero, so this relation is non-trivial. Therefore **every** generating set of $I$ with at least two elements is linearly dependent.

**Step 4: Conclude — $I$ has no basis.**

No generating set of $I$ is linearly independent: those of size $\le 1$ do not generate (Step 2), those of size $\ge 2$ are dependent (Step 3). By the free-generation equivalence, $I$ is not free.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|the characterisation of free generation]], a finite subset is a basis if and only if it generates and is linearly independent. Suppose $I$ had a basis $T$; let $s = |T|$.
>
> - If $s\le 1$, then $T$ is empty or a singleton. The empty set generates $\{0\}\neq I$. A singleton $\{h\}$ generates the principal ideal $(h)$, and by Step 2 no principal ideal equals $I$. So $T$ does not generate $I$ — contradiction.
> - If $s\ge 2$, then by Step 3 the set $T$ is linearly dependent, so it is not a basis — contradiction.
>
> Every possible size is excluded, so $I$ has no basis. By definition a module is free if and only if it has a basis; therefore $I = (X,Y)$ is **not a free $R$-module**, even though it is a submodule of the free module $R$ and is finitely generated and torsion-free. $\blacksquare$

**Step 5: Locate the cause — contrast with principal ideal domains.**

Over a PID every submodule of a free module is free; the failure here isolates the fact that $k[X,Y]$ is not a PID.

> [!note]- Derivation
> There is a structural theorem: *over a principal ideal domain, every submodule of a free module is free* (for finitely generated modules this is part of the structure theorem; in general it holds too). If $k[X,Y]$ were a PID, then $I$ — a submodule of the free module $R$ — would be forced to be free, contradicting Step 4.
>
> So this exercise *proves* $k[X,Y]$ is not a principal ideal domain, by exhibiting the non-principal ideal $I = (X,Y)$ (Step 2 is exactly the statement "$(X,Y)$ is not principal"). The structural reason $I$ fails to be free traces back to a single ring-theoretic defect: $k[X,Y]$ has ideals that genuinely need two generators. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ or over $k[X]$ — both PIDs — every ideal is principal, so every ideal *is* free of rank one as a module, and the phenomenon of this exercise cannot occur. The number of variables is decisive: $k[X]$ (one variable) is a PID; $k[X,Y]$ (two variables) is not.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $I = (X,Y)\trianglelefteq R = k[X,Y]$ is not a free $R$-module.
>
> *Generation.* By definition $I = \{fX + gY : f,g\in R\}$ is the set of $R$-linear combinations of $\{X,Y\}$, so $\{X,Y\}$ generates $I$.
>
> *Non-principality.* Suppose $I = (h)$. Then $h\mid X$ and $h\mid Y$. Total degree is additive on $k[X,Y]$, so $h\mid X$ forces $\deg h\le 1$; if $\deg h = 1$ then $h$ is a scalar multiple of $X$, which does not divide $Y$, so $\deg h = 0$. As $(h)\neq(0)$, $h$ is a non-zero constant, hence a unit, hence $(h) = R$. But $1\notin I$, so $I\neq R$ — contradiction. Thus $I$ is not principal, and every generating set has $\ge 2$ elements.
>
> *Every $\ge 2$-element generating set is dependent.* For any generating set $T$ and distinct non-zero $u,v\in T$, commutativity of $R$ gives $v\cdot u + (-u)\cdot v = vu - uv = 0$; the coefficients $v,-u$ are non-zero since $R$ is a domain, so this relation is non-trivial. In particular $Y\cdot X - X\cdot Y = 0$ shows $\{X,Y\}$ is dependent.
>
> *Conclusion.* A basis of $I$ would generate and be linearly independent. Size $\le 1$ fails to generate (non-principality); size $\ge 2$ is dependent. No size works, so $I$ has no basis: $I$ is not free. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**When torsion is unavailable, defeat freeness by showing no generating set can be both small enough and independent.** A module sitting inside an integral domain is torsion-free, so the quickest non-freeness argument — exhibit a torsion element, as for $\mathbb{Z}/2$ — is off the table. The next obstruction in the toolkit is a *generator-count-versus-relations* squeeze: prove the module needs at least two generators (so any generating set has size $\ge 2$), then prove any two generators must relate (so any size-$\ge 2$ set is dependent). A basis must be simultaneously generating and independent; if "generating" forces size $\ge 2$ and "size $\ge 2$" forces dependence, no basis can exist. The trigger is "is this torsion-free module free?"; the reaction is "what is the minimum number of generators, and do generators of that number necessarily relate?". This pattern recurs whenever a finitely generated torsion-free module fails to be free.

**Commutativity is a relation-manufacturing machine: any two elements of a commutative-ring module-ideal relate.** The relation $Y\cdot X - X\cdot Y = 0$ looks like a lucky feature of the specific pair $X,Y$, but Step 3 shows it is *universal*: in a commutative ring, *any* two elements $u,v$ of an ideal satisfy $v\cdot u - u\cdot v = 0$, and this relation is non-trivial whenever $u,v$ are non-zero in a domain. So the moment an ideal of a commutative domain needs two or more generators, it is *automatically* non-free — there is no need to search for a clever relation, commutativity supplies one for free. This is a sharp and reusable principle: **a non-principal ideal of a commutative domain is never free as a module over that domain.** It instantly tells you that $(2,X)\trianglelefteq\mathbb{Z}[X]$, $(X,Y)\trianglelefteq k[X,Y]$, the maximal ideal of any non-discrete-valuation local domain, and so on, all fail to be free. The deeper statement behind this is that for a commutative ring an ideal is free as a module if and only if it is principal and generated by a non-zero-divisor.

**A negative result about a *general* construction often proves itself by ruling out every parameter value.** The conclusion "$I$ has no basis" was reached not by analysing one candidate basis but by a *case split on the size* $s$ of a hypothetical basis: $s\le 1$ excluded by one argument, $s\ge 2$ by another, leaving no $s$. This "exhaust the parameter" tactic is the right move whenever the object you want to forbid comes with a numerical parameter — basis size, polynomial degree, chain length, dimension. It forces you to find, for each value of the parameter, a reason that value fails, and the union of those reasons is the proof. It also clarifies *why* the result holds: here, two genuinely different defects conspire — non-principality kills small bases, commutativity kills large ones — and naming both is more informative than a single opaque contradiction.

**Submodules of free modules are free *only* over nice rings — this exercise is the boundary case.** Over a principal ideal domain, a clean structural theorem holds: every submodule of a free module is again free (and a submodule of a free module of finite rank $n$ is free of rank $\le n$). This is why, over $\mathbb{Z}$ or $k[X]$, ideals — being submodules of the free rank-one module — are *always* free, in fact free of rank one because every ideal is principal. The ideal $I = (X,Y)$ over $k[X,Y]$ is the minimal counterexample showing the theorem *needs* the PID hypothesis: $k[X,Y]$ has Krull dimension two and possesses genuinely two-generated ideals, so submodules of its free modules can fail to be free. The exercise therefore does double duty — it exhibits a non-free submodule of a free module, *and* it proves $k[X,Y]$ is not a PID (the ideal $(X,Y)$ is not principal). Pairing it with [[Ex - The integers mod two is not a free module]] (a non-free module, defeated by torsion) and [[Ex - Generating sets that are not bases]] (a generating set that is not a basis, with the exchange lemma failing) completes the picture of exactly how modules over a general ring diverge from vector spaces: bases need not exist, need not be extractable from generating sets, and need not be inherited by submodules.
