# Notation

$R$ is a commutative ring with $1_R$ and $M$ is an [[Def - Module|R-module]]. For an element $m \in M$, the annihilator is $\operatorname{Ann}(m) \subseteq R$; for a subset $S \subseteq M$, $\operatorname{Ann}(S) \subseteq R$; for the whole module, $\operatorname{Ann}(M) \subseteq R$. Every annihilator is a subset of the *ring* $R$ — not of $M$. An element $m \neq 0_M$ with $\operatorname{Ann}(m) \neq \{0_R\}$ is called a **torsion element**. When the module needs emphasis one writes $\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)$. See [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2]] for the full notation registry.

# Axiom Motivation

This definition records a phenomenon that **simply does not exist for vector spaces**, and recognising the gap is the way to invent it. In a vector space over a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]], if a scalar $r \neq 0$ and a vector $m$ satisfy $rm = 0$, then $m = r^{-1}(rm) = r^{-1} \cdot 0 = 0$ — you divide by $r$. So the only scalar that can kill a nonzero vector is $0$ itself. The instant scalars come from a [[Def - Ring|ring]] rather than a field, division disappears, and a *nonzero* scalar can annihilate a *nonzero* element: in the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, the scalar $4 \neq 0$ kills the element $\bar 3 \neq \bar 0$, since $4 \cdot \bar 3 = \overline{12} = \bar 0$. This is **torsion**, and it is the first genuinely new feature of module theory. The annihilator is the bookkeeping device for it: instead of asking the yes/no question "is $m$ torsion?", we collect *all* the scalars that kill $m$ into one set and study that set.

So define, for a fixed $m \in M$, the set $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = \{r \in R : rm = 0_M\}$ of scalars that send $m$ to zero. The first thing to want is that this set be *structured* — not a random subset of $R$ but an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]]. Why should we expect that, and why is it the *right* expectation? Because $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is secretly a kernel. Consider the map $\varphi_m : R \to M$, $r \mapsto rm$ — "act on $m$". Module axioms (a) and (c) say exactly that $\varphi_m$ is an [[Def - Module Homomorphism|R-module homomorphism]] (additivity is $(r+s)m = rm + sm$; $R$-linearity is $\varphi_m(sr) = (sr)m = s(rm) = s\,\varphi_m(r)$). And $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is precisely $\ker \varphi_m$. The kernel of a module homomorphism is a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] of the source; the source here is $R$ as a module over itself, whose [[Def - Submodule|submodules]] are exactly the [[Def - Ideal|ideals]]. Therefore $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is an ideal — not by a special computation, but because *every annihilator is a kernel and every kernel-in-$R$ is an ideal*. If you want the computation directly: if $rm = 0$ and $sm = 0$ then $(r+s)m = rm + sm = 0$, so $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is closed under addition; and if $rm = 0$ then $(sr)m = s(rm) = s \cdot 0 = 0$ for any $s \in R$, so $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ absorbs multiplication. Closed under addition and absorbing multiplication: that is an ideal.

Next, we want to annihilate not just one element but a whole *set* $S$, or the whole module $M$. The desideratum is: $\operatorname{Ann}(S)$ should be the scalars killing **every** element of $S$ at once. A scalar $r$ kills every $m \in S$ exactly when $r \in \operatorname{Ann}(m)$ for every $m \in S$ — that is, $r \in \bigcap_{m \in S}\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. So $\operatorname{Ann}(S) = \bigcap_{m \in S}\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is *forced*: it is the only set that means "kills all of $S$". And this immediately gives, for free, that $\operatorname{Ann}(S)$ is again an ideal — an intersection of [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] is an ideal (the intersection of any family of submodules is a submodule). The special case $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$, the scalars killing every element of the module, is the most important: it measures how much of the ring $R$ acts as zero on $M$, and the quotient $R/\operatorname{Ann}(M)$ is the "honest" ring of scalars that $M$ actually feels.

Watch the design choices. Why intersection and not union for $\operatorname{Ann}(S)$? Because "kills *all*" is a universal quantifier, and universal quantifiers become intersections; a union would mean "kills *some* element of $S$", which is not even an ideal in general and is not the useful notion. Why is $\operatorname{Ann}$ a subset of $R$ and not of $M$? Because it is collecting *scalars*, not elements — the annihilator lives on the ring side, and confusing the two sides is the most common beginner error. And why does the whole construction matter? Because $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is the exact invariant that the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] turns into a classification: the cyclic submodule generated by $m$ satisfies $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$, so knowing the ideal $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ knows the cyclic module $Rm$ up to isomorphism. The annihilator is not a curiosity; it is the handle by which finitely generated [[Def - Module|modules]] over nice [[Def - Ring|rings]] are eventually classified.

# The Definition

Let $M$ be an [[Def - Module|R-module]].

**Annihilator of an element.** For $m \in M$, the **annihilator of $m$** is
$$\operatorname{Ann}(m) = \{r \in R : r \cdot m = 0_M\} \subseteq R.$$

**Annihilator of a subset.** For any subset $S \subseteq M$, the **annihilator of $S$** is the set of scalars killing every element of $S$:
$$\operatorname{Ann}(S) = \{r \in R : r \cdot m = 0_M \text{ for all } m \in S\} = \bigcap_{m \in S} \operatorname{Ann}(m) \subseteq R.$$

**Annihilator of the module.** Taking $S = M$ gives the **annihilator of $M$**:
$$\operatorname{Ann}(M) = \{r \in R : r \cdot m = 0_M \text{ for all } m \in M\} = \bigcap_{m \in M}\operatorname{Ann}(m) \subseteq R.$$

In every case the annihilator is a subset of the **ring** $R$, and in every case it is an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] of $R$:

- $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is an ideal because it is the kernel of the [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]] $\varphi_m : R \to M$, $r \mapsto rm$ (a kernel-in-$R$ is an ideal); directly, if $r, s \in \operatorname{Ann}(m)$ then $(r+s)m = rm + sm = 0_M$, and if $r \in \operatorname{Ann}(m)$ and $s \in R$ then $(sr)m = s(rm) = 0_M$.
- $\operatorname{Ann}(S)$ and $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$ are ideals because they are intersections of the ideals $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$, and an intersection of ideals is an ideal.

A module $M$ with $\operatorname{Ann}(M) = \{0_R\}$ is called **faithful** (the ring acts with no redundancy); an individual $m \neq 0_M$ with $\operatorname{Ann}(m) \neq \{0_R\}$ is a **torsion element**.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The annihilator is **the kernel of the "act on $m$" homomorphism, repackaged**. Fixing $m$, the map $R \to M$, $r \mapsto rm$, is a [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]], and $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is literally its kernel — so every theorem about kernels (it is a submodule of the source, hence an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]]; the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] applies) is a theorem about annihilators. Annihilators are not a new kind of object; they are kernels viewed from the module.

It is also **the module-theoretic analogue of a stabiliser**. The slogan "a ring acting on a module is like a group acting on a set" makes the cyclic submodule $Rm$ the analogue of an *orbit* and $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ the analogue of a *stabiliser* — the elements of the acting object that fix $m$ (here, send it to zero). The isomorphism $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is then the orbit–stabiliser theorem of module theory: orbit size is index of stabiliser, refined to "the orbit submodule is the ring modulo the stabiliser ideal".

For vector spaces the concept is **degenerate by design**: over a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]] $F$, any $m \neq 0$ has $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = \{0\}$, because a nonzero scalar is invertible and cannot kill a nonzero vector. So a nontrivial annihilator is precisely a *certificate that the scalars do not form a field* — it is the visible footprint of torsion, and torsion is exactly what linear algebra lacks.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3) = (4)$ in $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$.** Work in the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $M = \mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ and take $m = \bar 3$. A scalar $r \in \mathbb{Z}$ annihilates $\bar 3$ when $r \cdot \bar 3 = \overline{3r} = \bar 0$, i.e. when $12 \mid 3r$, i.e. when $4 \mid r$. So $\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3) = 4\mathbb{Z} = (4)$, the ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ generated by $4$. As a check on the cyclic-module isomorphism, $\mathbb{Z}\bar 3 = \{\bar 0, \bar 3, \bar 6, \bar 9\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/(4)$, a four-element module — and indeed $R/\operatorname{Ann}(\bar 3) = \mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.

**Is an instance: $\operatorname{Ann}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) = (n)$.** Regard $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. A scalar $r \in \mathbb{Z}$ kills *every* element $\bar a$ exactly when $\overline{ra} = \bar 0$ for all $a$ — taking $a = 1$ this needs $n \mid r$, and conversely $n \mid r$ gives $\overline{ra} = \bar 0$ for all $a$. Hence $\operatorname{Ann}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) = n\mathbb{Z} = (n)$. The "honest scalar ring" $R/\operatorname{Ann}(M) = \mathbb{Z}/(n) = \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ recovers the module's own ring — fitting, since $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ feels scalars only modulo $n$.

**Is an instance: $\operatorname{Ann}(M) = \{0_R\}$ for a faithful module.** For $R^n$ over $R$ (with $n \geq 1$), a scalar $r$ annihilates everything only if $r \cdot (1_R, 0, \ldots, 0) = (r, 0, \ldots, 0) = 0$, forcing $r = 0_R$. So $\operatorname{Ann}(R^n) = \{0_R\}$: the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^n$ is **faithful**. In particular $R$ itself, as a module over itself, has $\operatorname{Ann}(R) = \{0_R\}$, since $r \cdot 1_R = r$.

**Is an instance: $\operatorname{Ann}(M) = (X - \lambda)$ for an eigenvector setup.** Let $V$ be an $F[X]$-module via an operator $\alpha$, and suppose $V$ is one-dimensional spanned by an eigenvector $v$ with $\alpha(v) = \lambda v$. Then $f \in F[X]$ annihilates $v$ when $f(\alpha)(v) = f(\lambda)v = 0$, i.e. when $f(\lambda) = 0$, i.e. when $(X - \lambda) \mid f$. So $\operatorname{Ann}(v) = \operatorname{Ann}(V) = (X - \lambda)$, the ideal of polynomials vanishing at $\lambda$. The annihilator of an $F[X]$-module is generated by (a divisor of) the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ — annihilators recover the minimal polynomial.

**Is NOT an instance — a common confusion: $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is not a subset of $M$.** It is tempting to think of "the annihilator" as a set of *module elements* — perhaps the elements $m$ killed by some scalar, or killed by a given scalar. That is a different object (a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] of $M$, the torsion or $r$-torsion submodule), and it lives on the *module* side. The annihilator $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ lives on the *ring* side: it is a set of **scalars**. Mixing the two sides is the standard error. The clean statement of the duality: $\{m : rm = 0\}$ is a submodule of $M$ for fixed $r$, while $\{r : rm = 0\}$ is an ideal of $R$ for fixed $m$.

**Is NOT an instance: $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is not generally the whole ring or the zero ideal.** For $m = 0_M$, indeed $\operatorname{Ann}(0_M) = R$ (every scalar kills zero) — the one degenerate case. For $m$ a nonzero element of a faithful free module, $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = \{0_R\}$. But the *interesting* case is neither extreme: in $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ the annihilators of $\bar 1, \bar 2, \bar 3, \bar 4, \bar 6$ are $(12), (6), (4), (3), (2)$ respectively — a whole spectrum of proper nonzero ideals. The annihilator being a *proper, nonzero* ideal is exactly the signal that $m$ is a nontrivial torsion element.

**Corollary ($\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is an ideal — calibration).** For any $m \in M$, $\operatorname{Ann}(m) \trianglelefteq R$. *Calibration check:* it is non-empty ($0_R \in \operatorname{Ann}(m)$ since $0_R \cdot m = 0_M$); closed under addition ($(r+s)m = rm + sm$); and absorbs ring multiplication ($(sr)m = s(rm)$). If you can reproduce these you have understood that the ideal structure comes straight from module axioms (a) and (c) — equivalently, that $\operatorname{Ann}(m) = \ker(\varphi_m)$ for the homomorphism $\varphi_m : R \to M$, $r \mapsto rm$.

**Corollary (cyclic modules: $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$).** The cyclic submodule $Rm = \{rm : r \in R\}$ generated by $m$ is isomorphic, as an $R$-module, to the quotient $R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. *Calibration check:* apply the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] to $\varphi_m : R \to M$, whose image is $Rm$ and whose kernel is $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$. This is *the* reason annihilators matter: the ideal $\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ is a complete isomorphism invariant of the cyclic module $Rm$.

**Corollary (monotonicity and the module annihilator).** If $S \subseteq T \subseteq M$ then $\operatorname{Ann}(T) \subseteq \operatorname{Ann}(S)$ — a *larger* set is *harder* to annihilate, so its annihilator is *smaller*. *Calibration check:* a scalar killing all of $T$ in particular kills all of $S$. Consequently $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$ is the smallest of all the annihilators $\operatorname{Ann}(S)$, and if $M$ is generated by $m_1, \ldots, m_k$ then $\operatorname{Ann}(M) = \operatorname{Ann}(m_1) \cap \cdots \cap \operatorname{Ann}(m_k)$ — annihilating a finite generating set annihilates the whole module.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Cyclic modules and the structure theorem *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> The isomorphism $Rm \cong R/\operatorname{Ann}(m)$ shows every cyclic module is a quotient of the ring by an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]]. The structure theorem for [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] modules over a principal ideal domain writes such a module as a direct sum of cyclic pieces $R/(d_i)$, and the ideals $(d_i)$ are exactly annihilators — they are the invariant factors.

> [!tip] Minimal polynomial and Cayley–Hamilton *(from Linear Algebra)*
> For an $F[X]$-module coming from an operator $\alpha$ on a finite-dimensional vector space, $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$ is the ideal generated by the **minimal polynomial** of $\alpha$. Cayley–Hamilton — the characteristic polynomial annihilates $\alpha$ — is the statement that the characteristic polynomial lies in $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$.

> [!tip] Support and primary decomposition *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The annihilator ideal $\operatorname{Ann}(M)$ cuts out the **support** of $M$ in $\operatorname{Spec} R$ — the locus of prime ideals where $M$ does not vanish. Annihilators are the algebraic shadow of "where a sheaf is supported", linking module theory to the geometry of schemes.
