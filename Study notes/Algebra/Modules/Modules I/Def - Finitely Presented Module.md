# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a fixed ring (commutative, with $1$, unless stated otherwise) and all modules are left $R$-modules. For a module $M$, a **surjection from a free module** is a surjective [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]] $\varphi : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ out of the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^k = R \oplus \cdots \oplus R$; the double-headed arrow $\twoheadrightarrow$ marks surjectivity. The **kernel** $\ker \varphi = \{x \in R^k : \varphi(x) = 0\}$ is a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] of $R^k$, called the **relation module** (or **module of relations**) for the chosen surjection. The standard basis of $R^k$ is $e_1, \dots, e_k$; the generators of $M$ they produce are $m_i = \varphi(e_i)$. A **presentation matrix** is an $\ell \times k$ matrix over $R$ whose rows are generators of $\ker \varphi$. The full notation registry lives on the parent page [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2]].

# Axiom Motivation

The thing we want is a class of [[Def - Module|modules]] that can be **completely written down on a finite piece of paper** — communicated, stored, and computed with using only finitely much data. "[[Def - Finitely Generated Module|Finitely generated]]" is a first attempt: a module is finitely generated if finitely many elements $m_1, \dots, m_k$ produce all of it under $R$-linear combination. That fixes a *finite list of generators*. But it is only half of a finite description, and the present definition exists to supply the missing half.

See the gap concretely. Suppose $M$ is finitely generated by $m_1, \dots, m_k$. There is then, by the [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|surjection theorem]], a surjective homomorphism $\varphi : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ sending the standard basis vector $e_i$ to the generator $m_i$ — the free module $R^k$ is the universe of *formal* $R$-linear combinations of $k$ symbols, and $\varphi$ evaluates each formal combination $\sum_i a_i e_i$ to the actual element $\sum_i a_i m_i \in M$. To know $M$ you must know which formal combinations evaluate to the *same* element of $M$, equivalently which formal combinations evaluate to $0$. That information is exactly the kernel $K = \ker \varphi$ — the [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] of $R^k$ collecting every relation $\sum_i a_i m_i = 0$ that holds among the generators. The [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] makes this precise: $M \cong R^k / K$. So a module *is* a list of generators (the $k$, giving $R^k$) together with its module of relations (the $K$). Finite generation pins down the first; nothing yet pins down the second. And $K$, a submodule of $R^k$, may itself be enormous — possibly not describable by finitely many relations at all.

So the desideratum sharpens. We want $M$ describable by **finitely many generators and finitely many relations**. The generators are handled by finite generation: finitely many $m_i$, the integer $k$. The relations are the new demand: we want the relation module $K = \ker \varphi$ to be itself **finitely generated** — finitely many relations $n_1, \dots, n_\ell$ such that *every* relation among the generators is an $R$-linear combination of those $\ell$. A module admitting such a doubly-finite description is **finitely presented**. Reverse-engineering the definition, that is forced: "finite description" means finite generator data *and* finite relation data, and there is no third thing — a module is its generators and its relations, so controlling both controls the module entirely.

Now the calibration. *Weaken* the definition by dropping the demand on the kernel — keep only finite generation. Then $M$ has finitely many generators but possibly infinitely many independent relations; you can write down the generators but never finish writing the relations, so you cannot in general decide whether two formal combinations name the same element. Finitely generated is genuinely weaker than finitely presented: there exist (over badly-behaved, non-[[Def - Ring|Noetherian]] [[Def - Ring|rings]]) finitely generated modules whose relation module is not finitely generated. *Strengthen* the definition by demanding the relation module be not merely finitely generated but *free* of finite rank — that the relations themselves have no relations among *them*. This is strictly stronger; it is the notion of a module with a finite *free resolution* of length one, and it excludes finitely presented modules whose relations are entangled. Finitely presented is calibrated exactly between: finitely many generators, finitely many relations, with the relations allowed to be as entangled as they like.

One subtlety the definition must survive: a module has *many* surjections from free modules — many choices of generating set, many $k$, many $\varphi$. The definition picks out *one* $\varphi$ with finitely generated kernel. The reason this is legitimate, the reason "finitely presented" is a property of $M$ and not of the chosen presentation, is a lemma (sometimes called **Schanuel's lemma**, in the finitely-generated form): if $M$ is finitely generated and *some* surjection $R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ has finitely generated kernel, then *every* surjection from a finite free module has finitely generated kernel. So the existential "there exists a $\varphi$ with finitely generated kernel" is equivalent to the universal "every $\varphi$ has finitely generated kernel", and the definition is well-posed — exactly as "free" is well-posed despite a module having many bases.

The reason to fix precisely this definition is what it enables downstream. A finitely presented module can be recorded as a **matrix** — the rows being the finitely many relations — and matrices can be handed to algorithms, reduced to normal forms, and compared. The [[Thm - Invariance of Rank|structure theory]] of modules over a principal ideal domain runs by taking a presentation matrix and reducing it (Smith normal form); this only works when the presentation is finite. Finitely presented is the precise class on which the "module as a matrix of relations" picture, and hence computational module theory, is available.

# The Definition

Let $R$ be a ring and let $M$ be an $R$-module.

**Relation module.** Suppose $M$ is [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]], say by $k$ elements. By the [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|surjection theorem]] there is a surjective [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]]

$$\varphi : R^k \longtwoheadrightarrow M$$

from the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^k$ — concretely, choosing generators $m_1, \dots, m_k$ of $M$ and sending the standard basis vector $e_i \mapsto m_i$. The **relation module** (or **module of relations**) for this surjection is its kernel

$$K \ =\ \ker \varphi \ =\ \bigl\{ (a_1, \dots, a_k) \in R^k \ :\ a_1 m_1 + \cdots + a_k m_k = 0 \text{ in } M \bigr\},$$

a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] of $R^k$. Each element of $K$ records one relation holding among the generators. The [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] gives the canonical identification

$$M \ \cong\ R^k / \ker\varphi \ =\ R^k / K.$$

**Finitely presented module.** The module $M$ is **finitely presented** when it is finitely generated and, for some surjection $\varphi : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ from a finite free module, the relation module $K = \ker\varphi$ is **itself finitely generated**.

Equivalently and more symmetrically: $M$ is finitely presented when there is an **exact sequence**

$$R^\ell \ \xrightarrow{\ \alpha\ }\ R^k \ \xrightarrow{\ \varphi\ }\ M \ \longrightarrow\ 0$$

with both free modules of *finite* rank — "exact" meaning $\varphi$ is surjective and $\operatorname{im}\alpha = \ker\varphi$, so that $\alpha$ surjects $R^\ell$ onto the relation module. The integer $k$ counts the **generators**; the integer $\ell$ counts the **relations**.

**Presentation; presentation matrix.** The data $(k, \ell, \alpha)$ — or equivalently a choice of generators $m_1, \dots, m_k$ of $M$ together with generators $n_1, \dots, n_\ell$ of the relation module $K$ — is a **(finite) presentation** of $M$. Writing each relation generator in coordinates,

$$n_j \ =\ (r_{j1},\, r_{j2},\, \dots,\, r_{jk}) \ \in\ R^k, \qquad j = 1, \dots, \ell,$$

the element $n_j$ encodes the relation

$$r_{j1}\, m_1 + r_{j2}\, m_2 + \cdots + r_{jk}\, m_k \ =\ 0 \qquad \text{in } M.$$

The $\ell \times k$ matrix $A = (r_{ji})$ over $R$ is the **presentation matrix**. It records $M$ completely: $M$ is the module generated by symbols $m_1, \dots, m_k$ subject to the relations read off the rows of $A$, that is, $M \cong \operatorname{coker}(A) = R^k / (\text{row space of } A)$. The matrix $A$ is the finite piece of paper the definition was built to produce.

**Independence of the chosen presentation.** Finite presentation is a property of $M$ alone, not of the chosen surjection: if $M$ is finitely generated and *one* surjection from a finite free module has finitely generated kernel, then *every* such surjection does. Hence the existential in the definition ("for some $\varphi$") may be replaced by the universal ("for every $\varphi$") without changing the class of modules.

# Categorical Definition

There is no separate universal property defining "finitely presented" — the notion is built from the universal property of the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] rather than carrying one of its own. Stated categorically, $M$ is finitely presented exactly when it is a **cokernel of a map between two finitely generated free modules**:

$$M \ \cong\ \operatorname{coker}\bigl(\, \alpha : R^\ell \to R^k \,\bigr), \qquad \ell, k \ \text{finite},$$

where the cokernel of $\alpha$ is the [[Def - Quotient Module|quotient]] $R^k / \operatorname{im}\alpha$, the universal module receiving a map from $R^k$ that kills $\operatorname{im}\alpha$. Equivalently $M$ sits in a finite **presentation sequence** $R^\ell \xrightarrow{\alpha} R^k \to M \to 0$ of free modules.

The categorical content worth extracting is *functorial*: finitely presented modules are precisely the objects for which the **Hom functor commutes with direct limits (filtered colimits)** — the natural map $\varinjlim \operatorname{Hom}_R(M, N_i) \to \operatorname{Hom}_R(M, \varinjlim N_i)$ is an isomorphism for every filtered system $\{N_i\}$. This abstract characterisation, which one can take as the definition in a general category, says finite presentation is exactly the *finiteness* condition under which maps out of $M$ are controlled by finite data and so survive passage to a limit. Concretely it holds because a map out of $M = \operatorname{coker}(\alpha)$ is a map out of $R^k$ (a finite tuple of elements, by the free universal property) constrained by finitely many equations (the columns of $\alpha$) — finitely much data on both counts, and finitely much data passes through a filtered colimit.

How this relates to the concrete definition: the presentation sequence $R^\ell \to R^k \to M \to 0$ *is* the surjection $\varphi : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$ together with a finite generating set $\alpha(e_1), \dots, \alpha(e_\ell)$ of its kernel. The "cokernel of a matrix" formulation and the "finitely generated module with finitely generated relation module" formulation are the same statement, the matrix being the coordinates of $\alpha$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

Finite presentation is the **module-theoretic twin of a finitely presented group**. A group is finitely presented when it is $\langle x_1, \dots, x_k \mid w_1, \dots, w_\ell \rangle$ — finitely many generators subject to finitely many relator words — exhibiting it as a quotient of a free group by the normal closure of finitely many elements. Replace "free group" by "[[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^k$", "normal closure of relators" by "[[Def - Submodule|submodule]] generated by the relations $n_j$", and "$\langle \cdots \rangle$" by "$\operatorname{coker}$", and the group notion becomes the module notion verbatim. The presentation matrix is the abelianised, $R$-linear analogue of the list of relator words. Module presentations are better behaved than group presentations precisely because relations combine $R$-linearly rather than as words: the relation module is a genuine submodule, and the word problem becomes linear algebra over $R$.

One sharp compression: a finitely presented module is **a matrix, considered up to the moves that do not change its cokernel**. The module is $\operatorname{coker}(A)$ for an $\ell \times k$ matrix $A$ over $R$; two matrices present isomorphic modules exactly when one is obtained from the other by row and column operations (over $R$) together with adding or deleting trivial generators and relations. Module theory of finitely presented modules *is* the equivalence theory of matrices under these moves — which is why, over a principal ideal domain, the [[Thm - Invariance of Rank|structure theorem]] is proved by reducing the presentation matrix to **Smith normal form**, a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r)$, whence $M \cong \bigoplus_i R/(d_i)$ is read straight off the diagonal.

The placement among finiteness conditions compresses the concept by contrast. There is a chain of strictly increasing strength: **finitely generated** $\Leftarrow$ **finitely presented** $\Leftarrow$ **admits a finite free resolution** $\Leftarrow$ **free of finite rank**. Finitely generated controls only the generators; finitely presented controls generators and relations; a finite free resolution controls relations-among-relations and onward; free of finite rank has no relations at all. Finitely presented is the second rung — the weakest condition under which the module is recordable as a finite matrix. Over a [[Def - Ring|Noetherian]] ring the first two rungs collapse into one (every finitely generated module is finitely presented), and the distinction is invisible; the distinction is a feature of *non-Noetherian* rings.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: every finitely generated module over a Noetherian ring.** A ring $R$ is **Noetherian** when every submodule of a finitely generated $R$-module is itself finitely generated — equivalently, every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated, or $R$ has the ascending chain condition on [[Def - Ideal|ideals]]. If $R$ is Noetherian and $M$ is finitely generated, take any surjection $\varphi : R^k \twoheadrightarrow M$; the relation module $\ker\varphi$ is a submodule of the finitely generated module $R^k$, hence finitely generated by the Noetherian property, so $M$ is finitely presented. Thus **over a Noetherian ring, finitely generated $=$ finitely presented**, and the distinction vanishes. This covers nearly every ring met in practice: fields, $\mathbb{Z}$, principal ideal domains, polynomial rings $k[x_1, \dots, x_n]$ over a field (by the Hilbert basis theorem), and every quotient of these.

**Is an instance: every finitely generated module over a principal ideal domain.** A principal ideal domain is Noetherian — every ideal is generated by one element, hence finitely generated — so the previous example applies: every finitely generated module over a PID is finitely presented. Concretely, a finitely generated abelian [[Def - Group|group]] (a $\mathbb{Z}$-module) such as $\mathbb{Z}^2 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/6$ is finitely presented; its presentation matrix, after reduction, is the diagonal matrix encoding the invariant factors. This is the setting of the structure theorem.

**Is an instance: $\mathbb{Z}/n$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, presented by a $1\times1$ matrix.** The cyclic group $\mathbb{Z}/n$ is finitely generated by the single element $1$, giving the surjection $\varphi : \mathbb{Z} \twoheadrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/n$, $1 \mapsto 1$. Its relation module is $\ker\varphi = n\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the single element $n$. So $\mathbb{Z}/n$ has one generator and one relation: the presentation matrix is the $1 \times 1$ matrix $[\,n\,]$, and $\mathbb{Z}/n = \operatorname{coker}([n]) = \mathbb{Z}/(n)$. This is the smallest nontrivial presentation and the model for "$M$ recorded as a matrix".

**Is an instance: a free module of finite rank.** The free module $R^k$ is finitely presented: it is finitely generated by its standard basis, and the surjection may be taken to be the identity $\operatorname{id} : R^k \to R^k$, whose kernel is $\{0\}$ — finitely generated by the empty set. So $R^k$ is finitely presented with $\ell = 0$ relations; the presentation sequence is $0 \to R^k \to R^k \to 0$. Free of finite rank is the strongest finiteness condition and certainly implies finitely presented.

**Is NOT an instance: a finitely generated module over a non-Noetherian ring with non-finitely-generated relations.** Let $R = k[x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots]$ be the polynomial ring in *infinitely many* variables over a field — a ring that is *not* Noetherian, since the ideal $I = (x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots)$ is not finitely generated. The module $M = R/I \cong k$ is finitely generated: a single element, the image of $1$, generates it, giving $\varphi : R \twoheadrightarrow M$. But the relation module is $\ker\varphi = I$, and $I$ is *not* finitely generated. So $M$ is finitely generated but **not finitely presented** — you can name its one generator, but no finite list of relations suffices. This non-example is the entire point of the definition: it shows finitely presented is strictly stronger than finitely generated, and that the gap opens exactly when the ring is non-Noetherian.

**Is NOT an instance: an infinitely generated module.** A module that is not even finitely generated — for example $\mathbb{Q}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, or an infinite [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules|direct sum]] $\bigoplus_{i=1}^\infty R$ — cannot be finitely presented, since finite presentation requires finite generation as its first clause. This is the trivial failure mode and a reminder that finite presentation is finite generation *plus* a condition, never a weakening of it.

**Corollary ($M \cong \operatorname{coker}$ of the presentation matrix).** If $M$ is finitely presented with $k$ generators and relation generators $n_1, \dots, n_\ell$ assembled into the $\ell \times k$ matrix $A$, then $M \cong R^k / (\text{row space of } A) = \operatorname{coker}(A^{\mathsf T} : R^\ell \to R^k)$. *Calibration check:* if you can pass from a presentation to its matrix and back, you have grasped that a finitely presented module *is* a finite matrix up to equivalence.

**Corollary (finitely presented implies finitely generated, not conversely).** Finite presentation contains finite generation as its first clause, so every finitely presented module is finitely generated. The converse fails exactly over non-Noetherian rings, as the polynomial-ring-in-infinitely-many-variables example shows. *Calibration check:* knowing *where* the converse fails — that it holds over every Noetherian ring and fails only off them — tests that you have located finite presentation correctly in the hierarchy of finiteness conditions.

**Corollary (a finite presentation is finite communicable data).** A finitely presented module is determined, up to isomorphism, by a single matrix over $R$ of finite size. *Calibration check:* this is the "everything about the module on finitely much paper" slogan made literal, and it is the reason finitely presented modules are the objects of computational module theory — Smith normal form, Gröbner-basis methods, and the algorithmic structure theory all take a presentation matrix as input.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Structure Theorem for Modules over a PID *(from Modules II)*
> A finitely presented module over a principal ideal domain is the cokernel of a presentation matrix; reducing that matrix to **Smith normal form** $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r)$ reads off the decomposition $M \cong R^{k-r} \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_r)$. Finite presentation is exactly the hypothesis that makes this algorithmic proof run.

> [!tip] Coherent Module and Coherent Ring *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> Strengthening "finitely presented" to "finitely generated with *every* finitely generated submodule finitely presented" gives a **coherent module**; a ring coherent as a module over itself is a **coherent ring**. Coherence is the non-Noetherian generalisation of the good behaviour Noetherian rings get for free, and it is the precise condition under which finitely presented modules form an abelian category.

> [!tip] Finitely Presented Functors and Tor *(from Homological Algebra)*
> A finite presentation $R^\ell \to R^k \to M \to 0$ is the first two steps of a **free resolution** of $M$. Continuing the resolution and applying $\otimes$ or $\operatorname{Hom}$ computes the derived functors $\operatorname{Tor}$ and $\operatorname{Ext}$; finite presentation guarantees the first two terms of every resolution can be taken finite.
