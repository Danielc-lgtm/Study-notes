# Problem Statement

Regard the abelian group $\mathbb{Z}/2 = \{\,\overline{0},\overline{1}\,\}$ as a module over the [[Def - Ring|ring]] $\mathbb{Z}$, with the action $n\cdot \overline{x} = \overline{nx}$. Prove that $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is **not a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module**: there is no subset $S\subseteq \mathbb{Z}/2$ that generates $\mathbb{Z}/2$ freely.

Give the argument in two ways. First, directly: show that any candidate basis element $x$ is *torsion* — it satisfies $2x = 0$ — and a torsion element can never sit in a linearly independent set. Second, structurally: a non-zero free $\mathbb{Z}$-module always contains a copy of $\mathbb{Z}$ (its basis elements generate $\mathbb{Z}$-[[Def - Submodule|submodules]] isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$), and a two-element module has no room for the infinite group $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the ring $\mathbb{Z}$, the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}/2$, and the notion of a *free* module.

![[Def - Free Module#The Definition]]

Unpacking this for the case at hand: an *abelian group* is the same data as a $\mathbb{Z}$-**module**, because there is exactly one way to multiply a group element $x$ by an integer — repeated addition, $n\cdot x = x + x + \cdots + x$ ($n$ times), with negative $n$ using inverses and $0\cdot x = 0$. So a $\mathbb{Z}$-module *is* an abelian group, and a $\mathbb{Z}$-[[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]] is exactly a group homomorphism.

A subset $S\subseteq M$ **generates $M$ freely** when (i) $S$ generates $M$ — every element of $M$ is an $R$-linear combination of elements of $S$ — and (ii) every set function $\psi : S \to N$ into any $R$-module $N$ extends to an $R$-module homomorphism $\theta : M \to N$. A module that is freely generated by some subset is called **free**, and such a subset is a **basis**. By [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|the characterisation of free generation]], for a finite subset $S = \{m_1,\dots,m_k\}$ the following are equivalent: $S$ generates $M$ freely; $S$ generates $M$ and is **linearly independent** (the only relation $\sum r_i m_i = 0$ is the trivial one $r_1 = \cdots = r_k = 0$); every element of $M$ is *uniquely* an $R$-linear combination of $S$.

An element $x$ of a module is called **torsion** if $r\cdot x = 0$ for some *non-zero* ring element $r$. In $\mathbb{Z}/2$ every element is torsion: $2\cdot\overline{0} = \overline{0}$ and $2\cdot\overline{1} = \overline{2} = \overline{0}$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *prove a module is not free* problem — a negative structural claim. As the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] explains, a negative claim about freeness is established by exhibiting a property that *every* free module enjoys and that the module in question *fails*. The whole craft is choosing the right separating property.

**Assumption pattern.** The only hypothesis is "$\mathbb{Z}/2$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module." Two features make the proof go. First, the module is *finite* — in fact it has only two elements — whereas $\mathbb{Z}$ itself is infinite; this is the engine of the structural argument. Second, every element is *torsion* ($2x = 0$); this is the engine of the direct argument. Torsion is the single obstruction here, and recognising it as the obstruction is the key insight.

**Theorem routing.** There is no deep theorem; the route is the equivalence "freely generated $\iff$ generating and linearly independent" from [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation]]. For the direct proof, you contrapose: a torsion element fails linear independence, so it fails the equivalent freeness condition. For the structural proof, you use the half "free $\Rightarrow$ each basis element spans a copy of the ring": a basis element $b$ of a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module generates the submodule $\mathbb{Z}b\cong\mathbb{Z}$, and a module with a $\mathbb{Z}$-submodule must be infinite.

**Key decision point.** The crux is *what property to separate on*. A novice might try to chase the definition of free generation directly through arbitrary set functions $\psi$. The efficient move is to notice that the obstruction is *torsion*: $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is "all torsion", a free module is "torsion-free", and these are visibly incompatible. Once torsion is named, the proof is one line. The genuine content of the exercise is learning to scan for torsion the moment a module's freeness is in question.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Replace freeness by its linear-independence characterisation** (the [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|free-generation equivalence]]). Rather than work with the lifting property "every set function extends", swap to the concrete criterion: a free finite generating set is exactly a linearly independent generating set. This converts an abstract universal property into a checkable algebraic condition.

2. **Test a candidate basis element for torsion.** Given any proposed basis element $x$, evaluate $r\cdot x$ for small non-zero $r$. Finding *one* non-zero $r$ with $r\cdot x = 0$ exhibits a non-trivial relation $r\cdot x = 0$, which destroys linear independence.

3. **Build a homomorphism out of a free module by prescribing it on a basis** (the defining lifting property of a free module). In the structural proof: if $\mathbb{Z}/2$ were free on $S$, the set function sending a basis element to $1\in\mathbb{Z}$ would extend to a module map $\theta : \mathbb{Z}/2\to\mathbb{Z}$ — and we then derive a contradiction from what $\theta$ must do to $\overline{0}$.

4. **Use that a homomorphism kills zero and respects the action.** A module homomorphism $\theta$ satisfies $\theta(0) = 0$ and $\theta(r x) = r\,\theta(x)$. Applied to the relation $2x = 0$ inside $\mathbb{Z}/2$, this forces $2\,\theta(x) = 0$ in the target, which is impossible in $\mathbb{Z}$ unless $\theta(x) = 0$.

5. **Separate a class by an invariant property.** The conclusion "$\mathbb{Z}/2$ is not free" is obtained by naming a property — torsion-freeness, or containing a copy of $\mathbb{Z}$ — held by every member of the class "free [[Def - Module|modules]]" and failed by $\mathbb{Z}/2$.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> A free module is one with a *basis* — a generating set that is also linearly independent. To show $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is *not* free, you do not need to inspect every conceivable subset by hand. Instead, find a property that all free modules share and that $\mathbb{Z}/2$ visibly lacks. Ask: what relation does *every* element $x$ of $\mathbb{Z}/2$ satisfy that a basis element is forbidden to satisfy?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Every element $x$ of $\mathbb{Z}/2$ satisfies $2x = 0$, even when $x\neq 0$. This is a non-trivial relation: it has the form $r\cdot x = 0$ with the *non-zero* coefficient $r = 2$. Recall that a one-element set $\{x\}$ is linearly independent precisely when $r\cdot x = 0$ forces $r = 0$. So can $\{x\}$ ever be linearly independent in $\mathbb{Z}/2$?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> The only candidate generating sets of $\mathbb{Z}/2$ are $\{\overline{1}\}$ (since $\overline{0}$ generates nothing and a basis with both elements is dependent because $\overline{0}$ alone is dependent). But $2\cdot\overline{1} = \overline{0}$ with coefficient $2\neq 0$, so $\{\overline{1}\}$ is *not* linearly independent. By the equivalence in [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation]], a generating set that is not independent does not generate freely. Hence no subset generates $\mathbb{Z}/2$ freely.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For the structural proof, suppose $\mathbb{Z}/2$ were free on a basis containing some element $b$. The lifting property lets you define a module homomorphism $\theta : \mathbb{Z}/2\to\mathbb{Z}$ with $\theta(b) = 1$. Now apply $\theta$ to *both sides* of $2b = 0$. The left side gives $2\,\theta(b) = 2$; the right side gives $\theta(0) = 0$. So $2 = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ — false. The basis element generates a copy of $\mathbb{Z}$ that simply cannot fit inside a two-element group.

# Solution

The strategy is to identify the separating property. A free $\mathbb{Z}$-module is *torsion-free* (no non-zero element is killed by a non-zero integer), whereas in $\mathbb{Z}/2$ *every* element is killed by $2$. We give the direct linear-independence argument first, then the structural argument.

**Step 1: Reduce the search for a basis to a single candidate set.**

The only subset of $\mathbb{Z}/2$ that could possibly be a basis is $S = \{\overline{1}\}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The subsets of $\mathbb{Z}/2 = \{\overline{0},\overline{1}\}$ are $\emptyset$, $\{\overline{0}\}$, $\{\overline{1}\}$, and $\{\overline{0},\overline{1}\}$. A basis must *generate* $M$, so we discard those that do not.
>
> - $\emptyset$ generates only the zero submodule $\{\overline{0}\}\neq\mathbb{Z}/2$.
> - $\{\overline{0}\}$ generates $\{n\cdot\overline{0} : n\in\mathbb{Z}\} = \{\overline{0}\}\neq\mathbb{Z}/2$.
> - $\{\overline{0},\overline{1}\}$ does generate $\mathbb{Z}/2$, but it *contains* $\overline{0}$, and the relation $1\cdot\overline{0} = \overline{0}$ has non-zero coefficient $1$, so this set is not linearly independent. (More simply: a basis can never contain $0$.)
>
> Only $\{\overline{1}\}$ remains as a candidate: it generates, since $\overline{0} = 2\cdot\overline{1}$ and $\overline{1} = 1\cdot\overline{1}$.

**Step 2: The candidate $\{\overline{1}\}$ is linearly dependent — $\overline{1}$ is torsion.**

The element $\overline{1}$ satisfies the non-trivial relation $2\cdot\overline{1} = \overline{0}$, so $\{\overline{1}\}$ is not linearly independent.

> [!note]- Derivation
> A subset $\{m_1,\dots,m_k\}$ is **linearly independent** when the only solution of $\sum_i r_i m_i = 0$ is $r_1 = \cdots = r_k = 0$. For the one-element set $\{\overline{1}\}$ this says: $r\cdot\overline{1} = \overline{0}$ should force $r = 0$.
>
> But in $\mathbb{Z}/2$ we have $2\cdot\overline{1} = \overline{2} = \overline{0}$. Here the coefficient $r = 2$ is *non-zero* in $\mathbb{Z}$, yet $r\cdot\overline{1} = \overline{0}$. This is a non-trivial relation. Hence $\{\overline{1}\}$ is linearly **dependent**. The element $\overline{1}$ is *torsion*: a non-zero ring element annihilates it.

**Step 3: A dependent generating set does not generate freely — conclude.**

By the free-generation equivalence, since $\{\overline{1}\}$ generates but is not independent, it does not generate freely. As it was the only candidate, $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is not free.

> [!note]- Derivation
> [[Thm - Characterisations of Free Generation|The characterisation of free generation]] states that for a finite subset $S$, "$S$ generates $M$ freely" is equivalent to "$S$ generates $M$ **and** $S$ is linearly independent". The set $\{\overline{1}\}$ generates $\mathbb{Z}/2$ (Step 1) but is not linearly independent (Step 2), so it fails the second clause and therefore does *not* generate $\mathbb{Z}/2$ freely.
>
> By Step 1, $\{\overline{1}\}$ was the *only* subset that could serve as a basis. Since it fails, **no** subset of $\mathbb{Z}/2$ generates it freely. By definition, a module is free precisely when some subset generates it freely; therefore $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is not a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module. $\blacksquare$

**Step 4: The structural argument — a free module embeds $\mathbb{Z}$, which $\mathbb{Z}/2$ cannot.**

If $\mathbb{Z}/2$ were free, a basis element would generate a copy of $\mathbb{Z}$ inside the two-element module $\mathbb{Z}/2$ — impossible.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Suppose, for contradiction, that $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is free with basis $S$. Since $\mathbb{Z}/2\neq\{\overline{0}\}$, the basis $S$ is non-empty; pick $b\in S$.
>
> *Route via a homomorphism to $\mathbb{Z}$.* The defining lifting property of a free module says: any set function $\psi : S\to N$ into any $\mathbb{Z}$-module $N$ extends to a module homomorphism. Take $N = \mathbb{Z}$ and let $\psi$ send $b\mapsto 1$ (and the other basis elements anywhere, say to $0$). Let $\theta : \mathbb{Z}/2\to\mathbb{Z}$ be the resulting homomorphism, so $\theta(b) = 1$.
>
> Now use $2b = 0$, which holds because $b\in\mathbb{Z}/2$ and every element of $\mathbb{Z}/2$ has additive order dividing $2$. Apply $\theta$ to both sides. A [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]] satisfies $\theta(rx) = r\,\theta(x)$ and $\theta(0) = 0$, so
> $$0 = \theta(0) = \theta(2b) = 2\,\theta(b) = 2\cdot 1 = 2.$$
> Thus $2 = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, which is false. The contradiction shows $\mathbb{Z}/2$ has no basis.
>
> *Why this is the "embeds $\mathbb{Z}$" statement.* The same computation shows that the cyclic submodule $\mathbb{Z}b = \{nb : n\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ generated by a basis element is *free of rank one*, hence isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$: the map $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}b$, $n\mapsto nb$, is a surjective homomorphism, and it is injective because $nb = 0$ would (by feeding $b\mapsto 1$ as above) force $n = 0$. So a non-zero free $\mathbb{Z}$-module contains a subgroup isomorphic to the infinite group $\mathbb{Z}$. But $\mathbb{Z}/2$ has only two elements and so contains no infinite subgroup. Hence $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is not free.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is not a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module.
>
> *Direct proof.* A basis must generate $\mathbb{Z}/2$ and cannot contain $\overline{0}$ (the relation $1\cdot\overline{0} = \overline{0}$ has non-zero coefficient). The only subset that both generates and avoids $\overline{0}$ is $S = \{\overline{1}\}$. But $2\cdot\overline{1} = \overline{0}$ with $2\neq 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, so $\{\overline{1}\}$ is linearly dependent. By the characterisation of free generation, a generating set that is not linearly independent does not generate freely. Since $\{\overline{1}\}$ was the only candidate, no subset generates $\mathbb{Z}/2$ freely, so $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is not free.
>
> *Structural proof.* Suppose $\mathbb{Z}/2$ were free on a non-empty basis $S$, and fix $b\in S$. By the lifting property there is a module homomorphism $\theta : \mathbb{Z}/2\to\mathbb{Z}$ with $\theta(b) = 1$. Since $2b = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/2$,
> $$0 = \theta(0) = \theta(2b) = 2\,\theta(b) = 2,$$
> contradicting $2\neq 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$. Hence $\mathbb{Z}/2$ has no basis and is not free. Equivalently, each basis element $b$ would generate a submodule $\mathbb{Z}b\cong\mathbb{Z}$, but $\mathbb{Z}/2$ contains no infinite subgroup. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Torsion is the canonical obstruction to freeness — scan for it first.** When asked whether a module is free, the single most productive first move is to look for a *torsion* element: a non-zero $x$ with $r\cdot x = 0$ for some non-zero ring element $r$. A free module is torsion-free, because a basis element $b$ satisfies $rb = 0$ only when $r = 0$ — this is exactly its linear independence — and linear combinations of torsion-free generators stay torsion-free. So *any* torsion element is an instant certificate of non-freeness. The trigger is the question "is $M$ free?"; the reaction is "does some non-zero element of $M$ get killed by a non-zero scalar?". This disposes of $\mathbb{Z}/2$, $\mathbb{Z}/n$ for every $n\ge 2$, and every non-trivial finite abelian group as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules in one line each — every element of a finite group has finite order, hence is torsion. It also handles $\mathbb{R}$-modules: in a vector space there is no torsion, which is the structural reason *every* vector space is free.

**Prove a negative structural claim by exhibiting a separating property.** "$M$ is not free" is a statement about an infinite class of potential bases, and chasing it by inspecting subsets one at a time does not scale. The disciplined method is to find a property $P$ that *every* free module has and show $M$ fails $P$. Two separating properties did the job here, and both generalise. The first is *torsion-freeness* (used in the direct proof). The second is *contains a copy of the base ring* — every non-zero free $R$-module has a basis element $b$ with $Rb\cong R$, so it contains $R$ as a submodule; this is a cardinality or order obstruction whenever $M$ is "too small" or "too torsion" to house $R$. This separating-property pattern is the universal shape of negative classification arguments across algebra: a group is shown non-abelian by exhibiting two non-commuting elements, a ring non-Noetherian by exhibiting a non-terminating ascending chain of [[Def - Ideal|ideals]], a module non-projective or non-flat by the analogous failure of a lifting or exactness property. Name the invariant, then exhibit the failure.

**A module homomorphism out of a free module is *defined by its values on a basis* — and this is a weapon, not just a definition.** The structural proof did not merely cite that $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is free; it *used* the freeness hypothesis to manufacture a homomorphism $\theta : \mathbb{Z}/2\to\mathbb{Z}$ with prescribed value $\theta(b) = 1$, and then derived a contradiction by applying $\theta$ to a known relation. This is the standard way to exploit a freeness *assumption*: a free module is precisely the module out of which you may build a homomorphism by choosing the images of basis elements completely freely, with no consistency conditions. So whenever a hypothesis hands you "$M$ is free", read it as "I may map basis elements anywhere I like". Conversely, applying any homomorphism $\theta$ to an identity like $2b = 0$ propagates that identity into the target — here producing $2 = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$. The general lesson: relations in the source become relations in every target, so a relation that *cannot* hold in some target (e.g. $\mathbb{Z}$ is torsion-free) certifies that the source had no such structure to begin with.

**A $\mathbb{Z}$-module is just an abelian group, so module-freeness specialises to a statement about [[Def - Group|groups]].** The exercise quietly trades on the identification "$\mathbb{Z}$-module $=$ abelian group": there is exactly one $\mathbb{Z}$-action on an abelian group, namely repeated addition, so no extra data is involved. Under this dictionary, a *free $\mathbb{Z}$-module* is a *free abelian group*, and "$\mathbb{Z}/2$ is not free" becomes "the cyclic group of order two is not a free abelian group" — indeed the only free abelian groups are the direct sums $\mathbb{Z}^{(I)}$ of copies of $\mathbb{Z}$, all of which are infinite or trivial. Recognising this dictionary lets you import intuition in both directions: facts about free abelian groups ([[Def - Subgroup|subgroups]] of free abelian groups are free, finitely generated abelian groups decompose as free part plus torsion) are special cases of module theory over the PID $\mathbb{Z}$, and conversely the failure of freeness for finite groups is the prototype every "non-free module" example imitates. The companion exercise [[Ex - A submodule of a free module that is not free]] shows that over a *non-PID* ring even the torsion-free hypothesis is not enough to force freeness — pinpointing exactly how special $\mathbb{Z}$ is.
