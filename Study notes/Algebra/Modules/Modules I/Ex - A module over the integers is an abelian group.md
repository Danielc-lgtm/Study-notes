# Problem Statement

Let $R=\mathbb{Z}$. Prove that the data of a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is exactly the data of an abelian group:

1. **Forced action.** If $(M,+,0_M,\cdot)$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, then the scalar action $\mathbb{Z}\times M\to M$ is *completely determined* by the underlying abelian group $(M,+,0_M)$ — there is no freedom in choosing it. Explicitly, the module axioms force
$$n\cdot m \;=\; \underbrace{m+m+\cdots+m}_{n\text{ times}}\quad(n>0),\qquad 0\cdot m=0_M,\qquad (-n)\cdot m=-(n\cdot m).$$

2. **Existence and uniqueness.** Conversely, every abelian group $(A,+,0)$ carries one and only one $\mathbb{Z}$-module structure, given by the formula above.

3. **Morphisms agree.** Deduce that a function $f:M\to N$ between $\mathbb{Z}$-[[Def - Module|modules]] is a [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]] if and only if it is a homomorphism of abelian [[Def - Group|groups]]. Hence the theory of $\mathbb{Z}$-modules and the theory of abelian groups are *the same theory*.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are a commutative [[Def - Ring|ring]] $R$, an $R$-module, and an abelian group.

![[Def - Module#The Definition]]

Spelling out the four scalar axioms, with $r,r_1,r_2\in R$ and $m,m_1,m_2\in M$: (a) $(r_1+r_2)\cdot m=r_1\cdot m+r_2\cdot m$ — the action is *additive in the scalar*; (b) $r\cdot(m_1+m_2)=r\cdot m_1+r\cdot m_2$ — additive in the module element; (c) $r_1\cdot(r_2\cdot m)=(r_1r_2)\cdot m$ — *associativity* of the two multiplications; (d) $1_R\cdot m=m$ — the *unital* axiom. Axiom (i) demands separately that $(M,+,0_M)$ be an abelian group.

![[Def - Abelian Group#The Definition]]

The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ enters through one structural fact, the **cyclic generation of $\mathbb{Z}$ by $1$**: every integer is obtained from $1_{\mathbb{Z}}=1$ by repeated addition and negation. Positive $n$ is $1+1+\cdots+1$ ($n$ summands); $0$ is the empty sum; negative $-n$ is $-(1+\cdots+1)$. This is the additive analogue of "$\mathbb{Z}$ is the free group on one generator," and it is the entire reason the action is rigid.

A function $f:M\to N$ between $\mathbb{Z}$-modules is a **module homomorphism** if it respects both addition and the scalar action: $f(m_1+m_2)=f(m_1)+f(m_2)$ and $f(n\cdot m)=n\cdot f(m)$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$. A **homomorphism of abelian groups** asks only the first condition.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *two structures coincide* problem — show that "$\mathbb{Z}$-module" and "abelian group" are notational variants of one object. As the [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] records, the way to prove "category $\mathcal{C}$ is the category $\mathcal{D}$" is to produce, for each $\mathcal{C}$-object, a *unique* compatible $\mathcal{D}$-structure (and vice versa), and then check the morphism conditions agree. Here the abelian-group structure is visibly *part of* the module datum, so the only work is the uniqueness and existence of the scalar action.

**Assumption pattern.** The decisive hypothesis is the identity of the ring: $R=\mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{Z}$ is generated as a ring (indeed as an additive monoid-with-negation) by the *single* element $1$. The unital axiom (d) nails down what $1$ does — it acts as the identity — and the additivity axiom (a) lets that single fact propagate to *every* integer. The pattern "scalar ring generated by elements whose action is pinned by an axiom" is exactly what makes an action *forced* rather than *chosen*.

**Theorem routing.** There is no named theorem; the route is a three-part argument. Part one (forced action) is an *induction on $n$* using axiom (a) to climb from $1\cdot m=m$ to $n\cdot m=m+\cdots+m$, plus axiom (a) again at $r_1=n,r_2=-n$ to handle negatives and the zero. Part two (existence) checks the four axioms directly for the formula $n\cdot m=m+\cdots+m$ — this is bookkeeping with sums. Part three (morphisms) observes that once the action is "repeated addition," $f(n\cdot m)=n\cdot f(m)$ is *implied* by additivity $f(m_1+m_2)=f(m_1)+f(m_2)$, again by induction.

**Key decision point.** The crux is recognising that there is *nothing to choose*. A beginner expects "putting a module structure on $A$" to involve a decision; the insight is that axioms (d) and (a) together remove every degree of freedom. Concretely: $1$ must act as identity (axiom d), $2=1+1$ must therefore act as $m\mapsto m+m$ (axiom a), and the induction is unstoppable. The interesting content is not "find an action" but "prove the action you were going to write down is the *only* one."

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Pin a scalar's action via the unital axiom.** Axiom (d), $1_R\cdot m=m$, fixes the action of the generator $1\in\mathbb{Z}$. This is the *base case* of every subsequent induction.

2. **Propagate an action along ring addition using distributivity in the scalar.** Axiom (a), $(r_1+r_2)\cdot m=r_1\cdot m+r_2\cdot m$, converts knowledge of $r_1\cdot m$ and $r_2\cdot m$ into knowledge of $(r_1+r_2)\cdot m$. Applied with $r_1=n,r_2=1$ this is the *inductive step* climbing $n\cdot m\to(n+1)\cdot m$; applied with $r_1=n,r_2=-n$ it forces $(-n)\cdot m=-(n\cdot m)$.

3. **Verify a proposed action satisfies the module axioms.** For the converse, the formula $n\cdot m=m+\cdots+m$ is checked against axioms (a)–(d) one at a time — the standard "candidate-structure verification" move.

4. **Reduce a scalar-compatibility condition to an additivity condition.** For the morphism statement, $f(n\cdot m)=n\cdot f(m)$ is *deduced* from $f(m_1+m_2)=f(m_1)+f(m_2)$ by induction, rather than assumed — the operation of recognising one axiom as redundant given another.

5. **Identify two definitions by matching their data and their morphisms.** The conclusion "the theory of $\mathbb{Z}$-modules *is* the theory of abelian groups" is obtained by matching objects (Parts 1–2) and morphisms (Part 3) — the standard move for proving an *isomorphism of categories*.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Do not try to "construct" the action — instead, *assume* you have a $\mathbb{Z}$-module and ask how much freedom you had. Start at the one place an axiom directly tells you a value: what does axiom (d) say $1\cdot m$ must be?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> You know $1\cdot m=m$. Now compute $2\cdot m$ *without* choosing anything: write $2=1+1$ and apply the scalar-distributivity axiom (a), $(r_1+r_2)\cdot m=r_1\cdot m+r_2\cdot m$. You get $2\cdot m=1\cdot m+1\cdot m=m+m$. Iterate: this is an induction on $n$ establishing $n\cdot m=m+\cdots+m$ for every $n>0$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For $n=0$ and $n<0$: apply axiom (a) with $r_1=n$, $r_2=-n$. Since $n+(-n)=0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, the left side is $0\cdot m$. Separately, what is $0\cdot m$? Use $0=0+0$ and axiom (a) to show $0\cdot m=0_M$. Combining, $n\cdot m+(-n)\cdot m=0_M$, so $(-n)\cdot m$ is the additive inverse of $n\cdot m$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For the converse (every abelian group is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module): take the formula from Hints 2–3 as a *definition* of the action and verify axioms (a)–(d) directly — each is an identity about sums of copies of an element. For the morphism statement: a group homomorphism $f$ satisfies $f(m+m)=f(m)+f(m)$, hence $f(2m)=2f(m)$; induct to get $f(nm)=nf(m)$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$. So the scalar-compatibility axiom is *automatic*.

# Solution

The strategy is to show there is *no choice* in the scalar action: the unital axiom fixes how $1$ acts, and scalar-distributivity forces how every other integer acts, by induction. The converse and the morphism statement are then routine verifications.

**Step 1: The unital axiom fixes the action of $1$.**

In any $\mathbb{Z}$-module, $1\cdot m=m$ for every $m\in M$. This is the base case.

> [!note]- Derivation
> This is module axiom (d) read with $1_R=1\in\mathbb{Z}$: $1\cdot m=m$. No work — it is one of the four defining axioms. Its importance is that it is the *only* axiom that outputs a concrete value of the action with no hypotheses; every other determination is built on top of it.

**Step 2: Induction forces $n\cdot m=m+\cdots+m$ ($n$ copies) for every positive integer $n$.**

For $n\geq 1$, the value $n\cdot m$ is not free — it equals the $n$-fold sum of $m$ with itself.

> [!note]- Derivation
> We induct on $n\geq 1$. *Base case* $n=1$: $1\cdot m=m$, the one-fold sum, by Step 1.
>
> *Inductive step.* Suppose $n\cdot m=\underbrace{m+\cdots+m}_{n}$. In the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ we have $n+1$ literally as a sum, so apply module axiom (a), $(r_1+r_2)\cdot m=r_1\cdot m+r_2\cdot m$, with $r_1=n$ and $r_2=1$:
> $$(n+1)\cdot m=n\cdot m+1\cdot m=\Big(\underbrace{m+\cdots+m}_{n}\Big)+m=\underbrace{m+\cdots+m}_{n+1}.$$
> The first equality is axiom (a), the second is the inductive hypothesis together with Step 1. By induction the formula holds for all $n\geq 1$.
>
> The point: at no step did we *choose* anything. Each value $n\cdot m$ was *computed* from the previous one and the axioms. Any $\mathbb{Z}$-module structure on $(M,+,0_M)$ must agree with this formula.

**Step 3: The action on $0$ and on negative integers is also forced.**

We have $0\cdot m=0_M$, and $(-n)\cdot m=-(n\cdot m)$ for $n\geq 1$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *The scalar $0$.* In $\mathbb{Z}$, $0=0+0$. Apply axiom (a) with $r_1=r_2=0$:
> $$0\cdot m=(0+0)\cdot m=0\cdot m+0\cdot m.$$
> This is an equation in the abelian group $(M,+,0_M)$ of the form $x=x+x$. Adding $-x$ (which exists, since $M$ is a group under $+$) to both sides gives $0_M=x$. Hence $0\cdot m=0_M$.
>
> *Negative scalars.* Fix $n\geq 1$. In $\mathbb{Z}$, $n+(-n)=0$. Apply axiom (a) with $r_1=n$, $r_2=-n$:
> $$0_M=0\cdot m=(n+(-n))\cdot m=n\cdot m+(-n)\cdot m.$$
> Thus $(-n)\cdot m$ is *the* additive inverse of $n\cdot m$ in the group $(M,+)$, i.e. $(-n)\cdot m=-(n\cdot m)$. Inverses in a group are unique, so this value too is forced. Combining Steps 2 and 3: in *any* $\mathbb{Z}$-module the action is completely determined by $(M,+,0_M)$.

**Step 4: Every abelian group carries this action, and it satisfies all four axioms — existence and uniqueness.**

Given an abelian group $(A,+,0)$, define $n\cdot a$ by the Steps 2–3 formula. This is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and by Steps 1–3 it is the only one.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $(A,+,0)$ be an abelian group. Define, for $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $a\in A$,
> $$n\cdot a=\begin{cases}\underbrace{a+\cdots+a}_{n}&n>0\\[2pt]0&n=0\\[2pt]-\big(\underbrace{a+\cdots+a}_{-n}\big)&n<0.\end{cases}$$
> Axiom (i) holds by hypothesis: $(A,+,0)$ is an abelian group. We check the scalar axioms (a)–(d). All four reduce to elementary identities about sums of copies of an element; commutativity of $+$ is used freely to rearrange.
>
> - **(d) Unital:** $1\cdot a$ is the one-fold sum, $=a$. ✓
> - **(a) Additive in the scalar, $(m+n)\cdot a=m\cdot a+n\cdot a$:** for $m,n>0$, the left side is a sum of $m+n$ copies of $a$, which by associativity of $+$ is (sum of $m$ copies)$+$(sum of $n$ copies)$=m\cdot a+n\cdot a$. The cases involving signs follow because $(-k)\cdot a=-(k\cdot a)$ and $-(x+y)=(-x)+(-y)$ in an abelian group; a short case-check on the signs of $m,n,m+n$ completes it.
> - **(b) Additive in the element, $n\cdot(a+b)=n\cdot a+n\cdot b$:** for $n>0$, $n\cdot(a+b)$ is a sum of $n$ copies of $a+b$; by commutativity and associativity the $a$'s and $b$'s can be segregated, giving (sum of $n$ copies of $a$)$+$(sum of $n$ copies of $b$). The $n\leq 0$ cases follow by the negation identity as in (a).
> - **(c) Associative, $m\cdot(n\cdot a)=(mn)\cdot a$:** for $m,n>0$, $n\cdot a$ is a sum of $n$ copies of $a$, and taking $m$ copies of *that* yields a sum of $mn$ copies of $a$, which is $(mn)\cdot a$. Signs again handled by the negation identity.
>
> All four axioms hold, so $(A,+,0,\cdot)$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. *Uniqueness:* Steps 1–3 showed every $\mathbb{Z}$-module structure on a fixed abelian group must equal this formula, so this is the only one. Hence "abelian group" and "$\mathbb{Z}$-module" carry exactly the same data.

**Step 5: Module [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphisms]] of $\mathbb{Z}$-modules are precisely group homomorphisms.**

A function $f:M\to N$ between $\mathbb{Z}$-modules is a module homomorphism if and only if it is an abelian-group homomorphism.

> [!note]- Derivation
> ($\Rightarrow$) A module homomorphism satisfies $f(m_1+m_2)=f(m_1)+f(m_2)$ by definition — that *is* the abelian-group homomorphism condition. So every module homomorphism is a group homomorphism.
>
> ($\Leftarrow$) Conversely let $f:M\to N$ be an abelian-group homomorphism, so $f(m_1+m_2)=f(m_1)+f(m_2)$. We must check the extra module condition $f(n\cdot m)=n\cdot f(m)$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ — and the claim is that it is *free*. Induct on $n\geq 1$: for $n=1$, $f(1\cdot m)=f(m)=1\cdot f(m)$. Inductive step:
> $$f((n+1)\cdot m)=f(n\cdot m+m)=f(n\cdot m)+f(m)=n\cdot f(m)+f(m)=(n+1)\cdot f(m),$$
> using Step 2's formula, then additivity of $f$, then the inductive hypothesis, then Step 2 again. For $n=0$: $f(0\cdot m)=f(0_M)=0_N=0\cdot f(m)$ (a group homomorphism sends identity to identity). For $n<0$, write $n=-k$ with $k>0$: $f((-k)\cdot m)=f(-(k\cdot m))=-f(k\cdot m)=-(k\cdot f(m))=(-k)\cdot f(m)$, using that a group homomorphism commutes with negation.
>
> So $f(n\cdot m)=n\cdot f(m)$ holds for every $n\in\mathbb{Z}$ automatically. Hence the two notions of morphism coincide.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** For $R=\mathbb{Z}$: $\mathbb{Z}$-modules are exactly abelian groups, and $\mathbb{Z}$-module homomorphisms are exactly abelian-group homomorphisms.
>
> *The action is forced.* Let $(M,+,0_M,\cdot)$ be a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. Axiom (d) gives $1\cdot m=m$. By induction on $n\geq 1$, using axiom (a) with $r_1=n,r_2=1$, $\;(n+1)\cdot m=n\cdot m+1\cdot m$, so $n\cdot m=\underbrace{m+\cdots+m}_{n}$. From $0=0+0$ and axiom (a), $0\cdot m=0\cdot m+0\cdot m$, whence $0\cdot m=0_M$. From $n+(-n)=0$ and axiom (a), $0_M=0\cdot m=n\cdot m+(-n)\cdot m$, so $(-n)\cdot m=-(n\cdot m)$. Thus the action is determined by $(M,+,0_M)$.
>
> *Existence and uniqueness.* Given an abelian group $(A,+,0)$, define $n\cdot a$ by the same formula. Axiom (i) is the hypothesis; axioms (a)–(d) are verified as identities on sums of copies of an element, using commutativity and associativity of $+$ and the identity $(-k)\cdot a=-(k\cdot a)$. So $A$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and by the previous paragraph it is the unique one on $(A,+,0)$.
>
> *Morphisms.* A $\mathbb{Z}$-module homomorphism $f$ satisfies $f(m_1+m_2)=f(m_1)+f(m_2)$, i.e. is a group homomorphism. Conversely, if $f$ is a group homomorphism, induction on $|n|$ (base $f(1\cdot m)=f(m)$; step $f((n+1)m)=f(nm)+f(m)$; and $f(0_M)=0_N$, $f(-x)=-f(x)$) gives $f(n\cdot m)=n\cdot f(m)$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$, so $f$ is a module homomorphism. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**A scalar action is "forced" exactly when the ring is generated by elements whose action an axiom pins down.** The whole exercise turns on one observation: the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is generated, under addition and negation, by the single element $1$; the unital axiom (d) says how $1$ acts; and scalar-distributivity (a) lets that one fact propagate to every integer. There is no choice — "putting a $\mathbb{Z}$-module structure on $A$" is not a construction with options, it is a forced consequence. This is the module-theoretic shadow of "$\mathbb{Z}$ is the initial ring": for *any* ring $R$ there is a unique ring map $\mathbb{Z}\to R$, and correspondingly every $R$-module is *automatically* a $\mathbb{Z}$-module by restriction, with the $\mathbb{Z}$-action being repeated addition. The general trigger: whenever the scalar ring is generated by a small set of elements whose action is constrained by axioms, expect the action to be rigid; the freedom in a module structure lives entirely in how the *generators* act, and for $\mathbb{Z}$ there is exactly one generator acting in exactly one way.

**"Two definitions describe the same objects" is proven by matching data *and* morphisms — an isomorphism of categories.** It is not enough to show every abelian group can be made a $\mathbb{Z}$-module and conversely; one must also check that the *maps* agree, because a mathematical theory is its objects together with its morphisms. The exercise's three parts — forced action, existence/uniqueness, morphism agreement — are exactly the three things to verify for an *isomorphism of categories* $\mathbf{Ab}\cong\mathbb{Z}\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$. The reusable pattern: to claim "structure $X$ is just structure $Y$ in disguise," (i) build a bijection on objects, (ii) prove it has no choices (uniqueness), and (iii) show a function is an $X$-morphism if and only if it is a $Y$-morphism. The same triad establishes "a $k$-module is a $k$-vector space," "a representation of a group $G$ is a $k[G]$-module," and "an $F[X]$-module is a vector space with a chosen endomorphism" — each is an isomorphism of categories proven by this checklist.

**An axiom can be redundant: scalar-compatibility for $f$ follows from additivity when scalars are repeated addition.** Step 5 shows that for $\mathbb{Z}$-modules the morphism condition $f(n\cdot m)=n\cdot f(m)$ is *not an extra requirement* — it is implied by $f(m_1+m_2)=f(m_1)+f(m_2)$, because $n\cdot m$ is *itself* built from $+$. This is a recurring phenomenon: when one operation is definable from another, compatibility with the first is free given compatibility with the second. The same logic shows a ring homomorphism that respects $+$ and $\times$ automatically respects all polynomial expressions, and that a continuous group homomorphism $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is automatically $\mathbb{Q}$-linear. The lesson for problem-solving: before verifying a list of compatibility conditions, ask which ones are *consequences* of the others — often a definition carries redundant axioms, and recognising the redundancy is precisely what makes "two structures coincide" arguments short.

**This exercise is the template by which module theory generalises abelian-group theory.** Reading the result in reverse is illuminating: it says module theory, with a *general* scalar ring $R$, is "abelian-group theory where the integers have been replaced by $R$." Every module-theoretic concept specialises, when $R=\mathbb{Z}$, to a familiar abelian-group concept — a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] becomes a [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]], a [[Def - Quotient Module|quotient module]] becomes a quotient group, the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|module isomorphism theorems]] become the abelian-group isomorphism theorems, and the [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]] of an element becomes the set of integers killing it (its *order* generates that [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]]). When a module-theory definition or theorem looks unfamiliar, the first sanity check is to set $R=\mathbb{Z}$ and confirm it recovers the abelian-group statement you already know; if it does not, you have either misread the definition or found a phenomenon genuinely new to modules — and pinning down which is itself valuable.
