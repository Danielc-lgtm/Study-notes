# Notation Registry

- $R, S$ — commutative rings with a multiplicative identity $1_R$; from §3.3 onward $R$ is always a **Euclidean domain** unless stated otherwise
- $\varphi : R \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ — the **Euclidean function** of a Euclidean domain $R$: the size measure for which the Euclidean algorithm runs (in $\mathbb{Z}$ it is $|n|$, in $F[X]$ it is the degree)
- $\gcd(a, b)$ — a greatest common divisor of $a, b \in R$; in a Euclidean domain it exists and there are $x, y \in R$ with $ax + by = \gcd(a, b)$ (Bézout)
- $a \mid b$ — $a$ **divides** $b$, meaning $b = ac$ for some $c \in R$
- $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ — the **divisibility chain** of the invariant factors: each $d_i$ divides the next
- $A, B, C$ — matrices with entries in $R$, of size $m \times n$ unless stated; $A^{T}$ — the transpose
- $M_{m,n}(R)$ — the set of $m \times n$ matrices over $R$
- $\text{ER1, ER2, ER3}$ / $\text{EC1, EC2, EC3}$ — the three **elementary row operations** and the three **elementary column operations**
- $B = Q A T^{-1}$ — the relation between equivalent matrices: $Q$ and $T^{-1}$ are products of elementary matrices, hence invertible
- $\text{Fit}_k(A) \trianglelefteq R$ — the $k$-th **Fitting ideal** of $A$, generated by all $k \times k$ minors of $A$
- $R^m$, $R^n$ — the free $R$-module of rank $m$ (or $n$); its standard basis is $e_1, \dots, e_m$
- $N \leq R^m$ — $N$ is a submodule of the free module $R^m$
- $R/(d)$ — the cyclic module $R$ modulo the principal ideal $(d)$; when $d$ is a unit this is the zero module, when $d = 0$ it is $R$ itself
- $M_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus M_k$ — the direct sum of modules
- $C_n$ — the cyclic group of order $n$; $C_\infty \cong \mathbb{Z}$ — the infinite cyclic group
- $F$ — a field; $F[X]$ — the polynomial ring in one indeterminate over $F$, always a Euclidean domain
- $V$ — a finite-dimensional vector space over $F$; $\alpha : V \to V$ — a linear endomorphism (operator) of $V$
- $V_\alpha$ — the $F[X]$-module structure on $V$ in which $X$ acts as $\alpha$, so $f \cdot v = f(\alpha)(v)$
- $c(f)$ — the **companion matrix** of a monic polynomial $f$
- $J_m(\lambda)$ — the **Jordan block** of size $m$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$
- "conjugate" / "similar" — square matrices $A, A'$ with $A' = P A P^{-1}$ for some invertible $P$; equivalently they represent one operator in two bases

# Motivation

This topic is the payoff of the entire course. Three subjects that look unrelated — the classification of finitely generated abelian [[Def - Group|groups]], the rational canonical form of a matrix, and the Jordan normal form — turn out to be **one theorem**, the structure theorem for finitely generated [[Def - Module|modules]] over a Euclidean domain, read in three different rings. The previous topic, [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2]], built the language: [[Def - Module|modules]], [[Def - Submodule|submodules]], [[Def - Free Module|free modules]], [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finite generation]], and the slogan that a module is a vector space over a ring whose new content is the possible absence of a basis. This topic spends that language. It proves the structure theorem and then watches it specialise.

Begin with the concrete obstacle. Over a field, linear algebra already tells us that any matrix can be reduced by row and column operations to the block form $\begin{pmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ — a single number, the rank, classifies a matrix up to row/column equivalence. Over a ring this fails, and it fails for a reason worth naming. Consider $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$: no sequence of integer row and column operations can turn the $2$ into a $1$, because every such operation is an invertible integer matrix and $\det$ is multiplicative, so the entry $2$ — really the ideal $(2)$ it generates — is rigid. The honest replacement for "rank classifies a matrix" is the **Smith normal form**: every matrix over a Euclidean domain is equivalent to a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$ whose non-zero diagonal entries form a **divisibility chain** $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. Over a field every non-zero $d_i$ is a unit and can be scaled to $1$, recovering the old theorem; over $\mathbb{Z}$ the $d_i$ carry genuine arithmetic information, exactly the information the field threw away.

The Smith normal form is not an end in itself — it is the **computational engine** for the structure theorem. Here is the chain of reasoning, and it is short. A finitely generated module $M$ is, by [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|the surjection characterisation]], a quotient $R^m / N$ of a free module by a submodule of relations $N$. Over a Euclidean domain $N$ is itself free and finitely generated, so $N$ is the column span of an explicit relations matrix $A$. Reducing $A$ to Smith normal form means changing the basis of $R^m$ (row operations) and changing the generators of $N$ (column operations) until the relations become *uncoupled*: in the new basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$, the submodule $N$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$. The quotient then splits coordinatewise, $M \cong R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_r) \oplus R^{m-r}$, a direct sum of cyclic modules and a free part. That is the **structure theorem**, and the divisibility chain $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ is inherited verbatim from the Smith normal form.

Now watch the same theorem produce three famous results by changing $R$. With $R = \mathbb{Z}$, a module is an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]], and the theorem becomes the **classification of finitely generated abelian groups**: every such group is $C_{d_1} \times \cdots \times C_{d_r} \times C_\infty^{\,\ell}$ with $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. With $R = F[X]$, the doorway opened in the previous topic — an $F[X]$-module is a vector space $V$ with a chosen endomorphism $\alpha$, the indeterminate $X$ acting as $\alpha$ — turns the theorem into a classification of *operators up to similarity*. A finite-dimensional $V_\alpha$ has no free $F[X]$-part (because $F[X]$ is infinite-dimensional over $F$ and $V$ is not), so $V_\alpha \cong \bigoplus F[X]/(f_i)$ with $f_1 \mid \cdots \mid f_s$; each cyclic summand $F[X]/(f)$ carries $\alpha$ as the companion matrix $c(f)$, and the block-diagonal assembly is the **rational canonical form**. Refine the decomposition by the Chinese remainder theorem into prime-power pieces $F[X]/(p^n)$, work over $\mathbb{C}$ so that the primes are exactly the linear polynomials $X - \lambda$, and each piece $\mathbb{C}[X]/((X-\lambda)^a)$ carries $\alpha$ as a **Jordan block** — the **Jordan normal form**. The minimal and characteristic polynomials fall out of the decomposition for free. The whole of the canonical-form theory of linear algebra is the structure theorem wearing the costume $R = F[X]$. That unification — and the machine that delivers it — is what this topic is for.

# Concept Map

## §3.3 Matrices over Euclidean Domains

- **[[Def - Elementary Operations and Equivalent Matrices]]**
	- An **elementary row operation** on a matrix $A$ over $R$ is one of: (ER1) add $c \in R$ times row $i$ to row $j$; (ER2) swap rows $i$ and $j$; (ER3) multiply row $i$ by a *unit* $c \in R$ — each realised by left multiplication by an invertible elementary matrix, with **column** operations (EC1–EC3) realised by right multiplication. Two matrices $A, B$ are **equivalent** if a sequence of such operations carries one to the other, equivalently $B = QAT^{-1}$ for invertible $Q$ (rows) and $T^{-1}$ (columns). The unit restriction in ER3 is essential — over a ring you may scale by $-1$ but not by $2$ — and is exactly what makes equivalence over a ring coarser than over a field. Row operations change the basis of the ambient free module and column operations change a chosen generating set of a submodule, which is why this single notion drives the whole structure theory.

- **[[Thm - Smith Normal Form]]**
	- Every $m \times n$ matrix $A$ over a Euclidean domain $R$ is equivalent to a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, d_2, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$ with all $d_i \neq 0$ and the **divisibility chain** $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$; the $d_i$ are the **invariant factors** of $A$. The proof is a terminating algorithm: use the Euclidean algorithm to shrink the $(1,1)$-entry until it divides its whole row and column, clear them, and — the one subtle step — if some entry of the remaining block is not divisible by $d_{1}$, fold it back into position $(1,1)$ to reduce it further, then recurse. Over a field every $d_i$ is a unit and may be scaled to $1$, recovering the rank normal form $\begin{pmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$; over $\mathbb{Z}$ or $F[X]$ the $d_i$ carry genuine arithmetic. This is the computational engine behind every theorem in the topic.

- **[[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal]]**
	- A **$k \times k$ minor** of $A$ is the determinant of a $k \times k$ submatrix obtained by deleting all but $k$ rows and all but $k$ columns; the **$k$-th Fitting ideal** $\text{Fit}_k(A) \trianglelefteq R$ is the ideal *generated by all* $k \times k$ minors of $A$. A single matrix has many minors — one per choice of rows and columns — and the device of bundling them into one ideal washes out that choice. The Fitting [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] form a descending chain $\text{Fit}_1(A) \supseteq \text{Fit}_2(A) \supseteq \cdots$ because expanding a $(k+1)$-minor along a row writes it as a combination of $k$-minors. They are the invariant-extracting tool: minors themselves are not preserved by elementary operations, but the ideal they generate is.

- **[[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]]**
	- If $A$ and $B$ are equivalent matrices, then $\text{Fit}_k(A) = \text{Fit}_k(B)$ for every $k$; consequently the invariant factors of the Smith normal form satisfy $\text{Fit}_k(A) = (d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k)$, so each $d_k$ is determined up to a unit as a generator of $\text{Fit}_k(A)$ divided by a generator of $\text{Fit}_{k-1}(A)$. The proof checks one elementary operation at a time: a row swap or unit scaling changes each minor only by a unit, and a "add $c$ times a row" operation changes a minor $\det C$ to $\det C + c \det D$ with $D$ another minor of $A$, so the *ideal* is unchanged; invertibility of the operations gives the reverse inclusion. This is what makes "invariant factor" an honest name — the algorithm could in principle produce different $d_i$, but the Fitting ideals pin them down.

- **[[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain]]**
	- For a Euclidean domain $R$ and a submodule $N \leq R^m$, there is a basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ and elements $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ (with $0 \le r \le m$) such that $N$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$; in particular $N$ is itself **free of rank $r \leq m$**. The proof first shows (over any [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], by projecting onto the first coordinate and inducting) that $N$ needs at most $m$ generators, assembles those generators as the columns of a matrix, and applies Smith normal form — row operations re-coordinatise $R^m$, column operations re-choose the generators of $N$. The freeness fails badly over a non-PID: the ideal $(2, X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a submodule of the free module $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ but is not free, since it is not principal.

- **[[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain]]**
	- Every finitely generated module $M$ over a Euclidean domain $R$ is isomorphic to a direct sum of cyclic modules and a free part, $M \cong R/(d_1) \oplus R/(d_2) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_r) \oplus R^{\,\ell}$, with all $d_i \neq 0$ and the divisibility chain $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. The proof is two prior results glued: write $M \cong R^m/\ker\varphi$ via a surjection from a free module ([[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|surjection characterisation]] plus the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]]), then apply the submodule theorem to choose a basis of $R^m$ in which $\ker\varphi$ is generated by $d_i v_i$, so the quotient splits coordinatewise into $R/(d_i)$ summands and $\ell = m - r$ free copies of $R$. This single statement is the summit of the course; everything that follows is a specialisation.

- **[[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups]]**
	- Every finitely generated abelian group is isomorphic to $C_{d_1} \times C_{d_2} \times \cdots \times C_{d_r} \times C_\infty \times \cdots \times C_\infty$ with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$, where $C_\infty \cong \mathbb{Z}$; if the group is finite there are no $C_\infty$ factors and it is a product of finite cyclic groups. This is the structure theorem read at $R = \mathbb{Z}$, using that a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is exactly an abelian group: $R/(d_i)$ becomes $\mathbb{Z}/(d_i) = C_{d_i}$ and the free part $R^{\ell}$ becomes $C_\infty^{\,\ell}$. The practical workflow is to encode the group's generators and relations as a matrix and read the $d_i$ off the Fitting ideals. It refines, via the Chinese remainder theorem, to the prime-power form [[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|classified in group theory]].

- **[[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules]]**
	- If $R$ is a Euclidean domain and $a, b \in R$ are coprime ($\gcd(a,b) = 1$), then $R/(ab) \cong R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$ as $R$-modules. The isomorphism sends $(r_1 + (a), r_2 + (b)) \mapsto br_1 + ar_2 + (ab)$; surjectivity uses a Bézout relation $ax + by = 1$ to hit the generator $1$, and injectivity uses coprimality plus unique factorisation. This is the ring-theoretic Chinese remainder theorem rewritten in module language, and it is the tool that *refines* a coarse cyclic decomposition into a finer one by splitting each $R/(d)$ along the prime factorisation of $d$.

- **[[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem]]**
	- Every finitely generated module $M$ over a Euclidean domain $R$ is isomorphic to a direct sum $N_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus N_t$ in which each $N_i$ is either $R$ itself or a **prime-power cyclic module** $R/(p^n)$ for some prime $p \in R$ and $n \geq 1$. It is obtained from the structure theorem by factoring each invariant factor $d = p_1^{n_1} \cdots p_k^{n_k}$ into distinct prime powers and applying the Chinese remainder theorem to split $R/(d) \cong R/(p_1^{n_1}) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(p_k^{n_k})$. This is the *second* of the two canonical decompositions — the invariant-factor form has a divisibility chain, the primary form has prime powers — and it is the form that, over $R = \mathbb{C}[X]$, yields the Jordan normal form.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Structure Theorem over a General PID and Dedekind Domains *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> The proof above used the Euclidean function only to run the Smith-normal-form algorithm; the theorem itself is true over any [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], where one argues via gcd and ascending chains rather than an explicit algorithm. Over a **Dedekind domain** — the rings of integers of number fields — modules are no longer sums of cyclic pieces, but a structure theorem survives in the form "free part plus a single ideal class", and the obstruction to full freeness is measured by the ideal class group. See the algebraic number theory notes.

## §3.4 Normal Forms for Matrices

- **[[Def - The Module of a Linear Operator]]**
	- Given a vector space $V$ over a field $F$ and a linear endomorphism $\alpha : V \to V$, the **$F[X]$-module $V_\alpha$** is $V$ with the action $F[X] \times V \to V$, $(f, v) \mapsto f(\alpha)(v)$ — the indeterminate $X$ acts as $\alpha$, a constant acts as scalar multiplication, and a general polynomial acts as that polynomial evaluated at $\alpha$. If $V$ is finite-dimensional then $V_\alpha$ is a finitely generated $F[X]$-module, because any $F$-spanning set of $V$ also $F[X]$-generates $V_\alpha$ (the field $F$ sits inside $F[X]$). This construction is the bridge of the whole section: an isomorphism $V_\alpha \cong V'_\beta$ of $F[X]$-modules is exactly a basis change conjugating the matrix of $\alpha$ to that of $\beta$, so classifying $F[X]$-modules *is* classifying operators up to similarity.

- **[[Thm - Rational Canonical Form]]**
	- For any linear endomorphism $\alpha$ of a finite-dimensional $F$-vector space $V$, the module $V_\alpha$ decomposes as $V_\alpha \cong F[X]/(f_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus F[X]/(f_s)$ with monic $f_1 \mid f_2 \mid \cdots \mid f_s$ and *no free summand*, so there is a basis of $V$ in which $\alpha$ is block-diagonal with blocks the companion matrices $c(f_1), \dots, c(f_s)$. This is the structure theorem applied at $R = F[X]$: there is no $F[X]$-free part because $V$ is finite-dimensional over $F$ while $F[X]$ is not, and each cyclic summand $F[X]/(f)$ carries $\alpha$ as $c(f)$ in the basis $1, X, \dots, X^{\deg f - 1}$. It is genuinely canonical — the $f_i$ are pinned down by the structure theorem up to units and monic-ness leaves no freedom — and one reads the minimal polynomial off as $f_s$ and the characteristic polynomial as $f_1 \cdots f_s$.

- **[[Thm - Jordan Normal Form]]**
	- For an endomorphism $\alpha$ of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ over $\mathbb{C}$, the module $V_\alpha$ decomposes by primary decomposition as $V_\alpha \cong \bigoplus_i \mathbb{C}[X]/((X - \lambda_i)^{a_i})$ (the $\lambda_i$ need not be distinct), so there is a basis of $V$ in which $\alpha$ is block-diagonal with **Jordan blocks** $J_{a_i}(\lambda_i)$ — each block having $\lambda$ on the diagonal and $1$'s just below it. This is the primary decomposition theorem read at $R = \mathbb{C}[X]$, using that the primes of $\mathbb{C}[X]$ are exactly the linear polynomials $X - \lambda$ (fundamental theorem of algebra), and that $\mathbb{C}[X]/((X-\lambda)^a)$ carries $\alpha$ as a single Jordan block after the shift $Y = X - \lambda$. Unlike the rational canonical form it is canonical only up to reordering blocks; the minimal polynomial is $\prod_\lambda (X-\lambda)^{a_\lambda}$ with $a_\lambda$ the largest $\lambda$-block, and the number of $\lambda$-blocks is the dimension of the $\lambda$-eigenspace.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Spectral Theory and the Holomorphic Functional Calculus *(from Functional Analysis)*
> The Jordan form is the finite-dimensional spectral theorem: the eigenvalues are the spectrum and the Jordan blocks record how badly $\alpha$ fails to be diagonalisable. In infinite [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]] the polynomial action $f \mapsto f(\alpha)$ extends to a continuous-function or holomorphic-function action — the functional calculus — and the decomposition of $V_\alpha$ into eigenspace pieces becomes the spectral projection associated with a region of the spectrum. See the functional analysis and operator theory notes.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of this topic chase a small, recognisable set of goals, and they all descend from the structure theorem. The most characteristic target is **identifying a finitely generated module up to isomorphism** — given a module presented by generators and relations, name it as an explicit direct sum $R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_r) \oplus R^{\ell}$. When $R = \mathbb{Z}$ this is "which abelian group is this", and when $R = F[X]$ it is "what is this operator up to similarity". A closely related target is **computing the invariant factors $d_i$ of an explicit matrix**, either by running the Smith normal form algorithm or by computing Fitting ideals; these two routes are the workhorses, and a problem usually rewards whichever exploits its structure better. A third target is **deciding similarity or isomorphism**: two operators are similar exactly when their $F[X]$-modules agree, hence exactly when they share invariant factors, so similarity questions reduce to comparing canonical forms. A fourth is **reading off derived invariants** — the minimal polynomial ($f_s$, the last invariant factor over $F[X]$), the characteristic polynomial ($f_1 \cdots f_s$), the eigenspace dimensions (counts of Jordan blocks), the order of a finite abelian group ($d_1 \cdots d_r$). A fifth, more theoretical, is **establishing that a submodule is free, or finitely generated, and bounding its rank** — the kind of structural fact the submodule theorem delivers. These targets recur because the structure theorem is a *complete* classification: once you know the invariant factors, every other question about the module is a finite computation, so essentially every exercise either produces invariant factors or consumes them.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped, and each unlocks a specific step. **The ground ring is a Euclidean domain** is the master assumption of §3.3: it is precisely what makes the Euclidean algorithm available, hence Smith normal form, hence the entire structure theory; weaker rings lose the algorithm. **The ground ring is $\mathbb{Z}$** specialises everything to abelian groups and is the cue to read the structure theorem as the classification of finitely generated abelian groups. **The ground ring is $F[X]$** is the cue that the problem is secretly about a linear operator — and the precondition for that is just *a vector space with a chosen endomorphism*, so any operator-theoretic problem is an $F[X]$-module problem in disguise. **The ground ring is $\mathbb{C}[X]$**, or more generally a polynomial ring over an algebraically closed field, is the extra strength that makes every prime linear, unlocking the Jordan form via primary decomposition. **A module is given by an explicit presentation** — generators and a relations matrix — is the source that feeds directly into Smith normal form. **Two ring elements are coprime** is the trigger for the Chinese remainder theorem and hence for refining a cyclic module into prime-power pieces. **A module is a submodule of a free module** routes straight to the submodule theorem and a basis adapted to it. The discipline, as always, is to match a source to a target: a Euclidean domain plus a presentation routes through Smith normal form to invariant factors; $R = \mathbb{Z}$ routes the structure theorem to an abelian group; $R = F[X]$ routes it to a canonical form; coprimality routes a cyclic module to its primary decomposition. The [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in this topic is built from. When stuck, scan the list and try each. Everything is self-contained: a reader with no module theory background should be able to follow each operation from its description.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Reduce a matrix to Smith normal form.** Over a Euclidean domain, any matrix $A$ is equivalent to $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$ with $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$, by [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|the Smith normal form theorem]]. The trigger is *any explicit matrix* whose invariant content you need — a relations matrix of a module, the matrix of an operator. The algorithm: shrink the $(1,1)$-entry by the Euclidean algorithm until it divides its whole row and column, clear them, fold any non-divisible entry of the remaining block back into position $(1,1)$, recurse on the smaller block. The output diagonal *is* the list of invariant factors, and this is the single most-used operation in §3.3.

2. **Compute Fitting ideals to extract invariant factors without the algorithm.** The $k$-th [[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal|Fitting ideal]] $\text{Fit}_k(A)$ is generated by all $k \times k$ minors, it is unchanged under equivalence ([[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|invariance]]), and $\text{Fit}_k = (d_1 \cdots d_k)$, so $d_k$ is a generator of $\text{Fit}_k$ divided by one of $\text{Fit}_{k-1}$. The trigger is a small matrix, or one where a single small minor is obviously a unit — spotting one $1 \times 1$ or $2 \times 2$ minor equal to $\pm 1$ instantly gives $d_1 = 1$ or $d_2 = 1$ and short-circuits the algorithm. This is the *theoretical* route to invariant factors, complementary to operation 1's *computational* route.

3. **Present a finitely generated module as $R^m / N$ and read $N$ as a relations matrix.** Every finitely generated module sits in a surjection $R^m \twoheadrightarrow M$, so $M \cong R^m/\ker\varphi$ by the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]]; over a Euclidean domain $\ker\varphi$ is free with $\leq m$ generators ([[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|submodule theorem]]), so writing those generators as columns gives an explicit relations matrix. The trigger is any module described abstractly, or by generators-and-relations: this is the standard *first* move, because it converts a structural question into a concrete matrix and routes it to Smith normal form.

4. **Apply the structure theorem to split a module into cyclic pieces.** Once $M$ is presented and its relations matrix is in Smith normal form, [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|the structure theorem]] reads off $M \cong R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_r) \oplus R^{\ell}$. The trigger is "identify this module" or "classify these objects". Two bookkeeping reflexes: a unit $d_i$ makes $R/(d_i)$ the zero module, so drop it; the count $\ell = m - r$ of free copies is the difference between the number of generators and the number of non-zero invariant factors.

5. **Specialise the structure theorem by choosing the ring.** The same theorem is the classification of finitely generated abelian groups at $R = \mathbb{Z}$, and a canonical form for matrices at $R = F[X]$ — so a problem about abelian groups or about operators should be *recast as a module problem over the appropriate ring* and then solved by operations 3 and 4. The trigger is an abelian-group classification question (take $R = \mathbb{Z}$) or any "find a normal form / decide similarity" question for a linear map (take $R = F[X]$, form $V_\alpha$ via [[Def - The Module of a Linear Operator|the module of an operator]]).

6. **Build the $F[X]$-module of an operator.** Given $\alpha : V \to V$, equip $V$ with the action $f \cdot v = f(\alpha)(v)$; this $V_\alpha$ is finitely generated whenever $V$ is finite-dimensional. The trigger is any linear-algebra problem about a single endomorphism: similarity, canonical form, minimal or characteristic polynomial. The payoff is that *isomorphism of $F[X]$-modules is similarity of operators*, so all of operator theory becomes module theory, and the structure theorem hands over the rational canonical form. See [[Thm - Rational Canonical Form]].

7. **Split a cyclic module by the Chinese remainder theorem.** When $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules|the Chinese remainder theorem]] gives $R/(ab) \cong R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$. The trigger is a cyclic module $R/(d)$ whose modulus factors into coprime pieces — most importantly into prime powers. Iterating this is exactly how the invariant-factor form is refined to the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]], and over $\mathbb{C}[X]$ it is the step that turns the rational canonical form into the Jordan form.

8. **Read off derived invariants from the decomposition.** Once $M$ is in canonical form, finite data drops out for free: over $F[X]$ the minimal polynomial is the last invariant factor $f_s$, the characteristic polynomial is the product $f_1 \cdots f_s$, the eigenspace dimension is the number of Jordan blocks for that eigenvalue; over $\mathbb{Z}$ the order of a finite group is $d_1 \cdots d_r$. The trigger is any request for one of these invariants — do not compute it directly, compute the canonical form and read it off.

9. **Track what row versus column operations mean.** In Smith normal form, *row* operations change the basis of the ambient free module $R^m$ while *column* operations change the chosen generating set of the submodule $N$. The trigger is needing to produce not just the invariant factors but the actual adapted basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ in which $N = \langle d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r\rangle$ — accumulate the row operations into the invertible matrix $Q$ and the basis change is $Q^{-1}$. This is what upgrades "the invariant factors are $d_i$" to an explicit isomorphism.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Scaling a row or column by a non-unit to "clear" an entry
> Over a field, you reduce a non-zero entry to $1$ by dividing its row by that entry — and after years of Gaussian elimination this feels automatic. Over a ring it is *illegal*: elementary operation ER3 permits multiplying a row only by a **unit**, because only a unit-scaling matrix is invertible, and equivalence must be by invertible matrices. The cost of breaking the rule is visible in $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$: you *cannot* turn the $2$ into a $1$, since $\det$ is multiplicative and every legal operation has unit determinant, so the ideal $(2)$ is rigid. This rigidity is not a nuisance — it is the invariant factor $d_1 = 2$, the genuine arithmetic content the field discarded. The condition that licenses free scaling is exactly "$R$ is a field". See [[Def - Elementary Operations and Equivalent Matrices]].

> [!warning] 2. Treating individual minors as invariants
> Having learned that the Fitting ideals are invariants, it is tempting to believe each *minor* is preserved by elementary operations. It is not — only the **ideal generated by all $k \times k$ minors** survives. Adding $c$ times one row to another changes a minor $\det C$ to $\det C + c \det D$, a genuinely different number; what is preserved is that the new minor still lies in the ideal $\text{Fit}_k$, because $\det C$ and $\det D$ are both minors of the original. So a single minor can change wildly under equivalence while $\text{Fit}_k(A) = \text{Fit}_k(B)$ holds exactly. The correct statement is [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|the invariance of the Fitting ideal]], not of any minor; the standard error is to compute one minor of $A$, one of $B$, find them unequal, and wrongly conclude the matrices are inequivalent.

> [!warning] 3. Splitting $R/(d)$ by the Chinese remainder theorem when the factors are not coprime
> The decomposition $R/(ab) \cong R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$ is so useful that one is tempted to apply it to *any* factorisation $d = ab$. It requires $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, and fails without it. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$ is **not** isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$: the former has an element of order $4$, the latter does not, so they differ as groups even though $4 = 2 \cdot 2$. The proof of [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules|the Chinese remainder theorem]] uses a Bézout relation $ax + by = 1$, which exists precisely when $a$ and $b$ are coprime; without it surjectivity fails. The legal use is to factor $d$ into *distinct prime powers* $p_1^{n_1}, \dots, p_k^{n_k}$, which are pairwise coprime — this is exactly the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]].

> [!warning] 4. Expecting the Jordan normal form over an arbitrary field
> The Jordan form is so central that one forgets it needs an algebraically closed ground field. The primary decomposition $V_\alpha \cong \bigoplus F[X]/(p_i^{a_i})$ holds over any field, but the *primes* $p_i$ are only guaranteed to be linear — $p_i = X - \lambda$ — when $F$ is algebraically closed, and a linear prime is what produces a Jordan block. Over $\mathbb{R}$, the rotation by $90^\circ$ has $F[X]$-module $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2 + 1)$ with the *irreducible quadratic* $X^2 + 1$ as its prime; there is no real Jordan form, only the rational canonical form with the companion matrix of $X^2 + 1$. The condition that licenses the Jordan form is "$F$ algebraically closed" (in the course, $F = \mathbb{C}$); over a general field the honest universal answer is [[Thm - Rational Canonical Form|the rational canonical form]].

# Problem-Solving Strategy

Almost every problem in this topic is won by recognising that it is, in disguise, a request to compute or compare **invariant factors** — and the master move is therefore always the same: get the module into the form $R^m/N$, write $N$ as a matrix, and reduce that matrix to Smith normal form. The structure theorem is a *complete* classification, so once the invariant factors are in hand every further question is a finite computation. The skill is not in the computation but in the recognition: seeing that an abelian group, or a linear operator, or an abstract presentation, is really a finitely generated module over a Euclidean domain.

If the problem **hands you an explicit matrix and asks for its invariant factors**, you have a pure §3.3 computation, and there are two routes. The algorithmic route is to run Smith normal form directly: clear the first row and column using the Euclidean algorithm, fold non-divisible entries back to reduce $d_1$, recurse. The theoretical route is to compute [[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal|Fitting ideals]] — and this route is dramatically faster when the matrix is small or contains an obvious unit minor, because spotting a single $2 \times 2$ minor equal to $\pm 1$ immediately gives $d_1 = d_2 = 1$ with no algorithm at all, leaving only the top Fitting ideal (a single determinant) to compute. Choose the route the matrix rewards: large and sparse favours the algorithm, small or with conspicuous unit minors favours Fitting ideals. The worked example $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 5 \\ 3 & 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 0 & 7\end{pmatrix}$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ is the model — the minor $\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\3 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1$, so $d_1 = d_2 = 1$, and only $\text{Fit}_3 = (\det) = (3)$ remains, giving $d_3 = 3$.

If the problem **asks you to identify a finitely generated module**, the route is: present it as $R^m/N$ via [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|a surjection from a free module]] and the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]], read off the relations matrix from the generators of $N$, reduce to Smith normal form, and apply [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|the structure theorem]] to get $R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_r) \oplus R^{\ell}$. Discard any $R/(d_i)$ with $d_i$ a unit — it is the zero module. The whole subtlety here is *constructing the presentation*: when a module is described abstractly you must choose generators and find all relations among them, and once that is done the problem is mechanical.

If the problem **lives over $R = \mathbb{Z}$**, it is an abelian-group question: a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is an abelian group, so the structure theorem becomes [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups|the classification of finitely generated abelian groups]]. Encode the group's generators and relations as an integer matrix, compute invariant factors, and read off $C_{d_1} \times \cdots \times C_{d_r} \times C_\infty^{\,\ell}$. If you want the prime-power form instead of the divisibility-chain form, factor each $d_i$ into prime powers and apply [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules|the Chinese remainder theorem]] — this is [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]]. The two forms carry the same information; which one a problem wants is usually dictated by what it asks next (orders of elements favour prime-power form, the structure of [[Def - Subgroup|subgroups]] favours the invariant-factor form).

If the problem **is about a single linear operator** $\alpha$ — find a canonical form, decide whether two matrices are similar, compute a minimal or characteristic polynomial — recast it over $R = F[X]$. Form [[Def - The Module of a Linear Operator|the F[X]-module V_α]] in which $X$ acts as $\alpha$; since *isomorphism of $F[X]$-modules is similarity of operators*, every operator question is now a module question. The structure theorem gives $V_\alpha \cong \bigoplus F[X]/(f_i)$ with $f_1 \mid \cdots \mid f_s$ and no free part (because $V$ is finite-dimensional), and the [[Thm - Rational Canonical Form|rational canonical form]] is the block-diagonal of companion matrices $c(f_i)$. Two operators are similar exactly when their invariant factors agree. Read the minimal polynomial off as $f_s$ and the characteristic polynomial as $f_1 \cdots f_s$. If the ground field is $\mathbb{C}$ — or any algebraically closed field — refine by primary decomposition: every prime is linear, $\bigoplus \mathbb{C}[X]/((X-\lambda)^a)$, and each piece is a [[Thm - Jordan Normal Form|Jordan block]]. Do not attempt the Jordan form over a non-closed field; the rational canonical form is the universally available answer.

The thread through all of this is a single conviction worth internalising. **The Smith normal form is the engine and the structure theorem is the output, and every problem is the structure theorem read in the right ring.** Identifying an abelian group, classifying an operator, deciding similarity, computing a minimal polynomial — these look like four subjects and are one. The reason this viewpoint always repays adoption is that it replaces four separate bags of tricks with a single algorithm: present the module, reduce the matrix, read the invariant factors. When a problem resists, the question to ask is never "what trick applies here" but "what is the module, what is the ring, and what is the relations matrix" — and once those three are pinned down, the answer is a computation the structure theorem has already promised will terminate.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]]**: every matrix over a Euclidean domain is equivalent to $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$ with $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. This is the computational heart of the entire topic and the single most reused result. The recognisable setup is *any explicit matrix whose invariant content matters* — a module's relations matrix, an operator's matrix — and the move is always to reduce it and read the invariant factors off the diagonal. Its reusability comes from being the bridge between the concrete (a matrix of ring elements) and the structural (a direct-sum decomposition): it is what makes the structure theorem an *algorithm* rather than an existence statement. Internalise that row operations re-coordinatise the ambient module and column operations re-choose the submodule's generators — that dual reading is what lets you extract not just the $d_i$ but the explicit adapted basis.

- **[[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|The structure theorem]]**: every finitely generated module over a Euclidean domain is $R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_r) \oplus R^{\ell}$ with a divisibility chain. This is a *complete classification*, and that is exactly what makes it reusable: once a module is in this form, every question about it — its order, its torsion, its [[Def - Submodule|submodules]], whether it is free, whether it is isomorphic to another — is a finite computation on the $d_i$. The recognisable setup is "identify this module" or "classify these objects up to isomorphism". Its deepest reusability is that it is *ring-agnostic*: the very same theorem, with no new proof, becomes the classification of abelian groups and the canonical-form theory of linear algebra. When you meet a classification problem, the first question is which ring makes it an instance of this theorem.

- **[[Def - The Module of a Linear Operator|The F[X]-module of an operator]]**: $V$ with $X$ acting as $\alpha$ turns an endomorphism into a module. The reusable principle is the dictionary it sets up — *isomorphism of $F[X]$-modules equals similarity of operators*, submodules are $\alpha$-invariant [[Def - Subspace|subspaces]], the minimal polynomial is the [[Def - Annihilator|annihilator]] of the module. This converts every problem about a single linear map into a problem about a finitely generated $F[X]$-module, where the structure theorem is waiting. The recognisable setup is any linear-algebra question involving one fixed endomorphism: a canonical form, a similarity test, a minimal or characteristic polynomial, an eigenspace dimension. Reach for it the moment a problem fixes an operator and asks a structural question about it — it is the move that imports the whole machine of §3.3 into linear algebra.

- **[[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|Submodules of free modules are free]]**: over a Euclidean domain, a submodule of $R^m$ is free of rank $\leq m$, with a basis of $R^m$ adapted to it. Its reusability is as the *structural foundation* that makes the structure theorem possible: it guarantees that the relation module $\ker(R^m \to M)$ is itself free and finitely generated, so it can be written as a finite matrix and fed to Smith normal form. The recognisable setup is needing to control a submodule of a known free module — to bound the number of relations, or to know that relations can be encoded by a matrix at all. Keep sharp the *failure* over non-PIDs — $(2, X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a non-free submodule of a free module — because it is the sharpest reminder of exactly which hypothesis the whole theory rests on.

- **[[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|The Fitting ideals]]** $\text{Fit}_k(A)$: equivalent matrices share Fitting ideals, and $\text{Fit}_k = (d_1 \cdots d_k)$. The reusable role is twofold. As a *computational shortcut* it extracts invariant factors without running the full algorithm — and is unbeatable when a small unit minor is visible, collapsing several $d_i$ to units at a glance. As an *invariant* it is the rigorous justification that "invariant factor" is a well-defined notion: the Smith algorithm makes choices, but the $d_k$ it produces are pinned down up to units as $\text{Fit}_k / \text{Fit}_{k-1}$. The recognisable setup is a small matrix, or any need to *prove* two matrices inequivalent (exhibit differing Fitting ideals) — but never compare individual minors, which are not invariant.

# Bridges

1. **Linear algebra — the canonical forms *are* the structure theorem.** This is not an analogy: the rational canonical form and the Jordan normal form, the central classification results of a linear algebra course, are literally [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|the structure theorem]] read at $R = F[X]$. The construction making the bridge is [[Def - The Module of a Linear Operator|the F[X]-module V_α]] of an operator: a vector space with a chosen endomorphism is exactly a finitely generated $F[X]$-module, and a similarity $A' = PAP^{-1}$ of matrices is exactly an isomorphism of the corresponding modules. The structure theorem's cyclic summands $F[X]/(f_i)$ become the companion-matrix blocks $c(f_i)$ — the rational canonical form — and after primary decomposition over $\mathbb{C}$ they become Jordan blocks. Even the row/column reduction of a matrix over a field, the first thing taught in linear algebra, is Smith normal form in the case where every non-zero $d_i$ is a unit. What transfers is total: linear algebra over a field is module theory over $F[X]$, made degenerate by the field forcing every invariant factor to be trivial except for divisibility data.

2. **Group theory — the classification of finite abelian groups.** The structure theorem at $R = \mathbb{Z}$ is [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups|the classification of finitely generated abelian groups]], because a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is precisely an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]]. The invariant-factor form $C_{d_1} \times \cdots \times C_{d_r}$ with $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ and the prime-power form obtained by [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]] are the two decompositions a group theory course states — typically *without proof* — as [[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|the classification of finite abelian groups]]. This topic supplies the proof, and supplies it as a corollary of a result that was never about groups. The bridge teaches a lesson about mathematical economy: a theorem proved once at the right level of generality (modules over a Euclidean domain) discharges its debt in two subjects at once, and the Smith normal form is the concrete algorithm a group theorist can use to actually compute the decomposition of a group given by generators and relations.

3. **Homological algebra — presentations, syzygies, and the freeness of submodules.** A finitely generated module presented as $R^m/N$ is the first two terms of a process homological algebra makes systematic: a *free resolution* $\cdots \to R^{m_1} \to R^{m_0} \to M \to 0$. Over a Euclidean domain, [[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|the submodule theorem]] says the relation module $N$ is *itself free*, so the resolution stops after one step — every finitely generated module over a Euclidean (or PID) ring has a length-one free resolution, equivalently *projective dimension at most one*. This is a strong homological statement, and it is exactly why the higher derived functors $\text{Tor}^n$ and $\text{Ext}^n$ vanish for $n \geq 2$ over a PID. The Smith normal form, read homologically, is a *diagonalisation of the boundary map* $R^n \to R^m$ in this short resolution, and the invariant factors are the data classifying the resolution up to isomorphism.

4. **Commutative algebra — the structure theorem over a general PID and beyond.** The proof here used the Euclidean function only to run the Smith-normal-form algorithm, but the theorem holds over any [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], where the algorithm is replaced by gcd arguments and the ascending chain condition. This places the structure theorem inside the theory of modules over Noetherian rings, and identifies precisely the hypothesis it needs: *every ideal principal*. Weaken the ring to a **Dedekind domain** — the rings of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of number fields, where ideals factor uniquely but need not be principal — and the theorem deforms in an illuminating way: a finitely generated torsion-free module is no longer free but is a direct sum of *ideals*, and the failure of those ideals to be principal is measured by the ideal class group. So the structure theorem is the $h_K = 1$ case of a finer classification, and the gap between "Euclidean domain" and "Dedekind domain" is exactly the gap that algebraic number theory exists to measure.

# Insights

**The structure theorem is one theorem behind two entire subjects, and seeing the unity is the point of the topic.** It is worth stating with full precision how much is being unified, because the temptation is to treat the classification of abelian groups and the canonical forms of linear algebra as separate facts that happen to look alike. They are not alike — they are *identical*, the same theorem evaluated at two rings. The theorem says: a finitely generated module over a Euclidean domain is a direct sum of cyclic modules $R/(d_i)$ and a free part, with a divisibility chain. Feed it $R = \mathbb{Z}$ and, because a $\mathbb{Z}$-module *is* an abelian group, the cyclic modules $\mathbb{Z}/(d_i)$ become cyclic groups $C_{d_i}$ and the free part becomes copies of $\mathbb{Z}$ — the classification of finitely generated abelian groups, in one line. Feed it $R = F[X]$ and, because an $F[X]$-module *is* a vector space with a chosen endomorphism, the cyclic modules $F[X]/(f_i)$ become companion-matrix blocks and the theorem becomes the rational canonical form; refine over $\mathbb{C}$ and it becomes the Jordan form. The discipline this installs is to stop asking "what is the trick for this kind of problem" and start asking "what is the ring". Two famous, seemingly unrelated theorems collapse into one as soon as you are willing to see a group and an operator as the same kind of object — a module — differing only in which ring acts. That collapse is not a curiosity; it is the entire reason module theory was built, and it is the deliverable of this topic.

**The Smith normal form is the computational engine, and the divisibility chain is the residue of arithmetic that a field would have erased.** The structure theorem could be stated as a pure existence result, but what makes it powerful is that [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|the Smith normal form]] turns it into an *algorithm*: present the module as $R^m/N$, write $N$ as a matrix, reduce, read the invariant factors. Two things about this engine deserve to be held onto. First, the reduction is genuinely a *diagonalisation* — the analogue of putting a matrix in echelon form — but constrained by the fact that you may only scale by units; over a field that constraint is vacuous and every reduction ends at $\begin{pmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, so the only invariant is the rank. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ or $F[X]$ the constraint *bites*, and what survives the reduction is the divisibility chain $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. That chain is precisely the arithmetic information a field is too coarse to carry: a field cannot tell $2$ from $1$, but $\mathbb{Z}$ can, and the invariant factors are the record of those distinctions. Second, the divisibility chain is not an artefact of the algorithm — the algorithm makes arbitrary choices and could produce $d_i$ differing by units — but is pinned down by [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|the Fitting ideals]], with $d_k$ recoverable as $\text{Fit}_k/\text{Fit}_{k-1}$. So "invariant factor" is an honest, choice-free invariant of the module, and the Smith normal form is the constructive bridge from a concrete matrix to that invariant. The right mental image is: linear algebra over a field is module theory with the arithmetic deleted, and the invariant factors are what you recover when you stop deleting it.

**Presenting a module by a matrix is the move that makes the abstract concrete — and a relations matrix is a complete description.** The conceptual pivot of the topic is the realisation that a finitely generated module, however abstractly it is described, can always be *written down* as $R^m/N$ where $N$ is the column span of an explicit matrix of ring elements. This is possible because of two results working together: [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|every finitely generated module is a quotient of a free module]], and over a Euclidean domain [[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|the submodule of relations is itself free and finitely generated]] — so it, too, is the span of finitely many vectors, which become the columns of a matrix. The matrix *is* the module: it contains, in the most concrete possible form, exactly the information needed to recover $M$ up to isomorphism. This is why the topic feels computational rather than abstract — once a module is a matrix, the structure theorem is just linear algebra over $R$, and the Smith normal form does the rest. The lesson generalises far beyond this course: the recurring strategy in algebra is to find a *presentation* — a list of generators and relations — and the art is recognising that an object handed to you abstractly (an abelian group, an operator, a module) secretly *is* such a presentation. The relations matrix is the canonical example of that strategy succeeding completely: it is a finite, concrete object that determines an infinite, abstract one, and reducing it is the whole game.
