# Problem Statement

Let $A$ be the abelian group generated by three elements $a,b,c$ subject to the three relations
$$2a+3b+c=0,\qquad a+2b=0,\qquad 5a+6b+7c=0.$$
That is, $A=\mathbb{Z}^3/K$, where $K$ is the [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]] of $\mathbb{Z}^3$ generated by the relation vectors $(2,3,1)$, $(1,2,0)$, $(5,6,7)$ written in the basis $a,b,c$.

1. Form the **relation matrix** $X$ whose columns are the relation vectors.
2. Compute its Smith normal form, using the Fitting [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] to determine the invariant factors.
3. Read off $A$ as a direct product of cyclic [[Def - Group|groups]]. Decide, in particular, whether $A$ is the trivial group, an infinite group, or a finite group, and if finite, name it.

As stated the presentation gives no immediate clue what $A$ is — it is not even visibly non-trivial. The exercise is the standard pipeline that turns *any* finite presentation of an abelian group into an explicit product of cyclic [[Def - Group|groups]].

**Recall:**

An abelian group is the same thing as a [[Def - Module|module]] over the [[Def - Ring|ring]] $\mathbb{Z}$: the scalar action $n\cdot g$ is repeated addition. A **presentation by generators and relations** describes $A$ as a quotient $\mathbb{Z}^n/K$ — the free abelian group $\mathbb{Z}^n$ on the $n$ generators, modulo the [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]] $K$ generated by the relation vectors. Each relation $\sum_i r_i g_i=0$ becomes the vector $(r_1,\dots,r_n)\in\mathbb{Z}^n$.

The **relation matrix** $X$ collects the relation vectors as its columns (so $X$ is $n\times m$ for $n$ generators and $m$ relations).

![[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain#Statement]]

The mechanism behind this is the Smith normal form. Bringing $X$ to Smith normal form $\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_r,0,\dots)$ is exactly a change of basis of $\mathbb{Z}^n$ together with a change of generating set of $K$ (row operations re-coordinatise $\mathbb{Z}^n$, column operations re-choose the generators of $K$). In the resulting basis $v_1,\dots,v_n$ of $\mathbb{Z}^n$, the subgroup $K$ is generated by $d_1 v_1,\dots,d_r v_r$, so
$$A=\mathbb{Z}^n/K\cong\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(d_1)}\oplus\cdots\oplus\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(d_r)}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{\,n-r}.$$

![[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups#Statement]]

To find the $d_i$ we use the Fitting [[Def - Ideal|ideals]]. The **$k$-th Fitting ideal** $\operatorname{Fit}_k(X)$ is generated by all $k\times k$ minors ([[Def - Determinant|determinants]] of $k\times k$ submatrices) of $X$.

![[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal#The Definition]]

By [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|the invariance of Fitting ideals]], $\operatorname{Fit}_k(X)=(d_1\cdots d_k)$, so over $\mathbb{Z}$ the product $d_1\cdots d_k$ is the $\gcd$ of all $k\times k$ minors of $X$. A factor $d_i=1$ contributes $\mathbb{Z}/(1)=0$ and is dropped; a factor $d_i=0$ would contribute a free summand $\mathbb{Z}$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is an *identify a presented structure* problem: an abelian group is handed over by an opaque presentation, and the task is to compute its isomorphism type. It is the headline application of [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|the structure theorem]] — every such problem reduces to one Smith normal form computation.

**Assumption pattern.** The hypotheses are a finite list of generators and a finite list of $\mathbb{Z}$-linear relations. The recognisable signal is "abelian group, finitely many generators, finitely many relations" — the moment you see this, the entire downstream route is fixed: build the relation matrix and diagonalise it.

**Theorem routing.** [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain]] (specialised to $R=\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups]]) says $A\cong\bigoplus\mathbb{Z}/(d_i)\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n-r}$ where the $d_i$ are the invariant factors of the relation matrix. [[Thm - Smith Normal Form]] guarantees those invariant factors exist; [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]] lets us *extract* them as $d_1\cdots d_k=\gcd(k\times k\text{ minors})$ without doing a single row operation.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious part is realising that the relations $\sum r_i g_i=0$ are not equations to "solve" but *columns of a matrix to diagonalise*. Once a beginner sees that "the relation matrix records exactly the subgroup you quotient by, and Smith normal form is simultaneously a change of basis upstairs and a change of generators of that subgroup", the problem becomes purely computational. The second decision point is interpretive: a $d_i=1$ kills a generator entirely ($\mathbb{Z}/1=0$), so the answer can be far smaller than the number of generators — here three generators collapse to a single cyclic factor.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Encode a presentation as a relation matrix.** Turn each relation $\sum_i r_i g_i=0$ into the column vector $(r_1,\dots,r_n)$ and assemble the columns into the matrix $X$. This converts a group-theoretic description into a linear-algebraic object.

2. **Read invariant factors off the Fitting ideals.** Compute $\operatorname{Fit}_k(X)$ as the ideal generated by the $k\times k$ minors; over $\mathbb{Z}$ it is $(\gcd\text{ of those minors})$, and $d_1\cdots d_k$ is that $\gcd$.

3. **Spot a unit minor to terminate early.** If some $k\times k$ minor equals $\pm 1$, then $\operatorname{Fit}_k=(1)$, so $d_1\cdots d_k=1$, which forces $d_1=\cdots=d_k=1$. No further minors of that size need be examined.

4. **Convert invariant factors into a direct product** via [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups]]: $A\cong\bigoplus_i\mathbb{Z}/(d_i)\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n-r}$.

5. **Drop trivial factors.** A summand $\mathbb{Z}/(1)$ is the zero group; delete it. A summand $\mathbb{Z}/(0)=\mathbb{Z}$ is an infinite cyclic factor; keep it.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> An abelian group given by generators and relations is the quotient $\mathbb{Z}^n/K$: the free abelian group on the generators, modulo the subgroup $K$ generated by the relation vectors. Here $n=3$. Write each relation $\sum r_i g_i=0$ as a column $(r_1,r_2,r_3)$ and stack the three columns into a $3\times 3$ matrix $X$. Everything about $A$ is now encoded in $X$.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> You do **not** need to perform the Smith normal form reduction explicitly — the Fitting ideals hand you the invariant factors directly. Over $\mathbb{Z}$: $d_1=\gcd$ of all entries of $X$; $d_1 d_2=\gcd$ of all $2\times 2$ minors; $d_1 d_2 d_3=|\det X|$. So you only need to compute some entries, some $2\times 2$ minors, and one $3\times 3$ determinant.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Look for shortcuts. Is there an entry of $X$ equal to $\pm 1$? Then $\operatorname{Fit}_1=(1)$ and $d_1=1$. Is there a $2\times 2$ minor equal to $\pm 1$? Then $\operatorname{Fit}_2=(1)$ and $d_2=1$ as well. A single unit minor of size $k$ collapses all of $d_1,\dots,d_k$ to $1$. After that, only $\det X$ is left to compute.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> You should find $\operatorname{Fit}_1(X)=(1)$, $\operatorname{Fit}_2(X)=(1)$, and $\operatorname{Fit}_3(X)=(\det X)$. So $d_1=d_2=1$ and $d_3=|\det X|$. By [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups|the classification]], $A\cong\mathbb{Z}/(1)\oplus\mathbb{Z}/(1)\oplus\mathbb{Z}/(d_3)\cong\mathbb{Z}/(d_3)$. Compute $\det X$ to finish — it is $\pm 3$.

# Solution

The plan is the standard three-stage pipeline: presentation $\rightarrow$ relation matrix $\rightarrow$ Smith normal form (via Fitting ideals) $\rightarrow$ direct product.

**Step 1: Form the relation matrix.**

The three relation vectors $(2,3,1),(1,2,0),(5,6,7)$ become the columns of
$$X=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&5\\3&2&6\\1&0&7\end{pmatrix}.$$

> [!note]- Derivation
> The group is $A=\mathbb{Z}^3/K$ with $\mathbb{Z}^3$ free on the generators $a,b,c$ and $K=\langle(2,3,1),(1,2,0),(5,6,7)\rangle$.
>
> Read off the coordinates of each relation in the basis $(a,b,c)$:
> - $2a+3b+c=0$ gives the vector $(2,3,1)$.
> - $a+2b=0$, i.e. $1a+2b+0c=0$, gives $(1,2,0)$.
> - $5a+6b+7c=0$ gives $(5,6,7)$.
>
> Placing these as the columns of a matrix (convention: columns are relations, rows are generators):
> $$X=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&5\\3&2&6\\1&0&7\end{pmatrix}.$$
> Row $1$ corresponds to $a$, row $2$ to $b$, row $3$ to $c$; column $j$ is the $j$-th relation. So $A\cong\mathbb{Z}^3/(\text{column space of }X)$.

**Step 2: Find the Smith normal form via Fitting ideals.**

The Fitting ideals are $\operatorname{Fit}_1(X)=(1)$, $\operatorname{Fit}_2(X)=(1)$, $\operatorname{Fit}_3(X)=(3)$. Hence the invariant factors are $d_1=1,\ d_2=1,\ d_3=3$, and the Smith normal form is $\operatorname{diag}(1,1,3)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|invariance of Fitting ideals]], $\operatorname{Fit}_k(X)=(d_1\cdots d_k)$, and over the PID $\mathbb{Z}$ each is generated by the $\gcd$ of all $k\times k$ minors.
>
> **First Fitting ideal.** The $1\times 1$ minors are the entries of $X$. Among them is the entry $1$ (position $(1,2)$, and also $(3,1)$). An ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ containing $1$ is all of $\mathbb{Z}$:
> $$\operatorname{Fit}_1(X)=(1,\text{ other entries})=(1).$$
> So $d_1=1$.
>
> **Second Fitting ideal.** In principle we examine all $2\times 2$ minors, but we only need to find *one* equal to a unit. Take rows $\{1,2\}$ and columns $\{1,2\}$:
> $$\begin{vmatrix}2&1\\3&2\end{vmatrix}=2\cdot 2-1\cdot 3=4-3=1.$$
> A unit minor appears, so
> $$\operatorname{Fit}_2(X)=(1,\text{ other }2\times 2\text{ minors})=(1).$$
> Hence $d_1 d_2=1$, and since $d_1=1$ already, $d_2=1$.
>
> **Third Fitting ideal.** The only $3\times 3$ submatrix of a $3\times 3$ matrix is $X$ itself, so the sole $3\times 3$ minor is $\det X$. Expand along the third row (it has a zero, which is cheapest):
> $$\det X=1\cdot\begin{vmatrix}1&5\\2&6\end{vmatrix}-0\cdot(\,\cdots)+7\cdot\begin{vmatrix}2&1\\3&2\end{vmatrix}.$$
> Compute the cofactors: $\begin{vmatrix}1&5\\2&6\end{vmatrix}=6-10=-4$ and $\begin{vmatrix}2&1\\3&2\end{vmatrix}=4-3=1$. So
> $$\det X=1\cdot(-4)+7\cdot 1=-4+7=3.$$
> Therefore $\operatorname{Fit}_3(X)=(3)$, giving $d_1 d_2 d_3=3$; with $d_1=d_2=1$ this forces $d_3=3$.
>
> The divisibility chain $1\mid 1\mid 3$ holds, so the Smith normal form of $X$ is
> $$\operatorname{diag}(1,1,3).$$

**Step 3: Read off the group.**

Since $r=3$ (no zero invariant factors) there is no free part, and $A\cong\mathbb{Z}/(1)\oplus\mathbb{Z}/(1)\oplus\mathbb{Z}/(3)\cong\mathbb{Z}/3\cong C_3$, the cyclic group of order $3$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups|the classification of finitely generated abelian groups]] applied to the invariant factors $d_1=1,d_2=1,d_3=3$ of the relation matrix,
> $$A\cong\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(d_1)}\oplus\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(d_2)}\oplus\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(d_3)}=\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(1)}\oplus\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(1)}\oplus\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(3)}.$$
> Now $\mathbb{Z}/(1)$ is the quotient of $\mathbb{Z}$ by all of $\mathbb{Z}$ — the **trivial group** $0$ — so the first two summands vanish:
> $$A\cong 0\oplus 0\oplus\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(3)}\cong\frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(3)}\cong C_3.$$
> There are no $\mathbb{Z}/(0)=\mathbb{Z}$ summands (every invariant factor is non-zero, equivalently $\det X\ne 0$), so $A$ has no free part and is **finite**. Its order is $|A|=|d_1 d_2 d_3|=|\det X|=3$.
>
> So the opaque presentation $\langle a,b,c\mid 2a+3b+c=0,\ a+2b=0,\ 5a+6b+7c=0\rangle$ describes nothing more than the cyclic group $C_3$ of order three. The three generators were highly redundant: two of them ($d_1=d_2=1$) are killed outright by the relations.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Write $A=\mathbb{Z}^3/K$ where $K$ is generated by the relation vectors $(2,3,1),(1,2,0),(5,6,7)$. The relation matrix, columns being relations, is
> $$X=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&5\\3&2&6\\1&0&7\end{pmatrix}.$$
> Fitting ideals over $\mathbb{Z}$: an entry of $X$ equals $1$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_1(X)=(1)$; the $2\times 2$ minor on rows $\{1,2\}$, columns $\{1,2\}$ is $2\cdot 2-1\cdot 3=1$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_2(X)=(1)$; the unique $3\times 3$ minor is $\det X$, which by expansion along the third row is $1\cdot(6-10)+7\cdot(4-3)=-4+7=3$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_3(X)=(3)$. By [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|invariance of Fitting ideals]], $\operatorname{Fit}_k(X)=(d_1\cdots d_k)$, hence $d_1=1$, $d_2=1$, $d_3=3$; the Smith normal form of $X$ is $\operatorname{diag}(1,1,3)$. By [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups]],
> $$A\cong\mathbb{Z}/(1)\oplus\mathbb{Z}/(1)\oplus\mathbb{Z}/(3)\cong\mathbb{Z}/3=C_3.$$
> $A$ is the cyclic group of order $3$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**A presentation of an abelian group is a matrix in disguise, and Smith normal form is the decoder.** The single most important conceptual move is to stop reading $\sum r_i g_i=0$ as an "equation to solve" and start reading the collection of relations as the *columns of a matrix*. The group $A$ is then literally $\mathbb{Z}^n/(\text{column space of }X)$. Smith normal form does two things at once: row operations change the basis of the ambient $\mathbb{Z}^n$ (the "upstairs" coordinates), and column operations change the chosen generators of the relation subgroup $K$ (the "downstairs" relations) — neither alters the quotient $A$. In the final basis, the $i$-th basis vector is constrained only by $d_i$ times itself, giving the cyclic factor $\mathbb{Z}/(d_i)$. The trigger "abelian group by generators and relations" should fire the fixed reaction "build the relation matrix, diagonalise, read off cyclic factors" — this single pipeline identifies *any* finitely presented abelian group, no cleverness required.

**The Fitting ideals extract the invariant factors without ever doing a row operation — and unit minors collapse the answer fast.** Because $\operatorname{Fit}_k(X)=(d_1\cdots d_k)$ is an invariant ([[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]]), and over $\mathbb{Z}$ that ideal is generated by the $\gcd$ of all $k\times k$ minors, the invariant factors are read off telescopically: $d_1=\gcd(\text{entries})$, then $d_k=\gcd(k\times k\text{ minors})/\gcd((k-1)\times(k-1)\text{ minors})$. The crucial accelerator is that you need only *one* $k\times k$ minor equal to $\pm 1$ to conclude $\operatorname{Fit}_k=(1)$, hence $d_1=\cdots=d_k=1$. Here a single entry $1$ killed $d_1$ and a single $2\times 2$ minor equal to $1$ killed $d_2$, leaving only $\det X$ to compute. So when chasing Fitting ideals, *hunt for unit minors first*: each one you find eliminates a whole prefix of the invariant-factor chain.

**Invariant factors equal to $1$ mean redundant generators; the number of cyclic factors can be far below the number of generators.** A summand $\mathbb{Z}/(1)$ is the trivial group and disappears. So a presentation on three generators collapsed to a *single* cyclic factor $C_3$, because two invariant factors were $1$. The general reading: the count of invariant factors strictly greater than $1$ is the true number of cyclic pieces, and it can be much smaller than the generator count — the relations did most of the work. Conversely, the count of invariant factors equal to $0$ (equivalently, the rank deficiency of the relation matrix) is the rank of the free part $\mathbb{Z}^{n-r}$. Reading the Smith normal form is therefore a triage: factors equal to $1$ are discarded, factors equal to $0$ become $\mathbb{Z}$'s, and factors strictly between are the genuine torsion cyclic summands. The order of the (finite part of the) group is the product of the non-zero, non-unit invariant factors — here $|A|=3=|\det X|$, since for a square relation matrix with non-zero determinant the order equals $|\det X|$.

**The determinant of a square relation matrix is the order of the group (when finite).** When there are equally many relations as generators and the relation matrix $X$ is square, $A$ is finite precisely when $\det X\ne 0$, and then $|A|=|\det X|=|d_1\cdots d_n|$. This is an instantly checkable diagnostic: before any reduction, compute $\det X$ — if it is $0$ the group is infinite (has a free part), and if it is non-zero its absolute value is the group order. Here $\det X=3$ told us immediately that $A$ is finite of order $3$; the Fitting ideals then refined "order $3$" into the *structure* $C_3$ (which, being of prime order, is the only possibility — but for a composite order the invariant-factor chain is what distinguishes, say, $C_4$ from $C_2\times C_2$). This determinant test pairs naturally with the elementary-divisor refinement explored in [[Ex - Invariant factors and elementary divisors]].
