# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] (an integral domain with a Euclidean function), so in particular a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]]: every ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$ is **principal**, $I = (a)$ for some generator $a \in R$. Two elements $a, b \in R$ are **associates** if $a = ub$ for a unit $u$; equivalently $(a) = (b)$. We write $a \mid b$ for "$a$ divides $b$".

$A$ is an $m \times n$ matrix with entries in $R$. A **$k \times k$ minor** of $A$ is the determinant of a $k \times k$ submatrix — a matrix formed by deleting all but $k$ rows and all but $k$ columns of $A$. The **$k$th Fitting ideal** $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) \trianglelefteq R$ is the ideal generated by the set of all $k \times k$ minors of $A$. (When $k$ exceeds $\min(m,n)$ there are no $k \times k$ submatrices and $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = (0)$; by convention $\operatorname{Fit}_0(A) = (1) = R$.) Two matrices are [[Def - Elementary Operations and Equivalent Matrices|equivalent]] if one is reached from the other by elementary row and column operations (ER1)–(ER3), (EC1)–(EC3). The transpose of $A$ is $A^T$. The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]].

# Statement

> **Fitting Ideals are Invariants.** Let $R$ be a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] and $A$ an $m \times n$ matrix over $R$.
>
> 1. If $A$ and $B$ are [[Def - Elementary Operations and Equivalent Matrices|equivalent]] matrices, then for every $k$
> $$\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(B).$$
>
> 2. If $A$ has [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$ with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$, then for every $k$
> $$\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = (d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k).$$
>
> Consequently each invariant factor $d_k$ is **unique up to associates** — determined by $A$ up to multiplication by a unit — since $d_k$ is recovered as a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ divided by a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_{k-1}(A)$.

---

# Motivation

The [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] theorem promises that every matrix over a Euclidean domain is equivalent to a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots)$ with a divisibility chain $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. But the proof is an *algorithm*, and an algorithm full of free choices: which non-zero entry you swap into the corner, which order you clear the rows, which remainders the division algorithm hands you. Run it twice on the same matrix and you may well get two different diagonal matrices. Worse, you can take *any* Smith normal form and multiply a whole row by $-1$: the result is still diagonal, still satisfies the divisibility chain, but the diagonal entries have changed sign. So the literal output of the algorithm is not unique.

This is a real problem, because we want to *say things* with the invariant factors. We want to call $d_1, \dots, d_r$ "the invariant factors of $A$", treat them as numerical data attached to $A$, and use them — for instance — to decide whether two matrices are equivalent, or to read off the isomorphism type of a module. None of that is legitimate if the $d_k$ depend on how the algorithm was run. The word "invariant" in "invariant factor" is a promise that must be paid for.

What is true, and what this theorem establishes, is that the $d_k$ are unique *up to units* — up to associates. Sign changes are the only ambiguity, and over $\mathbb{Z}$ that means the $d_k$ are determined up to $\pm$; if you normalise them to be positive, they are determined outright. The divisibility chain plus this uniqueness is what makes Smith normal form a genuine *normal* form: a canonical representative of an equivalence class.

The strategy for proving uniqueness is the one that appears again and again when you must show an algorithmically-defined object is well-defined: **find a quantity, defined directly from the matrix without reference to the algorithm, that the algorithm cannot change, and then compute that quantity from the normal form.** Here the quantity is the family of Fitting ideals $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$, the ideals generated by the $k \times k$ minors. They are manifestly intrinsic to $A$ — minors are determinants of submatrices, no algorithm involved. The theorem has two halves matching this strategy exactly: first, *the Fitting ideals are unchanged by elementary operations* (so they are invariants of the equivalence class, not of the particular matrix); second, *for the Smith normal form they are easy to compute*, $\operatorname{Fit}_k = (d_1 \cdots d_k)$. Combining the two, the products $d_1 \cdots d_k$ are intrinsic to $A$ for every $k$, and dividing successive products recovers each $d_k$ up to a unit.

There is also a conceptual dividend. The Fitting ideals are not just a uniqueness device — they are themselves meaningful invariants. $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ measures, ideal-theoretically, "how close $A$ is to having rank less than $k$": it is the ideal cutting out the locus where all $k \times k$ minors vanish. The first non-trivial Fitting ideal detects torsion. The chain of Fitting ideals $R = \operatorname{Fit}_0 \supseteq \operatorname{Fit}_1 \supseteq \cdots$ is a coordinate-free invariant of the module presented by $A$, and the invariant factors are merely the successive quotients of this chain.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The literal precondition is a matrix over a Euclidean domain. The recognition skill is twofold: noticing that two matrices are equivalent (so part 1 applies) and noticing that a matrix is diagonal (so part 2 applies).

The first disguised source is **a pair of presentation matrices of the same module**. If a module is presented by generators and relations in two different ways, the two relation matrices are equivalent — a change of generators is a column operation, a change of relations a row operation. The non-obvious recognition is that "two presentations of one module" means "two equivalent matrices", so their Fitting ideals coincide; the Fitting ideals are therefore invariants *of the module*, computable from any presentation. *Example problem:* show that a numerical invariant extracted from a relation matrix does not depend on the chosen generators.

The second disguised source is **any diagonal matrix arising as a Smith normal form**. Once a matrix has been diagonalised, part 2 gives its Fitting ideals by a single product formula. The non-obvious step is that diagonalising trivialises the minor computation: of all $\binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{k}$ minors, only the ones picking $k$ diagonal entries can be non-zero, and they are products of $d_i$'s. *Example problem:* compute all Fitting ideals of $\operatorname{diag}(2, 6, 12)$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ without evaluating every $3 \times 3$ minor.

The third disguised source is **a matrix together with its transpose**. Since $\det(C) = \det(C^T)$ for any square submatrix, the $k \times k$ minors of $A^T$ are exactly the $k \times k$ minors of $A$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A^T) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$. The non-obvious consequence is that any argument proved for row operations transfers verbatim to column operations by transposing. *Example problem:* deduce the column-operation invariance from the row-operation invariance with one line.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusions are an equality of ideals and a product formula. Combined with other facts they become powerful structural statements.

Combine the conclusion with **the existence half of Smith normal form**. Smith normal form gives the existence of *some* invariant-factor chain; this theorem gives uniqueness up to associates. Together they make the multiset of associate-classes $\{[d_1], \dots, [d_r]\}$ a *complete invariant of the equivalence class of $A$*. The non-obvious payoff: two matrices over a Euclidean domain are equivalent if and only if they have the same Fitting ideals for all $k$ — a checkable criterion for matrix equivalence.

Combine the conclusion with **the cokernel module $R^m / A R^n$**. The Fitting ideals of $A$ are invariants of the module $M = R^m/AR^n$ presented by $A$, and they detect its torsion and free rank: the free rank is the number of trailing zero $d_k$'s, and the torsion submodule is $\bigoplus R/(d_k)$. The non-obvious consequence: the Fitting ideals give an intrinsic, presentation-independent fingerprint of a finitely generated module, feeding straight into the [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem]].

Combine the conclusion with **the chain of containments $\operatorname{Fit}_0 \supseteq \operatorname{Fit}_1 \supseteq \cdots$**. Because $d_k \mid d_{k+1}$, the products satisfy $(d_1 \cdots d_k) \supseteq (d_1 \cdots d_{k+1})$, so the Fitting ideals form a *descending chain*. The non-obvious result: the invariant factors are recovered as the successive quotients of this canonical descending chain of ideals, which is the cleanest coordinate-free way to package the data.

# Why Is It True

There are two things to see, one for each half of the theorem, and each is more transparent than it first looks.

**Why elementary operations do not change the Fitting ideal.** A $k \times k$ minor of $A$ is a determinant of a chunk of $A$. An elementary row operation perturbs the rows of $A$, hence perturbs each chunk, hence perturbs each minor. The claim is that, although the *individual minors* change wildly, the *ideal they generate* does not. Why would an ideal be more stable than its generators?

Consider the only operation that is genuinely interesting, adding $c$ times row $i$ to row $j$. Fix a $k \times k$ submatrix and watch its determinant. Three cases. If row $j$ is not among the chosen rows, the submatrix is untouched and its minor is literally unchanged. If both rows $i$ and $j$ are among the chosen rows, the submatrix undergoes the *same* row operation internally, and adding a multiple of one row to another never changes a determinant — minor unchanged. The one substantive case is when row $j$ is chosen but row $i$ is not. Then the new submatrix differs from the old one in row $j$, which has acquired an extra $c$ times the entries of row $i$. Expanding the determinant by *multilinearity in that row* — determinant is linear in each row separately — splits it cleanly: the new minor equals the old minor plus $c$ times the determinant of the submatrix you would get by *replacing* row $j$ with row $i$'s entries. But that second determinant is *also a minor of $A$* — just a different one, formed from a different choice of rows. So the new minor is (old minor) $+ c \cdot$ (another minor of $A$). Since an ideal is closed under addition and under multiplication by ring elements, the new minor lies in the ideal generated by the minors of $A$. Every minor of the new matrix lands inside $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$. Row operations are reversible, so the same reasoning run backwards gives $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(B)$, and the ideals are equal.

The key phrase is **multilinearity of the determinant in its rows**: it converts "a row got perturbed by a linear combination" into "the determinant got perturbed by a linear combination of other determinants", and a linear combination of generators stays inside the ideal. The other operations — swapping rows, scaling a row by a unit — change a minor only by a sign or a unit, which does not change the ideal at all. And column operations need no separate treatment: transposing the matrix turns columns into rows and leaves every determinant alone, so $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(A^T)$ and the row argument covers columns.

**Why the Fitting ideals of a diagonal matrix are the partial products.** Take $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots)$ with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. A $k \times k$ submatrix is obtained by choosing $k$ rows and $k$ columns. If the chosen rows and columns are not the *same* index set, then some chosen row has its diagonal entry in an unchosen column — that row of the submatrix is then all zeros (a diagonal matrix has its only non-zero entry on the diagonal), so the minor is $0$. The only submatrices with a chance of a non-zero determinant are the ones picking the same $k$ indices for rows and columns; such a submatrix is itself diagonal, with determinant $d_{i_1} d_{i_2} \cdots d_{i_k}$, a product of $k$ of the diagonal entries. So $\operatorname{Fit}_k(D)$ is generated by all products of $k$ distinct diagonal entries.

Now the divisibility chain does its work. Among all products of $k$ of the $d_i$, the *smallest* in the divisibility order is $d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k$ — you take the first $k$, the most-divided ones. Every other product of $k$ diagonal entries is a multiple of $d_1 \cdots d_k$: replacing each chosen index by a smaller one only divides further, since $d_i \mid d_j$ whenever $i \leq j$. So $d_1 \cdots d_k$ divides every generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_k(D)$, which means $d_1 \cdots d_k$ generates the whole ideal: $\operatorname{Fit}_k(D) = (d_1 \cdots d_k)$. The divisibility chain is precisely the condition that makes one minor — the top-left $k \times k$ one — divide all the others, collapsing the ideal to a single principal generator.

**Why this gives uniqueness.** Put the halves together. By the first half, $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ is the same for $A$ as for its Smith normal form $D$; by the second half, that common ideal is $(d_1 \cdots d_k)$. So the ideal $(d_1 \cdots d_k)$ depends only on $A$, not on the algorithm — for every $k$. Hence $(d_1 \cdots d_{k})$ and $(d_1 \cdots d_{k-1})$ are both intrinsic, and the invariant factor $d_k$ is obtained by dividing a generator of the first by a generator of the second. Different runs of the algorithm can only differ in the choice of generators of these fixed ideals — that is, by units. The invariant factors are unique up to associates, exactly as claimed.

# What Makes This Hard

The non-obvious step is the determinant expansion in the row-addition case: people get stuck because the individual minors of an equivalent matrix genuinely *are* different, and it is not at first clear why their ideal should be stable — the resolution is to expand the affected determinant by *multilinearity in the perturbed row*, which exhibits the new minor as (old minor) $+ c \cdot$ (a different minor of $A$), both inside $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$. The most common error is to prove only one containment $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ and forget that equality needs the reverse — which comes free, but *only* because elementary operations are invertible, so the same argument applies to the inverse operation. A second frequent slip is in part 2: failing to argue that off-diagonal submatrices have a zero row and so contribute nothing, and instead trying to handle all $\binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{k}$ minors.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Two independent computations. (1) Show a single elementary operation leaves $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ unchanged — reduce to row operations by transposing, then expand each affected minor by multilinearity to land it in $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$; use invertibility for the reverse containment. (2) Compute $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ of a diagonal matrix directly — only same-index submatrices have non-zero determinant, and the divisibility chain makes the top-left minor divide all others. Combining gives the product formula for $A$, and dividing successive products extracts each $d_k$ up to a unit.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Reduce to row operations.** Show $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(A^T)$, so it suffices to treat row operations.
   - *Hint:* The $k \times k$ minors of $A^T$ are the $k \times k$ minors of $A$, because $\det C = \det C^T$.
   - *Why needed:* Halves the work — column operations are handled by transposition.

2. **Handle row-addition.** Let $B$ be $A$ with $c$ times row $i$ added to row $j$. Show every $k \times k$ minor of $B$ lies in $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$.
   - *Hint:* Three cases by whether rows $i, j$ are in the chosen submatrix. The only substantive case (row $j$ in, row $i$ out) expands by multilinearity: new minor $= \det C + c \det D$ with $D$ another submatrix of $A$.
   - *Why needed:* This is the one operation where the minors really change; it gives $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$.

3. **Handle swaps and unit scalings, and close up.** Note swapping rows changes a minor by a sign and scaling a row by a unit changes it by a unit — neither changes the ideal. Then get $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ for every elementary $B$, and use invertibility for the reverse inclusion.
   - *Hint:* The inverse of an elementary operation is elementary, so $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(B)$ by the same argument applied to $B$.
   - *Why needed:* Upgrades containment to equality and extends from one operation to any chain — proving part 1.

4. **Compute Fitting ideals of a diagonal matrix.** For $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots)$, show a $k \times k$ submatrix has non-zero determinant only if it picks the same $k$ indices for rows and columns, with determinant a product of $k$ diagonal entries.
   - *Hint:* If a chosen row's diagonal entry sits in an unchosen column, that row of the submatrix is zero.
   - *Why needed:* Identifies the generators of $\operatorname{Fit}_k(D)$ as products of $k$ diagonal entries.

5. **Collapse to the top-left minor.** Using $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$, show $d_1 \cdots d_k$ divides every product of $k$ diagonal entries, so $\operatorname{Fit}_k(D) = (d_1 \cdots d_k)$.
   - *Hint:* Replacing a chosen index by a smaller one only divides further; the minimal product is the first $k$.
   - *Why needed:* Gives part 2; combined with part 1, $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = (d_1 \cdots d_k)$.

6. **Extract the invariant factors.** Conclude $d_k$ is a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ divided by a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_{k-1}(A)$, hence determined up to a unit.
   - *Hint:* Both ideals are principal (Euclidean domain); $d_k = (\text{gen of }\operatorname{Fit}_k)/(\text{gen of }\operatorname{Fit}_{k-1})$ up to associates.
   - *Why needed:* Delivers uniqueness up to associates — the headline corollary.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: The Fitting ideal is transpose-invariant
> **Statement:** For any $m \times n$ matrix $A$ over $R$ and any $k$, $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(A^T)$.
>
> **Hint:** A $k \times k$ submatrix of $A^T$ is the transpose of a $k \times k$ submatrix of $A$, and transposing preserves the determinant.
>
> **Why needed:** It reduces the invariance proof to row operations alone — column operations follow by transposing.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $C$ be a $k \times k$ submatrix of $A^T$, picked out by a set $S$ of $k$ rows and a set $T$ of $k$ columns of $A^T$. Transposing $A^T$ back to $A$, the rows of $A^T$ are the columns of $A$ and vice versa, so $C^T$ is the $k \times k$ submatrix of $A$ picked out by the column set $S$ and the row set $T$ — a genuine $k \times k$ submatrix of $A$. Since $\det(C) = \det(C^T)$ for any square matrix, every $k \times k$ minor of $A^T$ equals some $k \times k$ minor of $A$, and conversely. The two sets of minors coincide, so they generate the same ideal: $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(A^T)$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Multilinearity splits a perturbed minor
> **Statement:** Let $B$ be obtained from $A$ by adding $c$ times row $i$ to row $j$ (operation ER1). Then for any $k \times k$ submatrix, the corresponding minor $\det C'$ of $B$ satisfies $\det C' = \det C + c \det D$, where $\det C$ is the corresponding minor of $A$ and $\det D$ is another $k \times k$ minor of $A$. (This is the substantive case where row $j$ is chosen and row $i$ is not; otherwise $\det C' = \det C$.)
>
> **Hint:** The determinant is linear in each row separately; the chosen rows of $C'$ agree with those of $C$ except that row $j$ has gained $c$ times row $i$'s entries.
>
> **Why needed:** It exhibits every minor of $B$ as an $R$-linear combination of minors of $A$, hence inside $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Fix a $k \times k$ submatrix, given by a row set $S$ and column set $T$; let $C$ be the resulting submatrix of $A$ and $C'$ the resulting submatrix of $B$.
> >
> > *Case 1: $j \notin S$.* The operation only altered row $j$ of the full matrix, which is not selected, so $C' = C$ and $\det C' = \det C$.
> >
> > *Case 2: $i, j \in S$.* Both rows are selected. Inside the submatrix, row $j$ has gained $c$ times the entries of row $i$ *restricted to the columns $T$* — that is, $C'$ is obtained from $C$ by the internal row operation "add $c$ times row $i$ to row $j$". Adding a multiple of one row to another does not change a determinant, so $\det C' = \det C$.
> >
> > *Case 3: $j \in S$, $i \notin S$.* Now $C'$ and $C$ agree in every selected row except row $j$. Write the entries of $A$'s row $i$ in the columns $T$ as $(f_1, \dots, f_k)$. The $j$-row of $C$ is some tuple $(C_{j1}, \dots, C_{jk})$, and the $j$-row of $C'$ is $(C_{j1} + c f_1, \dots, C_{jk} + c f_k)$. The determinant is a multilinear function of the rows, so it is additive and $R$-homogeneous in the $j$-row alone:
> > $$\det C' = \det\begin{pmatrix} \vdots \\ C_{j\bullet} + c\, f \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix} = \det\begin{pmatrix} \vdots \\ C_{j\bullet} \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix} + c \det\begin{pmatrix} \vdots \\ f \\ \vdots \end{pmatrix} = \det C + c \det D,$$
> > where $D$ is the matrix obtained from $C$ by replacing its $j$-row with $(f_1, \dots, f_k)$. The matrix $D$ has rows indexed by $(S \setminus \{j\}) \cup \{i\}$ — those are $k$ genuine rows of $A$ — and columns $T$, so $\det D$ is a bona fide $k \times k$ minor of $A$. Hence $\det C' = \det C + c \det D$ with both $\det C$ and $\det D$ minors of $A$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Swaps and unit scalings change a minor by a unit
> **Statement:** If $B$ is obtained from $A$ by swapping two rows (ER2) or by multiplying a row by a unit $u$ (ER3), then each $k \times k$ minor of $B$ equals a unit times a $k \times k$ minor of $A$ — namely $\pm$ a minor (swap) or $u$ or $1$ times a minor (scaling). In particular $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$.
>
> **Hint:** Swapping two rows negates a determinant when both rows are selected; scaling a row by $u$ multiplies a determinant by $u$ when that row is selected.
>
> **Why needed:** It disposes of the two easy elementary operations, leaving only ER1 (Lemma 2) for substantive work.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > *Swap (ER2).* Let $B$ swap rows $i$ and $i'$ of $A$. For a $k \times k$ submatrix with row set $S$: if neither or only one of $i, i'$ lies in $S$, the swap merely relabels which row of $A$ supplies a given row of the submatrix, so the set of submatrices of $B$ equals the set of submatrices of $A$ and the minors are the same. If both $i, i' \in S$, the submatrix $C'$ of $B$ is $C$ with two rows interchanged, and a single row swap negates a determinant: $\det C' = -\det C$. In all cases each minor of $B$ is $\pm$ a minor of $A$. Since $-1$ is a unit, $\{$minors of $B\}$ and $\{$minors of $A\}$ generate the same ideal.
> >
> > *Unit scaling (ER3).* Let $B$ multiply row $i$ by a unit $u$. For a submatrix with row set $S$: if $i \notin S$ the submatrix is unchanged. If $i \in S$, the submatrix $C'$ is $C$ with one row multiplied by $u$, and $\det C' = u \det C$. So each minor of $B$ is $u$ or $1$ times a minor of $A$; as $u$ is a unit the generated ideals coincide. In both cases $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$.

> [!note]- Lemma 4: A diagonal matrix's only non-zero minors are diagonal
> **Statement:** Let $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$. A $k \times k$ submatrix of $D$ has non-zero determinant only if its row index set and column index set are equal, in which case the submatrix is diagonal and its determinant is the product of the corresponding $k$ diagonal entries.
>
> **Hint:** If a selected row's diagonal entry lies in an unselected column, that whole row of the submatrix is zero.
>
> **Why needed:** It identifies the generators of $\operatorname{Fit}_k(D)$ as products of $k$ diagonal entries — the input to the divisibility collapse of Lemma 5.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let a $k \times k$ submatrix be selected by a row set $S$ and column set $T$, each of size $k$. Suppose $S \neq T$; then there is an index $a \in S \setminus T$. The $a$-th row of $D$ has its only possibly-non-zero entry in column $a$ (it is a diagonal matrix). Since $a \notin T$, column $a$ is not selected, so every entry of the submatrix in the row coming from index $a$ is an off-diagonal entry of $D$, hence $0$. The submatrix has a zero row, so its determinant is $0$. Therefore a non-zero minor forces $S = T$. When $S = T = \{i_1 < \cdots < i_k\}$, the submatrix is itself diagonal with diagonal entries $d_{i_1}, \dots, d_{i_k}$ (entry $(a,b)$ of the submatrix is $D_{i_a i_b}$, zero unless $a = b$), and its determinant is $d_{i_1} d_{i_2} \cdots d_{i_k}$.

> [!note]- Lemma 5: The divisibility chain collapses the ideal to the top-left minor
> **Statement:** Let $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ be a divisibility chain in $R$. Then among all products of $k$ of the elements $d_1, \dots, d_r$ (distinct indices), each is a multiple of $d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k$. Consequently the ideal generated by all such products is the principal ideal $(d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k)$.
>
> **Hint:** A product $d_{i_1} \cdots d_{i_k}$ with $i_1 < \cdots < i_k$ has each $i_a \geq a$, and $d_a \mid d_{i_a}$.
>
> **Why needed:** It turns the many generators of $\operatorname{Fit}_k(D)$ found in Lemma 4 into the single generator $d_1 \cdots d_k$ — finishing the computation of $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ for a Smith normal form.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Take any product of $k$ distinct diagonal entries; order the indices $i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k$. Because the indices are distinct and increasing, $i_a \geq a$ for each $a$ (the $a$-th smallest of $k$ distinct positive integers is at least $a$). From the divisibility chain, $i_a \geq a$ gives $d_a \mid d_{i_a}$. Multiplying these $k$ divisibilities, $d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k$ divides $d_{i_1} d_{i_2} \cdots d_{i_k}$. So every generator of the ideal is a multiple of $d_1 \cdots d_k$, whence the ideal is contained in $(d_1 \cdots d_k)$. Conversely $d_1 \cdots d_k$ is itself one of the products (take $i_a = a$), so it lies in the ideal. Therefore the ideal generated by all $k$-fold products of the $d_i$ equals $(d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k)$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a Euclidean domain and $A$ an $m \times n$ matrix over $R$.
>
> **Part 1: equivalent matrices have equal Fitting ideals.** It suffices to show that a *single* elementary operation does not change $\operatorname{Fit}_k$; an arbitrary equivalence is a finite chain of such operations, and equality propagates along the chain.
>
> By Lemma 1, $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(A^T)$. A column operation on $A$ is a row operation on $A^T$, so if row operations preserve $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ then column operations do too. We therefore treat only row operations.
>
> *Operation ER1 (add $c$ times row $i$ to row $j$).* Let $B$ be the result and fix a $k \times k$ submatrix; let $\det C$ be the corresponding minor of $A$ and $\det C'$ of $B$. By Lemma 2, $\det C' = \det C + c \det D$ with $\det D$ a $k \times k$ minor of $A$ (and in the degenerate cases $\det C' = \det C$). Since $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ is an ideal — closed under addition and under multiplication by elements of $R$ — and contains both $\det C$ and $\det D$, it contains $\det C + c \det D = \det C'$. This holds for every $k \times k$ minor of $B$, so all generators of $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B)$ lie in $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$, giving $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$.
>
> *Operations ER2, ER3 (swap rows; scale a row by a unit).* By Lemma 3, each minor of $B$ is a unit multiple of a minor of $A$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ outright.
>
> *Reverse containment for ER1.* The operation ER1 is invertible: its inverse is "add $-c$ times row $i$ to row $j$", again an ER1. Applying the containment just proved to this inverse operation, which carries $B$ back to $A$, gives $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(B)$. Combined with $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$, we get $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$.
>
> Thus *every* elementary row operation, hence every elementary column operation, hence every finite sequence of them, preserves $\operatorname{Fit}_k$. If $A$ and $B$ are equivalent then $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(B)$ for all $k$.
>
> **Part 2: Fitting ideals of a Smith normal form.** Let $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$ with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. By Lemma 4, a $k \times k$ submatrix of $D$ has non-zero determinant only when its row and column index sets coincide, and then its determinant is a product $d_{i_1} \cdots d_{i_k}$ of $k$ diagonal entries. (If $k > r$ then every $k$-subset of indices includes a position with diagonal entry $0$, every such product is $0$, and $\operatorname{Fit}_k(D) = (0)$ — consistent with the formula, since $d_1 \cdots d_k$ then includes a zero factor.) Hence $\operatorname{Fit}_k(D)$ is generated by all $k$-fold products of the $d_i$. By Lemma 5, the divisibility chain forces this ideal to equal $(d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k)$. So
> $$\operatorname{Fit}_k(D) = (d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k).$$
>
> Now if $A$ has Smith normal form $D$, then $A$ and $D$ are equivalent, so by Part 1
> $$\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(D) = (d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k) \qquad \text{for every } k.$$
>
> **Uniqueness of the invariant factors.** The ideal $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ depends only on $A$, by construction (it is generated by the minors of $A$). For each $k$ with $1 \leq k \leq r$ we have just shown $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = (d_1 \cdots d_k)$ and $\operatorname{Fit}_{k-1}(A) = (d_1 \cdots d_{k-1})$ (with $\operatorname{Fit}_0(A) = R = (1)$). Both are principal ideals — $R$ is a Euclidean domain, hence a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] — and their generators are $d_1 \cdots d_k$ and $d_1 \cdots d_{k-1}$ up to units. The quotient of a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ by a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_{k-1}(A)$ is therefore $d_k$ up to a unit. Since the two ideals are intrinsic to $A$, the element $d_k$ is determined by $A$ up to multiplication by a unit — that is, up to associates. Hence the invariant factors of $A$ are well-defined up to associates. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Proving two integer matrices are not equivalent.** To show that two given matrices over $\mathbb{Z}$ cannot be transformed into one another by row and column operations, compute their Fitting ideals — equivalently, the gcd of all $k \times k$ minors for each $k$ — and exhibit a $k$ where they differ. The theorem applies because $\mathbb{Z}$ is a Euclidean domain, and the non-obvious point is that a *negative* statement ("no sequence of operations works") is settled by a finite computation of determinant-gcds, with no search over operations.

**Well-definedness of elementary divisors of a finite abelian group.** A finite abelian group is presented by an integer relation matrix; its elementary divisors are the invariant factors of that matrix. Since any two presentations give equivalent matrices, the Fitting-ideal theorem shows the invariant factors — hence the isomorphism type — do not depend on the chosen presentation. The non-obvious application is that the uniqueness half of the classification of finite abelian groups is a corollary of Fitting-ideal invariance.

**The greatest common divisor of all $k \times k$ minors is a similarity invariant.** For a square matrix $M$ over a field $k$, form the characteristic matrix $XI - M$ over the Euclidean domain $k[X]$; the gcd of its $k \times k$ minors (the "$k$th determinantal divisor") is invariant under equivalence of $XI - M$, hence under similarity of $M$. The theorem applies with $R = k[X]$, and the non-obvious recognition is that determinantal divisors of the characteristic matrix are genuine invariants of a linear operator, refining the characteristic polynomial.

**Detecting whether a module is free from a presentation.** Given a finitely generated $R$-module by a presentation matrix $A$, the module is free of rank $m - r$ exactly when $\operatorname{Fit}_r(A) = R$ for the largest $r$ with a non-zero minor (the torsion invariant factors are all units). The theorem applies for $R$ any Euclidean domain, and the non-obvious step is that freeness — a structural property — is detected by checking whether a Fitting ideal is the whole ring, a single ideal-membership test on minors.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith Normal Form]]** — the existence theorem this completes. Smith normal form provides *a* divisibility chain $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ by an algorithm with free choices; the present theorem proves the chain is unique up to associates. Existence plus uniqueness make Smith normal form a true normal form — a canonical representative of an equivalence class.

- **[[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal|Minors and Fitting Ideals]]** — the definitional input. The whole proof rests on the Fitting ideal being defined intrinsically from the minors of $A$, with no reference to any algorithm; that intrinsic definition is exactly what lets it serve as the algorithm-independent invariant.

- **[[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules]]** — the downstream consumer. The structure theorem's invariant-factor decomposition $\bigoplus R/(d_k) \oplus R^s$ is unique because the $d_k$ are unique up to associates; this theorem supplies that uniqueness, making the decomposition a complete invariant of the module.

- **[[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups|Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups]]** — the special case $R = \mathbb{Z}$. The uniqueness clause of the classification — that the invariant factors of a finitely generated abelian group are determined by the group — is precisely the uniqueness-up-to-associates statement here, with associates over $\mathbb{Z}$ being $\pm$, normalised away by taking positive representatives.

- **Determinantal ideals and Fitting invariants in commutative algebra** — the general framework. Over an arbitrary commutative ring the Fitting ideals $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ are still defined and still invariant under change of presentation, giving invariants of any finitely presented module; they cut out the loci where the module's rank jumps. The Euclidean-domain case here is where these ideals become *principal* and collapse to single invariant factors.
