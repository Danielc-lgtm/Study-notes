# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] — an integral domain with a Euclidean function — and hence a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]]: every ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$ is principal, $I = (a)$. The model examples are $\mathbb{Z}$ and $k[X]$ for a field $k$.

For finite $m$, the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^m$ is the set of column vectors $(r_1, \dots, r_m)^T$ with $r_i \in R$ and coordinatewise operations; its **standard basis** is $e_1, \dots, e_m$ with $e_i$ carrying $1_R$ in slot $i$. A **basis** of $R^m$ is a subset that generates it freely — equivalently, a subset such that every element is a *unique* $R$-linear combination of it; any basis of $R^m$ has exactly $m$ elements by [[Thm - Invariance of Rank|invariance of rank]]. A [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] $N \leq R^m$ is a subset closed under addition and the $R$-action. A submodule is **[[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]]** if there is a finite set $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with $N = Rx_1 + \cdots + Rx_n$; it is **free** if it has a basis. We write $a \mid b$ for "$a$ divides $b$". The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]].

# Statement

> **[[Def - Submodule|Submodules]] of [[Def - Free Module|Free Modules]] over a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean Domain]].** Let $R$ be a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] and let $N \leq R^m$ be a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] of the free module of rank $m$. Then:
>
> 1. **(Finite generation.)** $N$ is generated by at most $m$ elements.
>
> 2. **(Adapted basis / Stacked basis theorem.)** There exists a [[Def - Free Module|basis]] $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$, an integer $0 \leq r \leq m$, and elements $d_1, d_2, \dots, d_r \in R$ with
> $$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r,$$
> such that $N$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, d_2 v_2, \dots, d_r v_r$.
>
> 3. **(Freeness.)** Consequently $N$ is a [[Def - Free Module|free module]], of rank $r \leq m$. A submodule of a finitely generated free module over a Euclidean domain is itself free, of rank no larger.

---

# Motivation

Over a field, the submodule story is told in one breath: a [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] of a finite-dimensional vector space is again a vector space, automatically has a basis, and has [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] no larger than the ambient space. We use this so reflexively we forget it is a fact. The question is whether it survives the passage from fields to [[Def - Ring|rings]] — whether a *submodule* of a free module is again free.

It is genuinely not obvious, and over a general ring it is *false*. The standard counterexample lives one ring away from a field: take $R = \mathbb{Z}[X]$ and the submodule $(2, X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$, the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] of polynomials with even constant term. This is a submodule of the rank-$1$ free module $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ itself. If it were free it would be free of rank $1$ (any two elements $f, g$ of an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] satisfy the relation $g \cdot f - f \cdot g = 0$, killing rank $\geq 2$), so it would be a *principal* ideal — generated by one element. But $(2, X)$ is not principal: no single polynomial divides both $2$ and $X$ except units, and units generate all of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. So $(2, X)$ is a submodule of a free module that is not free. Freeness of [[Def - Submodule|submodules]] is not free; it is a property of the ring.

Which property? The counterexample fails because $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ has a non-principal ideal. The natural hypothesis to *rule that out* is that $R$ be a principal ideal domain — and a Euclidean domain is the most workable kind of principal ideal domain, the kind that comes with an explicit division algorithm. So the theorem says: over a Euclidean domain, the vector-space intuition is restored. Submodules of free [[Def - Module|modules]] are free, and rank does not increase.

But the theorem says more than "free". It produces an *adapted basis*: not just any basis of $N$, but one aligned with a cleverly chosen basis of the ambient $R^m$. You can rechoose the basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ so that the submodule $N$ sits inside it in the cleanest imaginable way — $N$ is spanned by *scalar multiples of the first $r$ basis vectors*, $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$. The submodule, viewed in the right coordinates, is a coordinate sub-box, stretched along each axis by the factor $d_i$. This "stacked basis" or "adapted basis" form is far stronger than bare freeness, and it is the form that powers the structure theory.

The strategy is the one to remember: **a submodule presented by generators is a matrix, and the matrix can be put in [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]].** Stack the generators of $N$ as the columns of a matrix $A$. Then Smith normal form diagonalises $A$ — and the two kinds of operations have two different meanings. Row operations re-express things in a new basis of the ambient $R^m$; column operations re-express the submodule by a new set of generators. Diagonalising $A$ therefore simultaneously rechooses the ambient basis and the generators until the relationship between them is diagonal. The diagonal entries are the $d_i$, the new ambient basis is the $v_i$, and the adapted-basis form falls out. The whole theorem is Smith normal form, read through the dictionary "rows = ambient basis, columns = submodule generators".

Why care? Because this is the bridge from matrices to module structure. Every finitely generated module over $R$ is a quotient $R^m / N$ of a free module by a submodule; the adapted basis presents $N$ as a coordinate sub-box, so the quotient splits as $\bigoplus R/(d_i) \oplus R^{m-r}$ — and that is the [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem]]. The adapted-basis theorem is the geometric heart; the structure theorem is what you read off it.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The literal precondition is a submodule $N \leq R^m$ of a finite-rank free module over a Euclidean domain. The recognition skill is spotting when a problem hands you such a submodule in disguise.

The first disguised source is **an ideal of $R$, or a finitely generated module of relations**. An ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$ *is* a submodule of the rank-$1$ free module $R$. More broadly, the relations among a generating set of any module form a submodule of a free module — the "syzygy module". The non-obvious recognition is that "the relations satisfied by these generators" is itself a submodule of some $R^n$, so the theorem applies to it and the relations are themselves freely generated. *Example problem:* show that the module of relations among finitely many elements of $R^m$ is free.

The second disguised source is **the kernel of a homomorphism between free modules**. If $\phi : R^n \to R^k$ is an $R$-module map, $\ker\phi$ is a submodule of the free module $R^n$. The non-obvious step is that kernels of maps of free modules are automatically free — there is no separate "is the kernel free?" worry. *Example problem:* given an explicit linear map of free $\mathbb{Z}$-modules, exhibit a basis for its kernel.

The third disguised source is **a subgroup of a finitely generated free abelian group**. Taking $R = \mathbb{Z}$, a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^m$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-submodule of $\mathbb{Z}^m$. The non-obvious framing is that lattice subgroups — sublattices of $\mathbb{Z}^m$ — are exactly submodules of a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module, so every sublattice has a basis of at most $m$ vectors, adapted to a basis of the ambient lattice. *Example problem:* prove every subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^m$ is free abelian of rank $\leq m$.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusions are finite generation, an adapted basis, and freeness. Combined with other facts they yield the structure theory.

Combine the adapted basis with **the quotient $R^m / N$**. With $N$ generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ inside the basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$, the quotient decomposes coordinatewise as $\bigoplus_{i=1}^r R/(d_i) \;\oplus\; R^{m-r}$. The non-obvious payoff: the adapted basis converts an opaque quotient module into an explicit direct sum of cyclic modules — this *is* the [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem]].

Combine freeness of submodules with **a surjection from a free module onto a finitely generated module**. Every finitely generated $M$ is $R^m / K$ with $K = \ker(R^m \twoheadrightarrow M)$; the theorem makes $K$ finitely generated, so $M$ is *finitely presented* — finitely many generators and finitely many relations. The non-obvious consequence: over a Euclidean domain "finitely generated" upgrades for free to "finitely presented", which is what makes module computations terminate.

Combine the rank inequality $r \leq m$ with **invariance of rank**. Since [[Thm - Invariance of Rank|invariance of rank]] makes the rank of a free module well-defined, "$\operatorname{rank}(N) \leq \operatorname{rank}(R^m)$" is a genuine numerical inequality. The non-obvious result: rank is monotone under inclusion of free modules over a Euclidean domain — a submodule cannot have more independent directions than its ambient module, restoring a basic dimension-counting tool.

# Why Is It True

The theorem feels surprising stated abstractly — "submodules of free [[Def - Module|modules]] are free" — but it is almost forced once you set it up as a matrix problem. There are two intuitions, one for each conclusion.

**Why $N$ is finitely generated (at most $m$ generators).** This is an induction on $m$ that peels off one coordinate at a time. Look at the *last* coordinate of the elements of $N$ — that is, the set of values $r$ that appear as the final entry of some vector $(r_2, \dots, r_m, r) \in N$. Wait — peel off the *first* coordinate instead, it is the same idea: collect the set $I$ of all values $a$ appearing as the first coordinate of some element of $N$. This set $I$ is an ideal of $R$ (if $a$ and $a'$ are achievable first coordinates, so is $a - a'$, by subtracting the witnessing vectors; and so is $ra$, by scaling). Since $R$ is a principal ideal domain, $I = (a)$ for a single generator $a$, witnessed by one chosen element $n \in N$ whose first coordinate is exactly $a$.

Now $n$ does almost all the work. Any element of $N$ has first coordinate a multiple $ra$ of $a$; subtracting $r \cdot n$ kills the first coordinate, landing the result in the submodule $N' = N \cap (\{0\} \times R^{m-1})$ — the part of $N$ living in the last $m-1$ coordinates. So every element of $N$ is "a multiple of $n$, plus something in $N'$". And $N'$ is a submodule of $R^{m-1}$, one [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] smaller, so by induction it needs at most $m-1$ generators. Together with $n$, that is at most $m$ generators of $N$. The mechanism is: **the first-coordinate ideal is principal, so one element controls the first coordinate, and the rest is a lower-dimensional problem.** It is Gaussian elimination, one column at a time, with "principal ideal" supplying the single pivot.

**Why $N$ has an adapted basis (the stacked-basis form).** Now we know $N$ is generated by finitely many vectors $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with $n \leq m$. Each $x_i$ is a column vector in $R^m$. Stack them side by side as the columns of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$. This matrix is the *entire datum*: its columns are exactly the generators of $N$, and they live in coordinates given by the standard basis of $R^m$.

Apply Smith normal form to $A$. The crucial point is what the two kinds of operations *mean* for this picture:

- A **column operation** on $A$ replaces one generator of $N$ by an $R$-combination of generators. This does not change the submodule $N$ — it is the same submodule, described by a different generating set. Column operations are "rechoose the generators of $N$".
- A **row operation** on $A$ is a change of basis of the ambient $R^m$. Adding a multiple of one coordinate to another, or swapping coordinates, is exactly applying an invertible linear map to $R^m$, i.e. expressing every vector in a new basis. Row operations are "rechoose the basis of the ambient free module".

Smith normal form diagonalises $A$ to $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots)$ using both kinds of operations. Translate: there is a *new basis* $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ (produced by the row operations) and a *new generating set* of $N$ (produced by the column operations) such that, in the new basis, the $i$-th new generator is simply $d_i v_i$ — the $i$-th basis vector scaled by $d_i$ — for $i \leq r$, and the remaining generators are zero. So $N$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$. The divisibility $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ is exactly the divisibility chain Smith normal form guarantees. The submodule, in the right coordinates, is the coordinate sub-box spanned by stretched copies of the first $r$ axes.

That this generating set is actually a *basis* of $N$ — that $N$ is free — is the last, easy step. The vectors $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ generate $N$; they are independent because the $v_i$ are independent (a relation $\sum c_i (d_i v_i) = 0$ is a relation $\sum (c_i d_i) v_i = 0$ among basis vectors, forcing each $c_i d_i = 0$, hence each $c_i = 0$ since $d_i \neq 0$ and $R$ is a domain). Independent generating set: $N$ is free, of rank $r \leq m$.

So the reason to expect the theorem: a submodule is a list of generators, a list of generators is a matrix, and Smith normal form diagonalises any matrix over a Euclidean domain — the two halves of the diagonalisation just happen to be "rechoose the ambient basis" and "rechoose the submodule generators", which is exactly the freedom the adapted-basis statement asks for. The non-principal-ideal counterexample $(2,X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$ marks the boundary precisely: it is where the first-coordinate ideal fails to be principal, so even step one — one element controlling a coordinate — breaks down.

# What Makes This Hard

The non-obvious move in part 1 is forming the ideal of first coordinates and using *principality* to get a single controlling element $n$ — people get stuck trying to handle all of $N$ at once instead of peeling one coordinate and inducting on the smaller submodule $N \cap (\{0\} \times R^{m-1})$. The non-obvious move in part 2 is the *interpretation* of Smith normal form: it is easy to apply the diagonalisation algorithm and hard to recognise that row operations mean "change of basis of the ambient $R^m$" while column operations mean "change of generators of $N$" — without that dictionary the diagonal matrix is just a diagonal matrix, not an adapted basis. The most common error is to claim $N$ is free *directly* from the diagonal matrix while forgetting to verify independence of $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$, or — more seriously — to forget that the theorem is false without the principal-ideal hypothesis and mistakenly believe submodules of free modules are always free.

---

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Two stages. First, show $N$ is finitely generated by inducting on the ambient rank $m$: the first-coordinate ideal is principal, so one element controls the first coordinate and the remainder is a submodule of $R^{m-1}$. Second, stack the resulting generators as the columns of a matrix, apply Smith normal form, and translate — row operations rechoose the basis of $R^m$, column operations rechoose the generators of $N$ — so the diagonal form exhibits $N$ as spanned by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$. Freeness then follows because the $v_i$ are independent.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **The first-coordinate ideal is principal.** Show $I = \{a \in R : (a, r_2, \dots, r_m) \in N \text{ for some } r_i\}$ is an ideal of $R$, hence $I = (a)$.
   - *Hint:* Closure under subtraction and scaling comes from subtracting and scaling the witnessing vectors. Principality is the PID hypothesis.
   - *Why needed:* Supplies a single element $n \in N$ with first coordinate $a$ that controls the first coordinate of all of $N$.

2. **One element plus a lower-dimensional submodule.** Pick $n \in N$ with first coordinate $a$. Show every element of $N$ is $r \cdot n$ plus an element of $N' = N \cap (\{0\} \times R^{m-1})$.
   - *Hint:* An arbitrary element has first coordinate $ra$; subtract $r \cdot n$ to kill it.
   - *Why needed:* Reduces generating $N$ to generating the smaller submodule $N' \leq R^{m-1}$.

3. **Induct to finish part 1.** By induction on $m$, $N' \leq R^{m-1}$ is generated by at most $m-1$ elements; adjoin $n$.
   - *Hint:* Base case $m = 0$ (or $m = 1$): a submodule of $R^0$ is zero; a submodule of $R$ is a principal ideal, one generator.
   - *Why needed:* Gives $N = Rn + Rn_2 + \cdots + Rn_m$, at most $m$ generators — part 1.

4. **Stack the generators into a matrix.** Write the generators $x_1, \dots, x_n$ ($n \leq m$) of $N$ as the columns of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$.
   - *Hint:* Each $x_i \in R^m$ is a column vector; $A$ has $A e_j = x_j$ in standard coordinates.
   - *Why needed:* Converts the submodule into a matrix to which Smith normal form applies.

5. **Apply Smith normal form and translate.** Diagonalise $A$ to $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots)$ with $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. Interpret: row operations give a new basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$; column operations give new generators of $N$; the new $i$-th generator is $d_i v_i$.
   - *Hint:* Row operations $=$ invertible change of basis of $R^m$; column operations $=$ re-spanning $N$, leaving $N$ unchanged.
   - *Why needed:* Produces the adapted basis — $N$ generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ — which is part 2.

6. **Conclude freeness.** Show $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ are independent, hence a basis of $N$.
   - *Hint:* A relation $\sum c_i (d_i v_i) = 0$ is a relation $\sum (c_i d_i) v_i = 0$ among basis vectors of $R^m$; independence of the $v_i$ and $R$ a domain force $c_i = 0$.
   - *Why needed:* Independent generating set means $N$ is free of rank $r \leq m$ — part 3.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: The first-coordinate set is a principal ideal
> **Statement:** Let $N \leq R^m$ and put $I = \{a \in R : (a, r_2, \dots, r_m) \in N \text{ for some } r_2, \dots, r_m \in R\}$, the set of values occurring as the first coordinate of an element of $N$. Then $I$ is an ideal of $R$, and since $R$ is a principal ideal domain, $I = (a)$ for some $a \in R$.
>
> **Hint:** To show closure, subtract or scale the *vectors* that witness membership.
>
> **Why needed:** Its single generator $a$ is the first coordinate of a chosen element $n \in N$ that controls the first coordinate of every element of $N$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > $I$ contains $0$: the zero vector $0 \in N$ has first coordinate $0$. Closure under subtraction: if $a, a' \in I$, choose witnesses $u = (a, r_2, \dots, r_m) \in N$ and $u' = (a', r_2', \dots, r_m') \in N$; since $N$ is a submodule, $u - u' = (a - a', r_2 - r_2', \dots) \in N$, and its first coordinate $a - a'$ lies in $I$. Closure under the $R$-action: for $r \in R$ and $a \in I$ with witness $u$, the vector $r u = (ra, rr_2, \dots) \in N$, so $ra \in I$. Hence $I$ is an ideal of $R$. Because $R$ is a Euclidean domain it is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], so $I = (a)$ for some $a \in R$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: One element controls the first coordinate
> **Statement:** With $I = (a)$ as in Lemma 1, fix $n = (a, a_2, \dots, a_m) \in N$ with first coordinate $a$. Then every element of $N$ can be written $r \cdot n + n'$ with $r \in R$ and $n' \in N' := N \cap (\{0\} \times R^{m-1})$.
>
> **Hint:** An arbitrary element of $N$ has first coordinate in $I = (a)$, hence a multiple of $a$; subtract the matching multiple of $n$.
>
> **Why needed:** It reduces "generate $N$" to "generate the lower-dimensional submodule $N'$", enabling the induction.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Take any $x = (r_1, r_2, \dots, r_m) \in N$. Its first coordinate $r_1$ lies in $I = (a)$ by definition of $I$, so $r_1 = r a$ for some $r \in R$. Consider
> > $$x - r \cdot n = (r_1, r_2, \dots, r_m) - r(a, a_2, \dots, a_m) = (r_1 - ra,\; r_2 - r a_2,\; \dots,\; r_m - r a_m) = (0,\; r_2 - r a_2,\; \dots).$$
> > This vector lies in $N$ (a submodule, closed under the operations) and has first coordinate $0$, so it lies in $N' = N \cap (\{0\} \times R^{m-1})$. Setting $n' = x - r n$, we have $x = r \cdot n + n'$ with $n' \in N'$, as claimed.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: A submodule of $R^m$ is generated by at most $m$ elements
> **Statement:** Every submodule $N \leq R^m$ is generated by at most $m$ elements.
>
> **Hint:** Induct on $m$; the inductive step is Lemmas 1 and 2, identifying $N' = N \cap (\{0\} \times R^{m-1})$ with a submodule of $R^{m-1}$.
>
> **Why needed:** It produces the finite generating set whose vectors become the columns of the matrix fed to Smith normal form.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Induct on $m$. For $m = 0$, $R^0 = \{0\}$ and its only submodule is $\{0\}$, generated by the empty set ($0$ elements). For $m = 1$, a submodule of $R^1 = R$ is an ideal, principal because $R$ is a PID, so generated by $1$ element.
> >
> > Inductive step, $m \geq 1$: assume every submodule of $R^{m-1}$ is generated by at most $m - 1$ elements, and let $N \leq R^m$. By Lemma 1 the first-coordinate ideal is $I = (a)$; fix $n \in N$ with first coordinate $a$. By Lemma 2, every element of $N$ is $r n + n'$ with $n' \in N' = N \cap (\{0\} \times R^{m-1})$. The submodule $N'$ consists of vectors with first coordinate zero; dropping that zero coordinate identifies $N'$ with a submodule of $R^{m-1}$. By the inductive hypothesis $N'$ is generated by at most $m - 1$ elements $n_2, \dots, n_m$ (here meaning at most $m-1$ of them). Then any $x \in N$ equals $r n + n'$ with $n' \in N'$ an $R$-combination of $n_2, \dots, n_m$, so $x$ is an $R$-combination of $n, n_2, \dots, n_m$. Hence $N$ is generated by the at most $m$ elements $n, n_2, \dots, n_m$.

> [!note]- Lemma 4: Row and column operations reinterpret basis and generators
> **Statement:** Let the columns of an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ over $R$ be vectors $x_1, \dots, x_n \in R^m$, generating a submodule $N \leq R^m$. Then: (a) applying an elementary column operation to $A$ produces a matrix whose columns generate the *same* submodule $N$; (b) applying an elementary row operation to $A$ corresponds to expressing every vector in a new basis of $R^m$ — there is an invertible matrix $Q$ such that the new columns are $Q x_1, \dots, Q x_n$, and equivalently the *old* columns are $d_i v_i$-type combinations of the new basis $Q^{-1} e_1, \dots, Q^{-1} e_m$.
>
> **Hint:** A column operation replaces a generator by an $R$-combination of generators; a row operation is left-multiplication by an invertible matrix, i.e. a change of basis.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the dictionary that turns the Smith normal form of $A$ into the adapted-basis statement: column ops re-span $N$, row ops re-coordinatise $R^m$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **(a) Column operations preserve $N$.** Operation (EC1) adds $c$ times column $i$ to column $j$: the new $j$-th column is $x_j + c x_i$, an $R$-linear combination of the old columns, while every other column is unchanged. So every new column lies in $N = Rx_1 + \cdots + Rx_n$; conversely (EC1) is invertible, so every old column lies in the span of the new ones. The submodule generated is unchanged. Operation (EC2) permutes columns — same set of generators. Operation (EC3) multiplies a column by a unit $u$: the new column $u x_i$ and the old $x_i = u^{-1}(u x_i)$ generate the same cyclic submodule, so the total span is unchanged. In every case the new columns generate the same $N$.
> >
> > **(b) Row operations are a change of basis.** Each elementary row operation is left-multiplication by an invertible $m \times m$ matrix; a composite of them is left-multiplication by a single invertible $Q$. The columns of $QA$ are $Q x_1, \dots, Q x_n$. Now $Q$ invertible means $Q$ is an automorphism of $R^m$: the vectors $f_i := Q^{-1} e_i$ form a basis of $R^m$ (the image of the standard basis under the automorphism $Q^{-1}$), and $Q x = y$ says exactly that the coordinates of $x$ in the basis $\{f_i\}$ equal the standard coordinates of $y$. Concretely, if $QA$ has $j$-th column $(y_{1j}, \dots, y_{mj})^T$, then $x_j = Q^{-1}(QA)_{\bullet j} = \sum_i y_{ij}\, Q^{-1} e_i = \sum_i y_{ij}\, f_i$. So performing row operations on $A$ amounts to rewriting each generator $x_j$ of $N$ in the new basis $f_1, \dots, f_m$ of $R^m$, with the entries of the transformed matrix being precisely the new coordinates.

> [!note]- Lemma 5: The adapted generators are independent
> **Statement:** Let $v_1, \dots, v_m$ be a basis of $R^m$ and $d_1, \dots, d_r \in R$ non-zero. Then $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ are linearly independent over $R$; hence if they generate a submodule $N$, they form a basis of $N$ and $N$ is free of rank $r$.
>
> **Hint:** A relation among the $d_i v_i$ is a relation among the $v_i$ with coefficients $c_i d_i$; use that $R$ is a domain.
>
> **Why needed:** It upgrades "$N$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$" to "$N$ is *free* with this basis", giving conclusion 3.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Suppose $c_1 (d_1 v_1) + c_2 (d_2 v_2) + \cdots + c_r (d_r v_r) = 0$ with $c_i \in R$. Regrouping, $(c_1 d_1) v_1 + \cdots + (c_r d_r) v_r = 0$ is an $R$-linear relation among the basis vectors $v_1, \dots, v_r$ of $R^m$. A basis is linearly independent (every element of $R^m$, in particular $0$, is a *unique* combination of basis vectors, and $\sum 0 \cdot v_i = 0$ is one such), so each coefficient vanishes: $c_i d_i = 0$ for every $i$. Since $R$ is an integral domain and $d_i \neq 0$, $c_i d_i = 0$ forces $c_i = 0$. Hence the only relation among $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ is the trivial one — they are linearly independent. A generating set that is linearly independent is a basis (every element of $N$ is then a *unique* combination, by the standard argument: two expressions differ by a relation, which is trivial). Therefore $N$ is free with basis $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$, of rank $r$; and $r \leq m$ since these are among $m$ scaled basis vectors.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a Euclidean domain and $N \leq R^m$ a submodule.
>
> **Part 1: $N$ is generated by at most $m$ elements.** This is Lemma 3, proved by induction on $m$. The first-coordinate set $I = \{a \in R : (a, r_2, \dots, r_m) \in N \text{ for some } r_i\}$ is an ideal (Lemma 1), hence principal, $I = (a)$, as $R$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]]. Fixing $n \in N$ with first coordinate $a$, every element of $N$ is $r n + n'$ with $n' \in N' = N \cap (\{0\} \times R^{m-1})$ (Lemma 2). Identifying $N'$ with a submodule of $R^{m-1}$ and applying the inductive hypothesis, $N'$ is generated by at most $m - 1$ elements; adjoining $n$, the submodule $N$ is generated by at most $m$ elements. Call them $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with $n \leq m$.
>
> **Part 2: the adapted basis.** Each generator $x_i$ is a vector in $R^m$, written as a column. Stack them as the columns of an $m \times n$ matrix
> $$A = \begin{pmatrix} \big| & \big| & & \big| \\ x_1 & x_2 & \cdots & x_n \\ \big| & \big| & & \big| \end{pmatrix}, \qquad n \leq m.$$
> By the [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] theorem, $A$ is equivalent to a diagonal matrix; since $A$ has no more columns than rows ($n \leq m$), the Smith normal form is
> $$\begin{pmatrix} d_1 & & & \\ & \ddots & & \\ & & d_r & \\ & & & 0 \\ & & & & \ddots \\ \hline & & 0 & \end{pmatrix}, \qquad d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r,\quad 0 \leq r \leq n \leq m,$$
> with $d_1, \dots, d_r$ non-zero (and the bottom $m - n$ rows all zero, there being only $n$ columns).
>
> Reading the reduction through Lemma 4: the Smith normal form is reached by a sequence of elementary row and column operations.
>
> - The **column operations** replace the generating set of $N$ by another generating set, leaving the submodule $N$ itself unchanged (Lemma 4(a)).
> - The **row operations** assemble into left-multiplication by a single invertible matrix $Q$, equivalently a change of basis of $R^m$ (Lemma 4(b)). Let $v_1, \dots, v_m$ be the corresponding new basis of $R^m$.
>
> In the new basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ and with the new generating set, the matrix of generators is the Smith normal form. Its $i$-th column, for $1 \leq i \leq r$, is $d_i e_i$ in the new coordinates — that is, the generator is $d_i v_i$. For $i > r$ the column is zero. Hence the new generating set of $N$ is exactly
> $$d_1 v_1,\; d_2 v_2,\; \dots,\; d_r v_r$$
> (the zero generators being discarded), and $N = R\,d_1 v_1 + \cdots + R\, d_r v_r$. The divisibility chain $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ is the one guaranteed by Smith normal form. This is conclusion 2.
>
> **Part 3: $N$ is free of rank $r \leq m$.** By Lemma 5, the elements $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ — non-zero scalar multiples of distinct members of a basis of $R^m$ — are linearly independent over $R$: a relation $\sum c_i (d_i v_i) = 0$ rewrites as $\sum (c_i d_i) v_i = 0$, forcing each $c_i d_i = 0$, hence each $c_i = 0$ since $R$ is a domain and $d_i \neq 0$. A linearly independent generating set is a [[Def - Free Module|basis]], so $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ is a basis of $N$, and $N$ is free of rank $r$. Since $r \leq m$, the rank of $N$ is at most the rank of $R^m$. This is conclusion 3. $\blacksquare$
>
> *Remark (the hypothesis is essential).* Conclusion 3 fails over rings that are not principal ideal domains. The ideal $(2, X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a submodule of the rank-$1$ free module $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, but it is not free: any two of its elements $f, g$ satisfy the non-trivial relation $g \cdot f - f \cdot g = 0$, so a free $(2,X)$ would have rank $1$, i.e. be principal — and $(2,X)$ is not a principal ideal. The proof above breaks at Lemma 1: over $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ the first-coordinate "ideal" need not be principal, so no single element $n$ controls the first coordinate.

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Every [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]] of a free abelian [[Def - Group|group]] of finite rank is free.** A subgroup $H$ of $\mathbb{Z}^m$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-submodule of $\mathbb{Z}^m$; the theorem (with $R = \mathbb{Z}$) makes $H$ free abelian of rank at most $m$, with a basis adapted to a basis of the ambient group. The non-obvious recognition is that a purely group-theoretic statement about [[Def - Subgroup|subgroups]] of $\mathbb{Z}^m$ is the Euclidean-domain submodule theorem, and that the proof simultaneously yields the Smith-normal-form sublattice description used in lattice geometry and integer programming.

**Kernels of integer matrices are free, and computing an integer null space.** Given a matrix $M$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, the integer solutions of $M\mathbf{x} = 0$ form the kernel of the map $\mathbb{Z}^n \to \mathbb{Z}^k$, a submodule of $\mathbb{Z}^n$; the theorem makes this kernel free of rank $\leq n$ and finitely generated, and Smith normal form computes a basis for it. The non-obvious application is that "the lattice of integer solutions of a linear system has a finite basis" — a fact integer linear algebra relies on — is this theorem, not a separate result.

**Syzygies over a polynomial ring in one variable.** Over $k[X]$ — a Euclidean domain — the module of relations (syzygies) among finitely many elements of $k[X]^m$ is a submodule of a free $k[X]$-module, hence itself free of finite rank with an adapted basis. The non-obvious step is that the first syzygy module is free for $k[X]$ (it is *not* free for $k[X,Y]$), so resolutions over $k[X]$ terminate immediately — the one-variable shadow of the Hilbert syzygy theorem.

**Sublattices and the index of a subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}^m$.** A finite-index subgroup $H \leq \mathbb{Z}^m$ has, by the theorem, an adapted basis $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_m v_m$ (here $r = m$ since the index is finite), and the index $[\mathbb{Z}^m : H]$ equals $|d_1 d_2 \cdots d_m|$, the absolute value of the determinant of the matrix expressing a basis of $H$ in a basis of $\mathbb{Z}^m$. The non-obvious application is that the lattice index — a counting quantity — is read off the invariant factors produced by the adapted-basis form.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith Normal Form]]** — the engine. The adapted basis *is* the Smith normal form of the matrix of generators, reinterpreted: column operations re-span $N$, row operations re-coordinatise the ambient $R^m$. Without Smith normal form there is no theorem; with it, the theorem is a translation.

- **[[Thm - Invariance of Rank|Invariance of Rank]]** — what makes "rank $\leq m$" meaningful. The conclusion that $N$ is free of rank $r \leq m$ is a statement about numbers; invariance of rank guarantees the rank of a free module is well-defined, so the inequality $r \leq m$ is a genuine comparison rather than an artefact of a chosen basis.

- **[[Def - Free Module|Free Modules]]** — the structure being detected. The theorem says the class of free modules is closed under taking submodules *when $R$ is a Euclidean domain* — a closure property false in general, and the boundary case $(2,X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$ shows exactly where it fails.

- **[[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules]]** — the immediate consequence. Presenting a finitely generated module as $R^m / N$ and applying the adapted basis, the quotient splits as $\bigoplus R/(d_i) \oplus R^{m-r}$; the structure theorem is the adapted-basis theorem pushed through a quotient.

- **The submodule-of-free counterexample over non-PIDs** — the sharp converse. Over $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, or any non-principal-ideal domain, submodules of free modules need not be free; the ideal $(2,X)$ is the standard witness. This marks the theorem's hypothesis as not improvable: "Euclidean domain" (or at least "principal ideal domain") is genuinely required.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Structure Theorem and the Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups *(from Algebra)*
> Presenting a finitely generated module as a quotient $R^m / N$ and applying the adapted basis to $N$ decomposes the module as $\bigoplus_i R/(d_i) \oplus R^{s}$. For $R = \mathbb{Z}$ this is the classification of finitely generated abelian groups as $\bigoplus \mathbb{Z}/d_i \oplus \mathbb{Z}^s$. See [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain]] and [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups]].

> [!tip] Lattices and the Geometry of Numbers *(from Number Theory)*
> A lattice in $\mathbb{R}^m$ is a free $\mathbb{Z}$-module; the adapted-basis theorem gives, for any sublattice, a basis aligned with a basis of the ambient lattice, and the product of the invariant factors is the sublattice index. This is the algebraic substrate of Minkowski's geometry of numbers and of lattice-reduction algorithms.
