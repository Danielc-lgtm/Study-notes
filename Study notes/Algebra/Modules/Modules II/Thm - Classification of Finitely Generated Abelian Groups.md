# Notation

An **abelian group** is a [[Def - Module|ℤ-module]]: the ring is the integers $\mathbb{Z}$, the module operation is the group's addition, and the $\mathbb{Z}$-action is repeated addition, $n \cdot g = g + \dots + g$ ($n$ times). The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] under $\varphi(n) = |n|$, so every theorem about modules over a Euclidean domain specialises to abelian groups. $C_d$ denotes the **cyclic group of order $d$**, which as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is $\mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}/(d)$; $C_\infty$ denotes the **infinite cyclic group**, which is $\mathbb{Z}$ itself. The symbol $\times$ is the direct product of groups, which for abelian groups coincides with the [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules|direct sum]] of $\mathbb{Z}$-modules. The relation $d \mid d'$ ("$d$ divides $d'$") means $d' = qd$ for an integer $q$; a chain $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r$ is a sequence in which each term divides the next. A group $A$ is [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] if $A = \mathbb{Z}a_1 + \dots + \mathbb{Z}a_k$ for finitely many elements $a_i$. The $d_i$ produced below are the **invariant factors** and the integer $s$ the **Betti number** (or **free rank**) of $A$. The symbol $\cong$ denotes group isomorphism. The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]].

# Statement

> **Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups.** Every [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] abelian group $A$ is isomorphic to a direct product of cyclic groups
> $$A \;\cong\; C_{d_1} \times C_{d_2} \times \dots \times C_{d_r} \times \underbrace{C_\infty \times \dots \times C_\infty}_{s},$$
> where $C_\infty \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is the infinite cyclic group, $s \geq 0$, the $d_i$ are integers $\geq 2$, and the divisibility chain
> $$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r$$
> holds.

> **Corollary (finite abelian groups).** If $A$ is a *finite* abelian group then there are no infinite-cyclic factors, so
> $$A \;\cong\; C_{d_1} \times C_{d_2} \times \dots \times C_{d_r}, \qquad d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r.$$

This is the master classification theorem for abelian groups. It says every finitely generated abelian group is built, by direct product, out of the simplest abelian groups there are — cyclic groups — split cleanly into a **free part** (the $s$ copies of $\mathbb{Z}$, recording the "non-torsion directions") and a **torsion part** (the finite cyclic factors $C_{d_i}$). The divisibility chain $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$ makes the list of invariant factors canonical. The result is obtained with no extra work from the [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a Euclidean domain]] by taking the ring to be $\mathbb{Z}$; this page proves the existence half, exactly as the lecture notes do, and exhibits the computation in a worked example.

# Motivation

A finite group, presented by generators and relations, is opaque. You might be told an abelian group $A$ is generated by three elements satisfying three relations and not be able to say — without work — whether $A$ is infinite, finite, or even the trivial group. The defining ambition of group theory is *classification*: to produce, for each kind of group, a complete and non-redundant list of isomorphism types, so that "which group is this?" has a finite, checkable answer. For non-abelian groups this ambition is monstrous — its endpoint is the classification of finite simple groups, thousands of pages long. The natural question is whether some class of groups is tame enough to be classified completely and cleanly. The abelian groups are that class, and this theorem is the classification.

It says: every finitely generated abelian group is, up to isomorphism, a product of cyclic groups, and the product can be arranged into a canonical normal form — a free part $\mathbb{Z}^s$ and a torsion part $C_{d_1} \times \dots \times C_{d_r}$ with $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$. The opaque "abelian group with three generators and three relations" becomes a transparent short list: an integer $s$ and a divisibility chain of integers $d_i$. Two finitely generated abelian groups are isomorphic *exactly* when these data agree. The isomorphism problem — undecidable for groups in general — is, for abelian groups, solved by comparing two lists of integers.

One should expect a result like this because abelian groups lack the feature that makes general groups wild: non-commuting elements. With every subgroup normal and conjugation trivial, there is no room for the semidirect-product "twisting" by which non-abelian groups assemble their pieces in complicated ways. The only way to build an abelian group from cyclic pieces is the untwisted way — the direct product — and the surprise is not that a classification exists but that it is this short.

The deeper reason to expect it, and the reason the proof is essentially free, is structural: a finitely generated abelian group *is* a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]]. The [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem]] already classifies finitely generated modules over *any* Euclidean domain — it says such a module is $\bigoplus R/(d_i) \oplus R^s$ with $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$. Reading this with $R = \mathbb{Z}$, and translating "$\mathbb{Z}$-module" into "abelian group", "$\mathbb{Z}/(d)$" into "$C_d$", and "$\mathbb{Z}$" into "$C_\infty$", *is* the classification. There is nothing to prove beyond the dictionary; the theorem is a corollary, and the lecture notes present it precisely as one. What this page adds beyond the translation is the practical content: *how to actually compute* the invariant factors of a concretely presented group, by Smith normal form and [[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal|Fitting ideals]] — which is what the worked example below demonstrates.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The literal precondition is *a finitely generated abelian group*. The skill is recognising, in a problem that does not announce "finitely generated abelian group", that the object in front of you is one — at which point the entire classification, and its computational machinery, becomes available.

The first disguised source is **an abelian group presented by generators and relations** — given by a finite generating set and a finite list of $\mathbb{Z}$-linear relations among the generators. Such a presentation is automatically a finitely generated abelian group, $A \cong \mathbb{Z}^m/K$ with $K$ the subgroup spanned by the relation vectors. The non-obvious recognition is that the relations assemble into an integer matrix, and the classification then runs *as the computation of that matrix's Smith normal form* — the invariant factors of the matrix are the invariant factors of the group. This is the source the worked example below exploits.

The second disguised source is **a quotient or subgroup of a known finitely generated abelian group**. Property: $A = B/N$ or $A \leq B$ with $B$ finitely generated abelian. A quotient of a finitely generated group is finitely generated; a subgroup of a finitely generated abelian group is finitely generated (a special feature of the abelian, Noetherian setting — $\mathbb{Z}$ is a Noetherian ring). The non-obvious recognition is that $A$, handed to you only as a piece of something else, still falls under the classification — and in particular the invariant factors of a quotient $\mathbb{Z}^n/L$ are read off the lattice $L$.

The third disguised source is **the additive group of a finite ring, or a finitely generated module over $\mathbb{Z}$ arising in another structure**. The additive group $(R, +)$ of any ring is abelian by the ring axioms; if $R$ is finite or finitely generated as an abelian group, the classification applies. The non-obvious step is that the object is presented as a ring, with multiplication foregrounded, and the abelian group hiding inside it — its additive structure — is easy to overlook. This is the route by which the classification pins down the additive structure of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and of rings of integers in number fields.

The fourth disguised source is **a finitely generated abelian group with a torsion hypothesis** — every element of finite order. Finite generation plus "every element torsion" forces the Betti number $s = 0$, so $A$ is finite. The non-obvious recognition is that the implication "finitely generated $+$ all elements torsion $\implies$ finite" is exactly the abelian miracle: it fails for non-abelian groups, and it is what makes the *finitely generated* abelian groups — not just the finite ones — completely classified.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusion is the normal form $C_{d_1} \times \dots \times C_{d_r} \times C_\infty^s$ with $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$. Combined with other facts it does more.

Combine the conclusion with **the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules|Chinese remainder theorem]]**. Each $C_{d_i}$, with $d_i = p_1^{n_1} \cdots p_k^{n_k}$, splits into prime-power cyclic groups $C_{p_j^{n_j}}$. The further result is the **elementary-divisor form**: every finitely generated abelian group is a product of prime-power cyclic groups and copies of $\mathbb{Z}$, which is the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]]. This combination converts the canonical invariant-factor form into the finest prime-power form — the same group, displayed two ways. It is non-obvious because the two forms look unrelated until coprime fusion is seen as the bridge.

Combine the conclusion with **partition counting**, restricted to a fixed finite order. The abelian groups of order $p^a$ correspond to partitions of the exponent $a$; the abelian groups of order $n = \prod p_i^{a_i}$ correspond to tuples of partitions, one per prime. The further result is an exact formula for the *number* of abelian groups of a given order — pure combinatorics of integer partitions. This is non-obvious because "how many groups of order $n$?" sounds like a question demanding group theory, yet for abelian groups it is a partition count.

Combine the conclusion with **the Betti number's well-definedness** ([[Thm - Invariance of Rank|invariance of rank]]). The classification yields *a* free rank $s$; invariance of rank certifies $s$ is intrinsic to $A$, independent of the chosen decomposition. The further result: the Betti number becomes a decidable invariant — two finitely generated abelian groups with different Betti numbers are non-isomorphic, checkable by computing two integers. This is non-obvious because it converts an isomorphism question into an integer comparison.

Combine the conclusion with **the largest invariant factor**. Because $d_i \mid d_r$ for all $i$ when the chain is written increasingly (here $d_r$ is the *last*, largest), $d_r$ is the **exponent** of the torsion part — the least common multiple of all element orders. The further result is a clean criterion: a finite abelian group is cyclic exactly when $r = 1$, i.e. when it contains an element of order $|A|$. This is non-obvious because the exponent looks like a quantity to be computed element by element, whereas the classification hands it over as the last invariant factor.

# Why Is It True

The intuition is a single sentence: **an abelian group is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, $\mathbb{Z}$ is a Euclidean domain, and the classification of modules over a Euclidean domain is already proved — so the theorem is a translation, not a new argument.**

Begin with the dictionary, because the whole theorem *is* the dictionary. An abelian group $A$ carries an addition. For any integer $n$ and element $g$, the expression $n \cdot g$ — meaning $g$ added to itself $n$ times (and $(-n) \cdot g = -(n \cdot g)$, $0 \cdot g = 0$) — equips $A$ with an action of the ring $\mathbb{Z}$. The module axioms $(m + n)g = mg + ng$, $m(ng) = (mn)g$, $1 \cdot g = g$ are just the elementary rules of repeated addition. So *an abelian group is exactly a $\mathbb{Z}$-module* — not analogous to one, literally one. "Finitely generated abelian group" is "finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module", "subgroup" is "submodule", "direct product" is "direct sum".

Now invoke what is already known. The [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem]] says: for *any* Euclidean domain $R$, every finitely generated $R$-module $M$ decomposes as
$$M \;\cong\; \frac{R}{(d_1)} \oplus \dots \oplus \frac{R}{(d_r)} \oplus R^s, \qquad d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r.$$
The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ are a Euclidean domain — the prototype, with $\varphi(n) = |n|$ and division with remainder being ordinary integer division. So the structure theorem applies *verbatim* with $R = \mathbb{Z}$. The only remaining task is to read the conclusion in group language. The cyclic module $\mathbb{Z}/(d)$ is the integers modulo $d$ under addition — that is the cyclic group $C_d$. The module $\mathbb{Z}$ itself is the integers under addition — the infinite cyclic group $C_\infty$. The direct sum of $\mathbb{Z}$-modules is the direct product of abelian groups. Substituting,
$$A \;\cong\; C_{d_1} \times \dots \times C_{d_r} \times \underbrace{C_\infty \times \dots \times C_\infty}_{s},$$
which is exactly the statement. The proof contributes no new mathematics; it contributes the recognition that the abelian-group question was an already-solved module question wearing different notation.

It is still worth seeing *why the structure theorem itself is true*, since that is the genuine content. The mechanism is diagonalisation of relations. A finitely generated abelian group is, by the [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|free-quotient theorem]], a quotient $\mathbb{Z}^m/K$ — a free abelian group on $m$ generators, with $K$ the subgroup recording every relation the generators satisfy. If the relations are tangled (a relation like $2a + 3b + c = 0$ ties three generators together), the quotient is hard to see. But over $\mathbb{Z}$ one can *change coordinates to untangle the relations*: [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] — integer row and column operations on the matrix of relations — produces a new basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $\mathbb{Z}^m$ along which $K$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$, each relation now touching a single coordinate. Row operations re-basis $\mathbb{Z}^m$; column operations re-choose the relations; the Euclidean division algorithm on $\mathbb{Z}$ is what lets you clear matrix entries and drive the divisibility chain $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$. Once the relations are diagonal, the quotient splits coordinate by coordinate: the $i$-th coordinate, constrained by "$d_i$ times it is zero", contributes $\mathbb{Z}/d_i\mathbb{Z} = C_{d_i}$; an unconstrained coordinate contributes a free $\mathbb{Z} = C_\infty$.

**Why finite abelian groups have no $C_\infty$ factors.** A copy of $C_\infty = \mathbb{Z}$ is infinite. If $A$ is finite it cannot contain an infinite subgroup, so the free rank $s$ must be $0$, and only the finite cyclic factors $C_{d_i}$ survive. Equivalently: every element of a finite group has finite order, but $\mathbb{Z}$ has elements of infinite order, so no $\mathbb{Z}$ summand is possible.

So the theorem should be unsurprising on two levels. At the level of bookkeeping, it is the structure theorem with $R = \mathbb{Z}$, read through a dictionary. At the level of mechanism, it holds because the relations among generators of an abelian group can always be diagonalised by integer row and column operations, after which the group falls apart into one-generator pieces.

# What Makes This Hard

The conceptual content is not in proving the theorem — once the structure theorem is in hand, the classification is a one-line translation, "$\mathbb{Z}$ is a Euclidean domain, so apply it" — but in *not mistaking the translation for a separate proof*; the genuine work was done in the structure theorem and is imported here. The practical difficulty is computational: extracting the invariant factors of a concretely presented group means computing the Smith normal form of its relation matrix, and the common errors are mishandling that computation — forgetting that $d_k$ is the $k$-th [[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal|Fitting ideal]] generator *divided by* the $(k-1)$-th rather than read off raw, and dropping the divisibility chain so that the factors come out in the wrong order. When returning to this theorem after months, focus rederivation on the dictionary $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\leftrightarrow$ abelian group and on the Smith-normal-form computation in the worked example.

Recognise that an abelian group is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module and that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a Euclidean domain. Apply the [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem]] with $R = \mathbb{Z}$, then translate its conclusion into group language via the dictionary $\mathbb{Z}/(d) = C_d$, $\mathbb{Z} = C_\infty$, direct sum $=$ direct product. For the finite case, observe that a finite group admits no infinite-cyclic factor.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Set up the dictionary.** Show that an abelian group is exactly a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, with $n \cdot g$ meaning repeated addition; identify $C_d$ with $\mathbb{Z}/(d)$, $C_\infty$ with $\mathbb{Z}$, and direct product with direct sum.
   - *Hint:* The module axioms for the $\mathbb{Z}$-action are the arithmetic rules of repeated addition; conversely a $\mathbb{Z}$-module forgets to an abelian group.
   - *Why needed:* It identifies the abelian-group problem with a module problem already solved.

2. **Apply the structure theorem with $R = \mathbb{Z}$.** Note $\mathbb{Z}$ is a Euclidean domain (with $\varphi(n) = |n|$), so a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module $A$ satisfies $A \cong \mathbb{Z}/(d_1) \oplus \dots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/(d_r) \oplus \mathbb{Z}^s$ with $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$.
   - *Hint:* This is the [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem]], invoked verbatim — no new argument.
   - *Why needed:* It is the entire mathematical content; everything else is translation.

3. **Translate into group language.** Rewrite $\mathbb{Z}/(d_i)$ as $C_{d_i}$, each $\mathbb{Z}$ as $C_\infty$, and $\oplus$ as $\times$, obtaining $A \cong C_{d_1} \times \dots \times C_{d_r} \times C_\infty^s$.
   - *Hint:* Use the dictionary from subgoal 1, term by term.
   - *Why needed:* It produces the theorem in the stated form.

4. **Specialise to the finite case.** If $A$ is finite, argue $s = 0$, so $A \cong C_{d_1} \times \dots \times C_{d_r}$.
   - *Hint:* $C_\infty \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is infinite; a finite group has no infinite subgroup, equivalently every element has finite order.
   - *Why needed:* It delivers the corollary — the classification of finite abelian groups.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: An abelian group is exactly a module over the integers
> **Statement:** Every abelian group $A$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module under the action $n \cdot g = \underbrace{g + \dots + g}_{n}$ (with $0 \cdot g = 0$ and $(-n) \cdot g = -(n \cdot g)$), and conversely every $\mathbb{Z}$-module is an abelian group under its addition. Group homomorphisms and $\mathbb{Z}$-module homomorphisms coincide; subgroups and submodules coincide; direct products and direct sums coincide.
>
> **Hint:** The module axioms for the $\mathbb{Z}$-action unwind to the elementary arithmetic of repeated addition.
>
> **Why needed:** It identifies "finitely generated abelian group" with "finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module", so the structure theorem applies.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $A$ be an abelian group, written additively. Define $n \cdot g$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, $g \in A$ by $0 \cdot g = 0$, $(n+1) \cdot g = n \cdot g + g$ for $n \geq 0$, and $(-n) \cdot g = -(n \cdot g)$. The [[Def - Module|module axioms]] hold:
> > $(m + n) g = mg + ng$ and $m(ng) = (mn)g$ are the standard laws of repeated addition (proved by induction on $|m|, |n|$), $n(g + h) = ng + nh$ holds because $A$ is abelian (the terms can be reordered), and $1 \cdot g = g$ by definition. So $A$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, and the action is *forced* — once the ring is $\mathbb{Z}$, there is no freedom in how $1$ (hence every integer) acts.
> >
> > Conversely, a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is in particular an abelian group under its addition (the additive part of the module axioms). A map $f : A \to B$ between abelian groups satisfies $f(g + h) = f(g) + f(h)$ if and only if it also satisfies $f(n \cdot g) = n \cdot f(g)$ for all $n$ (the latter follows from the former by induction), so [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]] and group homomorphism are the same condition. A subset closed under addition and negation is automatically closed under the $\mathbb{Z}$-action, so subgroup and submodule coincide. The direct product of abelian groups, with coordinatewise addition, is exactly the [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules|direct sum]] of the corresponding $\mathbb{Z}$-modules. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Cyclic modules over the integers are cyclic groups
> **Statement:** For an integer $d \geq 1$, the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}/(d)$ is the cyclic group $C_d$ of order $d$. The $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}$ itself is the infinite cyclic group $C_\infty$.
>
> **Hint:** $\mathbb{Z}/(d)$ is the integers modulo $d$ under addition; $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers under addition.
>
> **Why needed:** It translates each cyclic summand produced by the structure theorem into the corresponding cyclic group.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > The quotient module $\mathbb{Z}/(d)$ has underlying set the cosets $\{0 + (d), 1 + (d), \dots, (d-1) + (d)\}$ — exactly $d$ elements — with addition $(i + (d)) + (j + (d)) = (i + j) + (d)$. This is the cyclic group of order $d$: it is generated by the single element $1 + (d)$, every element being $k \cdot (1 + (d))$, and the additive order of that generator is $d$. Hence $\mathbb{Z}/(d) \cong C_d$.
> >
> > The module $\mathbb{Z}$ has underlying set all integers, with addition; it is generated by $1$, and $1$ has infinite additive order ($k \cdot 1 = k \neq 0$ for $k \neq 0$). So $\mathbb{Z}$ is the infinite cyclic group $C_\infty$. (Note $\mathbb{Z}/(1)$ is the trivial group, and $\mathbb{Z}/(0) = \mathbb{Z}$, consistent with discarding unit moduli and absorbing $d_i = 0$ into the free part.) $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: A finite abelian group has Betti number zero
> **Statement:** If $A$ is a finite abelian group, then in its decomposition $A \cong C_{d_1} \times \dots \times C_{d_r} \times C_\infty^s$ the free rank is $s = 0$.
>
> **Hint:** $C_\infty \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is infinite; a finite group cannot contain an infinite subgroup.
>
> **Why needed:** It produces the corollary: a finite abelian group is purely a product of finite cyclic groups.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Suppose, for contradiction, that $s \geq 1$. Then the decomposition $A \cong C_{d_1} \times \dots \times C_{d_r} \times C_\infty^s$ exhibits a subgroup isomorphic to $C_\infty \cong \mathbb{Z}$ — namely the set of tuples that are zero in every coordinate except one fixed $C_\infty$ factor. But $\mathbb{Z}$ is infinite, so $A$ would contain an infinite subgroup and hence be infinite, contradicting finiteness. Therefore $s = 0$, and
> > $$A \;\cong\; C_{d_1} \times C_{d_2} \times \dots \times C_{d_r}$$
> > with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r$. Equivalently: every element of a finite group has finite order, whereas a generator of a $C_\infty$ factor has infinite order, so no $C_\infty$ factor can occur. $\blacksquare$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $A$ be a finitely generated abelian group.
>
> **Step 1 — recognise $A$ as a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module.** By Lemma 1, the abelian group $A$ is a module over the ring $\mathbb{Z}$, with the integer $n$ acting by $n \cdot g = g + \dots + g$ ($n$ summands). A finite generating set for $A$ as a group is a finite generating set for $A$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module, so $A$ is a [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] $\mathbb{Z}$-module.
>
> **Step 2 — the integers form a Euclidean domain.** $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] under the Euclidean function $\varphi(n) = |n|$: given integers $a$ and $b \neq 0$ there are $q, r$ with $a = bq + r$ and $0 \leq |r| < |b|$ (ordinary division with remainder), and $|ab| \geq |b|$ for non-zero $a$.
>
> **Step 3 — apply the structure theorem.** By the [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a Euclidean domain]], applied with $R = \mathbb{Z}$, there exist a non-negative integer $s$ and integers $d_1, \dots, d_r$, each $\geq 2$, with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r$, such that
> $$A \;\cong\; \frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(d_1)} \oplus \frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(d_2)} \oplus \dots \oplus \frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(d_r)} \oplus \underbrace{\mathbb{Z} \oplus \dots \oplus \mathbb{Z}}_{s}.$$
>
> **Step 4 — translate into group language.** By Lemma 2, each cyclic module $\mathbb{Z}/(d_i)$ is the cyclic group $C_{d_i}$, and each $\mathbb{Z}$ summand is the infinite cyclic group $C_\infty$. By Lemma 1, the direct sum of $\mathbb{Z}$-modules is the direct product of abelian groups. Substituting,
> $$A \;\cong\; C_{d_1} \times C_{d_2} \times \dots \times C_{d_r} \times \underbrace{C_\infty \times \dots \times C_\infty}_{s}, \qquad d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r.$$
> This proves the theorem.
>
> **The corollary — finite abelian groups.** Suppose $A$ is finite. By Lemma 3 the free rank satisfies $s = 0$, since a $C_\infty \cong \mathbb{Z}$ factor would make $A$ infinite. Hence
> $$A \;\cong\; C_{d_1} \times C_{d_2} \times \dots \times C_{d_r}, \qquad d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r,$$
> a direct product of finite cyclic groups. $\blacksquare$

# Worked Example — Identifying a Group from Generators and Relations

This is the computation that makes the classification *effective*: given a group by generators and relations, the worked procedure below produces its invariant factors. It is the example from the lecture notes.

> [!example] The group $A$ and its presentation
> Let $A$ be the abelian group generated by $a, b, c$ subject to the three relations
> $$2a + 3b + c = 0, \qquad a + 2b = 0, \qquad 5a + 6b + 7c = 0.$$
> As things stand it is *not even obvious whether $A$ is the zero group*. We will identify it exactly.

**Step 1 — write $A$ as a quotient $\mathbb{Z}^3/K$.** Three generators $a, b, c$ mean $A$ is a quotient of the free abelian group $\mathbb{Z}^3$ (with standard basis $e_1, e_2, e_3$ mapping to $a, b, c$). The three relations are the statements that three specific elements of $\mathbb{Z}^3$ map to $0$, so the relation subgroup $K$ is generated by the relation vectors $(2,3,1)$, $(1,2,0)$, $(5,6,7)$:
$$A \;\cong\; \frac{\mathbb{Z}^3}{\bigl\langle (2,3,1),\ (1,2,0),\ (5,6,7) \bigr\rangle}.$$

**Step 2 — assemble the presentation matrix.** Place the relation vectors as the *columns* of a $3 \times 3$ integer matrix:
$$X \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 5 \\ 3 & 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 0 & 7 \end{pmatrix}.$$
The invariant factors $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid d_3$ of $A$ are the diagonal entries of the [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] of $X$.

**Step 3 — find the invariant factors via Fitting ideals.** Rather than carry out row and column operations, compute the [[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal|Fitting ideals]] $\operatorname{Fit}_k(X)$ — the ideal generated by all $k \times k$ minors — and use that $\operatorname{Fit}_k(X) = (d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k)$ ([[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|Fitting ideals are invariants]]). Then $d_k$ is recovered by dividing a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ by a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_{k-1}$.

> [!note]- The Fitting-ideal computation
> **First Fitting ideal $\operatorname{Fit}_1(X)$.** This is generated by all $1 \times 1$ minors — the matrix entries themselves. The entries include $1$ (the $(1,2)$ entry), and the ideal $(1) = \mathbb{Z}$ is the whole ring regardless of the other entries. So
> $$\operatorname{Fit}_1(X) = (1), \qquad\text{hence}\qquad d_1 = 1.$$
>
> **Second Fitting ideal $\operatorname{Fit}_2(X)$.** This is generated by all $2 \times 2$ minors. In principle one checks every $2 \times 2$ submatrix, but it is enough to find one minor equal to a unit: the top-left $2 \times 2$ submatrix has determinant
> $$\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 2 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 3 = 1.$$
> So $\operatorname{Fit}_2(X) = (1)$, and $d_1 d_2 = 1$ gives $d_2 = 1$.
>
> **Third Fitting ideal $\operatorname{Fit}_3(X)$.** For a $3 \times 3$ matrix the only $3 \times 3$ minor is the determinant:
> $$\det X = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 & 5 \\ 3 & 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 0 & 7 \end{vmatrix}.$$
> Expanding along the third row: $\det X = 1 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 5 \\ 2 & 6 \end{vmatrix} - 0 + 7 \cdot \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 1 \cdot (6 - 10) + 7 \cdot (4 - 3) = -4 + 7 = 3.$
> So $\operatorname{Fit}_3(X) = (3)$, and $d_1 d_2 d_3 = 3$ with $d_1 = d_2 = 1$ gives $d_3 = 3$.

**Step 4 — read off the group.** The invariant factors are $d_1 = 1$, $d_2 = 1$, $d_3 = 3$. By the classification,
$$A \;\cong\; \frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(1)} \oplus \frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(1)} \oplus \frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(3)}.$$
Now $\mathbb{Z}/(1)$ is the *trivial* group (the modulus is a unit), so the first two summands vanish, leaving
$$A \;\cong\; \frac{\mathbb{Z}}{(3)} \;\cong\; C_3.$$
The opaque presentation — three generators, three tangled relations — describes nothing more than the cyclic group of order $3$. (Note also $\det X = 3 \neq 0$: the matrix is invertible over $\mathbb{Q}$, so $K$ has full rank $3$, the Betti number is $s = 3 - 3 = 0$, and $A$ is finite of order $|\det X| = 3$ — a useful sanity check.)

> [!tip] The general procedure
> To identify any abelian group given by generators and relations: (1) write it as $\mathbb{Z}^m/K$; (2) form the matrix $X$ whose columns are the relation vectors; (3) compute the invariant factors $d_k = \operatorname{Fit}_k(X)/\operatorname{Fit}_{k-1}(X)$ from the Fitting ideals (or directly by Smith normal form); (4) read off $A \cong \bigoplus C_{d_k} \oplus \mathbb{Z}^{m - r}$, discarding $C_{d_k}$ for any $d_k$ that is a unit. The Betti number is $m$ minus the number of non-zero $d_k$, equivalently $m - \operatorname{rank}_{\mathbb{Q}}(X)$.

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Homology groups of a topological space.** The homology groups $H_n(Y; \mathbb{Z})$ of a finite simplicial complex $Y$ are finitely generated abelian groups, computed as the quotient of a group of cycles by a subgroup of boundaries — that is, presented exactly as $\mathbb{Z}^m/K$ with $K$ the image of a boundary matrix. The classification supplies their normal form: the Betti number $s$ is the topological Betti number (counting $n$-dimensional holes), and the invariant factors $d_i$ are the **torsion coefficients**, genuine topological invariants distinguishing, for instance, the Klein bottle from the torus. The non-obvious recognition is that the boundary matrix *is* a presentation matrix, so computing homology is running the worked example of this page.

**The multiplicative group of a finite field is cyclic.** The non-zero elements of a finite field $\mathbb{F}_q$ form a finite abelian group $\mathbb{F}_q^\times$ of order $q - 1$ under multiplication. By the classification it is $C_{d_1} \times \dots \times C_{d_r}$; one shows $r = 1$ because every element satisfies $x^{d_r} = 1$, so the polynomial $X^{d_r} - 1$ has all $q - 1$ field elements as roots, forcing $d_r \geq q - 1$. Hence $\mathbb{F}_q^\times$ is cyclic. The non-obvious application is that "$\mathbb{F}_q^\times$ is cyclic", usually proved by totient sums, routes instead through the classification's largest-invariant-factor target.

**The ideal class group of a number field.** The ideal class group of a number field is a *finite* abelian group measuring the failure of unique factorisation in its ring of integers. The classification gives it a canonical form $C_{d_1} \times \dots \times C_{d_r}$, and the invariant factors are arithmetic invariants of the field. The non-obvious recognition is that a deep object of algebraic number theory, defined as a quotient of fractional ideals by principal ones, is a finite abelian group to which the classification applies outright — turning it into a short list of integers.

**Lattices and the cokernel of an integer matrix.** A *lattice* $L \leq \mathbb{Z}^n$ of full rank is the relation subgroup of the finite abelian group $\mathbb{Z}^n/L$, and the order of that group is $|\det|$ of any matrix whose columns are a basis of $L$ — the covolume of the lattice. The classification, run on the basis matrix, gives both the order and the precise group structure. The non-obvious application is that a geometric quantity (the index of a sublattice) and an algebraic one (the structure of the quotient group) are simultaneously delivered by the Smith normal form computation of the worked example.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain]]** — the parent theorem of which this is the case $R = \mathbb{Z}$. The classification is *literally* the structure theorem read with the ring fixed to be the integers and the module language translated to group language; the proof imports the structure theorem and adds only the dictionary.

- **[[Thm - Classification of Finite Abelian Groups|Classification of Finite Abelian Groups]]** — the same corollary, met earlier in group theory and *deferred* there to exactly this point. The group-theory page states the finite case and notes its proof "will be proved in Chapter 3 as a special case of the classification of modules over certain rings"; the present page is where that deferred proof is discharged.

- **[[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|Primary Decomposition Theorem]]** — the refinement. Applying the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules|Chinese remainder theorem]] to each invariant factor $d_i$ splits $C_{d_i}$ into prime-power cyclic groups, converting the invariant-factor form into the elementary-divisor form — every finitely generated abelian group as a product of prime-power cyclic groups and copies of $\mathbb{Z}$.

- **[[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith Normal Form]] and [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|Fitting Ideals]]** — the computational engine. The classification is an existence statement; Smith normal form makes it *effective*, turning "find the invariant factors of a presented group" into a finite matrix computation, and the [[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal|Fitting ideals]] give an often-faster route to the same invariant factors, as the worked example shows.

- **[[Thm - Invariance of Rank|Invariance of Rank]]** — the guarantee that the Betti number is well-defined. The classification produces *some* free rank $s$; invariance of rank certifies $s$ is intrinsic to the group, so "the Betti number" is an honest, basis-independent invariant.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Finitely Generated Modules over a PID *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> The same classification holds over any principal ideal domain, with abelian groups being the case $R = \mathbb{Z}$. Recognising "abelian group $=$ $\mathbb{Z}$-module" is the gateway: once a group is seen as a module, the entire structure theory of modules over a PID — and the canonical forms of matrices over $F[X]$ — becomes available.

> [!tip] Smith Normal Form Algorithms *(from Computational Algebra)*
> The worked example shows the classification is genuinely computable: the invariant factors of a presented group are the diagonal entries of a Smith normal form, obtained by integer row and column operations or, faster, by Fitting-ideal (minor) computations. The same algorithm underlies effective computation of homology groups and of canonical forms of matrices.
