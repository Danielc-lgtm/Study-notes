# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a commutative ring with $1$ (from §3.3 onwards usually a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]], though the present definition needs only a commutative ring). An **$m \times n$ matrix over $R$** is a rectangular array $A = (A_{ij})$ with $A_{ij} \in R$, $1 \le i \le m$ (row index), $1 \le j \le n$ (column index). The set of such matrices is $M_{m \times n}(R)$, and $M_n(R) = M_{n \times n}(R)$ is the ring of square matrices. The **identity matrix** $I_n \in M_n(R)$ has $1$ on the diagonal and $0$ elsewhere. A square matrix $P \in M_n(R)$ is **invertible** (a **unit** of the ring $M_n(R)$) when there is $P^{-1} \in M_n(R)$ with $PP^{-1} = P^{-1}P = I_n$; the invertible matrices form the group $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$. A **unit** of $R$ is an element $c \in R$ with a multiplicative inverse $c^{-1} \in R$; over $\mathbb{Z}$ the units are $\pm 1$, over a field every nonzero element is a unit. The symbol $E_{ij}$ denotes the matrix with $1$ in position $(i,j)$ and $0$ elsewhere, so any matrix is $\sum_{ij} A_{ij} E_{ij}$. A matrix $A \in M_{m \times n}(R)$ is read as the matrix of a [[Def - Module Homomorphism|homomorphism]] $R^n \to R^m$ in the standard bases, with column $j$ recording the image of the $j$-th basis vector. The full notation registry lives on the parent page [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]].

# Axiom Motivation

We are after a notion of "simplifying a matrix without losing the essential information it carries", and the whole definition can be reverse-engineered from a single demand: **decide in advance what the matrix is allowed to represent, then permit exactly the moves that preserve it.** A matrix over $R$ has two faces, and §3.3 cares about both at once. As a map, an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ is a [[Def - Module Homomorphism|homomorphism]] $R^n \to R^m$ written in the standard bases. As a presentation, the columns of $A$ are a chosen list of generators of a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] $N = \operatorname{im}(A) \le R^m$, and $A$ records that submodule together with the free module it sits inside. The right operations are the ones that leave *the map up to change of basis* — equivalently, *the submodule up to change of generators and ambient basis* — completely unchanged, while moving the entries around freely. Get that right and "simplify the matrix" becomes "find the cleanest matrix in the same equivalence class", which is the content of the [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]].

Start from linear algebra and ask what is allowed there. Over a field, Gaussian elimination uses three moves on the rows of a matrix: add a multiple of one row to another, swap two rows, scale a row by a nonzero constant. Each move is reversible, and each move corresponds to left-multiplying by an invertible matrix. The reversibility is the whole point: it guarantees that elimination never destroys information, only repackages it. Now transplant this to a ring $R$. Two of the three moves carry over verbatim: adding $c$ times row $i$ to row $j$ (any $c \in R$, no constraint, because the inverse move subtracts $c$ times row $i$), and swapping two rows (its own inverse). The third move is where the ring bites. Over a field "scale a row by a nonzero constant $c$" is reversible because $c^{-1}$ exists. Over a ring, $c^{-1}$ exists **only if $c$ is a unit**. Scaling a row of an integer matrix by $2$ is not reversible inside $M_m(\mathbb{Z})$ — there is no integer matrix undoing it — so it must be forbidden. The definition therefore *cannot* say "multiply a row by any nonzero element"; it must say "multiply a row by a **unit**", and this restriction is forced by the demand for reversibility, not chosen for taste.

Watch what breaks if we get the unit condition wrong. *Weaken* it to "scale a row by any nonzero $c \in R$." Then over $\mathbb{Z}$ the matrix $(2)$ — a $1 \times 1$ matrix — would be "equivalent" to $(1)$, by scaling the row by $\tfrac12$... except $\tfrac12 \notin \mathbb{Z}$, so really the weakened operation, applied as left multiplication, multiplies by the matrix $(2)$, which is *not invertible* over $\mathbb{Z}$. The relation "equivalent" would stop being symmetric, stop being an equivalence relation, and — fatally — the [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] would collapse: every nonzero integer matrix would reduce to one with $1$'s, erasing exactly the arithmetic content (the invariant factors) the theorem exists to expose. The example the source gives is decisive: over $\mathbb{Z}$, the matrix $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ *cannot* be brought to $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, because no legal operation turns a $2$ into a $1$. If the weakened operation were legal, it could, and Smith normal form would say nothing. *Strengthen* the operation instead — forbid scaling entirely, keeping only "add a multiple" and "swap" — and you lose the ability to normalise the sign or unit-multiple of a pivot; the Smith normal form would then be unique only up to *permutation and unit-rescaling of rows*, which is a weaker and clumsier statement. Permitting exactly unit-scaling is the Goldilocks choice: reversible, and just enough freedom to clean up units.

There is a second design decision hiding in "row **and** column". Why both? Because the two faces of the matrix demand it. Row operations on $A$ correspond to **left** multiplication, $A \mapsto QA$ with $Q$ invertible; column operations correspond to **right** multiplication, $A \mapsto AT^{-1}$ with $T^{-1}$ invertible. In the map picture $R^n \xrightarrow{A} R^m$, left multiplication by $Q$ changes the basis of the **target** $R^m$, and right multiplication by $T^{-1}$ changes the basis of the **source** $R^n$. A matrix that records a map up to *independent* change of basis at source and target must be allowed to move under *both*. In the presentation picture this reads even more vividly. The columns of $A$ generate a submodule $N \le R^m$. A **column** operation replaces one generator of $N$ by a combination of generators — it changes the *chosen generating set of $N$* but not $N$ itself, because the new generators still span exactly $N$. A **row** operation re-expresses everything in a new basis of the ambient $R^m$ — it changes the *coordinate system* in which both $R^m$ and its submodule $N$ are written, carrying $N$ to an isomorphic copy. Keep only row operations and you can re-coordinatise the ambient module but are stuck with the generators you were handed; keep only column operations and you can tidy the generators but never the ambient frame. To reach the maximally simple matrix you need both knobs, and the definition gives you both.

The reason to package these six operations as "elementary" and then *immediately* repackage them as multiplication by invertible matrices is the last piece of motivation. Performing a sequence of elementary operations is, by construction, left-multiplying by a product of invertible matrices and right-multiplying by another product of invertible matrices. Products of invertible matrices are invertible. So "reachable by a sequence of elementary operations" implies "$B = QAT^{-1}$ for some invertible $Q, T$." The converse — that *every* such $QAT^{-1}$ is reachable by elementary operations — is the genuinely useful direction, and over a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] it is true because the elementary matrices generate $\mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ (this uses the Euclidean algorithm; it can fail over a general ring). The point of the equivalence is to free you from thinking step-by-step: to *check* that two matrices are equivalent you exhibit invertible $Q, T$; to *produce* an equivalent simple matrix you run elementary operations. The definition is built so that the procedural description and the algebraic description name the same relation.

# The Definition

This is a compound definition. It introduces, in sequence: the three **elementary row operations** and the three **elementary column operations**; the **elementary matrices** that realise them; and the relation of **equivalence** of matrices, with its algebraic characterisation. Fix a commutative ring $R$ with $1$ and an $m \times n$ matrix $A = (A_{ij})$ with entries in $R$.

## Elementary row operations

An **elementary row operation** on $A$ is one of the following three transformations. Each is realised by **left** multiplication $A \mapsto EA$ by an invertible square matrix $E \in M_m(R)$, the **elementary matrix** of the operation.

- **(ER1) Transvection — add a multiple of one row to another.** For $i \ne j$ and any $c \in R$, replace row $j$ by $(\text{row } j) + c \cdot (\text{row } i)$, leaving all other rows fixed. The elementary matrix is $I_m + c\,E_{ji}$ — the identity with the single off-diagonal entry $c$ inserted in position $(j,i)$. It is invertible with inverse $I_m - c\,E_{ji}$ (subtract $c$ times row $i$ from row $j$). No constraint is placed on $c$: any ring element is permitted, because the inverse operation exists for every $c$.

- **(ER2) Transposition — swap two rows.** For $i \ne j$, interchange rows $i$ and $j$, leaving all other rows fixed. The elementary matrix is the **permutation matrix** $P_{ij}$, obtained from $I_m$ by swapping rows $i$ and $j$; its off-diagonal nonzero entries are the $1$'s in positions $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$. It is invertible and equal to its own inverse, $P_{ij}^2 = I_m$.

- **(ER3) Dilation — scale a row by a unit.** For a row index $i$ and a **unit** $c \in R^\times$ (an element with a two-sided multiplicative inverse $c^{-1} \in R$), replace row $i$ by $c \cdot (\text{row } i)$. The elementary matrix is the **diagonal matrix** $D_i(c)$, equal to $I_m$ except for the entry $c$ in position $(i,i)$. It is invertible with inverse $D_i(c^{-1})$. The hypothesis that $c$ is a unit is essential: if $c$ were a non-unit, $D_i(c)$ would not be invertible over $R$ and the operation would be irreversible.

## Elementary column operations

An **elementary column operation** is the column analogue: **(EC1)** add $c \in R$ times column $i$ to column $j$ ($i \ne j$); **(EC2)** swap columns $i$ and $j$; **(EC3)** scale column $i$ by a unit $c \in R^\times$. Each is realised by **right** multiplication $A \mapsto AE$ by an invertible square matrix $E \in M_n(R)$, where $E$ is the corresponding elementary matrix (of size $n$): respectively $I_n + c\,E_{ij}$, the permutation matrix $P_{ij}$, and the diagonal matrix $D_i(c)$. The asymmetry — column operations multiply on the right, and the off-diagonal entry of the transvection matrix sits in position $(i,j)$ rather than $(j,i)$ — is exactly what makes right multiplication act on columns rather than rows.

> [!note]- Why these matrices act as claimed
> For (ER1): $(I_m + cE_{ji})A$ has $\big((I_m + cE_{ji})A\big)_{k\ell} = A_{k\ell} + c\,(E_{ji}A)_{k\ell}$, and $(E_{ji}A)_{k\ell}$ equals $A_{i\ell}$ when $k = j$ and $0$ otherwise; so row $j$ of the product is $A_{j\ell} + cA_{i\ell}$ and every other row is unchanged — precisely "add $c$ times row $i$ to row $j$." For (EC1): $A(I_n + cE_{ij})$ has $\big(A(I_n + cE_{ij})\big)_{k\ell} = A_{k\ell} + c\,(AE_{ij})_{k\ell}$, and $(AE_{ij})_{k\ell} = A_{ki}$ when $\ell = j$ and $0$ otherwise; so column $j$ becomes $A_{kj} + cA_{ki}$ — "add $c$ times column $i$ to column $j$." The permutation and diagonal cases are the corresponding standard computations: left multiplication permutes/scales rows, right multiplication permutes/scales columns.

## Equivalent matrices

Two matrices $A, B \in M_{m \times n}(R)$ are **equivalent**, written $A \sim B$, when one can be obtained from the other by a finite sequence of elementary row and column operations.

**Algebraic characterisation.** If $A \sim B$ then
$$B = QAT^{-1}$$
for some invertible matrices $Q \in \mathrm{GL}_m(R)$ and $T \in \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$. Indeed, performing the row operations amounts to left-multiplying $A$ by the product $Q$ of the corresponding elementary matrices, and performing the column operations amounts to right-multiplying by the product of column elementary matrices, which is written $T^{-1}$; both $Q$ and $T^{-1}$ are products of invertible matrices, hence invertible. (The form $QAT^{-1}$, rather than $QAT$, is chosen so that $A \mapsto B$ corresponds to the change of basis $T$ on the source $R^n$ and $Q$ on the target $R^m$ of the homomorphism $A : R^n \to R^m$.) Over a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] the converse also holds — every pair $Q, T^{-1}$ of invertible matrices is a product of elementary matrices, so $B = QAT^{-1}$ implies $A \sim B$ — and there $\sim$ is *exactly* the relation $B = QAT^{-1}$.

The relation $\sim$ is an **equivalence relation**: it is reflexive (the empty sequence of operations gives $A \sim A$), symmetric (every elementary operation has an inverse elementary operation, so a sequence taking $A$ to $B$ reverses to one taking $B$ to $A$), and transitive (concatenate sequences).

**Geometric reading of the two kinds of operation.** Regard $A$ as the matrix, in the standard bases, of a homomorphism $A : R^n \to R^m$, with the columns of $A$ generating the submodule $N = \operatorname{im}(A) \le R^m$.

- A **row operation** corresponds to left multiplication by $Q$ and **changes the basis of the ambient free module $R^m$** — it re-coordinatises the target, carrying the submodule $N$ to the isomorphic submodule $QN$.
- A **column operation** corresponds to right multiplication by $T^{-1}$ and **changes the chosen generators of the submodule $N$** — it replaces the generating set (the columns) by another generating set of the *same* submodule $N$, since the columns of $AT^{-1}$ and of $A$ have the same $R$-span.

Thus equivalence is precisely "same submodule of a free module, up to change of generators and change of ambient basis" — which is why the Smith normal form of $A$ reads off the structure of $N \le R^m$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

This is **Gaussian elimination, ported from a field to a ring, with the scaling operation amputated to unit-scaling.** Over a field $k$, the three elementary row operations are exactly the moves of row reduction, and the elementary matrices generate $\mathrm{GL}_n(k)$; two matrices over a field are equivalent if and only if they have the same rank, and every matrix is equivalent to a block matrix $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. The single edit when passing to a ring is the (ER3)/(EC3) constraint "$c$ a unit" replacing "$c$ nonzero." That one edit is responsible for the entire difference between linear algebra and module theory at the level of matrices: over a field the only equivalence invariant is the rank $r$, whereas over a Euclidean domain a finer invariant survives — the [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|invariant factors]] $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ — precisely because the inability to rescale a $2$ to a $1$ leaves arithmetic residue in the normal form. Linear algebra is the special case "$R$ a field, where every nonzero scalar is a unit, so the invariant factors are all $1$ and only the rank remains."

The relation $A \sim B \iff B = QAT^{-1}$ is the **double-coset** description of equivalence. The group $\mathrm{GL}_m(R) \times \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ acts on the set $M_{m \times n}(R)$ by $(Q, T) \cdot A = QAT^{-1}$, and the equivalence classes are exactly the orbits of this action — equivalently, the double cosets $\mathrm{GL}_m(R) \backslash M_{m\times n}(R) / \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$. Finding a "simplest representative" of an equivalence class is the orbit-classification problem for this group action; the Smith normal form is a choice of canonical representative, one per orbit. This is the same conceptual move as classifying conic sections up to the affine group, or matrices up to *similarity* (conjugation, $A \mapsto PAP^{-1}$, one group acting by conjugation) — except equivalence uses *two independent* copies of the general linear group acting on the two sides, which is why it is coarser than similarity and admits the clean Smith normal form, while similarity requires the subtler rational and Jordan canonical forms.

Two cautions on the analogy. First, over a general commutative ring (not Euclidean, not even a PID) the converse direction fails: there exist invertible $Q, T$ with $B = QAT^{-1}$ that are *not* products of elementary matrices, so "$B = QAT^{-1}$" can be strictly weaker than "$A \sim B$ by elementary operations." The phenomenon is measured by the Whitehead group $K_1(R)$ and the special Whitehead group $SK_1(R)$ of algebraic $K$-theory; for a Euclidean domain these vanish and the two notions coincide, which is exactly the regime §3.3 works in. Second, equivalence of matrices is **not** the same as similarity: similar matrices are equivalent (take $T = Q$), but equivalent matrices need not be similar — equivalence forgets the eigenvalue/characteristic-polynomial data that similarity retains, because it allows the source and target bases to be changed independently.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: clearing a row over $\mathbb{Z}$.** Take $A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ over $\mathbb{Z}$. The column operation (EC1) "add column $1$ to column $2$"... is not quite what is wanted; the source's reduction adds column $2$ to column $1$, then proceeds: $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right) \rightsquigarrow \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 3 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ by adding column $2$ to column $1$, then $\rightsquigarrow \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ by adding column $1$ to column $2$, then $\rightsquigarrow \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ by subtracting $3$ times row $1$ from row $2$. Every move used only (EC1)/(ER1) with integer coefficients — all legal, since (ER1)/(EC1) place no constraint on the multiplier $c$. The end matrix $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is the [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] of $A$, and the example shows the typical workflow: shuffle a unit (here a $1$) into the pivot by Euclidean-algorithm steps, then use it to clear its row and column.

**Is an instance: scaling by a unit over $\mathbb{Z}$.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the units are $+1$ and $-1$. So (ER3) permits exactly one nontrivial dilation: multiplying a row by $-1$. This is enough to normalise the sign of a pivot — to turn an invariant factor $-3$ into $+3$ — and explains the source's remark that the invariant factors are unique only **up to associates** (up to unit multiples): the freedom (ER3) grants is precisely the freedom to multiply each $d_k$ by a unit.

**Is an instance: any change of basis.** If $Q \in \mathrm{GL}_m(R)$ and $T \in \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ are arbitrary invertible matrices *over a Euclidean domain*, then $A$ and $QAT^{-1}$ are equivalent — because over a Euclidean domain $Q$ and $T^{-1}$ each factor as products of elementary matrices, so $QAT^{-1}$ is reachable from $A$ by the corresponding sequence of elementary operations. This is the converse direction of the algebraic characterisation, and it is what lets one *verify* equivalence by producing invertible $Q, T$ rather than exhibiting an explicit operation sequence.

**Is NOT an instance: scaling a row by a non-unit.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$, multiplying a row by $2$ is **not** an elementary operation: $2$ is not a unit of $\mathbb{Z}$, so the matrix $D_i(2)$ is not invertible over $\mathbb{Z}$ and the move is irreversible. Consequently $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ are **not equivalent** over $\mathbb{Z}$: no sequence of legal operations turns the $1$ into a $2$ (nor the $2$ into a $1$). This non-instance is the entire reason the Smith normal form over $\mathbb{Z}$ carries arithmetic content; over a field, where $2$ *is* a unit, the two matrices *would* be equivalent.

**Is NOT an instance: deleting a row, or transposing the matrix.** Striking out a row of $A$, or replacing $A$ by its transpose $A^{\mathsf T}$, are not elementary operations and do not in general preserve equivalence — the first changes the size $m \times n$ and so cannot even be compared, the second swaps the roles of source and target. (Transposition does preserve the *Fitting ideals*, since $\det C = \det C^{\mathsf T}$, but that is a separate invariance, recorded on [[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal]]; it is not an instance of matrix equivalence.)

**Is NOT an instance (over a general ring): an invertible matrix that is not a product of elementaries.** Over a commutative ring $R$ that is not a PID there can exist $Q \in \mathrm{GL}_n(R)$ that is *not* a product of elementary matrices. For such $Q$, the matrix $QA$ satisfies $QA = Q\,A\,I^{-1}$ — it has the algebraic form $QAT^{-1}$ — yet $QA$ may **not** be reachable from $A$ by a sequence of elementary row operations. This shows the converse of the algebraic characterisation genuinely *requires* a hypothesis like "Euclidean domain"; the two notions of equivalence diverge over a bad ring.

**Corollary (equivalence is generated by reversible moves, hence an equivalence relation).** Because each of (ER1)–(ER3) and (EC1)–(EC3) is realised by an *invertible* matrix, each has an inverse operation of the same kind. Reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity of $\sim$ follow immediately. *Calibration check:* if you can name the inverse operation of each of the six (subtract instead of add; swap again; scale by $c^{-1}$), you have understood why the unit hypothesis in (ER3)/(EC3) is not optional.

**Corollary (equivalence preserves invertibility).** If $A$ is square and $A \sim B$, then $A$ is invertible if and only if $B$ is, since $B = QAT^{-1}$ with $Q, T$ invertible. More finely, equivalence preserves every quantity that is unchanged by left and right multiplication by invertible matrices — in particular the [[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal|Fitting ideals]] $\operatorname{Fit}_k$, by [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]]. *Calibration check:* this is the bridge from "elementary operations" to "invariants" — anything you can prove is preserved by a single elementary operation is automatically an invariant of the whole equivalence class.

> [!tip] Smith Normal Form *(from Modules II — §3.3)*
> Elementary operations are the moves; the question they pose is "what is the simplest matrix in each equivalence class?" Over a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] the answer is the **Smith normal form**: every matrix is equivalent to a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$ with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$. See [[Thm - Smith Normal Form]].

> [!tip] Fitting Ideals are Invariants *(from Modules II — §3.3)*
> To know the Smith normal form is *unique* one needs a quantity preserved by every elementary operation. Checking each operation in turn shows the [[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal|Fitting ideals]] are unchanged, so they are invariants of the equivalence class and pin down the $d_k$. See [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]].

> [!tip] Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain *(from Modules II — §3.3)*
> Reading the columns of $A$ as relations among generators, the geometric meaning of column operations (change generators) and row operations (change ambient basis) turns the Smith normal form of a presentation matrix into a direct-sum decomposition of the module. See [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain]].

> [!tip] Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain *(from Modules II — §3.3)*
> Because column operations change the generators of a submodule $N \le R^m$ and row operations change the basis of $R^m$, the Smith normal form produces a basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ with $N$ generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ — so every submodule of a free module is free. See [[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain]].
