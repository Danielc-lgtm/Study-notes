# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a commutative ring with $1$ (for the sharp results, a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] or a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]]). $A = (A_{ij})$ is an $m \times n$ matrix over $R$. For an integer $k$ with $1 \le k \le \min(m,n)$, a **$k \times k$ submatrix** of $A$ is obtained by choosing a $k$-element set of row indices $I = \{i_1 < \cdots < i_k\} \subseteq \{1, \dots, m\}$ and a $k$-element set of column indices $J = \{j_1 < \cdots < j_k\} \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$ and keeping only the entries $A_{ij}$ with $i \in I$, $j \in J$; it is written $A[I, J] \in M_k(R)$. The **determinant** $\det : M_k(R) \to R$ is the usual Leibniz-formula determinant, $\det B = \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{t} B_{t,\sigma(t)}$, defined for matrices over any commutative ring. An **ideal** $I \trianglelefteq R$ is an additive subgroup closed under multiplication by every element of $R$; the ideal **generated by** a subset $X \subseteq R$, written $(X)$, is the smallest ideal containing $X$, namely the set of finite sums $\sum_t r_t x_t$ with $r_t \in R$, $x_t \in X$. The full notation registry lives on the parent page [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]].

# Axiom Motivation

The goal is sharply posed by the [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]]. That theorem produces, for any matrix $A$ over a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]], an equivalent diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$ with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ — but the *algorithm* that builds it makes choices, and a reader who runs it twice may get different $d_k$. The source flags this honestly: multiply a whole row by $-1$ and the diagonal entries change. So the $d_k$ as the algorithm spits them out are not manifestly **invariants** of $A$. We need a quantity that (i) is computable directly from $A$, with no algorithm and no choices, and (ii) is provably unchanged by every [[Def - Elementary Operations and Equivalent Matrices|elementary operation]], hence constant on each equivalence class, and (iii) determines the $d_k$ up to the unavoidable unit-multiple ambiguity. Reverse-engineer the definition from these three demands and you arrive at the Fitting [[Def - Ideal|ideals]].

Start with demand (ii), invariance under elementary operations, because it is the most restrictive. An elementary row operation is left multiplication $A \mapsto QA$ by an invertible matrix; a column operation is right multiplication $A \mapsto AT^{-1}$. What functions of a matrix survive multiplication by invertible matrices? The determinant is the prototype — $\det(QAT^{-1}) = \det Q \cdot \det A \cdot \det(T^{-1})$, and when $Q, T^{-1}$ are invertible their [[Def - Determinant|determinants]] are units, so $\det A$ changes only by a unit. That is encouraging, but $\det$ is defined only for *square* matrices and only sees the whole matrix at once; it cannot distinguish $\operatorname{diag}(1, 6)$ from $\operatorname{diag}(2, 3)$ (both have determinant $6$), yet these have different invariant factors. We need determinant-like data at *every size* $k$, not just $k = m = n$. The fix is to look at the [[Def - Determinant|determinants]] of all the $k \times k$ **submatrices** — the **$k \times k$ minors**. There are many of them (one for each choice of $k$ rows and $k$ columns), and individually a single minor is *not* invariant: a row operation that adds row $i$ into a row $j$ lying in the chosen submatrix, with row $i$ *outside* it, genuinely changes that minor's value. So a single minor will not serve demand (ii).

Here is the decisive move, and it is the only nonobvious step in the whole definition. A single minor is not invariant, but the *collection* of all $k \times k$ minors transforms in a controlled way: under the row operation just described, the new minor equals $\det C + c \det D$, where $\det C$ and $\det D$ are both minors of the *old* matrix $A$ (this is the cofactor expansion along the altered row — see the collapsible note below). The new minor is therefore an $R$-linear combination of old minors. That is exactly the closure property an **ideal** has: [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] are closed under addition and under multiplication by [[Def - Ring|ring]] elements. So although no individual minor is invariant, the **ideal generated by all the $k \times k$ minors** is preserved: every new minor lands inside the ideal of old minors, and since elementary operations are reversible the containment goes both ways and the ideals are equal. The ideal is the right object precisely because "$R$-linear combination of minors" is the operation an elementary row/column operation performs on minors, and an ideal is by definition the structure closed under that operation. This is why the definition packages the minors into an ideal rather than, say, taking their greatest common divisor or their list: the ideal is the smallest structure making demand (ii) provable, and over a PID it *equals* the gcd (the gcd is a generator of the ideal), so nothing is lost.

Now check demands (i) and (iii). Demand (i), no-choices computability, is immediate: $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ is read straight off $A$ by listing every $k \times k$ submatrix, taking determinants, and generating an ideal — there is no algorithm with branch points. Demand (iii), recovering the $d_k$, is the payoff. For a diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r)$ with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$, the only nonzero $k \times k$ minors come from choosing $k$ of the diagonal entries, and such a minor is a product $d_{i_1} \cdots d_{i_k}$; the divisibility chain forces the *smallest* such product to be $d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k$ (taking the first $k$ diagonal entries), and it divides all the others. Hence $\operatorname{Fit}_k = (d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k)$ for a Smith-normal-form matrix, and by invariance the same holds for *any* matrix equivalent to it. From the nested ideals one recovers each invariant factor by division: $d_k$ is a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ divided by a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_{k-1}$, which pins $d_k$ down up to a unit — exactly the ambiguity the source warned was unavoidable.

Test the calibration. *Weaken* the definition — keep the *list* of minors, or their *set*, instead of the ideal they generate. Then demand (ii) fails: the list of minors of $QA$ is a different list from the list of minors of $A$ (the example $\det C + c\det D$ is a new value not on the old list), so the list is not an invariant. Only after closing up under addition and $R$-multiplication — passing to the ideal — does invariance hold. *Strengthen* the definition — replace the ideal generated by *all* $k \times k$ minors by, say, the ideal generated by the **principal** $k\times k$ minors (those using rows $I$ equal to columns $J$). Then invariance fails again: an elementary operation does not preserve the property "this submatrix is principal," so the smaller ideal is not closed under the operations and is not an invariant. The definition is pinned exactly at "the ideal generated by **all** $k \times k$ minors": all of them, so that the linear-combination closure has every minor available; an ideal, so that the closure is realised. Finally note the *indexing* must run over all $k$ from $1$ to $\min(m,n)$: a single $k$ recovers only $d_1 \cdots d_k$; you need the whole nested chain $\operatorname{Fit}_1 \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_2 \subseteq \cdots$ to recover each individual $d_k$ by successive division.

> [!note]- The cofactor-expansion identity that forces the ideal
> Let $B$ be the result of adding $c$ times row $i$ of $A$ to row $j$ ($i \ne j$). Fix a $k \times k$ minor of $A$, the determinant of the submatrix $C = A[I, J]$. The corresponding submatrix $C'$ of $B$ depends on which rows lie in $I$. **If $j \notin I$**: row $j$ is not in the submatrix, so $C' = C$ and the minor is unchanged. **If $j \in I$ and $i \in I$**: $C'$ is obtained from $C$ by adding $c$ times one of its own rows to another, an operation that does not change a determinant, so $\det C' = \det C$. **If $j \in I$ but $i \notin I$**: $C'$ agrees with $C$ except in its $j$-row, which is now $(C_{j1} + c f_1, \dots, C_{jk} + c f_k)$ where $(f_1, \dots, f_k)$ are the entries of row $i$ of $A$ in the columns $J$. Expanding $\det C'$ along that row by multilinearity gives $\det C' = \det C + c \det D$, where $D$ is $C$ with its $j$-row replaced by $(f_1, \dots, f_k)$. Crucially $D = A[I', J]$ where $I'$ replaces $i$ for $j$ — so $\det D$ is *also* a $k \times k$ minor of $A$. Hence $\det C' = \det C + c\det D$ is an $R$-linear combination of $k \times k$ minors of $A$, i.e. $\det C' \in \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$. Row swaps change a minor by a sign, scalings by a unit; both keep the minor inside $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$. Since transposition leaves determinants and hence Fitting ideals unchanged, column operations reduce to row operations. So every minor of $B$ lies in $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$, giving $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$; reversibility gives the opposite containment. This is the proof of [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]]; it is also the precise reason the *ideal*, and nothing smaller, is the invariant.

# The Definition

This is a compound definition: it introduces the **minor** of a matrix and then the **Fitting ideal**. Fix a commutative [[Def - Ring|ring]] $R$ with $1$ and an $m \times n$ matrix $A = (A_{ij})$ over $R$.

## Minor

Let $k$ be an integer with $1 \le k \le \min(m, n)$. A **$k \times k$ minor** of $A$ is the determinant of a $k \times k$ **submatrix** of $A$ — a matrix obtained from $A$ by deleting all but $k$ of the rows and all but $k$ of the columns. Concretely, choose a set of row indices $I = \{i_1 < \cdots < i_k\} \subseteq \{1, \dots, m\}$ and a set of column indices $J = \{j_1 < \cdots < j_k\} \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$, each of size $k$; the resulting submatrix is $A[I, J] = (A_{i_r j_s})_{1 \le r, s \le k} \in M_k(R)$, and the associated minor is the ring element
$$\det A[I, J] \ \in\ R.$$
A matrix has many minors of each size — one for every pair $(I, J)$ of index sets — because we are free to choose which rows and columns to discard. The number of $k \times k$ minors is $\binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{k}$. (When $k > \min(m,n)$ there are no $k \times k$ submatrices, and by convention the empty product gives a single $0 \times 0$ minor equal to $1$ at $k = 0$.)

## Fitting ideal

For each $k$, the **$k$-th Fitting ideal** of $A$, written $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$, is the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] of $R$ generated by the set of all $k \times k$ minors of $A$:
$$\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) \ =\ \big(\, \det A[I, J] \ :\ I \subseteq \{1,\dots,m\},\ J \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\},\ |I| = |J| = k \,\big) \ \trianglelefteq\ R.$$
Equivalently, $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ is the set of all finite $R$-linear combinations $\sum_t r_t \cdot \det A[I_t, J_t]$ of $k \times k$ minors, with coefficients $r_t \in R$. By convention $\operatorname{Fit}_0(A) = R$ (the unit ideal), and $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = (0)$ for $k > \min(m, n)$, since there are then no $k \times k$ minors.

**The chain of Fitting ideals.** The Fitting ideals are *nested decreasingly*:
$$R \ =\ \operatorname{Fit}_0(A) \ \supseteq\ \operatorname{Fit}_1(A) \ \supseteq\ \operatorname{Fit}_2(A) \ \supseteq\ \cdots\ \supseteq\ \operatorname{Fit}_{\min(m,n)}(A) \ \supseteq\ (0).$$
The containment $\operatorname{Fit}_{k+1}(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ holds because each $(k+1)\times(k+1)$ minor, expanded by cofactors along any row, is an $R$-linear combination of $k \times k$ minors, hence lies in $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$, which is closed under $R$-linear combinations.

**Key fact — invariance, proved on the theorem page.** Equivalent matrices have **equal** Fitting ideals:
$$A \sim B \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(B) \quad \text{for every } k.$$
This is [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]]. It holds even though equivalent matrices typically have completely different *individual* minors; the *ideal* survives because each elementary operation sends a minor to an $R$-linear combination of minors. Consequently the Fitting ideals are invariants of the equivalence class of $A$ — invariants of the homomorphism $A : R^n \to R^m$ up to change of basis, equivalently of the [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] $\operatorname{im}(A)$ up to change of generators and ambient basis.

**Key fact — recovering the invariant factors.** If $A$ is over a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] with [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$, where $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$, then
$$\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) \ =\ (d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k) \qquad (1 \le k \le r), \qquad \operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = (0)\ \ (k > r).$$
Since each invariant factor satisfies $d_k = (d_1\cdots d_k)/(d_1 \cdots d_{k-1})$, it follows that **$d_k$ is determined up to multiplication by a unit** as a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ divided by a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_{k-1}(A)$. The Fitting ideals thus pin down the Smith normal form, proving the invariant factors are genuinely invariant (up to associates), which the bare algorithm did not make evident.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The Fitting ideal is the **rank stratification of a matrix, made into ideal-theoretic data over a ring.** Over a field $k$, the $k$-th Fitting ideal of a matrix $A$ is either the unit ideal $(1)$ or the zero ideal $(0)$: it is $(1)$ exactly when *some* $k \times k$ minor is nonzero, i.e. when $\operatorname{rank} A \ge k$, and $(0)$ otherwise. So over a field the chain $\operatorname{Fit}_0 \supseteq \operatorname{Fit}_1 \supseteq \cdots$ is $(1), \dots, (1), (0), \dots, (0)$, with the drop from $(1)$ to $(0)$ occurring exactly after $\operatorname{Fit}_r$ where $r = \operatorname{rank} A$ — the Fitting ideals encode nothing beyond the rank. The classical fact that *the rank of a matrix over a field equals the largest $k$ for which some $k \times k$ minor is nonzero* is precisely the statement "$\operatorname{Fit}_k \ne (0) \iff k \le \operatorname{rank}$." Over a ring the Fitting ideals refine this: they record not merely *whether* a $k \times k$ minor is nonzero but the full ideal those minors generate, and that extra arithmetic — the actual elements $d_1 \cdots d_k$, not just zero-or-nonzero — is the invariant-factor information that has no field-level counterpart.

In commutative algebra, the Fitting ideals are the **Fitting ideals of the cokernel module**, and this is their true home. If $A : R^n \to R^m$ is a homomorphism with cokernel $M = R^m / \operatorname{im}(A)$ — so $A$ is a *presentation matrix* of $M$, its columns the relations among $m$ generators — then $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ depends only on the module $M$, not on the chosen presentation. The reason is exactly the invariance theorem: two presentation matrices of the same module are related by elementary operations together with stabilisation (adding trivial generators and relations), and Fitting ideals are unchanged by all of these. One therefore writes $\operatorname{Fit}_k(M)$ for the Fitting ideal of *the module*. This makes the Fitting ideals a genuine **module invariant**, computable from any presentation, and they detect structural features: $M$ can be generated by $j$ elements if and only if $\operatorname{Fit}_{m-j}(A) = R$, and the **annihilator** of $M$ satisfies $\operatorname{Fit}_0(M) \subseteq \operatorname{Ann}(M)$ with equality up to radical. The Smith-normal-form computation of §3.3 is the special case "$R$ a Euclidean domain", where the structure theorem makes everything explicit and the Fitting ideals become the principal ideals $(d_1 \cdots d_k)$.

In algebraic geometry the same construction reappears as **determinantal ideals and Fitting-ideal schemes**. Given a matrix of functions (a matrix over a polynomial or coordinate ring), the locus where its rank drops below $k$ is cut out by the vanishing of all $k \times k$ minors — that is, by the Fitting ideal $\operatorname{Fit}_k$. The Fitting ideals thus stratify a space by the rank of a varying linear map, and the *Fitting ideal scheme* $V(\operatorname{Fit}_k)$ is the natural scheme structure on a degeneracy locus. The minors-generate-an-ideal pattern is one and the same in three guises: the invariant factors of a matrix over $\mathbb{Z}$, the structure of a module over a ring, and the degeneracy loci of a map of vector bundles.

A caution on what the Fitting ideal *forgets*. The Fitting ideals capture the invariant factors $d_k$ but **not** the rank $r$ as an independent number in the way one might first guess — $r$ is recovered as the largest $k$ with $\operatorname{Fit}_k \ne (0)$, which is fine — and they capture a finitely generated module over a PID *completely* (the $d_k$ plus the free rank determine the module up to isomorphism). But over a more general ring two non-isomorphic modules can share all Fitting ideals; the Fitting ideals are a *complete* invariant only in favourable cases (such as finitely generated modules over a PID). They are best thought of as the *strongest easily-computable* invariant, exact when the structure theorem applies and a useful approximation otherwise.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: minors of a $2 \times 2$ integer matrix.** Take $A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ over $\mathbb{Z}$. The $1 \times 1$ minors are the four entries $2, -1, 1, 2$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_1(A) = (2, -1, 1, 2) = (1)$ — the unit ideal, because the entry $1$ already generates everything. The only $2 \times 2$ minor is $\det A = 2\cdot 2 - (-1)\cdot 1 = 5$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_2(A) = (5)$. The chain is $(1) \supseteq (5) \supseteq (0)$, and reading off invariant factors: $d_1$ generates $\operatorname{Fit}_1 = (1)$ so $d_1 = \pm 1$, and $d_1 d_2$ generates $\operatorname{Fit}_2 = (5)$ so $d_1 d_2 = \pm 5$, giving $d_2 = \pm 5$. The Smith normal form is $\operatorname{diag}(1, 5)$ — matching the elementary-operation computation on [[Def - Elementary Operations and Equivalent Matrices]], and obtained here with no algorithm at all.

**Is an instance: a $3 \times 3$ presentation matrix.** Let $X = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 1 & 5 \\ 3 & 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 0 & 7\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ over $\mathbb{Z}$, the presentation matrix of the abelian [[Def - Group|group]] $A = \mathbb{Z}^3 / \langle (2,3,1), (1,2,0), (5,6,7)\rangle$. The entry $1$ in position $(3,2)$ is a $1 \times 1$ minor, so $\operatorname{Fit}_1(X) = (1, \dots) = (1)$ and $d_1 = 1$. Among the $2 \times 2$ minors, the top-left $\det\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 3 & 2\end{smallmatrix}\right) = 4 - 3 = 1$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_2(X) = (1, \dots) = (1)$ and $d_1 d_2 = \pm 1$, giving $d_2 = 1$. The single $3 \times 3$ minor is $\det X = 3$ (expand: $2(14-0) - 1(21-6) + 5(0-2) = 28 - 15 - 10 = 3$), so $\operatorname{Fit}_3(X) = (3)$ and $d_3 = 3$. Hence $A \cong \mathbb{Z}/1 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/1 \oplus \mathbb{Z}/3 \cong \mathbb{Z}/3$. The Fitting ideals decided the structure without ever forming the Smith normal form — and the worked shortcut (spotting one small minor equal to a unit immediately collapses $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ to $(1)$) is the practical reason the definition is useful.

**Is an instance: the Smith normal form itself.** For $B = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0)$ with $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$, the only nonzero $k \times k$ minors come from choosing $k$ rows and the *same* $k$ columns among the first $r$ (any other choice hits a zero row or column), and each equals a product $d_{i_1}\cdots d_{i_k}$ of $k$ distinct diagonal entries. The divisibility chain $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots$ makes $d_1 d_2 \cdots d_k$ (the product of the *first* $k$) divide every such product, so it generates the ideal: $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B) = (d_1 \cdots d_k)$. This is the computation behind the key fact, and it shows why the divisibility condition $d_1 \mid \cdots \mid d_r$ in the Smith normal form is exactly what makes the Fitting ideals *principal with the clean generator* $d_1\cdots d_k$.

**Is NOT an instance: a single chosen minor is not an invariant.** The number $\det A[I, J]$ for a *fixed* pair $(I, J)$ is **not** invariant under equivalence. Concretely, over $\mathbb{Z}$ the matrix $A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ has top-left $1 \times 1$ minor $A_{11} = 1$; subtracting row $1$ from row $2$ gives the equivalent $B = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with top-left minor still $1$, but adding row $2$ to row $1$ gives $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with top-left $1 \times 1$ minor now $2$. The individual minor changed. Only the *ideal* $\operatorname{Fit}_1 = (1)$ is stable across all three. This non-instance is the whole reason the definition takes an ideal and not a number.

**Is NOT an instance: the set or list of minors is not an invariant.** Equivalent matrices generally have different *collections* of minors. For $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ the $1\times1$ minors are $\{2, 0, 0, 3\}$ and the $2 \times 2$ minor is $6$; an elementary operation produces an equivalent matrix such as $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 3\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ whose $1 \times 1$ minors are $\{2, 0, 2, 3\}$ — a different set. The lists differ; the ideals $\operatorname{Fit}_1 = (2,3) = (1)$ and $\operatorname{Fit}_2 = (6)$ agree. Demand (ii) of the motivation fails for the list and holds only after closing up into the ideal.

**Is NOT an instance: $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ for $k$ exceeding the size.** If $A$ is $m \times n$ and $k > \min(m, n)$, there is no $k \times k$ submatrix, so $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = (0)$ by convention — it carries no information and is not a genuine instance of the "ideal generated by minors" beyond the trivial one. Likewise $\operatorname{Fit}_0(A) = R$ always. The informative Fitting ideals are exactly $\operatorname{Fit}_1, \dots, \operatorname{Fit}_{\min(m,n)}$.

**Corollary (Fitting ideals form a decreasing chain).** $\operatorname{Fit}_{k+1}(A) \subseteq \operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ for every $k$, because cofactor expansion writes any $(k+1)\times(k+1)$ minor as an $R$-linear combination of $k \times k$ minors. *Calibration check:* if you can carry out one cofactor expansion and see that every term is a $k \times k$ minor times a ring element, you have verified the chain — and seen the same mechanism that proves invariance.

**Corollary (invariant factors are unique up to associates).** Combining the invariance $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(B)$ for $A \sim B$ with the formula $\operatorname{Fit}_k = (d_1 \cdots d_k)$, the product $d_1 \cdots d_k$ is determined up to a unit by the equivalence class of $A$, hence so is each $d_k = (d_1\cdots d_k)/(d_1 \cdots d_{k-1})$. *Calibration check:* this resolves the source's worry that "multiply a row by $-1$ changes the $d_k$" — it changes them only by the unit $-1$, exactly the associate ambiguity, and nothing more. This is [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]].

**Corollary (rank from Fitting ideals).** The rank $r$ of the Smith normal form — equivalently the rank of $A$ as a homomorphism — is the largest $k$ with $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) \ne (0)$. *Calibration check:* over a field every nonzero $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ is $(1)$, and this corollary recovers the classical "rank = size of the largest nonvanishing minor."

> [!tip] Fitting Ideals are Invariants *(from Modules II — §3.3)*
> The whole point of packaging minors into an ideal is that the ideal survives every [[Def - Elementary Operations and Equivalent Matrices|elementary operation]]: $A \sim B$ implies $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = \operatorname{Fit}_k(B)$. This is what makes the invariant factors genuine invariants. See [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]].

> [!tip] Smith Normal Form *(from Modules II — §3.3)*
> Fitting ideals give the *uniqueness* half of the Smith normal form: the existence half is the elimination algorithm, but only $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = (d_1 \cdots d_k)$ shows the diagonal entries are forced. See [[Thm - Smith Normal Form]].

> [!tip] Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain *(from Modules II — §3.3)*
> Reading $A$ as a presentation matrix of a module $M$, the Fitting ideals $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A) = (d_1\cdots d_k)$ are invariants of $M$ itself, and the $d_k$ are exactly the orders of the cyclic torsion summands $R/(d_k)$ in the decomposition of $M$. See [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain]].
