# Problem Statement

Work over the Euclidean domain $\mathbb{Z}$, with Euclidean function $\varphi(n)=|n|$.

1. Put the $2\times 2$ matrix
$$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$$
into **Smith normal form** by a sequence of elementary row and column operations, recording each move. State the invariant factors $d_1,d_2$. Then verify the answer independently by computing the Fitting [[Def - Ideal|ideals]]: confirm $\operatorname{Fit}_1(A)=(d_1)$ and $\operatorname{Fit}_2(A)=(d_1 d_2)$.

2. Do the same for the $3\times 3$ matrix
$$B=\begin{pmatrix}2&4&4\\-6&6&12\\10&4&16\end{pmatrix}$$
over $\mathbb{Z}$: reduce it to Smith normal form $\operatorname{diag}(d_1,d_2,d_3)$ with $d_1\mid d_2\mid d_3$, and cross-check $d_1\cdots d_k$ against $\operatorname{Fit}_k(B)$ for $k=1,2,3$.

The point of the exercise is purely mechanical fluency: to run the Smith normal form algorithm by hand and to use the Fitting [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] as a built-in error check, since they are computable directly from the original matrix without doing any reduction.

**Recall:**

The three elementary row operations on a matrix over a [[Def - Ring|ring]] $R$ are: add an $R$-multiple of one row to another; swap two rows; multiply a row by a *unit* of $R$. The three elementary *column* operations are the same with "row" replaced by "column". Each corresponds to multiplication by an invertible matrix — row operations on the left, column operations on the right.

![[Def - Elementary Operations and Equivalent Matrices#The Definition]]

Two matrices are **equivalent** when one is reachable from the other by such operations; equivalently $B=QAT^{-1}$ for invertible $Q,T$.

![[Thm - Smith Normal Form#Statement]]

The non-zero diagonal entries $d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_r$ produced are the **invariant factors** of $A$. They are determined only up to multiplication by units of $R$ — over $\mathbb{Z}$, up to sign — so we conventionally take them positive.

The verification tool is the Fitting ideal. A **$k\times k$ minor** of $A$ is the determinant of a $k\times k$ submatrix (choose any $k$ rows and any $k$ columns and delete the rest). The **$k$-th Fitting ideal** $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ is the ideal of $R$ generated by *all* $k\times k$ minors of $A$.

![[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal#The Definition]]

By [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|the invariance of Fitting ideals]], equivalent matrices have equal Fitting ideals, and for a matrix in Smith normal form $\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_r,0,\dots)$ the only minors that matter are the diagonal ones, giving
$$\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)=(d_1 d_2\cdots d_k).$$
Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain each $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ is principal, generated by the $\gcd$ of all $k\times k$ minors; dividing a generator of $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ by one of $\operatorname{Fit}_{k-1}$ recovers $d_k$ up to a unit.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *normal-form computation* — the most routine problem class for [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4|§3.3]]. There is a guaranteed terminating algorithm; the only question is executing it cleanly and not making an arithmetic slip. The Fitting-ideal cross-check exists precisely because the algorithm has many bookkeeping steps where a sign or a multiple can go wrong.

**Assumption pattern.** The hypothesis is just "a concrete matrix over $\mathbb{Z}$". The single feature that the algorithm exploits is that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]]: the Euclidean function $\varphi(n)=|n|$ lets us, whenever an entry is not divisible by the pivot, run the division algorithm to *strictly shrink* $\varphi$ of the pivot. That strict decrease is what guarantees termination.

**Theorem routing.** [[Thm - Smith Normal Form]] guarantees the target form exists and tells us *how* to reach it (the three-move algorithm). [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]] supplies the independent check: because $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ is unchanged by every elementary operation, we can compute $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ from the *original* matrix and compare with $d_1\cdots d_k$ read off the *final* matrix; agreement confirms the reduction.

**Key decision point.** The algorithm has a subtle part — step (iii) of [[Thm - Smith Normal Form]] — that is easy to skip. After clearing the first row and column you get $\operatorname{diag}(d,\,C)$, and it is tempting to just recurse on $C$. But that yields a *diagonal* matrix, not necessarily one with the divisibility chain $d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots$. If some entry of $C$ is not divisible by $d$, you must first add a row of $C$ into the pivot row to drag a non-multiple of $d$ into the pivot, shrink the pivot, and re-clear. Recognising when this extra step is needed — and when it is not — is the one place where attention is required.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Pivot placement by swaps.** Use a row swap and a column swap (operations ER2, EC2) to move a chosen non-zero entry into the $(1,1)$ position, so it can serve as the pivot.

2. **Euclidean reduction of the pivot.** When an entry $A_{1j}$ in the pivot row (or $A_{i1}$ in the pivot column) is not divisible by the pivot $A_{11}$, write $A_{1j}=qA_{11}+r$ by the division algorithm, subtract $q$ times the pivot column from column $j$ (operation EC1) to place $r$ in position $(1,j)$, then swap it into the pivot. The Euclidean value $\varphi$ of the pivot strictly drops.

3. **Clearing a row and column by the pivot.** Once the pivot divides every entry of its row and column, subtract the appropriate multiples of the pivot column from the other columns (EC1) and of the pivot row from the other rows (ER1) to zero out the rest of the first row and column.

4. **Enforcing divisibility (step iii).** If after clearing, the residual block $C$ has an entry not divisible by the pivot $d$, add the offending row/column into the pivot's, reduce the pivot again, and re-clear — repeating until $d$ divides all of $C$.

5. **Recursion on the residual block.** Apply the whole procedure to the smaller matrix $C$ sitting in rows and columns $2,\dots,n$; the divisibility chain is then automatic.

6. **Sign normalisation by a unit multiple.** Multiply a row or column by the unit $-1$ (operation ER3/EC3) to make each invariant factor positive — legal because $-1$ is a unit of $\mathbb{Z}$.

7. **Verification via Fitting ideals.** Compute $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ from the original matrix as the $\gcd$ of all $k\times k$ minors, and check $\operatorname{Fit}_k=(d_1\cdots d_k)$ against the reduced form.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Both reductions follow the same recipe: get a non-zero entry into the top-left corner, then use it as a pivot to kill everything else in its row and column. The cheapest start is to hunt for an entry equal to $\pm 1$, or an operation that creates one — a pivot of $1$ divides everything, so no Euclidean reduction is then needed. In matrix $A$, the entry $1$ is already present; in matrix $B$, every entry is even, so a $1$ will *not* appear immediately and the first invariant factor will not be $1$.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> For the $3\times 3$ matrix $B$, once you have cleared the first row and column with the pivot $d_1$, you are left with $\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\,C)$ for a $2\times 2$ block $C$. Before recursing on $C$, check: does $d_1$ divide *every* entry of $C$? If yes, just reduce $C$. If no, you must perform step (iii) of [[Thm - Smith Normal Form]] — add a row of $C$ into the pivot row to bring a non-multiple of $d_1$ into play, shrink the pivot, and re-clear.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Use the Fitting ideals as a *predictor*, not just a checker. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, $\operatorname{Fit}_k(A)$ is the ideal generated by the $\gcd$ of all $k\times k$ minors. So $d_1=\gcd$ of all entries; $d_1 d_2=\gcd$ of all $2\times 2$ minors; $d_1 d_2 d_3=\det$ (for a $3\times 3$ matrix, the only $3\times 3$ minor). Compute these *first* — they tell you the answer before you do any reduction, so you know what you are aiming at.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Concretely for $B$: every entry is even, so $\gcd$ of entries $=2$, giving $d_1=2$. The determinant is $\det B=2\cdot(96-48)-4\cdot(-96-120)+4\cdot(-24-60)=96+864-336=624$, so $d_1 d_2 d_3=624$. The $\gcd$ of the $2\times 2$ minors works out to $4$, so $d_1 d_2=4$, hence $d_2=2$ and $d_3=624/4=156$. The Smith normal form of $B$ is $\operatorname{diag}(2,2,156)$ — now reproduce it by row and column operations.

# Solution

The strategy is identical in both parts: run the Smith normal form algorithm, then confirm with Fitting ideals. The Fitting ideals are computed independently of the reduction, so agreement is a genuine check.

## Part 1 — the matrix $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$

**Step 1: Reduce $A$ to Smith normal form by elementary operations.**

A short sequence of one column operation and one row operation gives $\operatorname{diag}(1,5)$, so the invariant factors are $d_1=1,\ d_2=5$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> We never need a Euclidean division here, because a pivot of $1$ appears immediately. Write each step as a column or row operation.
>
> Start:
> $$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.$$
>
> **(EC1) Add column $2$ to column $1$.** New column $1$ is $(2+(-1),\,1+2)=(1,3)$:
> $$\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\3&2\end{pmatrix}.$$
> The $(1,1)$ entry is now $1$, which divides every other entry, so no Euclidean reduction is needed.
>
> **(EC1) Add column $1$ to column $2$.** This clears the $(1,2)$ entry: new column $2$ is $(-1+1,\,2+3)=(0,5)$:
> $$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\3&5\end{pmatrix}.$$
>
> **(ER1) Subtract $3$ times row $1$ from row $2$.** This clears the $(2,1)$ entry: new row $2$ is $(3-3\cdot 1,\,5-3\cdot 0)=(0,5)$:
> $$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&5\end{pmatrix}.$$
>
> The result is diagonal with $1\mid 5$, so it is already in Smith normal form. The invariant factors are
> $$d_1=1,\qquad d_2=5.$$
> This matches the source-text computation, which used the move sequence $\text{EC1},\text{EC1},\text{ER1}$ on the equivalent matrix $\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$.

**Step 2: Verify via Fitting ideals.**

The $1\times 1$ minors of $A$ are its entries, whose $\gcd$ is $1$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_1(A)=(1)=(d_1)$. The single $2\times 2$ minor is $\det A=5$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_2(A)=(5)=(d_1 d_2)$. Both agree with Step 1.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By [[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants|the invariance of Fitting ideals]], $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ is unchanged by elementary operations, so we may compute it from the *original* $A$ and compare with the diagonal form.
>
> **First Fitting ideal.** The $1\times 1$ minors are the four entries: $2,\,-1,\,1,\,2$. The ideal they generate is
> $$\operatorname{Fit}_1(A)=(2,-1,1,2)=(\gcd(2,1,1,2))=(1).$$
> For the Smith normal form $\operatorname{diag}(1,5)$ the corollary $\operatorname{Fit}_k=(d_1\cdots d_k)$ gives $\operatorname{Fit}_1=(d_1)=(1)$. **Agreement.** So $d_1=\pm 1$, and the positive normalisation is $d_1=1$.
>
> **Second Fitting ideal.** There is only one $2\times 2$ submatrix of a $2\times 2$ matrix, namely $A$ itself, so the only $2\times 2$ minor is
> $$\det A=2\cdot 2-(-1)\cdot 1=4+1=5.$$
> Hence $\operatorname{Fit}_2(A)=(5)$. The corollary gives $\operatorname{Fit}_2=(d_1 d_2)$, so $d_1 d_2=\pm 5$. **Agreement.** Combined with $d_1=1$, this forces $d_2=5$.
>
> The two Fitting ideals have pinned down $d_1=1$ and $d_2=5$ *without performing any reduction at all* — confirming Step 1 independently.

## Part 2 — the matrix $B=\begin{pmatrix}2&4&4\\-6&6&12\\10&4&16\end{pmatrix}$

**Step 3: Predict the answer with Fitting ideals.**

Computing the three Fitting ideals from $B$ directly: $\operatorname{Fit}_1(B)=(2)$, $\operatorname{Fit}_2(B)=(4)$, $\operatorname{Fit}_3(B)=(624)$. Therefore $d_1=2$, $d_2=4/2=2$, $d_3=624/4=156$, and the predicted Smith normal form is $\operatorname{diag}(2,2,156)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **First Fitting ideal.** The $1\times 1$ minors are the nine entries $2,4,4,-6,6,12,10,4,16$. Every one is even and $\gcd$ of $\{2,4,6,10,\dots\}=2$, so
> $$\operatorname{Fit}_1(B)=(2),\qquad d_1=2.$$
>
> **Third Fitting ideal.** The only $3\times 3$ minor is $\det B$. Expanding along the first row,
> $$\det B=2\,(6\cdot 16-12\cdot 4)-4\,((-6)\cdot 16-12\cdot 10)+4\,((-6)\cdot 4-6\cdot 10).$$
> Term by term: $6\cdot 16-12\cdot 4=96-48=48$; $(-6)\cdot 16-12\cdot 10=-96-120=-216$; $(-6)\cdot 4-6\cdot 10=-24-60=-84$. So
> $$\det B=2\cdot 48-4\cdot(-216)+4\cdot(-84)=96+864-336=624.$$
> Hence $\operatorname{Fit}_3(B)=(624)$ and $d_1 d_2 d_3=624$.
>
> **Second Fitting ideal.** $\operatorname{Fit}_2(B)$ is generated by the $\gcd$ of all nine $2\times 2$ minors. A representative sample, choosing two rows and two columns:
> $$\begin{vmatrix}2&4\\-6&6\end{vmatrix}=12+24=36,\qquad \begin{vmatrix}2&4\\-6&12\end{vmatrix}=24+24=48,\qquad \begin{vmatrix}4&4\\6&12\end{vmatrix}=48-24=24,$$
> $$\begin{vmatrix}2&4\\10&4\end{vmatrix}=8-40=-32,\qquad \begin{vmatrix}2&4\\10&16\end{vmatrix}=32-40=-8,\qquad \begin{vmatrix}-6&6\\10&4\end{vmatrix}=-24-60=-84.$$
> The $\gcd$ of $\{36,48,24,32,8,84,\dots\}$ is $\gcd(36,8)=4$, and no minor introduces an odd factor or a factor of $2$ only, so
> $$\operatorname{Fit}_2(B)=(4),\qquad d_1 d_2=4.$$
>
> **Reading off the invariant factors.** From the chain $\operatorname{Fit}_k=(d_1\cdots d_k)$:
> $$d_1=\frac{\gcd\operatorname{Fit}_1}{1}=2,\qquad d_2=\frac{4}{2}=2,\qquad d_3=\frac{624}{4}=156.$$
> Check the divisibility chain: $2\mid 2\mid 156$. Predicted Smith normal form:
> $$\operatorname{diag}(2,2,156).$$

**Step 4: Reproduce $\operatorname{diag}(2,2,156)$ by elementary row and column operations.**

Pull a $2$ into the corner, clear the first row and column to reach $\operatorname{diag}(2,\,C)$ with $2$ dividing $C$, then reduce the $2\times 2$ block $C$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> $$B=\begin{pmatrix}2&4&4\\-6&6&12\\10&4&16\end{pmatrix}.$$
> The $(1,1)$ entry is already $2$, the $\gcd$ of all entries, so it is the correct pivot — no Euclidean reduction needed.
>
> **Clear column $1$.** The pivot $2$ divides $-6$ and $10$.
> - (ER1) Add $3$ times row $1$ to row $2$: new row $2=(-6+6,\,6+12,\,12+12)=(0,18,24)$.
> - (ER1) Subtract $5$ times row $1$ from row $3$: new row $3=(10-10,\,4-20,\,16-20)=(0,-16,-4)$.
> $$\begin{pmatrix}2&4&4\\0&18&24\\0&-16&-4\end{pmatrix}.$$
>
> **Clear row $1$.** The pivot $2$ divides $4$ and $4$.
> - (EC1) Subtract $2$ times column $1$ from column $2$: new column $2$ entries $(4-4,\,18-0,\,-16-0)=(0,18,-16)$.
> - (EC1) Subtract $2$ times column $1$ from column $3$: new column $3$ entries $(4-4,\,24-0,\,-4-0)=(0,24,-4)$.
> $$\begin{pmatrix}2&0&0\\0&18&24\\0&-16&-4\end{pmatrix}.$$
> Now $B$ has the form $\operatorname{diag}\!\big(2,\,C\big)$ with $C=\begin{pmatrix}18&24\\-16&-4\end{pmatrix}$. Every entry of $C$ ($18,24,-16,-4$) is divisible by the pivot $2$, so **step (iii) is not needed** — we may recurse directly on $C$.
>
> **Reduce $C=\begin{pmatrix}18&24\\-16&-4\end{pmatrix}$.** We work inside rows/columns $2,3$.
> - (EC1) Add column $3$ to column $2$ (of $C$): new first column of $C$ is $(18+24,\,-16+(-4))=(42,-20)$. So $C\rightsquigarrow\begin{pmatrix}42&24\\-20&-4\end{pmatrix}$.
> - The entry $-4$ has the smallest $\varphi$-value; swap it into the pivot of $C$. (EC2) swap the two columns of $C$, then (ER2) swap the two rows of $C$: $C\rightsquigarrow\begin{pmatrix}-4&-20\\24&42\end{pmatrix}$.
> - The pivot $-4$ divides $-20$ and $24$ but **not** $42$ ($42=(-4)(-10)+2$). Reduce: (ER1) add $6$ times the pivot row to the other row: new second row $=(24+6(-4),\,42+6(-20))=(0,-78)$. So $C\rightsquigarrow\begin{pmatrix}-4&-20\\0&-78\end{pmatrix}$. Then (EC1) subtract $5$ times column $1$ from column $2$: new second column $=(-20-5(-4),\,-78-0)=(0,-78)$. So $C\rightsquigarrow\begin{pmatrix}-4&0\\0&-78\end{pmatrix}$.
> - (ER3) Multiply each row of $C$ by the unit $-1$ to normalise signs: $C\rightsquigarrow\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&78\end{pmatrix}$.
>
> But $4\nmid 78$, so $\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&78\end{pmatrix}$ is diagonal yet **not** in Smith normal form — this is exactly the situation step (iii) of [[Thm - Smith Normal Form]] handles. Apply it to this $2\times 2$ block:
> - (EC1) Add column $2$ to column $1$: first column $\rightsquigarrow(4,78)$, giving $\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\78&78\end{pmatrix}$.
> - The pivot $4$ does not divide $78$. Reduce: $78=4\cdot 19+2$. (ER1) subtract $19$ times row $1$ from row $2$: row $2\rightsquigarrow(78-76,\,78-0)=(2,78)$, giving $\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\2&78\end{pmatrix}$. The entry $2$ has the smaller $\varphi$; (ER2) swap rows: $\begin{pmatrix}2&78\\4&0\end{pmatrix}$.
> - Pivot is now $2$, which divides $78,4,0$. Clear: (ER1) subtract $2$ times row $1$ from row $2$: row $2\rightsquigarrow(0,-156)$, giving $\begin{pmatrix}2&78\\0&-156\end{pmatrix}$; (EC1) subtract $39$ times column $1$ from column $2$: column $2\rightsquigarrow(0,-156)$, giving $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&-156\end{pmatrix}$.
> - (ER3) multiply row $2$ by the unit $-1$: $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&156\end{pmatrix}$.
>
> Reassembling, the full matrix is now
> $$\operatorname{diag}(2,2,156),$$
> with $2\mid 2\mid 156$, so it is in Smith normal form. This matches the prediction of Step 3 exactly.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Part 1.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$, apply to $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ the operations: add column $2$ to column $1$, add column $1$ to column $2$, subtract $3\times$ row $1$ from row $2$. The result is $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&5\end{pmatrix}$, which is in Smith normal form, so the invariant factors are $d_1=1,\ d_2=5$. Verification: the $1\times 1$ minors $\{2,-1,1,2\}$ have $\gcd 1$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_1(A)=(1)=(d_1)$; the unique $2\times 2$ minor is $\det A=5$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_2(A)=(5)=(d_1 d_2)$. Both Fitting ideals agree with the reduction.
>
> **Part 2.** Over $\mathbb{Z}$, for $B=\begin{pmatrix}2&4&4\\-6&6&12\\10&4&16\end{pmatrix}$: every entry is even with overall $\gcd 2$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_1(B)=(2)$; the $\gcd$ of all $2\times 2$ minors (e.g. $36,48,24,-32,-8,-84$) is $4$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_2(B)=(4)$; the unique $3\times 3$ minor is $\det B=624$, so $\operatorname{Fit}_3(B)=(624)$. From $\operatorname{Fit}_k=(d_1\cdots d_k)$ we get $d_1=2,\ d_2=2,\ d_3=156$. Reduction: with pivot $2$ in the corner, clear the first row and column ($\text{ER1},\text{ER1},\text{EC1},\text{EC1}$) to reach $\operatorname{diag}(2,\,C)$ with $C=\begin{pmatrix}18&24\\-16&-4\end{pmatrix}$, every entry of which is divisible by $2$; reducing $C$ (and applying step (iii) of [[Thm - Smith Normal Form]] once, since the intermediate diagonal $\operatorname{diag}(4,78)$ violates $4\mid 78$) yields $\operatorname{diag}(2,156)$. The full result is $\operatorname{diag}(2,2,156)$, in Smith normal form, matching the Fitting-ideal prediction. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**The Smith normal form algorithm is a guaranteed-terminating procedure, and the Euclidean function is the termination certificate.** Every step that fails to make obvious progress — an entry not divisible by the pivot — is converted, via the division algorithm, into a *strict decrease* of $\varphi$ evaluated at the pivot. Since $\varphi$ takes values in $\mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$, it cannot decrease forever, so the algorithm halts. This is the entire reason the hypothesis "$R$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]]" appears in [[Thm - Smith Normal Form]]: it is not used to *define* the normal form, only to *guarantee you can reach it by hand*. When you run the algorithm, the mental model should be "I am driving the pivot's $\varphi$-value down to its minimum"; once the pivot divides its whole row and column you have hit a local floor, and clearing is then free. The same algorithm runs over any Euclidean domain — $k[X]$, the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — with $\varphi$ being degree or norm instead of absolute value.

**Compute the Fitting ideals first: they are an oracle for the answer, not merely a check.** Because $\operatorname{Fit}_k$ is invariant under every elementary operation ([[Thm - Fitting Ideals are Invariants]]), and equals $(d_1\cdots d_k)$ for a matrix in Smith normal form, the invariant factors are recoverable directly from the original matrix: over a PID, $d_1\cdots d_k=\gcd$ of all $k\times k$ minors, so $d_1=\gcd(\text{entries})$, $d_2=\gcd(2\times 2\text{ minors})/d_1$, and so on. This means you can know the target $\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_r)$ before doing any reduction at all — which is invaluable, because the reduction is long and error-prone, and knowing the destination lets you catch mistakes immediately. The trigger "I must find a Smith normal form" should fire two parallel reactions: *run the algorithm* and *compute the Fitting ideals*, then demand they agree. The general principle: whenever a long computation has a cheaply computable invariant attached to its output, compute the invariant first and use it as a guard rail.

**Hunting for a unit entry short-circuits the hardest part of the algorithm — and its absence is informative.** A pivot equal to a unit ($\pm 1$ over $\mathbb{Z}$) divides everything, so no Euclidean reduction is needed and the first invariant factor is forced to be $1$. In Part 1 the matrix $A$ contained a $1$ outright, making the reduction three trivial moves. In Part 2 the matrix $B$ had *every entry even*, which immediately tells you $d_1=2$ rather than $1$ — the absence of an attainable unit is exactly the statement "$\operatorname{Fit}_1\ne(1)$". So before grinding the algorithm, scan for entries that are units, or one-step combinations producing a unit; if none exist, the $\gcd$ of the entries is your first invariant factor and the whole form is "scaled up" accordingly. This is the matrix-level shadow of the fact, seen in [[Ex - Generating sets that are not bases]], that units are the elements that generate everything.

**Step (iii) — enforcing divisibility — is the one non-obvious move, and it is needed exactly when a residual diagonal violates the chain.** After clearing the first row and column you obtain $\operatorname{diag}(d,\,C)$. If $d$ already divides every entry of $C$ you may recurse immediately (as happened with $C=\begin{pmatrix}18&24\\-16&-4\end{pmatrix}$, all even). But if reduction of a sub-block produces a diagonal like $\operatorname{diag}(4,78)$ with $4\nmid 78$, you are *not done*: a diagonal matrix is in Smith normal form only when the divisibility chain $d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots$ holds. The fix is mechanical — add one column into another to break the diagonal, drag a non-multiple of the pivot into the pivot position, reduce, and re-clear — and it is precisely step (iii) of [[Thm - Smith Normal Form]]. The lesson generalises: "diagonal" and "Smith normal form" are different conditions, and the gap between them is always closed by the add-a-row/column-then-reduce manoeuvre. When a reduction bottoms out at a diagonal, always run the divisibility check before declaring victory.
