# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] — an integral domain carrying a function $\varphi : R \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ that supports division with remainder — and modules are [[Def - Module|R-modules]]. For a finite $m$, the [[Def - Free Module|free module]] $R^m = R \oplus \dots \oplus R$ is the [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules|direct sum]] of $m$ copies of $R$, with elements the $m$-tuples $(r_1, \dots, r_m)$ and coordinatewise $R$-action; the integer $m$ is its **rank**. For $d \in R$, the ideal $(d) = \{rd : r \in R\}$ is the principal ideal generated by $d$, and $R/(d)$ is the corresponding cyclic quotient module. The relation $d \mid d'$ ("$d$ divides $d'$") means $d' = qd$ for some $q \in R$; a chain $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r$ is a sequence in which each term divides the next. The symbol $\cong$ denotes module isomorphism, and $\twoheadrightarrow$ a surjective homomorphism. A module $M$ is [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] if $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_k$ for some finite list $m_1, \dots, m_k \in M$. The $d_i$ produced below are called the **invariant factors** of $M$, and the number $s$ of free summands the **free rank**. The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]].

# Statement

> **Structure Theorem for [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|Finitely Generated Modules]] over a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean Domain]].** Let $R$ be a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] and let $M$ be a [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] $R$-module. Then there exist a non-negative integer $s$ and non-zero, non-unit elements $d_1, d_2, \dots, d_r \in R$, with the divisibility chain
> $$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r,$$
> such that
> $$M \;\cong\; \frac{R}{(d_1)} \oplus \frac{R}{(d_2)} \oplus \dots \oplus \frac{R}{(d_r)} \oplus \underbrace{R \oplus \dots \oplus R}_{s}.$$

Every finitely generated module over a Euclidean domain is therefore a direct sum of cyclic pieces $R/(d_i)$ — the **torsion part** — and a free piece $R^s$ — the **free part**. The chain condition $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r$ is what makes the list of $d_i$ canonical: without it, the same module could be written with the cyclic factors permuted or regrouped, and "the invariant factors" would not be a well-defined notion. The free rank $s$ and the invariant factors $d_i$ (up to multiplication by units) are uniquely determined by $M$; this page proves the existence half — that *some* such decomposition exists — which is the part the lecture notes establish here.

# Motivation

A finitely generated module is, on the face of it, an open-ended object. The definition says only that there is *some* finite list of generators $m_1, \dots, m_k$ with $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_k$, and those generators satisfy *some* collection of relations. Two such descriptions of the same module can look entirely unalike, and given a module presented by generators and relations you cannot, in general, even tell at a glance whether it is the zero module. What is missing is a **normal form**: a canonical, smallest-possible way to write each finitely generated module, computed by a fixed procedure, so that two [[Def - Module|modules]] are isomorphic exactly when their normal forms agree.

This theorem supplies that normal form, and the form it supplies is the simplest one imaginable. It says every finitely generated module over a Euclidean domain is just a direct sum of two kinds of utterly transparent pieces: copies of $R$ itself (the free part), and cyclic quotients $R/(d)$ (the torsion part). There is nothing more exotic than that. A module that looked complicated — generators tangled in relations — is revealed to be a stack of one-generator [[Def - Module|modules]], each either a free copy of $R$ or a single $R/(d)$. The whole apparatus of generators and relations collapses to a short list of [[Def - Ring|ring]] elements $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$ and one integer $s$.

One should expect a result like this precisely because of where the Euclidean structure lives in the prerequisite chain. A finitely generated module is, by the [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|free-quotient theorem]], a quotient $R^m/K$ of a free module by a [[Def - Submodule|submodule]] of relations $K$. The relation submodule $K$ is itself a submodule of the free module $R^m$, and over a Euclidean domain [[Def - Submodule|submodules]] of free modules are extremely well-behaved — the [[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|submodule theorem]] says $K$ is itself free and can be aligned with a basis of $R^m$. Concretely, [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] lets you choose a basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ so that $K$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ along that very basis. Once relations and generators are *simultaneously diagonalised* in this way, the quotient $R^m/K$ splits coordinate by coordinate, and each coordinate contributes either $R/(d_i)$ (a constrained coordinate) or $R$ (an unconstrained one). The decomposition is not a surprise; it is what diagonalisation always gives.

Why care that this normal form exists? Because it is the master theorem from which two famous classifications fall out as special cases, each obtained by choosing the [[Def - Ring|ring]] $R$. Taking $R = \mathbb{Z}$ — where modules are abelian [[Def - Group|groups]] — yields the [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups|classification of finitely generated abelian groups]]: every such group is a product of cyclic [[Def - Group|groups]] and copies of $\mathbb{Z}$. Taking $R = F[X]$ — where a module is a vector space equipped with a linear operator — yields the [[Thm - Rational Canonical Form|rational]] and [[Thm - Jordan Normal Form|Jordan]] canonical forms of matrices. The structure theorem is the single result behind all of it; the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition theorem]] then refines the torsion part further by splitting each $R/(d_i)$ into prime-power pieces.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The literal precondition is a finitely generated module over a Euclidean domain. The skill is recognising when a problem hands you one without saying so.

The first disguised source is **a module presented by generators and relations** — explicitly, a module given as a quotient $R^m/K$ where $K$ is spanned by a finite list of relation vectors. Any such presentation is automatically a finitely generated module: the images of the standard generators of $R^m$ generate the quotient. The non-obvious recognition is that the structure theorem then applies *with the presentation matrix as the input to the algorithm* — you do not need to know anything about $M$ beyond the matrix whose columns are the relations. *Example problem:* an abelian group is defined by three generators subject to three relations; to identify it, assemble the relations into a $3 \times 3$ integer matrix and compute its Smith normal form. This is exactly the worked example carried out on the [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups|abelian-group classification page]].

The second disguised source is **a quotient of a free module of finite rank**. By the [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|free-quotient theorem]], "finitely generated" and "quotient of $R^m$" are the same condition, so any module arising as $R^m/K$ — for instance the cokernel of a homomorphism between free modules — is a legitimate input. The non-obvious step is to recognise the cokernel of a matrix as a finitely generated module and feed the matrix to Smith normal form. *Example problem:* given an $R$-linear map $\alpha : R^n \to R^m$, describe the cokernel $R^m / \operatorname{im}(\alpha)$ — it is governed entirely by the invariant factors of the matrix of $\alpha$.

The third disguised source is **a finite module over a Euclidean domain**, or more generally **a module on which every element is killed by some non-zero ring element** (a torsion module). A finite module is finitely generated — the whole module is a finite generating set — and it is pure torsion, so the structure theorem applies and the free rank $s$ comes out $0$. The non-obvious recognition is that finiteness alone, with no generators named, unlocks the entire classification, forcing $M$ to be a direct sum of cyclic pieces $R/(d_i)$ with no free part. *Example problem:* show that a finite abelian group is a product of cyclic groups — finiteness gives both finite generation and the vanishing of the free rank.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusion is the decomposition $M \cong R/(d_1) \oplus \dots \oplus R/(d_r) \oplus R^s$. Combined with other facts it does more.

Combine the conclusion with **the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules|Chinese remainder theorem]]**. Each invariant factor $d_i$ factors into prime powers in the Euclidean domain $R$ (Euclidean domains are unique factorisation domains), and the Chinese remainder theorem splits the corresponding cyclic module $R/(d_i)$ into a direct sum of prime-power cyclic modules $R/(p^n)$. The further result is the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]]: $M$ is a direct sum of a free module and modules of the single shape $R/(p^n)$. This combination is non-obvious because it trades the *canonical* invariant-factor form for the *finest* prime-power form — the two carry the same information but expose different structure.

Combine the conclusion with **specialisation of the ring $R$**. The theorem is stated for an arbitrary Euclidean domain, so each concrete choice of $R$ produces a concrete classification theorem. With $R = \mathbb{Z}$ the modules are abelian groups and the conclusion is the [[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups|classification of finitely generated abelian groups]]. With $R = F[X]$ for a field $F$, a finitely generated torsion module is a finite-dimensional vector space carrying a linear operator, and the decomposition into cyclic $F[X]$-modules is the [[Thm - Rational Canonical Form|rational canonical form]] of that operator. The further result is that a single proof, run once, yields several of the most-used classification theorems in algebra — the non-obvious payoff is that "classify a module over a PID" subsumes "classify an abelian group" and "classify a matrix up to similarity".

Combine the conclusion with **the well-definedness of rank** ([[Thm - Invariance of Rank|invariance of rank]]). The structure theorem produces *a* decomposition; invariance of rank guarantees that the free rank $s$ — the number of $R$ summands — is an honest invariant, independent of how the decomposition was obtained. The further result is that $s$ becomes a usable certificate: two finitely generated modules with different free ranks are non-isomorphic, decidably and mechanically. This is non-obvious because it converts "are these two modules isomorphic?" — an unbounded search over all possible isomorphisms — into the comparison of two integers (and, with uniqueness of the $d_i$, two short lists).

# Why Is It True

The intuition is a single sentence: **diagonalise the relations, and a quotient that was tangled falls apart coordinate by coordinate.**

Here is the situation, stated plainly. A finitely generated module $M$ is, by the [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|free-quotient theorem]], a quotient $R^m/K$. You should picture $R^m$ as a grid of $m$ free coordinates, and $K$ as the submodule of relations — the things you are declaring to be zero. If $K$ were arbitrary, the quotient could be genuinely complicated, because a relation might tangle several coordinates together: a relation like $(2, 3, 1)$ ties the first, second and third coordinates into one constraint that cannot be read off any single axis. The whole difficulty of understanding $M$ is the difficulty of understanding how $K$ sits inside $R^m$.

The escape is to *change coordinates so that the relations untangle*. This is exactly the move you make in linear algebra to understand a linear map: you pick clever bases so the matrix becomes diagonal, and then the map acts independently on each coordinate. The analogous fact for a submodule $K$ of a free module $R^m$ over a Euclidean domain is the content of the [[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|submodule theorem]], proved via [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]]: there is a *new basis* $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ such that $K$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, d_2 v_2, \dots, d_r v_r$ — each generator of $K$ is a multiple of a single basis vector. The relations have been aligned with the axes. Where a generator of $K$ once tangled three coordinates, in the new basis it touches exactly one.

Why does the Euclidean structure make this possible? Because Smith normal form is produced by *row and column operations* on the matrix whose columns generate $K$, and over a Euclidean domain the division algorithm lets you clear entries: given two entries, divide one by the other with remainder and the strictly smaller remainder lets you make progress, exactly as in the integer or polynomial Euclidean algorithm. Row operations amount to changing the basis of $R^m$; column operations amount to changing the generating set of $K$. Drive the matrix to a diagonal $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r)$ with the divisibility chain $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$, and you have simultaneously found the good basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ and the good generators $d_i v_i$ of $K$. The divisibility chain is forced by the algorithm: the smallest entry produced divides everything, and this cascades.

Now finish. With $K$ generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ along the basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$, the quotient $R^m/K$ decomposes one basis vector at a time. The $i$-th coordinate direction $R v_i$ contributes, to the quotient, the cyclic module $R v_i / (d_i v_i) \cong R/(d_i)$ for $i \leq r$, because the relation imposed on that coordinate is exactly "$d_i$ times this coordinate is zero". For $i > r$ no relation touches the coordinate $v_i$ at all, so it contributes a full free copy of $R$. A direct sum decomposition of $R^m$ along its basis, quotiented by a submodule that is itself a direct sum along the same basis, becomes the direct sum of the coordinatewise quotients:
$$\frac{R^m}{K} \;\cong\; \frac{R v_1}{(d_1 v_1)} \oplus \dots \oplus \frac{R v_r}{(d_r v_r)} \oplus R v_{r+1} \oplus \dots \oplus R v_m \;\cong\; \frac{R}{(d_1)} \oplus \dots \oplus \frac{R}{(d_r)} \oplus R^{m-r}.$$
That is the whole theorem, and the reason to expect it is exactly this: the only obstacle to understanding $M$ is the tangling of relations, and over a Euclidean domain the division algorithm always untangles them, after which a quotient of a diagonalised pair is a direct sum of one-coordinate quotients.

# What Makes This Hard

The difficulty is not in the final assembly — once you know $K$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ along a basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$, the splitting of $R^m/K$ into $\bigoplus R/(d_i) \oplus R^s$ is a routine coordinatewise calculation; the entire weight of the theorem rests on the imported [[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|submodule/Smith-normal-form theorem]], which is where the Euclidean division algorithm is actually consumed. The single non-obvious move is the strategy itself: rather than studying $M$ directly, present it as $R^m/K$ and then diagonalise the *relations* — the reflex to apply Smith normal form to a presentation matrix rather than to attack the module head-on. The most common errors are forgetting that the basis of $R^m$ along which $K$ is diagonalised is a *new* basis, not the standard one, and dropping the divisibility chain $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$ — which is not optional decoration but the condition that makes the $d_i$ canonical.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Do not study $M$ directly. Present it as a quotient $R^m/K$ of a free module by a relation submodule, using the free-quotient theorem. Then apply the submodule/Smith-normal-form theorem to choose a basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ along which $K$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$ with $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$. Finally split the quotient coordinate by coordinate.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Present the module as a quotient of a free module.** Show $M \cong R^m/K$ for some finite $m$ and some submodule $K \leq R^m$.
   - *Hint:* Finite generation gives a surjection $\varphi : R^m \twoheadrightarrow M$; apply the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem]] with $K = \ker\varphi$.
   - *Why needed:* It converts the abstract module into a concrete quotient that the diagonalisation theorem can act on.

2. **Diagonalise the relation submodule.** Show there is a basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ and elements $d_1 \mid \dots \mid d_r$ such that $K$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$.
   - *Hint:* This is the [[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|submodule theorem]]: $K$ is generated by at most $m$ elements, assemble them into a matrix, put it in [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] by row and column operations; row operations re-basis $R^m$, column operations re-generate $K$.
   - *Why needed:* It aligns the relations with a basis, untangling the quotient.

3. **Split the quotient along the basis.** Show that quotienting $R^m = Rv_1 \oplus \dots \oplus Rv_m$ by $K = (d_1 v_1) \oplus \dots \oplus (d_r v_r)$ gives the direct sum of the coordinatewise quotients.
   - *Hint:* A direct sum modulo a sub-direct-sum is the direct sum of the quotients: $\bigl(\bigoplus A_i\bigr)/\bigl(\bigoplus B_i\bigr) \cong \bigoplus (A_i/B_i)$ when $B_i \leq A_i$.
   - *Why needed:* It performs the actual decomposition, turning the diagonalised quotient into a direct sum.

4. **Identify each summand.** Show $Rv_i/(d_i v_i) \cong R/(d_i)$ for $i \leq r$, and $Rv_i \cong R$ for $i > r$.
   - *Hint:* $v_i$ is a basis vector, so $Rv_i \cong R$ via $rv_i \mapsto r$; this isomorphism sends the submodule $(d_i v_i)$ to the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $(d_i)$.
   - *Why needed:* It puts the decomposition into the stated form $\bigoplus R/(d_i) \oplus R^s$.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: A finitely generated module is a quotient of a finite-rank free module
> **Statement:** If $M$ is a finitely generated $R$-module, then $M \cong R^m/K$ for some finite $m$ and some submodule $K \leq R^m$.
>
> **Hint:** Finite generation produces a surjection from $R^m$; the first isomorphism theorem turns the surjection into a quotient.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the starting move — it converts the abstract module into a concrete quotient on which Smith normal form can act.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Since $M$ is [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]], write $M = Rm_1 + \dots + Rm_m$. By the [[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|free-quotient theorem]] there is a surjective module homomorphism
> > $$\varphi : R^m \twoheadrightarrow M, \qquad \varphi(r_1, \dots, r_m) = r_1 m_1 + \dots + r_m m_m.$$
> > Let $K = \ker\varphi \leq R^m$. By the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem for modules]], $\varphi$ descends to an isomorphism $R^m/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$, that is,
> > $$M \;\cong\; R^m/K. \qquad \blacksquare$$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The relation submodule is diagonal with respect to a basis
> **Statement:** Let $K \leq R^m$ be a submodule, $R$ a Euclidean domain. Then there is a basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ and elements $d_1, \dots, d_r \in R$ with $0 \leq r \leq m$ and $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r$, such that $K$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, d_2 v_2, \dots, d_r v_r$.
>
> **Hint:** $K$ is generated by at most $m$ elements; collect them as the columns of a matrix and put the matrix in Smith normal form. Row operations re-basis $R^m$; column operations re-generate $K$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the heart of the proof — it untangles the relations by aligning them with a basis.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > This is the [[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|submodule theorem for free modules over a Euclidean domain]]; the full argument is on that page, and the engine is [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]]. In outline: a submodule of $R^m$ is generated by at most $m$ elements $x_1, \dots, x_n$ with $n \leq m$. Regarding each $x_j$ as a column vector of length $m$, assemble the $m \times n$ matrix $A = (x_1 \mid \dots \mid x_n)$. Over a Euclidean domain, $A$ can be reduced by row and column operations to **Smith normal form**: a matrix that is zero off the diagonal with diagonal entries $d_1, d_2, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0$ satisfying $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r$. A row operation on $A$ corresponds to changing the basis of $R^m$; a column operation corresponds to changing the generating set of $K$. Hence after the reduction there is a new basis $v_1, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ for which $K$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, \dots, d_r v_r$. The divisibility chain is part of the definition of Smith normal form. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: A direct sum modulo a sub-direct-sum splits coordinatewise
> **Statement:** Suppose a module $P$ decomposes as a direct sum $P = P_1 \oplus \dots \oplus P_m$, and $K \leq P$ is a submodule of the form $K = K_1 \oplus \dots \oplus K_m$ with each $K_i \leq P_i$. Then
> $$P/K \;\cong\; (P_1/K_1) \oplus (P_2/K_2) \oplus \dots \oplus (P_m/K_m).$$
>
> **Hint:** The coordinatewise quotient map $P \to \bigoplus P_i/K_i$ is surjective with kernel exactly $K$; apply the first isomorphism theorem.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the mechanism that turns the diagonalised quotient $R^m/K$ into a direct sum of one-coordinate quotients.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Define $\psi : P_1 \oplus \dots \oplus P_m \to (P_1/K_1) \oplus \dots \oplus (P_m/K_m)$ by reducing each coordinate modulo its $K_i$:
> > $$\psi(p_1, \dots, p_m) = (p_1 + K_1, \dots, p_m + K_m).$$
> > This is a module homomorphism, being the direct sum of the $m$ quotient maps $P_i \to P_i/K_i$, each of which is a homomorphism. It is **surjective**: any tuple $(p_1 + K_1, \dots, p_m + K_m)$ is the image of $(p_1, \dots, p_m)$. Its **kernel** is
> > $$\ker\psi = \{(p_1, \dots, p_m) : p_i + K_i = 0 \text{ for all } i\} = \{(p_1, \dots, p_m) : p_i \in K_i\} = K_1 \oplus \dots \oplus K_m = K.$$
> > By the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem for modules]], $P/\ker\psi \cong \operatorname{im}\psi$, that is, $P/K \cong (P_1/K_1) \oplus \dots \oplus (P_m/K_m)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 4: A basis vector spans a free copy of $R$, and the diagonal relation becomes a principal [[Def - Ideal|ideal]]
> **Statement:** Let $v$ be one vector of a basis of a free module, so $Rv$ is the cyclic submodule it spans. Then $Rv \cong R$ as $R$-modules, and under this isomorphism the submodule $(dv) = R\,(dv)$ corresponds to the principal ideal $(d) \leq R$. Consequently $Rv/(dv) \cong R/(d)$.
>
> **Hint:** The map $r \mapsto rv$ is an isomorphism $R \to Rv$ because $v$ is part of a basis (no non-trivial relation $rv = 0$); track where it sends $dv$.
>
> **Why needed:** It identifies each coordinatewise quotient as a standard cyclic module $R/(d_i)$ or a free copy of $R$, producing the stated form.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $v$ be a member of a basis of a free $R$-module. The map $\theta : R \to Rv$, $\theta(r) = rv$, is a module homomorphism (it respects addition and the $R$-action because $(r + r')v = rv + r'v$ and $(sr)v = s(rv)$). It is **surjective** by definition of the cyclic submodule $Rv = \{rv : r \in R\}$. It is **injective**: if $\theta(r) = rv = 0$ then, because $v$ is a member of a basis and therefore [[Def - Free Module|linearly independent]] on its own, $r = 0$. Hence $\theta : R \xrightarrow{\ \cong\ } Rv$ is an isomorphism.
> >
> > Under $\theta$, the element $d \in R$ maps to $dv$, so the principal ideal $(d) = \{rd : r \in R\}$ maps onto $\{rdv : r \in R\} = R(dv)$, the cyclic submodule of $Rv$ generated by $dv$. An isomorphism carrying a submodule onto a submodule induces an isomorphism of the quotients, so
> > $$Rv/R(dv) \;\cong\; R/(d).$$
> > Taking $d = d_i$ gives $Rv_i/(d_i v_i) \cong R/(d_i)$. For an unconstrained coordinate, $K_i = 0$, and $Rv_i/0 = Rv_i \cong R$. $\blacksquare$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] and $M$ a [[Def - Finitely Generated Module|finitely generated]] $R$-module.
>
> **Step 1 — present $M$ as a quotient of a free module.** By Lemma 1, finite generation of $M$ yields a surjection $\varphi : R^m \twoheadrightarrow M$ for some finite $m$, and the [[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|first isomorphism theorem for modules]] gives
> $$M \;\cong\; R^m/K, \qquad K := \ker\varphi \leq R^m.$$
> So it suffices to decompose $R^m/K$.
>
> **Step 2 — diagonalise the relation submodule $K$.** By Lemma 2 — the [[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|submodule theorem]], whose proof runs [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith normal form]] — there is a basis $v_1, v_2, \dots, v_m$ of $R^m$ and elements $d_1, \dots, d_r \in R$ with $0 \leq r \leq m$ and
> $$d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r,$$
> such that $K$ is generated by $d_1 v_1, d_2 v_2, \dots, d_r v_r$. Concretely, the original isomorphism reads
> $$M \;\cong\; \frac{R^m}{\bigl(d_1 v_1,\ d_2 v_2,\ \dots,\ d_r v_r\bigr)}.$$
>
> **Step 3 — express both $R^m$ and $K$ as direct sums along the basis.** Since $v_1, \dots, v_m$ is a basis,
> $$R^m \;=\; Rv_1 \oplus Rv_2 \oplus \dots \oplus Rv_m.$$
> The submodule $K$ is generated by the elements $d_i v_i$, each of which lies in the single summand $Rv_i$; therefore, setting $K_i = R(d_i v_i) = (d_i v_i)$ for $i \leq r$ and $K_i = 0$ for $r < i \leq m$,
> $$K \;=\; K_1 \oplus K_2 \oplus \dots \oplus K_m, \qquad K_i \leq Rv_i.$$
>
> **Step 4 — split the quotient coordinate by coordinate.** By Lemma 3, a direct sum modulo a sub-direct-sum is the direct sum of the coordinatewise quotients:
> $$M \;\cong\; \frac{R^m}{K} \;\cong\; \frac{Rv_1}{K_1} \oplus \frac{Rv_2}{K_2} \oplus \dots \oplus \frac{Rv_m}{K_m}.$$
>
> **Step 5 — identify each summand.** By Lemma 4, for each constrained coordinate $i \leq r$ the basis vector $v_i$ satisfies $Rv_i \cong R$, and this isomorphism carries the submodule $K_i = (d_i v_i)$ to the principal ideal $(d_i)$, so
> $$\frac{Rv_i}{K_i} \;\cong\; \frac{R}{(d_i)} \qquad (1 \leq i \leq r).$$
> For each unconstrained coordinate $i > r$ we have $K_i = 0$, so
> $$\frac{Rv_i}{K_i} \;=\; Rv_i \;\cong\; R \qquad (r < i \leq m).$$
> Writing $s = m - r$ for the number of unconstrained coordinates and assembling Steps 4 and 5,
> $$M \;\cong\; \frac{R}{(d_1)} \oplus \frac{R}{(d_2)} \oplus \dots \oplus \frac{R}{(d_r)} \oplus \underbrace{R \oplus \dots \oplus R}_{s},$$
> with $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \dots \mid d_r$. (If some $d_i$ is a unit, the summand $R/(d_i)$ is the zero module and may be discarded; if some $d_i = 0$, the summand $R/(0) \cong R$ may be absorbed into the free part. Discarding the trivial summands leaves the $d_i$ non-zero non-units, as stated.) This is the required decomposition. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Identifying an abelian group from a presentation matrix.** Let $A$ be the abelian group generated by $a, b, c$ subject to relations $2a + 3b + c = 0$, $a + 2b = 0$, $5a + 6b + 7c = 0$. The structure theorem applies because $A$ is a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module — $A \cong \mathbb{Z}^3/K$ with $K$ spanned by the relation vectors. Computing the Smith normal form of the $3 \times 3$ matrix of relations (its invariant factors, read off via [[Def - Minor and Fitting Ideal|Fitting ideals]], turn out to be $1, 1, 3$) reveals $A \cong C_3$. The non-obvious recognition is that a group described by tangled relations — not visibly even non-trivial — is governed entirely by an integer matrix.

**Cokernel of an integer matrix.** Given an integer $m \times n$ matrix $X$, describe the abelian group $\mathbb{Z}^m/\operatorname{im}(X)$. The structure theorem applies because the cokernel is a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module presented by $X$ itself. Its Smith normal form $\operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_r)$ gives the cokernel as $\mathbb{Z}/(d_1) \oplus \dots \oplus \mathbb{Z}/(d_r) \oplus \mathbb{Z}^{m-r}$. The non-obvious application is that a question about the cokernel of a linear map — which sounds like linear algebra — is the structure theorem with $R = \mathbb{Z}$, and the same statement over $R = F[X]$ governs the cokernel of $XI - A$ and hence the [[Thm - Rational Canonical Form|rational canonical form]] of $A$.

**A linear operator on a finite-dimensional vector space.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $F$ and $\alpha : V \to V$ a linear map; make $V$ into an $F[X]$-module via $X \cdot v = \alpha(v)$. Since $F[X]$ is a Euclidean domain and $V$ is finitely generated over it (any $F$-basis generates), the structure theorem applies and decomposes $V$ into cyclic $F[X]$-modules $F[X]/(d_i)$. The non-obvious recognition is that "classify a matrix up to similarity" is "apply the structure theorem with $R = F[X]$" — the invariant factors $d_i$ are precisely the invariant factors of the matrix, and the decomposition is the rational canonical form.

**Modules over the Gaussian integers.** The Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ form a Euclidean domain under the norm $\varphi(z) = |z|^2$. Any finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}[i]$-module — for instance an ideal class or a quotient ring $\mathbb{Z}[i]/(z)$ regarded as a module — therefore decomposes as $\bigoplus \mathbb{Z}[i]/(d_i) \oplus \mathbb{Z}[i]^s$. The non-obvious application is that the entire classification machinery transfers verbatim to $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — the proof never used anything about $R$ beyond the existence of a Euclidean function — so number-theoretic objects built from Gaussian integers inherit a complete structural description.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Submodules of Free Modules over a Euclidean Domain|Submodule Theorem]] and [[Thm - Smith Normal Form|Smith Normal Form]]** — the engine of the proof. The structure theorem is, in essence, the submodule theorem with the quotient taken: once Smith normal form gives a basis of $R^m$ along which $K$ is diagonal, the structure theorem is the routine coordinatewise splitting of $R^m/K$. The relationship is literal dependence — Lemma 2 *is* the submodule theorem, invoked verbatim.

- **[[Thm - Finitely Generated Modules and Surjections from a Free Module|Finitely Generated Modules as Quotients of Free Modules]]** — the prerequisite that makes the strategy possible. That theorem presents every finitely generated module as $R^m/K$; the structure theorem is what happens when you then diagonalise $K$. Without the free-quotient presentation there would be nothing for Smith normal form to act on.

- **[[Thm - Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups|Classification of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups]]** — the special case $R = \mathbb{Z}$. A $\mathbb{Z}$-module is an abelian group, $\mathbb{Z}/(d)$ is the cyclic group $C_d$, and $\mathbb{Z}$ is the infinite cyclic group; the structure theorem read at $R = \mathbb{Z}$ is exactly the classification of finitely generated abelian groups.

- **[[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|Primary Decomposition Theorem]]** — the refinement. The structure theorem produces the invariant-factor form $\bigoplus R/(d_i) \oplus R^s$; primary decomposition splits each $R/(d_i)$ by the [[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules|Chinese remainder theorem]] using the prime factorisation of $d_i$, yielding the finer prime-power form $\bigoplus R/(p^n) \oplus R^s$.

- **[[Thm - Invariance of Rank|Invariance of Rank]]** — the well-definedness guarantee for the free part. The structure theorem yields *some* number $s$ of free summands; invariance of rank certifies that $s$ does not depend on the chosen decomposition, so the free rank is a genuine invariant of $M$.

- **Rational and Jordan Canonical Forms** — the special case $R = F[X]$. A finite-dimensional vector space with a linear operator is a finitely generated torsion $F[X]$-module; the structure theorem decomposes it into cyclic $F[X]$-modules, which is the [[Thm - Rational Canonical Form|rational canonical form]], and splitting further by [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]] gives the [[Thm - Jordan Normal Form|Jordan normal form]].

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Rational and Jordan Canonical Forms *(from Linear Algebra / Modules over F[X])*
> Taking the Euclidean domain to be the polynomial ring $F[X]$, a finite-dimensional vector space carrying a linear operator becomes a finitely generated $F[X]$-module. The structure theorem decomposes it into cyclic modules $F[X]/(d_i)$ — this is the [[Thm - Rational Canonical Form|rational canonical form]] of the operator — and refining by [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]] produces the [[Thm - Jordan Normal Form|Jordan normal form]].

> [!tip] Structure Theorem over a Principal Ideal Domain *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> The same decomposition $M \cong \bigoplus R/(d_i) \oplus R^s$ holds whenever $R$ is any principal ideal domain, not just a Euclidean one. The proof is identical in shape, but Smith normal form must be replaced by an existence argument that does not rely on a division algorithm — the divisibility chain is then produced by repeatedly extracting greatest common divisors of matrix entries.
