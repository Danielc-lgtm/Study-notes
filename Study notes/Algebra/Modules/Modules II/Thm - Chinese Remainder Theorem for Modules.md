# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] — an integral domain carrying a function $\varphi : R \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ supporting division with remainder. For $a \in R$, the ideal $(a) = \{ra : r \in R\}$ is the principal ideal generated by $a$, and $R/(a)$ is the cyclic quotient module; an element $r + (a)$ of $R/(a)$ is the coset of $r$. Two elements $a, b \in R$ are **coprime** if $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, meaning the only common divisors of $a$ and $b$ are units; equivalently, by the [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean algorithm]], there exist $x, y \in R$ with the **Bézout relation** $ax + by = 1$. The [[Def - Direct Sum of Modules|direct sum]] $R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$ is the module of pairs with coordinatewise operations. The symbol $\cong$ denotes module isomorphism, and a [[Def - Module Homomorphism|module homomorphism]] is a map of abelian groups commuting with the $R$-action. The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Modules II — §3.3–3.4]].

# Statement

> **Chinese Remainder Theorem for Modules.** Let $R$ be a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] and let $a, b \in R$ be **coprime**, $\gcd(a, b) = 1$. Then
> $$\frac{R}{(ab)} \;\cong\; \frac{R}{(a)} \oplus \frac{R}{(b)}$$
> as $R$-[[Def - Module|modules]].

The single cyclic module $R/(ab)$, governed by one composite modulus $ab$, splits into a direct sum of two cyclic [[Def - Module|modules]] governed by the coprime factors $a$ and $b$ separately. The hypothesis $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ is essential: without coprimality the two factors share structure and the splitting fails (see *Sources and Targets* and *What Makes This Hard*). This is the module-theoretic form of the classical Chinese remainder theorem; specialised to $R = \mathbb{Z}$ it recovers the statement that for coprime integers $m, n$ one has $\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — a system of congruences modulo $m$ and modulo $n$ has a unique simultaneous solution modulo $mn$.

---

# Motivation

The classical Chinese remainder theorem is a statement about solving congruences: if you know a number's remainder modulo $3$ and its remainder modulo $5$, and $3$ and $5$ are coprime, then you know its remainder modulo $15$, and conversely every pair of remainders is achievable by exactly one [[Def - Residue|residue]] modulo $15$. It is one of the oldest theorems in number theory, and its content is that arithmetic modulo a composite number $mn$ — when $m$ and $n$ are coprime — is *nothing more* than two independent copies of arithmetic modulo the factors, run in parallel.

This page lifts that statement out of number theory and into module theory, and the reason for doing so is leverage. The classical theorem is about $\mathbb{Z}$; but the same proof, written in the language of [[Def - Ring|rings]] and modules, works for *any* Euclidean domain — and Euclidean domains include the polynomial [[Def - Ring|ring]] $F[X]$, the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, and more. By proving the theorem once at this level of generality, every instance of "split a cyclic module by a coprime factorisation of its modulus" becomes available across all of these settings at once. The lecture notes flag this explicitly: the proof is "just that of the Chinese remainder theorem written in ring language".

Why does one want this particular statement, packaged this particular way? Because it is the missing tool in the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition]] of finitely generated modules. The [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem]] decomposes a finitely generated module over a Euclidean domain into cyclic pieces $R/(d_i)$ — but the moduli $d_i$ are arbitrary ring elements, possibly composite. To refine this into the cleanest possible form, where every cyclic piece is governed by a *prime power*, you need to break each $R/(d_i)$ apart along the prime factorisation $d_i = p_1^{n_1} \cdots p_k^{n_k}$. Distinct prime powers are coprime, so the Chinese remainder theorem is exactly the instrument that performs this break-up:
$$\frac{R}{(d_i)} \;\cong\; \frac{R}{(p_1^{n_1})} \oplus \dots \oplus \frac{R}{(p_k^{n_k})}.$$
Without this theorem the structure theorem stops at the invariant-factor form; with it, the structure theorem reaches the primary-decomposition form. The Chinese remainder theorem is the bridge between the two faces of the classification of modules.

One should *expect* a result like this because of the slogan that coprimality means independence. If $a$ and $b$ have no common factor, then a constraint imposed modulo $a$ and a constraint imposed modulo $b$ cannot interfere — they pull on entirely separate "directions". The composite modulus $ab$ packages the two constraints into one, but the package is loss-free: the pair of factors can always be recovered. The honest content of the proof is to exhibit the explicit isomorphism that does the unpacking, and to see precisely where coprimality — in the form of a Bézout relation $ax + by = 1$ — is consumed.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The literal precondition is a cyclic module $R/(ab)$ over a Euclidean domain together with a *coprime* factorisation $ab$ of the modulus. The skill is recognising when a problem hands you a modulus that admits such a factorisation.

The first disguised source is **a cyclic module whose modulus has a known prime factorisation**. Any element $d$ of a Euclidean domain factors as $d = p_1^{n_1} \cdots p_k^{n_k}$ into prime powers (Euclidean domains are unique factorisation domains), and *distinct prime powers are automatically coprime* — a prime power $p^n$ and a prime power $q^m$ with $p, q$ non-associate primes share no irreducible factor. The non-obvious recognition is that any cyclic module $R/(d)$ with $d$ composite is *already* a hidden direct sum: the prime factorisation of $d$ is a coprime factorisation, so the theorem (iterated) applies. *Example problem:* show that $\mathbb{Z}/360\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/5\mathbb{Z}$ — immediate from the coprime factorisation $360 = 8 \cdot 9 \cdot 5$.

The second disguised source is **a cyclic module presented over $F[X]$ by a polynomial that factors into coprime parts**. For $R = F[X]$, two polynomials are coprime exactly when they share no common root in an algebraic closure (equivalently, no common irreducible factor). The non-obvious recognition is that "the characteristic polynomial of an operator factors as a product of pairwise-coprime polynomials" is the hypothesis of the Chinese remainder theorem in disguise, and the splitting it produces is the decomposition of the underlying vector space into generalised eigenspaces. *Example problem:* if the minimal polynomial of a linear operator $\alpha$ is $(X - \lambda)(X - \mu)$ with $\lambda \neq \mu$, then $F[X]/((X-\lambda)(X-\mu)) \cong F[X]/(X-\lambda) \oplus F[X]/(X-\mu)$ — and $\alpha$ is diagonalisable.

The third disguised source is **a system of two simultaneous congruence-type constraints**. Any time a problem imposes "$x \equiv u \pmod a$ and $x \equiv v \pmod b$" with $a, b$ coprime — whether the variable $x$ ranges over integers, polynomials, or any Euclidean domain — the solution set is a single coset modulo $ab$, because the constraint map into $R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$ is a bijection from $R/(ab)$. The non-obvious step is to see a pair of independent constraints as a single element of a direct sum. *Example problem:* find the polynomial of least degree congruent to $X$ modulo $X^2 + 1$ and to $1$ modulo $X - 1$ — there is exactly one modulo $(X^2+1)(X-1)$.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusion is the isomorphism $R/(ab) \cong R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$. Combined with other facts it does more.

Combine the conclusion with **iteration over a prime factorisation**. Applying the theorem repeatedly to a modulus $d = p_1^{n_1} \cdots p_k^{n_k}$ — peeling off one coprime factor at a time — decomposes $R/(d)$ into a direct sum of prime-power cyclic modules $R/(p_i^{n_i})$. The further result is the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition theorem]]: every finitely generated module over a Euclidean domain is a direct sum of a free module and modules of the single form $R/(p^n)$. This combination is non-obvious because a *two-factor* statement, iterated, produces a *complete* prime-power decomposition.

Combine the conclusion with **the structure theorem's invariant-factor form**. The [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem]] gives $M \cong \bigoplus R/(d_i) \oplus R^s$; the Chinese remainder theorem converts this *invariant-factor* form into the *elementary-divisor* (prime-power) form, and conversely, prime-power factors with distinct primes fuse back into invariant factors. The further result is that the two canonical forms of a finitely generated module are interchangeable, with the Chinese remainder theorem as the explicit dictionary. This is non-obvious because the two forms look unrelated until you see that coprime fusion is the bridge.

Combine the conclusion with **a counting or ring-structure argument**. Specialised to $R = \mathbb{Z}$, the isomorphism $\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — which is in fact a ring isomorphism — yields the multiplicativity of the Euler totient function, $\varphi_{\text{Euler}}(mn) = \varphi_{\text{Euler}}(m)\varphi_{\text{Euler}}(n)$ for coprime $m, n$, by counting units on each side. The further result is a foothold in analytic and elementary number theory: many multiplicative functions are multiplicative *because* of this splitting. This is non-obvious because a structural module isomorphism becomes, after counting, an arithmetic identity.

# Why Is It True

The intuition is a single sentence: **coprimality means the two factors pull in independent directions, so a constraint modulo $a$ and a constraint modulo $b$ never interfere — and the Bézout relation is the explicit witness of that independence.**

Start with the picture. The module $R/(ab)$ records ring elements "up to multiples of $ab$". The module $R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$ records *pairs*: a ring element seen modulo $a$ alongside a (possibly different) ring element seen modulo $b$. The claim is that these two modules carry exactly the same information — that knowing an element modulo $ab$ is the same as knowing it modulo $a$ and modulo $b$ separately. Why should that be true? Because $ab$ is "as composite as $a$ and $b$ together", and when $a$ and $b$ are coprime there is no overlap to double-count: the modulus $ab$ is the product of two genuinely separate constraints.

Now make it precise by building the isomorphism. The map that does the work goes *from* the pair side *to* the composite side:
$$\phi : \frac{R}{(a)} \oplus \frac{R}{(b)} \longrightarrow \frac{R}{(ab)}, \qquad (r_1 + (a),\ r_2 + (b)) \longmapsto b r_1 + a r_2 + (ab).$$
The combination $b r_1 + a r_2$ is chosen with care. Look at it modulo $a$: the term $a r_2$ vanishes, leaving $b r_1$. Look at it modulo $b$: the term $b r_1$ vanishes, leaving $a r_2$. So the formula bakes in the right *cross-talk-free* behaviour — the first input controls the answer modulo $a$, the second controls it modulo $b$, and they do not interfere. (One first has to check this is well-defined, i.e. that changing $r_1$ by a multiple of $a$ or $r_2$ by a multiple of $b$ changes $br_1 + ar_2$ only by a multiple of $ab$; it does, because $b \cdot (xa) = (ab)x$ and $a \cdot (yb) = (ab)y$.)

Here is where coprimality enters, and it enters exactly once, to make $\phi$ **surjective**. The module $R/(ab)$ is generated, as an $R$-module, by the single element $1 + (ab)$ — every [[Def - Coset|coset]] is $r \cdot (1 + (ab))$. So to prove $\phi$ hits everything, it is enough to prove $\phi$ hits the *generator* $1 + (ab)$. And this is precisely what a Bézout relation provides: because $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, the Euclidean algorithm yields $x, y \in R$ with
$$ax + by = 1.$$
Feed the pair $(y + (a),\ x + (b))$ into $\phi$:
$$\phi(y + (a),\ x + (b)) = by + ax + (ab) = 1 + (ab).$$
The generator is hit. Since $\phi$ is a module homomorphism, $r \cdot (1 + (ab)) = \phi(r \cdot (y + (a), x + (b)))$ is hit too, for every $r$ — so $\phi$ is onto. **This is the entire role of coprimality:** it is the existence of the Bézout combination that expresses $1$ in terms of $a$ and $b$, and without $1$ in the image, the generator of $R/(ab)$ would be unreachable.

Injectivity is then the independence of the constraints, read in the other direction. Suppose $\phi(r_1 + (a), r_2 + (b)) = 0$, i.e. $b r_1 + a r_2$ is a multiple of $ab$. Reduce that statement modulo $a$: since $ab \mid (br_1 + ar_2)$ certainly $a \mid (br_1 + ar_2)$, and $a \mid ar_2$, so $a \mid br_1$. Now coprimality bites a second time, through unique factorisation: $a$ divides the product $b r_1$ and shares no factor with $b$, so $a$ must divide $r_1$ — that is, $r_1 + (a) = 0$. Symmetrically $b \mid r_2$, so $r_2 + (b) = 0$. The input pair was zero to begin with; the kernel is trivial.

So the theorem is true for exactly the reason the slogan says. The composite modulus $ab$ bundles two constraints; coprimality guarantees the bundle is loss-free — the Bézout relation reconstructs the generator, hence surjectivity, and unique factorisation forbids hidden overlap, hence injectivity. Strip out coprimality and both halves fail at once: there is no Bézout relation, and $a$ can divide $b r_1$ without dividing $r_1$.

# What Makes This Hard

The proof itself is short, so the difficulty is not technical bulk but knowing *where the hypothesis is spent*: coprimality is used in exactly two places — once as a Bézout relation $ax + by = 1$ to get surjectivity (the generator $1$ must be in the image), and once via unique factorisation to get injectivity ($a \mid br_1$ with $\gcd(a,b)=1$ forces $a \mid r_1$) — and a common error is to prove well-definedness and the homomorphism property, which need *no* coprimality at all, and then forget that the real content lives entirely in those two steps. The other non-obvious point is the choice of the map: writing $\phi(r_1, r_2) = b r_1 + a r_2$ rather than the naive $r_1 + r_2$ is what makes the first coordinate control the answer modulo $a$ and the second modulo $b$; people often try the diagonal map and find it is neither well-defined nor an isomorphism. Finally, the direction matters — the clean map runs *from* the direct sum *to* $R/(ab)$, because surjectivity onto $R/(ab)$ reduces to hitting a single generator, whereas the reverse map has no such shortcut.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Write down the explicit map $\phi : R/(a) \oplus R/(b) \to R/(ab)$, $(r_1, r_2) \mapsto br_1 + ar_2$. Check it is a well-defined module homomorphism (no coprimality needed). Then use the Bézout relation $ax + by = 1$ to show $\phi$ hits the generator $1 + (ab)$, hence is surjective; and use unique factorisation to show the kernel is trivial. The key idea is that the formula $br_1 + ar_2$ is engineered so that coordinate one controls the answer modulo $a$ and coordinate two controls it modulo $b$.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Define the map and check it is well-defined.** Set $\phi(r_1 + (a), r_2 + (b)) = br_1 + ar_2 + (ab)$, and show the value does not depend on the chosen representatives.
   - *Hint:* If $r_1 = r_1' + xa$ and $r_2 = r_2' + yb$, then $br_1 + ar_2 = br_1' + ar_2' + (xab + yab)$, and $xab + yab \in (ab)$.
   - *Why needed:* A formula on [[Def - Coset|cosets]] is meaningless until it is shown independent of representatives.

2. **Check $\phi$ is a module homomorphism.** Show $\phi$ respects addition and the $R$-action.
   - *Hint:* Both operations on $R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$ are coordinatewise, and $br_1 + ar_2$ depends $R$-linearly on $(r_1, r_2)$.
   - *Why needed:* The conclusion is an isomorphism *of modules*; $\phi$ must be a module map.

3. **Show $\phi$ is surjective, using the Bézout relation.** Use $\gcd(a,b) = 1$ to obtain $x, y$ with $ax + by = 1$, and show $\phi(y + (a), x + (b)) = 1 + (ab)$; conclude $\phi$ is onto.
   - *Hint:* $R/(ab)$ is generated by $1 + (ab)$; a homomorphism whose image contains the generator is surjective.
   - *Why needed:* This is the first of the two places coprimality is consumed.

4. **Show $\phi$ is injective.** Suppose $br_1 + ar_2 \in (ab)$; deduce $a \mid r_1$ and $b \mid r_2$, so the input pair is zero.
   - *Hint:* From $br_1 + ar_2 = abx$ get $a \mid br_1$; coprimality and unique factorisation give $a \mid r_1$. Symmetrically for $b$.
   - *Why needed:* This is the second place coprimality is consumed; it completes the proof that $\phi$ is an isomorphism.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: The map is a well-defined module homomorphism
> **Statement:** Let $a, b \in R$. The assignment $\phi(r_1 + (a),\ r_2 + (b)) = br_1 + ar_2 + (ab)$ defines a module homomorphism $\phi : R/(a) \oplus R/(b) \to R/(ab)$. (Coprimality is *not* needed here.)
>
> **Hint:** Well-definedness: changing $r_1$ by a multiple of $a$, or $r_2$ by a multiple of $b$, changes $br_1 + ar_2$ only by a multiple of $ab$.
>
> **Why needed:** It establishes that $\phi$ is a legitimate module map — the object the rest of the proof shows is an isomorphism.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Well-defined.** Suppose $(r_1 + (a),\ r_2 + (b)) = (r_1' + (a),\ r_2' + (b))$, so $r_1 - r_1' \in (a)$ and $r_2 - r_2' \in (b)$; write $r_1 = r_1' + xa$ and $r_2 = r_2' + yb$ for some $x, y \in R$. Then
> > $$b r_1 + a r_2 = b(r_1' + xa) + a(r_2' + yb) = (b r_1' + a r_2') + (xab + yab).$$
> > The correction term $xab + yab = (x + y)(ab)$ lies in the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $(ab)$, so $br_1 + ar_2 + (ab) = br_1' + ar_2' + (ab)$. The value of $\phi$ is therefore independent of the representatives, and $\phi$ is well-defined.
> >
> > **Module homomorphism.** Addition and the $R$-action on $R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$ are coordinatewise. For two inputs,
> > $$\phi\bigl((r_1, r_2) + (r_1', r_2')\bigr) = b(r_1 + r_1') + a(r_2 + r_2') + (ab) = \phi(r_1, r_2) + \phi(r_1', r_2'),$$
> > using distributivity in $R$. For $r \in R$,
> > $$\phi\bigl(r \cdot (r_1, r_2)\bigr) = b(rr_1) + a(rr_2) + (ab) = r\bigl(br_1 + ar_2\bigr) + (ab) = r \cdot \phi(r_1, r_2).$$
> > Hence $\phi$ is a module homomorphism. No use was made of $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: $R/(ab)$ is generated by the single element $1 + (ab)$
> **Statement:** As an $R$-module, $R/(ab)$ is cyclic: it is generated by $1 + (ab)$, meaning every element is $r \cdot (1 + (ab))$ for some $r \in R$.
>
> **Hint:** A coset $r + (ab)$ is by definition $r$ times the coset of $1$.
>
> **Why needed:** It reduces the proof of surjectivity of $\phi$ to the single task of hitting the generator $1 + (ab)$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Every element of $R/(ab)$ is a coset $r + (ab)$ for some $r \in R$. The $R$-action on the quotient module is $r \cdot (s + (ab)) = rs + (ab)$, so
> > $$r \cdot (1 + (ab)) = r \cdot 1 + (ab) = r + (ab).$$
> > Thus every element of $R/(ab)$ is an $R$-multiple of $1 + (ab)$, i.e. $R/(ab) = R \cdot (1 + (ab))$. In particular, a module homomorphism into $R/(ab)$ is surjective as soon as its image contains $1 + (ab)$: if $1 + (ab) = \psi(z)$ then $r + (ab) = r \cdot \psi(z) = \psi(r z)$ for every $r$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Surjectivity from a Bézout relation
> **Statement:** If $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, then the homomorphism $\phi$ of Lemma 1 is surjective.
>
> **Hint:** The Euclidean algorithm gives $ax + by = 1$; feed $(y + (a),\ x + (b))$ into $\phi$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the first of the two steps where coprimality is consumed; together with injectivity it makes $\phi$ an isomorphism.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Because $R$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, the Euclidean algorithm produces a **Bézout relation**: there exist $x, y \in R$ with
> > $$ax + by = 1.$$
> > Apply $\phi$ to the pair $(y + (a),\ x + (b))$:
> > $$\phi(y + (a),\ x + (b)) = b y + a x + (ab) = (ax + by) + (ab) = 1 + (ab).$$
> > So the generator $1 + (ab)$ of $R/(ab)$ lies in the image of $\phi$. By Lemma 2, a module homomorphism into $R/(ab)$ whose image contains $1 + (ab)$ is surjective: for any $r + (ab)$,
> > $$\phi\bigl(r \cdot (y + (a),\ x + (b))\bigr) = r \cdot \phi(y + (a),\ x + (b)) = r \cdot (1 + (ab)) = r + (ab).$$
> > Hence $\phi$ is surjective. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 4: Injectivity from unique factorisation
> **Statement:** If $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, then the homomorphism $\phi$ of Lemma 1 has trivial kernel.
>
> **Hint:** From $br_1 + ar_2 \in (ab)$ deduce $a \mid br_1$; coprimality plus unique factorisation forces $a \mid r_1$. Symmetrically $b \mid r_2$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the second step where coprimality is consumed; with surjectivity it completes the isomorphism.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Suppose $\phi(r_1 + (a),\ r_2 + (b)) = 0 + (ab)$, that is,
> > $$b r_1 + a r_2 \in (ab), \qquad\text{so}\qquad b r_1 + a r_2 = ab x \ \text{ for some } x \in R.$$
> > Rearrange: $b r_1 = ab x - a r_2 = a(bx - r_2)$. Hence $a \mid b r_1$. Now $a$ divides the product $b \cdot r_1$, and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$ means $a$ and $b$ share no [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|irreducible]] factor. Since $R$, being a Euclidean domain, is a unique factorisation domain, the irreducible factors of $a$ must all appear in $r_1$ — that is, $a \mid r_1$, so $r_1 + (a) = 0$.
> >
> > Symmetrically, from $b r_1 + a r_2 = ab x$ we get $a r_2 = ab x - b r_1 = b(ax - r_1)$, so $b \mid a r_2$; coprimality and unique factorisation give $b \mid r_2$, hence $r_2 + (b) = 0$.
> >
> > Therefore $(r_1 + (a),\ r_2 + (b)) = (0 + (a),\ 0 + (b))$, the zero element of $R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$. The kernel of $\phi$ is trivial, so $\phi$ is injective. $\blacksquare$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] and let $a, b \in R$ satisfy $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.
>
> **The map.** Define
> $$\phi : \frac{R}{(a)} \oplus \frac{R}{(b)} \longrightarrow \frac{R}{(ab)}, \qquad \phi\bigl(r_1 + (a),\ r_2 + (b)\bigr) = b r_1 + a r_2 + (ab).$$
>
> **Step 1 — $\phi$ is a well-defined module homomorphism.** By Lemma 1: if $r_1 = r_1' + xa$ and $r_2 = r_2' + yb$, then
> $$b r_1 + a r_2 = (b r_1' + a r_2') + (x + y)(ab),$$
> and $(x+y)(ab) \in (ab)$, so the value of $\phi$ is independent of representatives. Since addition and the $R$-action on $R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$ are coordinatewise and $br_1 + ar_2$ is $R$-linear in $(r_1, r_2)$, $\phi$ respects both operations. (This step uses nothing about coprimality.)
>
> **Step 2 — $\phi$ is surjective.** The module $R/(ab)$ is generated by $1 + (ab)$ (Lemma 2): every coset is $r \cdot (1 + (ab))$. As $R$ is Euclidean and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, the Euclidean algorithm yields a **Bézout relation** $ax + by = 1$ for some $x, y \in R$. Then
> $$\phi\bigl(y + (a),\ x + (b)\bigr) = by + ax + (ab) = (ax + by) + (ab) = 1 + (ab),$$
> so the generator is in the image. For an arbitrary $r + (ab)$, since $\phi$ is a homomorphism,
> $$\phi\bigl(r \cdot (y + (a),\ x + (b))\bigr) = r \cdot \phi(y + (a),\ x + (b)) = r \cdot (1 + (ab)) = r + (ab).$$
> Hence $\phi$ is surjective.
>
> **Step 3 — $\phi$ is injective.** Suppose $\phi(r_1 + (a),\ r_2 + (b)) = 0 + (ab)$, so $b r_1 + a r_2 = ab x$ for some $x \in R$. Then
> $$b r_1 = a b x - a r_2 = a(b x - r_2), \qquad\text{so}\qquad a \mid b r_1.$$
> Since $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, the elements $a$ and $b$ share no irreducible factor; $R$ is a Euclidean domain, hence a unique factorisation domain, so $a \mid b r_1$ forces $a \mid r_1$, i.e. $r_1 + (a) = 0$. Symmetrically, $a r_2 = b(a x - r_1)$ gives $b \mid a r_2$, and coprimality forces $b \mid r_2$, i.e. $r_2 + (b) = 0$. Thus the only element of the kernel is $(0 + (a),\ 0 + (b))$, and $\phi$ is injective.
>
> **Conclusion.** The map $\phi$ is a bijective module homomorphism, hence a module isomorphism:
> $$\frac{R}{(a)} \oplus \frac{R}{(b)} \;\cong\; \frac{R}{(ab)}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Diagonalisability of a linear operator from a coprime minimal polynomial.** Let $\alpha$ be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ over a field $F$, making $V$ an $F[X]$-module. If the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ factors into *distinct* linear factors $(X - \lambda_1) \cdots (X - \lambda_k)$, these factors are pairwise coprime, so the Chinese remainder theorem (iterated) splits the relevant cyclic $F[X]$-modules into a direct sum of one-dimensional pieces $F[X]/(X - \lambda_i)$ — on each of which $\alpha$ acts as the scalar $\lambda_i$. The non-obvious recognition is that "the minimal polynomial has distinct roots" is the coprimality hypothesis in disguise, and the resulting splitting *is* diagonalisability.

**Multiplicativity of the Euler totient function.** Specialise to $R = \mathbb{Z}$: for coprime $m, n$, the Chinese remainder theorem gives $\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, and this is in fact a *ring* isomorphism. Counting units on both sides — an element is a unit in a product ring exactly when each coordinate is — yields $\varphi_{\text{Euler}}(mn) = \varphi_{\text{Euler}}(m)\,\varphi_{\text{Euler}}(n)$. The non-obvious application is that a number-theoretic identity central to analytic number theory is a unit-count of the module isomorphism on this page.

**Idempotents and ring decompositions.** When $R/(ab) \cong R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$ is read as a decomposition of the ring $R/(ab)$, the two summands correspond to a pair of complementary idempotents $e, 1 - e$ — elements with $e^2 = e$ — built directly from the Bézout coefficients ($e = by + (ab)$ works, where $ax + by = 1$). The non-obvious recognition is that the abstract existence of a Bézout relation manufactures concrete idempotents, and idempotents are exactly what split a ring into a direct product.

**Interpolation over a Euclidean domain.** The classical statement "a polynomial is determined modulo $\prod (X - c_i)$ by its values at the distinct points $c_i$" is the Chinese remainder theorem for $R = F[X]$ with the pairwise-coprime moduli $X - c_i$. Lagrange interpolation is the explicit inverse isomorphism. The non-obvious application is that a staple of numerical analysis — polynomial interpolation — is an instance of the same coprime-splitting theorem, with the Bézout relations encoding the Lagrange basis polynomials.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|Primary Decomposition Theorem]]** — the principal downstream user. Primary decomposition is obtained by applying the Chinese remainder theorem, iterated, to each invariant factor $d_i$ of the [[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|structure theorem]]: factoring $d_i$ into prime powers and splitting $R/(d_i)$ accordingly. The Chinese remainder theorem is the precise tool that converts the invariant-factor form into the prime-power form.

- **[[Thm - Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a Euclidean Domain|Structure Theorem]]** — the supplier of the cyclic modules to be split. The structure theorem decomposes a finitely generated module into cyclic pieces $R/(d_i)$ with arbitrary moduli; the Chinese remainder theorem then breaks each piece down along the prime factorisation of its modulus.

- **Classical Chinese Remainder Theorem for Integers** — the special case $R = \mathbb{Z}$. For coprime integers $m, n$, the theorem reads $\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the original number-theoretic statement that a pair of congruences modulo coprime moduli has a unique simultaneous solution. The lecture notes describe the present proof as "the Chinese remainder theorem written in ring language".

- **[[Thm - Chinese Remainder Theorem for Cyclic Groups|Chinese Remainder Theorem for Cyclic Groups]]** — the same statement in the language of groups. $C_{mn} \cong C_m \times C_n$ for coprime $m, n$ is exactly this theorem with $R = \mathbb{Z}$, since a cyclic group $C_n$ is the $\mathbb{Z}$-module $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ and the direct product of abelian groups is their direct sum.

- **[[Thm - Isomorphism Theorems for Modules|First Isomorphism Theorem for Modules]]** — the standard alternative route. The theorem can also be proved by exhibiting the *reduction* map $R/(ab) \to R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$, $r + (ab) \mapsto (r + (a),\ r + (b))$, computing its kernel as $(a) \cap (b) = (ab)$ (using coprimality), and applying the first isomorphism theorem. The construction-and-verification proof on this page and the kernel-computation proof are two views of the same isomorphism.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Elementary-Divisor Form of the Structure Theorem *(from Modules over a PID)*
> Applying the Chinese remainder theorem to every invariant factor converts the structure theorem's invariant-factor form $\bigoplus R/(d_i) \oplus R^s$ into the *elementary-divisor* form $\bigoplus R/(p_j^{n_j}) \oplus R^s$, in which every cyclic summand is governed by a prime power. This finer form is the content of the [[Thm - Primary Decomposition Theorem|primary decomposition theorem]] and is the version used to produce the Jordan normal form.

> [!tip] Sheaves and Local-Global Principles *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The Chinese remainder theorem is the affine, finite shadow of a pervasive principle: information about a global object is the same as compatible information about its pieces over a covering. Coprime ideals correspond to disjoint closed subschemes, and the splitting $R/(ab) \cong R/(a) \oplus R/(b)$ is the statement that functions on a disjoint union are pairs of functions on the parts.
