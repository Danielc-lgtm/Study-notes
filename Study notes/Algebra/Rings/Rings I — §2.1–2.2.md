# Notation Registry

- $R, S, T$ — rings; in this course always commutative with a multiplicative identity
- $(R, +, \cdot, 0_R, 1_R)$ — the full data of a ring: the underlying set, addition, multiplication, the additive identity, the multiplicative identity
- $0_R$, or just $0$ — the additive identity of $R$; $1_R$, or just $1$ — the multiplicative identity of $R$
- $-r$ — the additive inverse of $r$; $r - s$ means $r + (-s)$
- $r^{-1}$ or $v$ — a multiplicative inverse of a unit $r$, when one exists
- $R^\times$ — the **group of units** of $R$, the elements possessing a multiplicative inverse
- $S \leq R$ — $S$ is a subring of $R$
- $I \trianglelefteq R$ (the source writes $I \triangleleft R$) — $I$ is an ideal of $R$
- $(a)$ or $aR$ — the **principal ideal** generated by $a$, the set $\{a \cdot r : r \in R\}$
- $(a_1, \dots, a_k)$ — the ideal generated by $a_1, \dots, a_k$, the set $\{a_1 r_1 + \cdots + a_k r_k : r_i \in R\}$
- $(A)$ — the ideal generated by a subset $A \subseteq R$
- $R[X]$ — the **polynomial ring** in one indeterminate $X$ with coefficients in $R$
- $R[[X]]$ — the ring of formal power series; $R[X, X^{-1}]$ — the ring of Laurent polynomials
- $\deg f$ — the degree of a polynomial $f$, the largest $m$ with $a_m \neq 0$
- $R \times S$ — the product ring, with coordinatewise addition and multiplication
- $\varphi : R \to S$ — a ring homomorphism
- $\ker \varphi$, $\operatorname{im} \varphi$ — the kernel and image of $\varphi$
- $R/I$ — the quotient ring (the set of additive cosets $r + I$)
- $r + I$ — the coset of $r$ in $R/I$; the quotient map sends $r \mapsto r + I$
- $R \cong S$ — $R$ and $S$ are isomorphic rings
- $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ — the usual number systems, now regarded as rings
- $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — the Gaussian integers $\{a + ib : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$; $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ — the integers modulo $n$
- $\iota : \mathbb{Z} \to R$ — the unique ring homomorphism from $\mathbb{Z}$ into any ring $R$
- $\operatorname{char}(R)$ — the characteristic of $R$, the non-negative generator of $\ker \iota$

# Motivation

A ring is what you get when you take a group and ask for *more arithmetic*. A group gives you one operation. A ring gives you two — addition and multiplication — interlocked by the distributive law, and the canonical example to keep in mind throughout is the one you have known the longest: $\mathbb{Z}$, the integers. In a ring you may add, subtract, and multiply, but you may **not** divide. That one missing capability is the entire personality of the subject. If you also demand division, you get a field; ring theory is precisely the study of what survives when division is taken away, and the surprise is how much does.

The rings in this course are all **commutative with a $1$**, and the reason is stated plainly in the source: such rings behave like "number systems", and we want to ask the questions of number theory inside them — divisibility, factorization, primes, congruences. But most commutative rings do not behave like $\mathbb{Z}$. The integers have unique factorization, a Euclidean algorithm, and the property that every ideal is generated by a single element; a generic ring has none of these. So the organizing question of the whole subject is the one the source names explicitly: take the special properties of $\mathbb{Z}$, ask which of them survive into an arbitrary ring, and ask how those properties relate to one another. This topic, §2.1–2.2, builds the vocabulary in which that question can even be posed.

The deep content is §2.2, and it is a deliberate re-run of group theory. There you learned that to understand a group you take it apart, and the tool was the quotient $G/N$ — but the quotient only made sense when $N$ was a **normal** subgroup. Rings have the exact same story with the nouns changed. The structure-preserving maps are [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphisms]]; the substructures you are *allowed to quotient by* are not subrings but a new and slightly stranger object, the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]]; and the quotient construction $R/I$ produces a new ring. The [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|isomorphism theorems]] reappear verbatim, because they were never really about groups — they are statements about any algebraic structure with a sensible notion of quotient. If you have internalized the group case, the ring case costs almost nothing to learn, and this topic page leans on that analogy at every step.

But there is one genuine difference, and it is worth flagging at the outset because it changes how you should *feel* about quotients. In finite group theory you quotient to make things **simpler**: $G/N$ is smaller than $G$, and the point is to reduce to a more tractable group. In ring theory you quotient to make things **more interesting**. The polynomial ring $\mathbb{R}[X]$ is, frankly, a dull object — an infinite list of coefficients. But $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2 + 1)$ is the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$, one of the richest objects in mathematics. Quotienting a ring is less like factoring and more like *imposing a relation*: you decree that $X^2 + 1 = 0$ and watch a new number system crystallize out. The headline application of the isomorphism theorems, accordingly, is not "simplify" but **identify**: given an explicitly constructed quotient $R/I$, discover its true name. That single skill — recognizing $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)$ as $\mathbb{C}$ in disguise — is what §2.2 is built to install.

# Concept Map

## §2.1 Definitions and Examples

- **[[Def - Ring]]**
	- A **ring** is a quintuple $(R, +, \cdot, 0_R, 1_R)$ in which $(R, +, 0_R)$ is an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]], multiplication $\cdot$ is associative with two-sided identity $1_R$, and multiplication distributes over addition on both sides. In this course every ring is additionally **commutative** ($a \cdot b = b \cdot a$) and has a multiplicative identity, which not all authors require. Finite sums and products make sense by induction, but infinite ones do not — convergence is not a ring-theoretic notion. The canonical example is $\mathbb{Z}$; the smallest example is the zero ring $\{0\}$, in which $1_R = 0_R$, and a ring is non-zero exactly when $1_R \neq 0_R$.

- **[[Def - Subring]]**
	- A subset $S \subseteq R$ is a **subring**, written $S \leq R$, if it contains both $0_R$ and $1_R$ and is closed under $+$, $-$, and $\cdot$, so that the restricted operations make $S$ a ring in its own right. The tower $\mathbb{Z} \leq \mathbb{Q} \leq \mathbb{R} \leq \mathbb{C}$ is the prototype; the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + ib : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ are a subring of $\mathbb{C}$. The crucial contrast with the coming notion of [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] is the closure demand: a subring need only absorb products of *its own* elements, whereas an ideal must absorb products with *every* element of $R$. Because a subring must contain $1_R$, a proper [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] is never a subring.

- **[[Def - Unit and Field]]**
	- An element $u \in R$ is a **unit** if there exists $v \in R$ with $u \cdot v = 1_R$; whether $u$ is a unit depends on $R$ and not on $u$ alone, since $2$ is a unit in $\mathbb{Q}$ but not in $\mathbb{Z}$. A **field** is a non-zero ring in which *every* non-zero element is a unit — a ring where division (by anything non-zero) is restored. Thus $\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ are fields while $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ are not, and $\mathbb{Q}[\sqrt 2]$ is. The units of $R$ form an abelian group $R^\times$ under multiplication, and fields are exactly the rings with $R^\times = R \setminus \{0\}$.

- **[[Def - Polynomial Ring]]**
	- For a ring $R$, a **polynomial** is a formal expression $f = a_0 + a_1 X + \cdots + a_n X^n$ with $a_i \in R$, and the **polynomial ring** $R[X]$ is the set of all such polynomials with the familiar addition and the convolution product $f \cdot g = \sum_i \big(\sum_{j} a_j b_{i-j}\big) X^i$. A polynomial is a *sequence of coefficients*, not a function: in $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[X]$ the polynomial $X^2 + X$ is non-zero yet induces the zero function. The **degree** is the largest $m$ with $a_m \neq 0$, and $f$ is **monic** if $a_m = 1$; $R$ embeds in $R[X]$ as the constant polynomials. Relatives include the formal power series $R[[X]]$ (where $1 - X$ becomes a unit) and the Laurent polynomials $R[X, X^{-1}]$.

## §2.2 Homomorphisms, Ideals, Quotients and Isomorphisms

- **[[Def - Ring Homomorphism]]**
	- A **ring homomorphism** $\varphi : R \to S$ is a function preserving both operations and both identities: $\varphi(r_1 + r_2) = \varphi(r_1) + \varphi(r_2)$, $\varphi(r_1 r_2) = \varphi(r_1)\varphi(r_2)$, $\varphi(0_R) = 0_S$, and $\varphi(1_R) = 1_S$. The last condition is a genuine extra demand — unlike for groups, where preserving the identity is automatic — and it is what makes the inclusion $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$, $n \mapsto (n, 0)$, *fail* to be a ring homomorphism. An **isomorphism** is a bijective homomorphism, and its inverse is automatically a homomorphism. The **kernel** $\ker\varphi = \{r : \varphi(r) = 0_S\}$ and **image** $\operatorname{im}\varphi$ are defined as for groups, and $\varphi$ is injective if and only if $\ker\varphi = \{0_R\}$.

- **[[Def - Ideal]]**
	- A subset $I \subseteq R$ is an **ideal**, written $I \trianglelefteq R$, if it is a subgroup of $(R, +, 0_R)$ and satisfies *strong closure*: whenever $a \in I$ and $b \in R$, the product $a \cdot b$ lies in $I$. Strong closure is the defining gap from a [[Def - Subring|subring]] — a subring absorbs products of its own elements, an ideal absorbs products with the whole ring — and it is the precise ring analogue of the way a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] is closed not just under internal multiplication but under conjugation by everything. An ideal containing a unit is all of $R$, so a **proper ideal** contains no units and no $1_R$, hence is never a subring. The principal ideal $(a) = aR$ and the finitely-generated ideal $(a_1, \dots, a_k)$ are the standard constructions; in $\mathbb{Z}$ *every* ideal is principal, of the form $n\mathbb{Z}$.

- **[[Def - Quotient Ring]]**
	- For an ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$, the **quotient ring** $R/I$ is the set of additive cosets $r + I$ with $0_{R/I} = 0_R + I$, $1_{R/I} = 1_R + I$, and operations $(r_1 + I) + (r_2 + I) = (r_1 + r_2) + I$ and $(r_1 + I)(r_2 + I) = r_1 r_2 + I$. Addition is well-defined because $I$ is an additive subgroup; multiplication is well-defined *exactly because of strong closure* — perturbing representatives by $a_1, a_2 \in I$ changes the product by $r_1 a_2 + r_2 a_1 + a_1 a_2$, all of which lie in $I$. The quotient map $r \mapsto r + I$ is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel $I$. Forming $R/I$ amounts to *imposing the relation* "$a = 0$ for all $a \in I$": $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is arithmetic mod $n$, and $\mathbb{C}[X]/(X) \cong \mathbb{C}$.

- **[[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings]]**
	- For any ring homomorphism $\varphi : R \to S$, the kernel $\ker\varphi$ is an ideal of $R$ and the map $r + \ker\varphi \mapsto \varphi(r)$ is a ring isomorphism $R/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$. Every homomorphism therefore factors as a quotient projection followed by an injection, just as for groups; the additive and bijectivity content is inherited free from the group theorem, and only multiplicativity needs a one-line check. This is the master tool for **identifying a quotient**: to prove $R/I \cong T$, build a surjective homomorphism $R \to T$ with kernel exactly $I$. Its signature application is $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$ via evaluation at $i$.

- **[[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem for Rings]]**
	- If $R \leq S$ is a subring and $J \trianglelefteq S$ is an ideal, then $R \cap J$ is an ideal of $R$, the set $R + J$ has $(R + J)/J = \{r + J : r \in R\}$ as a subring of $S/J$, and $R/(R \cap J) \cong (R + J)/J$. It compares the subring $R$ with its image under the quotient map $S \to S/J$, and is proved in one stroke by feeding the composite $R \hookrightarrow S \to S/J$ into the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]]: the kernel of that composite is $R \cap J$ and its image is $(R+J)/J$. Note the asymmetry — $J$ must be an ideal, $R$ need only be a subring.

- **[[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem for Rings]]**
	- If $I \trianglelefteq R$ and $J \trianglelefteq R$ with $I \subseteq J$, then $J/I$ is an ideal of $R/I$ and $(R/I)\big/(J/I) \cong R/J$. Quotienting in two stages — first kill $I$, then kill the image $J/I$ — yields the same ring as quotienting by the larger ideal $J$ in one step. It is the cancellation law for quotient rings, the analogue of $\frac{a/c}{b/c} = \frac{a}{b}$, and like the second theorem it drops straight out of the first applied to the map $r + I \mapsto r + J$, whose kernel is $J/I$.

- **[[Thm - Ideal Correspondence]]**
	- For an ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$, the subrings of the quotient $R/I$ correspond bijectively with the subrings of $R$ that **contain $I$**, via $L \mapsto \{x \in R : x + I \in L\}$, and the very same formula restricts to a bijection between the *ideals* of $R/I$ and the *ideals* of $R$ containing $I$. It is the verbatim ring analogue of the subgroup correspondence, with the normal-subgroup correspondence becoming the ideal correspondence. Its strategic value is the reverse direction: since interesting quotients carry interesting ideals, the correspondence lets you produce previously invisible ideals of $R$ from ideals discovered upstairs in a more transparent quotient.

- **[[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials]]**
	- If $F$ is a field and $f, g \in F[X]$ with $g \neq 0$, there exist $q, r \in F[X]$ with $f = gq + r$ and $\deg r < \deg g$. It is the polynomial mirror of integer division, with the degree playing the role of absolute value as the measure of size; the proof is induction on $\deg f$, repeatedly subtracting the monomial multiple $a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} g$ to cancel the leading term — a step that *requires* $F$ to be a field so that $b_m^{-1}$ exists. Its consequence is that every ideal of $F[X]$ is principal, and concretely it lets you reduce any coset in $F[X]/(g)$ to a unique representative of degree less than $\deg g$.

- **[[Def - Characteristic of a Ring]]**
	- For any ring $R$ there is a **unique** ring homomorphism $\iota : \mathbb{Z} \to R$, forced because $\iota$ must send $1_\mathbb{Z} \mapsto 1_R$ and then $n \mapsto 1_R + \cdots + 1_R$; its kernel is an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$, hence equals $n\mathbb{Z}$ for a unique $n \geq 0$, and that $n$ is the **characteristic** $\operatorname{char}(R)$. Thus $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ have characteristic $0$, while $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic $n$, so every natural number is the characteristic of some ring. The characteristic is used sparingly, but fields of non-zero characteristic are a fertile source of examples and counterexamples.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Prime and Maximal Ideals *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> Now that you have ideals and quotient rings, you can grade ideals by the *kind* of ring they produce. An ideal $P$ is **prime** exactly when $R/P$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] (no zero-divisors), and **maximal** exactly when $R/P$ is a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]]. This translates two delicate properties of $I$ into two familiar properties of the quotient, and is the entry point to commutative algebra and algebraic geometry. See [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Modules and the Isomorphism Theorems Again *(from Module Theory)*
> A **module** over a ring $R$ is an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]] carrying a compatible $R$-action — the ring analogue of a vector space, with the field of scalars relaxed to a ring. The submodule, quotient module, and three isomorphism theorems are this very page re-run a third time, with "ideal" replaced by "submodule". An ideal of $R$ is precisely a submodule of $R$ viewed as a module over itself. See [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2]].

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of an introductory ring theory section pursue a small, recognizable set of goals. The dominant one is **identifying a ring up to isomorphism**: you are handed an explicitly constructed quotient $R/I$, or a ring defined by some construction, and asked which familiar ring it equals — typically the answer is a number system like $\mathbb{C}$, a field $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, a product, or the zero ring. A second target is **deciding whether a given subset is an ideal, a subring, or neither**, which is a closure check but a subtle one, because the strong-closure axiom of an ideal is easy to confuse with the weaker closure of a subring. A third is **classifying the ideals of a specific ring** — proving every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ or of $F[X]$ is principal, or listing the ideals of a small finite ring. A fourth is **determining the units** of a ring, equivalently asking how far $R$ is from being a field. A fifth is **computing an invariant**: the characteristic, the degree of a polynomial, the order of a finite ring. These targets recur because each pins down a different facet of a ring: you understand a commutative ring once you know its ideals, its quotients (and their names), its units, and its characteristic.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The assumptions are equally stereotyped. **A ring is presented concretely** — as a polynomial ring, a quotient, a product, a ring of functions, or a number system — and the first move is to read off which axioms are cheap to verify and which structure is visible. **A homomorphism is given or can be built**, and the instant you have one you also have an ideal (its kernel) and a quotient (its image), with the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] standing ready to convert the homomorphism into an isomorphism. **An ideal is given by generators**, $(a)$ or $(a_1, \dots, a_k)$, so that membership becomes the solvable question "is this element an $R$-combination of the generators". **The ground ring is a field**, which switches on the [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]] in $F[X]$ and makes every non-zero element invertible. **A polynomial relation is imposed**, as in $R[X]/(f)$, and the Euclidean algorithm then supplies unique low-degree coset representatives. The recurring discipline is to route a source to a target: a presented quotient routes through a constructed homomorphism and the first isomorphism theorem to an identification; a field ground ring routes through the Euclidean algorithm to a principal-ideal or unique-representative conclusion; generators route through strong closure to an ideal-membership decision. The [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything here is self-contained: a reader with no ring theory background should be able to follow each operation from the description alone.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Check the ring axioms by leaning on a known ring.** To verify a candidate set is a ring, you almost never check all the axioms from scratch — you exhibit it as a subring of, or a quotient of, or a product of, a ring you already trust. The associativity and distributivity then come for free, and only the closure conditions need attention. The trigger is any "show this is a ring" request: ask first whether the set sits inside $\mathbb{C}$, inside $R[X]$, or arises as $R/I$ or $R \times S$.

2. **Distinguish subring closure from ideal strong closure.** A [[Def - Subring|subring]] must contain $1_R$ and absorb products of *its own* elements; an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] must absorb products with *every* element of $R$ and (being proper) contains no $1_R$. When testing a subset, decide which object you want and check the matching closure: for an ideal, take a general $a \in I$ and a general $b \in R$ and confirm $ab \in I$. The trigger is any "is this a subring / an ideal" question; the strong-closure check is the discriminating step.

3. **Build a ring homomorphism to expose structure.** To understand a ring $R$, map it to a ring you understand. A [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]] $\varphi : R \to S$ immediately hands you an ideal $\ker\varphi$ and a subring $\operatorname{im}\varphi$. The most reliable homomorphism to keep in stock is **evaluation**: $R[X] \to R$, $f \mapsto f(c)$, sending a polynomial to its value at a fixed $c$. The trigger is any request to identify a quotient or to produce an ideal of a polynomial ring.

4. **Apply the first isomorphism theorem to name a quotient.** If you can write a *surjective* homomorphism $\varphi : R \to T$ whose kernel is exactly $I$, then $R/I \cong T$. This is how an abstractly defined quotient acquires a concrete identity. The trigger: the problem says "show $R/I \cong \ldots$" or "what is this quotient". The skill lies entirely in guessing the target $T$ and the map — and the guess is usually read off from the relation $I$ imposes (kill $X^2+1$, and $X$ behaves like $i$, so map to $\mathbb{C}$).

5. **Impose a relation by quotienting.** Forming $R/I$ is the act of *decreeing* that every element of $I$ equals zero. Unlike a group quotient, which shrinks toward something simpler, a ring quotient often manufactures a richer ring: $\mathbb{R}[X]$ is dull, $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1) = \mathbb{C}$ is not. The trigger is wanting a ring in which some equation holds — adjoin a root, force a congruence, collapse a coefficient — and the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] then lets facts about $R/I$ be lifted back to $R$.

6. **Reduce a coset to a canonical representative via the Euclidean algorithm.** In $F[X]/(g)$ with $F$ a field, every coset $f + (g)$ equals $r + (g)$ for the unique remainder $r$ from $f = gq + r$ with $\deg r < \deg g$. This converts the unwieldy infinite set of representatives into a finite-dimensional space of low-degree polynomials. The trigger is any computation inside a quotient of a polynomial ring over a field; division-with-remainder is what makes such quotients concrete.

7. **Generate an ideal from elements you possess.** From any element $a$ you obtain the principal ideal $(a) = aR$; from any finite set you obtain $(a_1, \dots, a_k) = \{\sum a_i r_i\}$. The trigger: you have located elements that *ought* to vanish in some quotient, or you want the smallest ideal containing certain elements so as to feed it into a quotient or the isomorphism theorems. Recognizing a given ideal as principal is often the key simplification.

8. **Exploit that a unit in an ideal forces the whole ring.** If a proper ideal $I$ ever contains a [[Def - Unit and Field|unit]] $u$, then strong closure gives $1_R = u \cdot u^{-1} \in I$, hence $I = R$. So proper ideals are exactly the unit-free additive subgroups closed under strong multiplication. The trigger: you want to prove an ideal is the whole ring (find a unit in it) or, conversely, prove an ideal is proper (show it avoids every unit) — and in a field this dichotomy means the only ideals are $\{0\}$ and the whole field.

9. **Use uniqueness of the map out of $\mathbb{Z}$.** Every ring receives exactly one homomorphism $\iota : \mathbb{Z} \to R$, because $1_\mathbb{Z} \mapsto 1_R$ is forced and the rest follows by additivity. Its kernel is $n\mathbb{Z}$, and $n = \operatorname{char}(R)$. The trigger is any question about how the integers sit inside $R$, about whether $1_R$ has finite additive order, or about characteristic; this also forbids exotic homomorphisms — there is no choice in how $\mathbb{Z}$ maps anywhere.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Forming the quotient $R/S$ by a subring $S$
> Cosets of an additive subgroup always partition $R$, so it is tempting to quotient by any [[Def - Subring|subring]]. But the *multiplication* $(r_1 + S)(r_2 + S) = r_1 r_2 + S$ is well-defined only when $S$ has strong closure, that is, only when $S$ is an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]]. Take $\mathbb{Z} \leq \mathbb{Q}$: perturbing the representative $0$ by $1 \in \mathbb{Z}$ and multiplying by $\tfrac12$ moves you out of $\mathbb{Z}$, so the product of cosets depends on the representatives. Worse, a proper ideal contains no $1_R$ while a subring must — so the two notions barely overlap. Strong closure, not mere subgroup closure, is the exact admission ticket to quotienting.

> [!warning] 2. Treating a polynomial as the function it induces
> It is natural to think of $f \in R[X]$ as the function $c \mapsto f(c)$ and to declare two polynomials equal when they agree everywhere. They are not the same: a [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial]] is a sequence of coefficients, a *formal* object. In $(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})[X]$ the polynomial $X^2 + X$ has non-zero coefficients, hence is non-zero, yet $f(0) = f(1) = 0$, so it induces the identically-zero function. Over an infinite field the function does determine the polynomial, which is why the confusion survives undergraduate calculus — but over a finite ring it is simply false, and the whole theory of $R[X]$ depends on the formal viewpoint.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming every ideal is principal
> The integers and $F[X]$ both have the property that every ideal is generated by a single element, and after enough examples it is tempting to expect this always. It does not hold in general: in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ the ideal $(2, X)$ of polynomials with even constant term needs *two* generators and is not principal — no single polynomial generates it. The principal-ideal property is special, traceable to the presence of a [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]], and pinning down exactly which rings enjoy it is a central project of [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4]] and [[Rings III — §2.5–2.6]].

> [!warning] 4. Expecting a quotient map to preserve units, or non-zero-ness
> Because the quotient map $R \to R/I$ is a homomorphism, it is tempting to assume it reflects properties faithfully. It does not. A non-zero element can map to zero (that is the entire point of a kernel), a non-unit can become a unit — $2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ is no unit, but its image in $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$ is, with inverse $2$ — and a perfectly good ring can collapse: if $I = R$ then $R/I$ is the zero ring. Quotienting is *controlled forgetting*; it changes which elements are zero and which are invertible, and that is exactly why it produces new rings.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic are won at the moment you classify what you are being asked, so begin there. Almost every exercise is one of five types, and each has a characteristic assumption pattern and a characteristic route to the answer.

If the problem **asks you to identify a quotient** — to attach a concrete name to $R/I$ — then you are looking at the central problem of the chapter, and the route runs through the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]]. Do not try to understand $R/I$ by staring at cosets. Instead, guess the answer $T$, construct a surjective ring homomorphism $\varphi : R \to T$, and verify its kernel is exactly $I$; the theorem then certifies $R/I \cong T$ for free. The whole difficulty is concentrated in the guess, and the guidance is this: a quotient $R/I$ *imposes the relation* that everything in $I$ vanishes, so ask what the elements of $R$ become once that relation holds. For $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)$ the relation is $X^2 = -1$, so $X$ behaves exactly like $i$, and the homomorphism is evaluation at $i$ landing in $\mathbb{C}$. For $\mathbb{C}[X]/(X)$ the relation is $X = 0$, so a polynomial collapses to its constant term, and the map is evaluation at $0$ landing in $\mathbb{C}$. The pattern "kill a polynomial, adjoin a root, evaluate" handles a large fraction of these problems. When the problem instead hands you a subring and an ideal of an ambient ring, the [[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|second isomorphism theorem]] is the pre-packaged identification; when it hands you nested ideals, the [[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|third]] is.

If the problem **asks whether a subset is an ideal or a subring**, the strategy is to first decide which object is in play and then check the matching closure condition, because that is where all the discriminating power lives. Both objects are additive subgroups, so that part is routine. The fork is multiplicative: a [[Def - Subring|subring]] must contain $1_R$ and need only be closed under products of its own elements, whereas an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] must absorb multiplication by *every* element of $R$ and, if proper, contains no $1_R$. So to test for an ideal, take a *general* $a$ in the set and a *general* $b$ in the whole ring and ask whether $ab$ stays inside; to test for a subring, only multiply elements already in the set, but also confirm $1_R$ is present. A frequent shortcut is to recognize the candidate as the kernel of an obvious homomorphism — the set of polynomials with zero constant term is the kernel of evaluation at $0$ — which proves it is an ideal with no closure computation at all.

If the problem **asks you to classify the ideals of a ring**, the productive assumption to look for is the presence of a division-with-remainder, that is, a [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]]. The argument that every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $n\mathbb{Z}$ is the template: take a non-zero ideal $I$, let $n$ be its smallest positive element, and use division-with-remainder to show every other element of $I$ is a multiple of $n$ — the remainder $r = m - qn$ lies in $I$ and is smaller than $n$, so it must be zero. The identical argument with degree replacing absolute value shows every ideal of $F[X]$ is principal. Whenever a ring comes with a notion of "size" that can be reduced by subtracting multiples of a generator, this argument runs, and recognizing that is the key decision.

If the problem **asks for the units of a ring** or whether the ring is a field, work element by element and remember that being a unit is relative to the ring. In $R[X]$, the unit question is settled by degree: $(1-X)$ is not a unit because multiplying by anything non-zero raises the degree past $0$, so the product can never be $1$. In $R[[X]]$ the same $1 - X$ *is* a unit, with inverse the geometric series, because power series have no top degree to obstruct the cancellation. For a quotient, an element is a unit precisely when its coset has an inverse coset, and this can switch on relative to $R$ — non-units of $R$ routinely become units in $R/I$. The strategic fact is that proper ideals avoid all units, so a field, having only the ideals $\{0\}$ and itself, is the extreme case.

Finally, a meta-strategy threads through all of the above: **a quotient is a relation made into a ring, and the isomorphism theorems are how you read its consequences.** If you can find any ideal $I$, then $R/I$ is the ring in which the relations of $I$ hold, the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] guarantees that the ideals of $R/I$ are faithful images of the ideals of $R$ above $I$, and the [[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|third isomorphism theorem]] lets you quotient in stages. The reason this viewpoint is always worth adopting is that it converts every structural question about the abstract object $R/I$ into a concrete question about $R$ and the generators of $I$ — and concrete questions are the ones you can actually answer.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|First Isomorphism Theorem]]**: $R/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$. This is the workhorse for *identification*. The recognizable setup is "here is a quotient $R/I$, what ring is it?" or "show these two rings are isomorphic". The common pattern is to combine it with a cleverly chosen homomorphism — almost always evaluation of a polynomial at a well-chosen point — so that an opaque quotient becomes a familiar number system in a single step. It is also the proof engine for the second and third isomorphism theorems, so the effort spent internalizing it pays compound interest. Recognize its applicability whenever you can find a structure-preserving map whose kernel is exactly the ideal you are quotienting by; the art is reading the target ring off the relation the ideal imposes.

- **[[Def - Ideal|Ideals]]** (as the things you can quotient by): a subset is an ideal exactly when it has strong closure, equivalently exactly when it is a kernel. The reusable move is the equivalence itself — to *produce* an ideal, build a homomorphism and take its kernel; to *use* an ideal, remember it is the kernel of the quotient map $R \to R/I$ and that $a \in I, b \in R \Rightarrow ab \in I$ is a licence to absorb any ring element into $I$. Ideals are the hinge on which every quotient turns, and the discipline of checking strong closure (not the weaker subring closure) is the single most common source of correct-versus-incorrect answers in this topic.

- **[[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials]]**: over a field $F$, any $f, g$ satisfy $f = gq + r$ with $\deg r < \deg g$. Its two standard uses are structural and computational. Structurally, it forces every ideal of $F[X]$ to be principal — the smallest-degree-element argument — which is the prototype for the principal-ideal-domain theory of later topics. Computationally, it gives every coset in $F[X]/(g)$ a unique representative of degree below $\deg g$, turning an infinite quotient into a finite-dimensional, hands-on object. Reach for it whenever a polynomial ring over a field appears and you need either to understand its ideals or to compute inside a quotient.

- **[[Def - Quotient Ring|The quotient construction]]** $R/I$: the set of cosets $r + I$ made into a ring. It is more than a theorem — it is a *machine* for building new rings, and its reusability is that almost every interesting commutative ring in algebra is presented as a quotient of a polynomial ring. The typical use is to *impose a relation*: to construct a ring where some equation holds, write down the polynomial ring, form the ideal generated by (relation minus zero), and quotient. Recognizing that an unfamiliar ring is secretly such a quotient is often the move that unlocks a problem, because it routes you straight to the first isomorphism theorem.

- **[[Def - Unit and Field|The unit dichotomy in fields]]**: a field has exactly two ideals, $\{0\}$ and itself, because any ideal containing a non-zero element contains a unit and hence everything. This is constantly reused as a *rigidity* statement: a ring homomorphism out of a field is either injective or zero (its kernel, being an ideal, is one of the two), the only quotients of a field are the field itself and the zero ring, and "the ground ring is a field" is the hypothesis that switches on the Euclidean algorithm. Whenever a field appears as a coefficient ring or a domain, this dichotomy is the reason the surrounding theory becomes clean.

# Bridges

1. **Group theory — the isomorphism theorems are the same theorems.** A ring is, by axiom, an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]] $(R, +, 0_R)$ carrying a compatible multiplication, and this is not a loose analogy but the literal mechanism by which §2.2 works. The kernel of a ring homomorphism is an additive subgroup, so its well-definedness and the injectivity criterion $\ker\varphi = \{0\}$ are *inherited verbatim* from group theory; the proofs of all three ring isomorphism theorems explicitly say "the additive and bijective content comes free from the group case, we only check multiplicativity". The [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] is the ring counterpart of the [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] — both are defined by a closure condition stronger than mere substructure (strong closure versus conjugation-invariance), and both are characterized as exactly the kernels, hence exactly the things you may quotient by. The whole of [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]] is the rehearsal for this chapter.

2. **Linear algebra and module theory — vector spaces are rings' close cousins.** A vector space over a field is an abelian group with a scalar action, and a [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|module]] over a ring is the same with the field relaxed to a ring. The isomorphism theorems run a third time in that setting, with "ideal" replaced by "submodule"; and the connection is tighter than analogy, because an ideal of $R$ is *precisely* a submodule of $R$ regarded as a module over itself. When $R$ is a field $F$, the rings $F[X]/(g)$ from this chapter are also finite-dimensional $F$-vector spaces — the Euclidean algorithm exhibits $\{1, X, \dots, X^{\deg g - 1}\}$ as a basis — so $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$ is simultaneously a ring isomorphism and a statement that $\mathbb{C}$ is two-dimensional over $\mathbb{R}$.

3. **Number theory — modular arithmetic is a quotient ring.** The ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is literally the [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient]] of $\mathbb{Z}$ by the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $n\mathbb{Z}$, and "reduction mod $n$" is the quotient homomorphism — the same construction that group theory used for the cyclic group, now carrying its multiplication too. The classification of the ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ as exactly the $n\mathbb{Z}$ is the ring-theoretic form of the fact that the integers are ordered and admit division with remainder, and the [[Def - Characteristic of a Ring|characteristic]] of a ring is read off from the kernel of the unique map $\mathbb{Z} \to R$. The first isomorphism theorem applied to $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/m \times \mathbb{Z}/n$ is the Chinese remainder theorem, and the question of which $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ are fields — exactly the prime moduli — is the prototype of the prime-ideal theory in [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4]].

4. **Algebraic geometry — rings as functions, quotients as subspaces.** The source's example of the ring of all continuous functions $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is the first hint of a profound dictionary: a space is studied through its ring of functions, and *imposing a geometric constraint corresponds to passing to a quotient ring*. Forcing functions to vanish on a subset is forming the quotient by the ideal of functions vanishing there. This is the seed of the correspondence between affine varieties and quotients of polynomial rings, in which points become [[Def - Ideal|maximal ideals]] and the geometry of a variety is recovered from the algebra of its coordinate ring — the subject that the prime and maximal ideals unlocked above are built to formalize.

# Insights

**To understand a ring, understand its ideals and its quotients.** A ring presented as a set with two operation tables is unreadable. The productive viewpoint — the one §2.2 exists to install — is that a ring is understood through the homomorphisms into and out of it, and a homomorphism out of $R$ is the same datum as an ideal (its kernel) together with a quotient (its image). So the structural content of a ring is carried by its lattice of ideals and the family of quotient rings they produce. This is why the first questions to ask about any new ring are "what are its ideals?" and "what do the quotients look like?", and it is why the entire later theory — prime ideals, maximal ideals, principal ideal domains, Noetherian rings — is organized around ideals rather than elements. The elements of a ring are the scenery; the ideals are the structure.

**The true name of "ideal" is "kernel", and the true name of a quotient ring is the relation it imposes.** The textbook definition of an ideal — an additive subgroup with strong closure — is the right thing to *check* but the wrong thing to *picture*. Strong closure is a verification criterion; it tells you nothing about why the concept matters. The operational meaning is that the ideals of $R$ are exactly the kernels of ring homomorphisms out of $R$, equivalently exactly the subsets you are permitted to quotient by. So when you read "$I$ is an ideal", do not picture a closure computation — picture the quotient map $R \to R/I$. And the quotient $R/I$ itself has a true name that is almost never its written form: $R/I$ is *the ring in which the relations of $I$ hold*. The string "$\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)$" is just a recipe; its true name is $\mathbb{C}$, the ring you get by adjoining a square root of $-1$. The job of the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] is to take you from the recipe to the true name, and a problem is solved when you have made that translation.

**Group quotients simplify; ring quotients enrich.** This is the one place where the group analogy, otherwise so faithful, points the wrong way emotionally, and it is worth holding onto. In finite group theory you quotient by a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] to obtain a *smaller, simpler* group — the quotient is a reduction, a step toward the simple-group atoms. In ring theory the same construction usually moves in the opposite direction: you quotient to obtain a *more interesting* ring. The polynomial ring $\mathbb{R}[X]$ is an inert object, an infinite sequence of coefficients with no surprises; its quotient $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)$ is $\mathbb{C}$, with its rotations and roots of unity and fundamental theorem of algebra. The reason is structural. A group quotient *erases* a distinction and so loses information. A ring quotient *imposes a relation*, and a new relation among the generators can force a rich and unexpected arithmetic into existence — the elements were already there as cosets, but the relation makes them interact in a new way. The [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] then turns this into a working tool: because interesting quotients carry interesting ideals, you can deliberately quotient down to a transparent ring, find ideals there, and pull them back to discover ideals of $R$ you would never have spotted directly.

**The whole subject is the project of measuring how far a ring is from $\mathbb{Z}$.** The source states the program explicitly, and it is worth keeping in view as the thread that ties §2.1–2.2 to everything that follows. The integers have a list of good properties: division with remainder, every ideal principal, unique factorization into primes, no zero-divisors. A general commutative ring has *some* subset of these, and ring theory is the systematic study of which properties travel together and which can be present without the others. This chapter supplies the apparatus for the study — ideals to phrase "principal", quotients and homomorphisms to compare rings, the [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]] as the property that $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[X]$ share and that *forces* the principal-ideal property. The later topics name the resulting classes of ring — Euclidean domains, principal ideal domains, unique factorization domains, integral domains — and prove the implications between them. Every one of those classes is a different answer to the question "how much of $\mathbb{Z}$ does this ring keep?", and the definitions on this page are the words in which the question is asked.
