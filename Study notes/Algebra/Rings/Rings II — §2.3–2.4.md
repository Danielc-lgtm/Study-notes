# Notation Registry

- $R, S, T$ — rings; in this course always commutative with a multiplicative identity, and from §2.4 onward almost always an integral domain
- $0_R$, or just $0$ — the additive identity; $1_R$, or just $1$ — the multiplicative identity
- $R^\times$ — the group of units of $R$
- $I \trianglelefteq R$ — $I$ is an ideal of $R$ (the source writes $I \triangleleft R$)
- $R/I$ — the quotient ring; $r + I$ — the coset of $r$
- $(a)$ or $aR$ — the principal ideal generated by $a$, the set $\{a r : r \in R\}$
- $(a_1, \dots, a_k)$ — the ideal generated by $a_1, \dots, a_k$
- $a \mid b$ — "$a$ divides $b$": there exists $c \in R$ with $b = ac$; equivalently $(b) \subseteq (a)$
- "$a$ and $b$ are associates" — $a = bc$ for a unit $c$; equivalently $(a) = (b)$; equivalently $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$
- $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ or $\operatorname{Field of fractions}(R)$ — the field of fractions of an integral domain $R$ (the source writes $F$); $\tfrac{a}{b}$ — the element it represents
- $\operatorname{char}(R)$ — the characteristic of $R$
- $N(z)$ — the **norm** $z \bar z = |z|^2$ on a subring of $\mathbb{C}$; on $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$
- $\varphi : R \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ — a **Euclidean function** on a Euclidean domain
- $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ — the usual number systems as rings; $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — the Gaussian integers
- $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}] = \{a + b\sqrt{-5} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ — the running counterexample of the chapter
- $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ — the polynomial ring in $n$ indeterminates, defined inductively as $((R[X_1])[X_2])\cdots[X_n]$
- ED, PID, UFD — Euclidean domain, principal ideal domain, unique factorization domain
- $\gcd(a_1, \dots, a_n)$, $\operatorname{lcm}(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ — greatest common divisor and least common multiple, each defined only up to associates

# Motivation

[[Rings I — §2.1–2.2]] ended with a program rather than a result: the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ have a list of excellent arithmetic properties — no zero-divisors, division with remainder, every ideal principal, unique factorization into primes — and a general commutative ring has only *some* of them. Ring theory is the study of which of these properties travel together. This chapter is the first serious instalment of that program. It does two things. §2.3 isolates the single most basic of $\mathbb{Z}$'s properties, the absence of zero-divisors, names the [[Def - Ring|rings]] that have it (**integral domains**), and shows that this one property is enough to make a ring "fraction-able" — every integral domain sits inside a field of fractions exactly the way $\mathbb{Z}$ sits inside $\mathbb{Q}$. §2.4 then tackles factorization head-on and discovers that the naive picture inherited from $\mathbb{Z}$ — "every number is a unique product of primes" — is *false* in general, and builds the hierarchy of ever-better rings on which more and more of it becomes true.

The reason to care about zero-divisors is concrete and immediate. In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ the elements $2$ and $3$ are both non-zero, yet $2 \cdot 3 = 0$. A ring where this happens has no hope of supporting a sensible theory of factorization: cancellation fails (from $ax = ay$ you cannot conclude $x = y$), the equation $x^2 = x$ acquires spurious roots, and "$a$ divides $b$" stops behaving the way divisibility should. An [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] is exactly the ring in which this pathology is outlawed, and the payoff is large for the price: cancellation returns, and — the headline theorem of §2.3 — every integral domain can be enlarged to a field by formally inverting its non-zero elements. The construction of the [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] is nothing but the construction of $\mathbb{Q}$ from $\mathbb{Z}$ — pairs $(a,b)$ thought of as $a/b$, with $(a,b) \sim (c,d)$ when $ad = bc$ — run in complete generality. This is the chapter's first instance of a recurring move: take a familiar construction on $\mathbb{Z}$, identify the *minimal* property that makes it work, and run it on every ring that has that property.

The deep content is §2.4, and it begins with a genuine surprise. In $\mathbb{Z}$ the irreducible numbers (those with no non-trivial factorization) are exactly the prime numbers (those satisfying Euclid's lemma, $p \mid ab \Rightarrow p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$), and every integer factors uniquely into them. It is tempting to assume this holds everywhere. It does not. In the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the number $6$ has *two* genuinely different factorizations into irreducibles, $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$, and the reason is precisely that here the irreducible elements *fail* to be prime. So "irreducible" and "prime" — words that undergraduate number theory treats as synonyms — come apart in general rings, and unique factorization comes apart with them. This single example reorganizes the whole subject: factorization theory becomes the study of *when irreducible and prime coincide*, and the rings on which they do are the well-behaved ones.

The organizing structure of the chapter is therefore a chain of ever-better rings, each defined by retaining more of $\mathbb{Z}$'s arithmetic:
$$\text{fields} \subset \text{Euclidean domains} \subset \text{principal ideal domains} \subset \text{unique factorization domains} \subset \text{integral domains}.$$
At the bottom, an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] merely lacks zero-divisors. A [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] additionally has the theorem you actually want — unique factorization into irreducibles. A [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] has every ideal generated by a single element, the property of $\mathbb{Z}$ that powers the proof of unique factorization. A [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] has a division-with-remainder algorithm, the property of $\mathbb{Z}$ that is *easiest to check* and that forces all the others. The two theorems that make this a chain rather than a list — [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] and [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]] — are the spine of §2.4. Each strict inclusion is witnessed by a real example, and recognizing which class a given ring belongs to is the practical skill the chapter installs.

# Concept Map

## §2.3 Integral Domains, Field of Fractions, Maximal and Prime Ideals

- **[[Def - Integral Domain]]**
	- A non-zero ring $R$ is an **integral domain** if it has no zero-divisors: whenever $a \cdot b = 0_R$, at least one of $a, b$ is $0_R$. Equivalently it is a ring in which cancellation holds — $ac = bc$ with $c \neq 0$ forces $a = b$. Every field is an integral domain (if $ab = 0$ and $b \neq 0$, multiply by $b^{-1}$), every subring of an integral domain is an integral domain, and so $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}, \mathbb{Z}[i]$ all qualify as [[Def - Subring|subrings]] of $\mathbb{C}$; the standard non-example is $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, where $2 \cdot 3 = 0$. A further good source: if $R$ is an integral domain then so is the polynomial ring $R[X]$ (the leading coefficient of a product is the product of the leading coefficients), hence so is $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$.

- **[[Thm - Finite Integral Domains are Fields]]**
	- Every finite integral domain is a field. The proof fixes a non-zero $a$ and studies the additive-group homomorphism "multiply by $a$", $b \mapsto a \cdot b$; its kernel is trivial precisely because there are no zero-divisors, so the map is injective, and an injective self-map of a *finite* set is surjective, so some $b$ satisfies $a b = 1_R$. Finiteness is essential — $\mathbb{Z}$ is an infinite integral domain that is not a field. This is the cleanest illustration of the slogan that finiteness converts injectivity into surjectivity, and it is the reason $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field for prime $p$.

- **[[Def - Field of Fractions]]**
	- For an integral domain $R$, a **field of fractions** is a field $F$ with $R \leq F$ as a subring, in which every element can be written $a \cdot b^{-1}$ for some $a, b \in R$ with $b \neq 0$. It is the abstract specification of "the smallest field containing $R$" — $\mathbb{Q}$ is the field of fractions of $\mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{R}(X)$, the field of rational functions, is the field of fractions of the polynomial ring $\mathbb{R}[X]$. The definition is given by a *property* rather than a construction; that a ring satisfying the property exists at all is the content of the next theorem, and that it is unique up to isomorphism makes "*the*" field of fractions legitimate.

- **[[Thm - Existence of the Field of Fractions]]**
	- Every integral domain $R$ has a field of fractions, built by literally repeating the construction of $\mathbb{Q}$ from $\mathbb{Z}$: take pairs $S = \{(a, b) : b \neq 0\}$, impose the equivalence relation $(a, b) \sim (c, d) \iff ad = bc$, and define $F = S/\!\sim$ with $\tfrac{a}{b} + \tfrac{c}{d} = \tfrac{ad + bc}{bd}$ and $\tfrac{a}{b} \cdot \tfrac{c}{d} = \tfrac{ac}{bd}$. The integral-domain hypothesis is used in exactly one place — proving $\sim$ transitive — where one cancels a non-zero denominator. The map $r \mapsto \tfrac{r}{1}$ embeds $R$ into $F$, and $\tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{a}{1}\cdot(\tfrac{b}{1})^{-1}$ shows every element is a fraction. Its importance is that it lets field techniques be imported to study any integral domain.

- **[[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal]]**
	- A proper ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$ (so $I \neq R$) is **maximal** if no ideal lies strictly between $I$ and $R$, and **prime** if $a b \in I$ forces $a \in I$ or $b \in I$. Primeness is a converse of the ideal axiom — an ideal absorbs a product when *one* factor is inside; a prime ideal *detects* a product, forcing one factor inside whenever the product is. In $\mathbb{Z}$ the prime [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] are exactly $\{0\}$ and the $p\mathbb{Z}$ for $p$ a prime number, and these $p\mathbb{Z}$ are also the maximal ideals; the inclusion of these notions is governed by the next theorem.

- **[[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]]**
	- For an ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$: $I$ is maximal if and only if $R/I$ is a field, and $I$ is prime if and only if $R/I$ is an integral domain. Both equivalences are pure translations — maximality is "no ideal strictly between", which by the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] means $R/I$ has only the ideals $\{0\}$ and itself, which characterizes a field; primeness is the literal restatement of "$R/I$ has no zero-divisors". Since every field is an integral domain, every maximal ideal is prime; the converse fails, witnessed by $\{0\} \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$ (prime, not maximal) and $(X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X, Y]$ with $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}[Y]$ a domain but not a field. A corollary: an integral domain has characteristic $0$ or a prime number.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Spec of a Ring *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Once prime ideals are in hand, the set of *all* prime ideals of $R$ — the **prime spectrum** $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$ — can be made into a topological space, and a commutative ring becomes the ring of functions on its own spectrum. Maximal ideals are the "ordinary points", non-maximal primes are "generic points" of subvarieties. This is the foundation of scheme theory. See [[Rings III — §2.5–2.6]].

## §2.4 Factorization in Integral Domains

- **[[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements]]**
	- Working in an integral domain $R$: a non-zero non-unit $a$ is **irreducible** if in every factorization $a = xy$ one of $x, y$ is a unit, and **prime** if $a \mid xy$ always forces $a \mid x$ or $a \mid y$. Irreducibility says "$a$ cannot be broken up"; primeness is Euclid's lemma, "$a$ detects products". Every prime is irreducible (a short cancellation argument), but the converse fails: in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the element $2$ is irreducible — no element has norm $2$ — yet not prime, since $2 \mid 6 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ while $2$ divides neither factor. Both properties depend on the ambient ring, not the element: $2$ is prime in $\mathbb{Z}$ but a unit in $\mathbb{Q}$.

- **[[Def - Euclidean Domain]]**
	- An integral domain $R$ is a **Euclidean domain** if it carries a **Euclidean function** $\varphi : R \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ such that $\varphi(ab) \geq \varphi(b)$ for all non-zero $a, b$, and division with remainder works: for any $a$ and any $b \neq 0$ there exist $q, r$ with $a = bq + r$ and either $r = 0$ or $\varphi(r) < \varphi(b)$. It abstracts the one feature shared by integer division ($\varphi(n) = |n|$) and polynomial division over a field ($\varphi(f) = \deg f$): a notion of "size" that a remainder can always be forced to shrink. The Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ are Euclidean with $\varphi(z) = N(z) = |z|^2$, by a geometric "nearest lattice point" argument.

- **[[Def - Principal Ideal Domain]]**
	- An integral domain $R$ is a **principal ideal domain** if every ideal is principal — for each $I \trianglelefteq R$ there is some $a$ with $I = (a)$. This is exactly the property of $\mathbb{Z}$ that "every ideal is $n\mathbb{Z}$", lifted to a definition, and it is the structural backbone of factorization theory: in a principal ideal domain every irreducible is prime, and every ascending chain of ideals stabilizes. The standard non-example is $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, where the ideal $(2, X)$ of polynomials with even constant term needs two generators; this also shows $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not Euclidean, even though $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ separately are.

- **[[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]]**
	- Every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain. The proof is the smallest-element argument from $\mathbb{Z}$ with absolute value replaced by the Euclidean function $\varphi$: given a non-zero ideal $I$, pick $b \in I$ minimizing $\varphi$, and for any $a \in I$ write $a = bq + r$; the remainder $r = a - bq$ lies in $I$ and would have $\varphi(r) < \varphi(b)$ unless $r = 0$, so $r = 0$ and $a \in (b)$, giving $I = (b)$. This is the first link of the chain, and it explains why every concrete Euclidean domain ($\mathbb{Z}$, $F[X]$, $\mathbb{Z}[i]$) automatically has all ideals principal — one never checks principality directly.

- **[[Def - Unique Factorization Domain]]**
	- An integral domain $R$ is a **unique factorization domain** if every non-zero non-unit can be written as a product of irreducibles, and that product is unique up to reordering and replacing factors by associates. It is the abstract home of the fundamental theorem of arithmetic — the property one actually wants, since it makes greatest common divisors, prime factorizations, and the rest of elementary number theory available. The non-example is $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, where $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ are two essentially different factorizations into irreducibles. In a unique factorization domain irreducible and prime coincide.

- **[[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]]**
	- Every principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain. *Existence* of a factorization uses that ascending chains of ideals stabilize in a principal ideal domain: an element with no factorization into irreducibles would generate an infinite strictly ascending chain $(r) \subsetneq (r_1) \subsetneq (r_2) \subsetneq \cdots$, a contradiction. *Uniqueness* uses that in a principal ideal domain every irreducible is prime: a prime in one factorization must divide — hence be an associate of — some factor in the other, and cancelling it (legal, integral domain) inducts down. This is the second link of the chain, and it is where "principal ideals" pays for itself by delivering unique factorization.

- **[[Def - Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple]]**
	- For elements $a_1, \dots, a_n$ of an integral domain, a **greatest common divisor** is an element $d$ dividing every $a_i$ such that any other common divisor $d'$ divides $d$; a **least common multiple** is dually an element divisible by every $a_i$ and dividing every other common multiple. Both are defined by a *universal property* among divisors, so each is determined only up to associates, and neither need exist in a general integral domain. In a unique factorization domain both always exist and are computed the schoolbook way: take, exponent by exponent, the minimum (for $\gcd$) or maximum (for $\operatorname{lcm}$) over the prime factorizations.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Modules over a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|Principal Ideal Domain]] *(from Module Theory)*
> The structure theorem for finitely generated [[Def - Module|modules]] over a principal ideal domain — every such module is a direct sum of cyclic pieces — is the engine behind both the classification of finite abelian [[Def - Group|groups]] and the Jordan/rational canonical forms of a matrix. It is exactly the principal-ideal property of §2.4 that the proof consumes. See [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2]].

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of this chapter pursue a recognizable handful of goals, and each is a question about *where a ring sits in the hierarchy* $\text{field} \subset \text{ED} \subset \text{PID} \subset \text{UFD} \subset \text{domain}$. The most common target is **classifying a given ring** — showing $R$ is, or is not, an integral domain / Euclidean domain / principal ideal domain / unique factorization domain. A positive answer high in the chain is usually proved by exhibiting structure (a Euclidean function, say); a negative answer is proved by exhibiting a *witness* (a pair of zero-divisors, a non-principal ideal, two distinct irreducible factorizations). A second target is **deciding the status of an element** — is this element a unit, irreducible, prime? — which in rings inside $\mathbb{C}$ is almost always settled by a norm computation. A third is **identifying an ideal as prime or maximal**, which by [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]] is converted to identifying the quotient $R/I$ as a domain or a field. A fourth is **constructing or recognizing a field of fractions**, typically proving some explicitly given field is the field of fractions of a given integral domain. A fifth, more computational, is **exhibiting a factorization** or computing a greatest common divisor. These targets recur because each pins down one rung of the hierarchy: you have understood an integral domain once you know its units, its irreducibles and whether they are prime, its prime and maximal ideals, and which factorization theorems it supports.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **The ring is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$** — a number ring like $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ or $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ — which immediately makes it an integral domain (subrings of fields are domains) and, crucially, switches on the **norm** $N(z) = z\bar z$, a multiplicative map to $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ that converts statements about units and divisibility into solvable Diophantine equations. **The ring is finite**, which by [[Thm - Finite Integral Domains are Fields]] collapses "integral domain" and "field" into one notion and is the route to proving $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field. **A Euclidean function is available** — $|n|$ on $\mathbb{Z}$, $\deg f$ on $F[X]$, $|z|^2$ on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — which by [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] and [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]] hands you, in two steps, every ideal principal and unique factorization, for free. **The ring is a principal ideal domain**, which makes every irreducible prime and every ascending ideal chain stabilize — the two facts that unlock unique-factorization arguments. **An ideal is given by generators**, so that primeness and maximality become checkable membership questions, often best routed through the quotient. The recurring discipline is to route a source to a target along the chain: a Euclidean function routes through two theorems to a unique-factorization conclusion; a subring-of-$\mathbb{C}$ hypothesis routes through the norm to a units-or-irreducibility conclusion; generators of an ideal route through [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]] to a prime-or-maximal conclusion. The [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything here is self-contained: a reader with no ring theory background should be able to follow each operation from the description alone.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Inherit "integral domain" downward through subrings.** A subring of an integral domain is an integral domain, because a zero-divisor in the small ring is still a zero-divisor in the big one. Since every field is an integral domain, the fastest proof that some ring $R$ has no zero-divisors is to exhibit $R$ as a subring of a field — typically of $\mathbb{C}$. The trigger is any "show $R$ is an integral domain" request: before checking the definition element-by-element, ask whether $R$ sits inside $\mathbb{C}$ or inside $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ for $F$ a field.

2. **Cancel non-zero factors.** In an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]], $ac = bc$ with $c \neq 0$ implies $a = b$ — rewrite as $(a - b)c = 0$ and use the absence of zero-divisors. This is the operation that distinguishes integral domains from general rings, and it is invoked silently in almost every §2.4 proof: it is what lets you "divide both sides" inside the uniqueness argument of [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]]. The trigger is any equation of products where you want to strip a common factor; the precondition you must confirm is that the factor is non-zero and the ring is a domain.

3. **Pass to the field of fractions.** Any integral domain $R$ embeds in its [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]], so to use a *field* technique on $R$ — inverting elements, doing linear algebra, applying a result that needs division — first enlarge $R$ to $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$, work there, and then check the answer lands back in $R$. This is how $\mathbb{Z}$-problems are attacked with $\mathbb{Q}$, and how integral-domain polynomial questions are attacked over the fraction field. The trigger is any obstruction of the form "I could finish if only I could divide".

4. **Translate an ideal property into a quotient property.** By [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]], "$I$ is prime" means "$R/I$ is an integral domain" and "$I$ is maximal" means "$R/I$ is a field". Rather than verifying the prime/maximal condition on $I$ directly, identify the quotient ring $R/I$ — using the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] — and read off the answer. The trigger is any "is this ideal prime / maximal" question; the work shifts entirely to naming a quotient.

5. **Compute with a norm.** When $R \leq \mathbb{C}$, the norm $N(z) = z\bar z = |z|^2$ is a function $R \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ that is *multiplicative*, $N(zw) = N(z)N(w)$. Multiplicativity converts ring-theoretic questions into arithmetic of non-negative integers: $u$ is a unit only if $N(u) = 1$ (so units are found by solving $N(u) = 1$); if $N(a)$ is a prime integer then $a$ is irreducible (it cannot factor non-trivially because no element has the intermediate norm); and $a \mid b$ forces $N(a) \mid N(b)$. The trigger is *any* question about units, irreducibility, or divisibility in a subring of $\mathbb{C}$.

6. **Exhibit a Euclidean function to climb the hierarchy.** To prove a ring is a principal ideal domain or a unique factorization domain, do not attack those properties directly — instead produce a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean function]] $\varphi$, and then [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] and [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]] deliver both conclusions for free. For subrings of $\mathbb{C}$ the candidate $\varphi$ is always the norm, and the division-with-remainder axiom is checked by the geometric "is there a lattice point within distance $1$" picture. The trigger is wanting a strong structural conclusion (all ideals principal, unique factorization) about a concrete ring.

7. **Run the smallest-element argument.** Given an ideal $I$ in a ring with a notion of size (a Euclidean function, or just $|\cdot|$ on $\mathbb{Z}$), pick the non-zero element $b \in I$ of *minimal* size; then division with remainder forces every other element of $I$ to be a multiple of $b$, because the remainder lies in $I$ and is too small to be non-zero. This is the proof skeleton of [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]]. The trigger is any "show this ideal is principal" or "classify the ideals" task in a Euclidean domain.

8. **Use ascending chains and their stabilization.** In a principal ideal domain every chain $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ of ideals eventually stops growing — take the union (an ideal), write it as $(a)$, and note $a$ already lies in some $I_N$. This converts an "infinite descent" intuition into a rigorous tool: a process that would build a strictly ascending chain forever cannot exist. It is what proves *existence* of factorizations in [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]]. The trigger is any argument of the form "keep factoring and this never terminates — contradiction".

9. **Translate divisibility into ideal containment.** The dictionary $a \mid b \iff (b) \subseteq (a)$, and "$a, b$ associates" $\iff (a) = (b)$, lets every statement about divisibility be rephrased as a statement about principal ideals, where the lattice structure (intersection, sum) is available. A unit is exactly an element with $(a) = R$. The trigger is any divisibility manipulation that feels slippery at the element level — recast it as inclusions of ideals, where "irreducible" becomes "$(a)$ is maximal among proper principal ideals" and "prime" becomes "$(a)$ is a prime ideal".

10. **Find a multiplicative size that detects units.** More generally than the norm: any multiplicative function $\varphi$ to $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ with $\varphi(1) = 1$ forces every unit to satisfy $\varphi(u) = 1$, since $\varphi(u)\varphi(u^{-1}) = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. Conversely an element of minimal non-unit "size" is a strong candidate to be irreducible. The trigger is needing to pin down the unit group $R^\times$ of an unfamiliar ring, or to certify that a specific element cannot be factored.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming irreducible implies prime
> In $\mathbb{Z}$ the irreducible numbers and the prime numbers are the same set, so after a lifetime of elementary number theory it feels like a law that an element which "cannot be broken up" must "detect products". It is not. In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the element $2$ is [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|irreducible]] — a norm computation shows no element has norm $2$, so $2 = xy$ forces a unit — yet $2$ is *not prime*: it divides $6 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ but divides neither factor, since $N(2) = 4$ does not divide $N(1 \pm \sqrt{-5}) = 6$. The implication that *is* always valid runs the other way, prime $\Rightarrow$ irreducible. Irreducible $\Rightarrow$ prime becomes legal exactly when the ring is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] — and that equivalence is precisely what makes unique factorization work.

> [!warning] 2. Assuming factorization into irreducibles is unique
> Once you know every non-unit in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ *can* be written as a product of irreducibles, it is tempting to conclude the factorization is essentially unique, as in $\mathbb{Z}$. But $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ exhibits two factorizations of the *same* element into irreducibles, and the factors are not associates of each other ($2$ and $1+\sqrt{-5}$ have different norms). Existence of a factorization and *uniqueness* of a factorization are independent properties; a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] is defined by demanding *both*. The deep reason uniqueness fails here is operation 1: uniqueness needs irreducibles to be prime, and in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ they are not.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming every prime ideal is maximal (or conversely)
> Since every maximal ideal is prime, and in $\mathbb{Z}$ the non-zero primes $p\mathbb{Z}$ are exactly the maximal ideals, it is tempting to treat the two notions as interchangeable. They are not. The zero ideal $\{0\} \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$ is [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime]] — $\mathbb{Z}/\{0\} = \mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain — but not maximal, because $\{0\} \subsetneq 2\mathbb{Z} \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}$. Less degenerately, $(X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X, Y]$ is prime but not maximal, since $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}[Y]$ is a domain but not a field. The correct picture from [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]] is "field $\Rightarrow$ domain", hence "maximal $\Rightarrow$ prime", with the gap exactly the supply of integral domains that are not fields.

> [!warning] 4. Quotienting an integral domain and expecting an integral domain
> A quotient of a ring is a ring, so it is tempting to assume the quotient of an integral domain is again an integral domain. It is not: $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain but $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ has the zero-divisors $2$ and $3$. The quotient $R/I$ is an integral domain *exactly* when $I$ is a [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime ideal]] — that is the content of [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]]. So "is $R/I$ a domain?" is never automatic; it is precisely the question "is $I$ prime?", and one must check that. Likewise the field of fractions does *not* commute with quotients: passing to fractions and then quotienting is not the same as quotienting and then passing to fractions.

> [!warning] 5. Assuming greatest common divisors exist or are unique
> The phrase "the greatest common divisor of $a$ and $b$" suggests a unique, always-defined object, as it is for integers. Neither half is safe. A [[Def - Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple|greatest common divisor]] is defined by a universal property among common divisors, so it is determined only *up to associates* — in $\mathbb{Z}$ one writes "$\gcd = 6$" by the convention of choosing the positive representative, but $6$ and $-6$ are equally valid. And in a general integral domain a greatest common divisor *need not exist at all*; existence is guaranteed only once the ring is a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]], where it is built from prime factorizations. Writing "$\gcd(a,b)$" presupposes you are in a ring where the object exists.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic are won the moment you decide *which rung of the hierarchy* the question is about, so begin there. The chain $\text{field} \subset \text{ED} \subset \text{PID} \subset \text{UFD} \subset \text{integral domain}$ is not just a list of definitions — it is a decision tree for problem-solving, because each rung has a characteristic kind of evidence and a characteristic route to the conclusion.

If the problem **asks you to place a ring on the hierarchy** — to show it is, or is not, an integral domain, Euclidean domain, principal ideal domain, or unique factorization domain — the first fork is the *direction* of the claim. To prove a ring is *high* on the chain, the efficient strategy is to climb from above: do not attack "unique factorization" or "all ideals principal" directly, because those are quantified over infinitely many elements or ideals. Instead exhibit a single [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean function]], and let [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] followed by [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]] carry you down the chain for free — one construction yields three conclusions. For a subring of $\mathbb{C}$ the Euclidean function is always the norm, and the only real work is the division-with-remainder axiom, checked by the geometric picture of "is some lattice point within distance $1$". To prove a ring is *not* high on the chain, the strategy is opposite: produce a single explicit *witness*. Two non-zero elements with product zero witness "not an integral domain"; a non-principal ideal — the archetype is $(2, X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$ — witnesses "not a principal ideal domain"; two genuinely different irreducible factorizations of one element — the archetype is $6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ — witness "not a unique factorization domain". One example settles a negative claim; structure settles a positive one.

If the problem **asks about the status of a specific element** — is it a unit, is it irreducible, is it prime — and the ring sits inside $\mathbb{C}$, then the strategy is almost mechanical: compute the norm. The norm $N(z) = z\bar z$ is multiplicative, so $N$ turns the question into integer arithmetic. Units are found by solving $N(u) = 1$ — in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ this is $a^2 + 5b^2 = 1$, forcing $u = \pm 1$. Irreducibility is often immediate: if $N(a)$ is a *prime integer*, or if no element has a norm strictly between $1$ and $N(a)$ that could divide it, then $a$ cannot factor non-trivially. Primeness is the subtle one — by [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|definition]] it is Euclid's lemma — and the way to *disprove* it is to find a product $a \mid xy$ with $a \nmid x$ and $a \nmid y$, again detected by norms ($a \mid x \Rightarrow N(a) \mid N(x)$). The standing warning is that irreducible does not imply prime: you must check primeness separately unless you already know the ring is a principal ideal domain, in which case [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|the irreducible-equals-prime lemma]] does it for you.

If the problem **asks whether an ideal is prime or maximal**, do not wrestle with the definitions of prime and maximal directly. Translate. By [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]], primeness of $I$ is *exactly* "$R/I$ is an integral domain" and maximality is *exactly* "$R/I$ is a field", so the strategy is to identify the quotient ring $R/I$ — typically by building a surjective homomorphism $R \to T$ with kernel $I$ and invoking the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] — and then simply read off whether $T$ is a domain or a field. This is why the example $(X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ is settled instantly: $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}[Y]$, visibly a domain (so $(X)$ is prime) and visibly not a field (so $(X)$ is not maximal). The same translation runs in $\mathbb{Z}$: $p\mathbb{Z}$ is maximal because $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field, and $\{0\}$ is prime-not-maximal because $\mathbb{Z}/\{0\} = \mathbb{Z}$ is a domain that is not a field.

If the problem **involves a field of fractions** — constructing one, or recognizing a given field as one — the strategy is to remember that [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] is defined by a property, not a formula, so to prove "$F$ is the field of fractions of $R$" you verify the two conditions: $R$ is a subring of $F$, and every element of $F$ is $ab^{-1}$ with $a, b \in R$. The construction in [[Thm - Existence of the Field of Fractions]] is the universal template — equivalence classes of pairs — and the recurring use of the construction is less to *build* $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ from scratch than to *import field techniques*: when a problem about an integral domain stalls because you cannot divide, pass to the fraction field, finish there, and pull back.

Finally, a meta-strategy threads through all of the above: **every question in this chapter is the question "how much of $\mathbb{Z}$ survives here?"** The hierarchy is a stratification of that question, and knowing which stratum you are in tells you which theorems are available. In a Euclidean domain you have the Euclidean algorithm and everything below it. In a principal ideal domain you have unique factorization and "irreducible = prime" but maybe not an algorithm. In a bare integral domain you have only cancellation and a field of fractions — factorization may fail entirely. So the first diagnostic question for *any* problem is "what is the strongest property of this ring I have been given or can cheaply establish?", because that property is the key to the toolbox, and the chain of theorems [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] and [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]] tells you exactly which further tools come bundled with it.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients|Prime/maximal via quotients]]**: $I$ prime $\iff R/I$ a domain; $I$ maximal $\iff R/I$ a field. This is the single most reused result of the chapter because it converts a property of an *ideal* — a quantifier over products or over intervening ideals — into a property of a *ring*, the quotient, which you can identify by the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] and then inspect directly. The recognizable setup is any question naming an ideal and asking "prime?" or "maximal?"; the move is always the same, identify $R/I$ and look at it. It is also the conceptual glue of the chapter: it is what makes "integral domain" and "field" — properties of rings — into a *theory of ideals*, and it is the gateway to algebraic geometry, where prime ideals become points.

- **[[Def - Field of Fractions|The field of fractions]]**: every integral domain embeds in a smallest field. Its reusability is as a *technique transporter*. Many arguments need division — solving a linear equation, applying a result stated for fields, doing [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] counts — and an integral domain does not supply it. The standard move is to enlarge $R$ to $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$, carry out the field argument, and then verify the conclusion descends back to $R$. This is exactly how facts about $\mathbb{Z}$ are routinely proved by working in $\mathbb{Q}$, and how integral-domain polynomial problems are handled over the rational-function field. Reach for it whenever a proof is one division away from finishing.

- **The norm $N(z) = z\bar z$ on a subring of $\mathbb{C}$**: a multiplicative map $R \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. This is the workhorse for every concrete computation in number rings, and its power is entirely in multiplicativity: $N(zw) = N(z)N(w)$ lets a question about the ring be pushed down to a question about non-negative integers, which you can actually solve. Units have norm $1$, elements of prime norm are irreducible, and $a \mid b$ implies $N(a) \mid N(b)$ — so the norm is simultaneously the tool for finding $R^\times$, for proving irreducibility, and for *disproving* divisibility (the key step in showing irreducibles fail to be prime in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$). Whenever a problem lives in a subring of $\mathbb{C}$ and asks about units, factorization, or divisibility, the norm is the first thing to write down.

- **[[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains|ED Rightarrow PID]] and [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|PID Rightarrow UFD]]**: the two links of the chain. Their reusability is that they let you *buy strong conclusions cheaply*. Verifying "every ideal is principal" or "factorization is unique" directly is hard — both are statements quantified over infinitely many objects. But exhibiting one Euclidean function is concrete and finite, and these two theorems then deliver, in succession, every ideal principal and unique factorization. The standard pattern is: want unique factorization in $R$ $\to$ find a Euclidean function $\to$ done. Internalizing the chain means that for any of $\mathbb{Z}$, $F[X]$, $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ you never prove principality or unique factorization from scratch — you cite the chain.

- **The smallest-element / ascending-chain argument**: pick the minimal-size element of an ideal, or note that strictly ascending chains of ideals cannot run forever in a principal ideal domain. This appears in both directions of the chain — the minimal-element version proves [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains|ED Rightarrow PID]], the ascending-chain version proves the *existence* half of [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|PID Rightarrow UFD]]. Its reusability is as the rigorous form of "infinite descent is impossible": whenever an argument has the shape "keep breaking the object down and the process never terminates", reach for the ascending chain of ideals it generates and invoke stabilization to derive a contradiction. This is the same idea that, named **Noetherian**, organizes much of [[Rings III — §2.5–2.6|later commutative algebra]].

# Bridges

1. **Number theory — the failure of unique factorization is the origin of ideal theory.** The ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not an idle counterexample: rings of this shape, the **rings of integers** of number fields like $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$, are the central objects of algebraic number theory, and the discovery that they need not be [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domains]] is *why ideals were invented in the first place*. Kummer and Dedekind's resolution is that although *elements* may not factor uniquely, *ideals* do: in a ring of integers every non-zero ideal is uniquely a product of [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime ideals]] — such rings are called **Dedekind domains**. The factorization $6 = 2\cdot3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ becomes a single statement at the level of ideals, $(6) = \mathfrak{p}_1^2 \mathfrak{p}_2 \mathfrak{p}_3$, with the two element-factorizations being two ways of regrouping the same prime ideals. The exact obstruction — how badly unique factorization of elements fails — is measured by a finite abelian group, the **ideal class group**, which is trivial precisely when the ring is a principal ideal domain. So this chapter's distinction between irreducible and prime is, historically and literally, the seed of class field theory.

2. **Algebraic geometry — prime ideals are points.** [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]] says a prime ideal is one whose quotient is an integral domain, and an integral domain is exactly a ring of functions on an *irreducible* space — one not splittable into two proper closed pieces, because a splitting would produce zero-divisors. This is the entry to the geometry–algebra dictionary: to a commutative ring $R$ one attaches the space $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$ of *all* its prime ideals, and a maximal ideal corresponds to an ordinary point while a non-maximal prime corresponds to a whole irreducible subvariety viewed as a single "generic point". Under this dictionary, the [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient]] $R/I$ is the ring of functions on the subvariety cut out by $I$, the [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] of an integral domain is the field of rational functions on the corresponding irreducible variety, and a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] corresponds to a particularly simple curve. The whole of scheme theory is the systematic exploitation of "prime ideal = point".

3. **Linear algebra — the minimal polynomial is the generator of an ideal.** For a fixed matrix $A$ over a field $F$, the polynomials that annihilate it, $\{f \in F[X] : f(A) = 0\}$, form an ideal of $F[X]$. Because $F[X]$ is a Euclidean domain, hence by [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], this ideal is principal, generated by a single polynomial $m$ — and that generator *is* the **minimal polynomial** of $A$. So the existence of a minimal polynomial, the fact that it divides every other annihilating polynomial (the characteristic polynomial included, via Cayley–Hamilton), and its uniqueness up to a unit scalar are all immediate corollaries of the principal-ideal property. The chapter's structure theory is thus the hidden engine behind canonical forms; pushed further, the structure theorem for modules over a principal ideal domain gives the Jordan and rational canonical forms in full.

4. **Construction of fields — the field of fractions and the maximal-ideal quotient are the two field-making machines.** This chapter supplies two systematic ways to manufacture fields, and they are complementary. [[Thm - Existence of the Field of Fractions]] makes a field from an integral domain by *inverting* — formally adjoining reciprocals — and is how $\mathbb{Q}$ comes from $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{R}(X)$ from $\mathbb{R}[X]$. [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]] makes a field by *quotienting* by a maximal ideal — and is how $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ and the field $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$ arise. Together they generate, up to a point, all the fields of elementary algebra, and they are the prerequisite for the theory of field extensions: a finite field extension is built by repeatedly quotienting a polynomial ring by a maximal ideal $(f)$ with $f$ irreducible, while the function field of a variety is the field of fractions of its (integral-domain) coordinate ring. See [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8]].

# Insights

**The hierarchy is a measure of how much of $\mathbb{Z}$'s arithmetic survives.** The chain $\text{field} \subset \text{ED} \subset \text{PID} \subset \text{UFD} \subset \text{integral domain}$ looks like an arbitrary tower of definitions, but each step has a precise meaning as a *quantity of retained structure*. A bare [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] keeps only the most primitive property of $\mathbb{Z}$ — no zero-divisors, hence cancellation — and that is *just* enough to support a field of fractions, but factorization into primes can fail completely. A [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] keeps the fundamental theorem of arithmetic itself: every element is a unique product of irreducibles, so all of elementary multiplicative number theory — greatest common divisors, prime decompositions, the structure of divisors — becomes available. A [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] keeps a *structural* property, "every ideal is generated by one element", which is strictly stronger than unique factorization (the implication [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|PID Rightarrow UFD]] is not reversible: $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a unique factorization domain but not a principal ideal domain). A [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] keeps the most operational property, an actual *algorithm* for division with remainder, which is again strictly stronger. The right way to read the chain is: as you climb, you are not adding unrelated axioms, you are recovering more and more of the specific arithmetic of the integers — first cancellation, then unique factorization, then a one-generator ideal theory, finally an algorithm. The two theorems of §2.4 are the proof that these recoveries are *cumulative*, that owning a higher property automatically grants the lower ones; and the strictness of each inclusion, witnessed by a real ring, is the proof that they are genuinely different amounts of structure. To know which rung a ring occupies is to know exactly which theorems of number theory you may use inside it.

**The true name of a prime ideal is "the kernel of a map to an integral domain", and the contrast with a maximal ideal is the contrast between a domain and a field.** [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2]] taught that the true name of an ideal is "kernel of a homomorphism". This chapter refines that: a *prime* ideal is the kernel of a homomorphism *onto an integral domain*, and a *maximal* ideal is the kernel of a homomorphism *onto a field*. The textbook definition of prime — "$ab \in I$ forces $a \in I$ or $b \in I$" — is the right thing to *check* but the wrong thing to *picture*; it looks like a strange partial converse of the ideal axiom, and on its own it explains nothing. The operational meaning, delivered by [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]], is that $I$ is prime exactly when the quotient $R/I$ has no zero-divisors. Once you see it this way, the entire prime/maximal story becomes transparent and stops requiring memorization. *Every maximal ideal is prime* because every field is an integral domain — that is the whole proof. The converse *fails* exactly to the extent that integral domains can fail to be fields, and the canonical examples ($\{0\} \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$, with quotient the non-field domain $\mathbb{Z}$; $(X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$, with quotient the non-field domain $\mathbb{Z}[Y]$) are not separate facts to learn but the same observation applied twice. The lesson is general: when a definition is phrased as a delicate elementwise condition, look for the quotient it secretly controls — the property of the quotient is the concept's real identity, and the elementwise condition is merely its fingerprint.

**Factorization theory is the study of when "irreducible" and "prime" coincide.** This is the unifying frame of §2.4, and it reorganizes a dozen definitions into one storyline. The two notions answer different questions about a non-zero non-unit $a$. *Irreducible* is a statement about $a$ alone — "$a$ cannot be split", $a = xy \Rightarrow x$ or $y$ a unit — and it is what you need for factorizations to *exist* (you factor by repeatedly splitting until nothing splits further). *Prime* is a statement about how $a$ interacts with all other elements — Euclid's lemma, "$a \mid xy \Rightarrow a \mid x$ or $a \mid y$" — and it is what you need for factorizations to be *unique* (the cancellation-and-induction argument runs only because each irreducible factor on one side, being prime, must turn up among the factors on the other). In $\mathbb{Z}$ the two notions are accidentally equal, which is *why* the integers enjoy unique factorization, and centuries of number theory built on that coincidence without noticing it was a coincidence. The example $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ pulls them apart: there $2$ is irreducible but not prime, factorizations exist but are not unique, and the failure of uniqueness is *traceable, line by line*, to the failure of irreducibles to be prime. The implication prime $\Rightarrow$ irreducible is free in any integral domain; the reverse implication is the hard, valuable one, and [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|the lemma that in a principal ideal domain every irreducible is prime]] is the precise hypothesis under which it holds. So a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] can be re-described in one phrase as *an integral domain in which existence-of-factorization and uniqueness-of-factorization both hold because irreducible and prime have been forced to coincide*. Every other definition in §2.4 — Euclidean domain, principal ideal domain, greatest common divisor — is scaffolding for engineering, or exploiting, that coincidence.
