# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] — a non-zero commutative [[Def - Ring|ring]] with $1$ in which $ab = 0$ forces $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. We write $a \mid b$ for "$a$ divides $b$", and $(a) = aR = \{ar : r \in R\}$ for the [[Def - Ideal|principal ideal]] generated by $a$. A [[Def - Unit and Field|unit]] is an element with a multiplicative inverse; the set of units is denoted $R^\times$. The symbol $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ means the subring $\{a + b\sqrt{-5} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ of $\mathbb{C}$. The full registry of symbols for this chapter is on [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4]].

A warning that governs this entire page: **every one of the four notions below depends on the ring, not on the element.** The integer $2$ is prime in $\mathbb{Z}$ and a unit in $\mathbb{Q}$; the polynomial $2X$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ (since $2$ is a unit there) but reducible in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ (since $2 \cdot X$ splits it into two non-units). Divisibility, associates, irreducibility and primality are never properties of an element alone — they are properties of an element *inside a specified ring*.

# Axiom Motivation

We want to do number theory in a general integral domain: to factor elements into "atoms" and to know that the factorisation is essentially unique, exactly as the fundamental theorem of arithmetic factors every integer into primes. To even state such a goal we must first answer four prior questions. What does it mean for one element to be built out of another (**divisibility**)? When should two factorisations count as "the same" (**associates**)? What is an **atom** — an element that cannot be broken down? And what is the *multiplicative* property that makes atoms behave well under products (**primality**)? The four definitions on this page are the answers, and each is forced by the demand that the resulting theory reproduce ordinary arithmetic.

**Divisibility.** The starting point is the most literal possible reading of "$a$ goes into $b$": there should be a quotient. So $a \mid b$ should mean $b = ac$ for some $c \in R$. The only subtlety is that we are *not* in a field, so the quotient $c$ need not be unique-looking or even exist, and that is the whole interest — divisibility is a genuine partial structure, not the trivial "everything divides everything" of a field. The reformulation $a \mid b \iff (b) \subseteq (a)$ is worth internalising now: "$a$ divides $b$" is the same statement as "the multiples of $b$ are among the multiples of $a$", because $b$ is itself a multiple of $a$ exactly when the whole ideal it generates is. Divisibility is *ideal containment, reversed*. The larger the divisor, the smaller its set of multiples — and the bigger its principal ideal. This dictionary is what lets the entire theory be rephrased in the language of [[Def - Ideal|ideals]], which is where principal ideal domains get their power.

**Associates.** In $\mathbb{Z}$ we factor $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (-2) \cdot (-3)$, and we do not want to call this a failure of unique factorisation. The two factorisations differ only by units ($-1$), and a unit carries no arithmetic content — multiplying by a unit is reversible and changes nothing about divisibility. So we need an equivalence relation that quotients out this harmless ambiguity: $a$ and $b$ are **associates** if $a = bc$ for a unit $c$. Why insist $c$ be a *unit*, and not merely any element? Because we want the relation to be symmetric: if $a = bc$ with $c$ a unit then $b = ac^{-1}$, so each divides the other. If we allowed $c$ to be arbitrary we would just have recovered one-directional divisibility. Indeed the cleanest characterisation is exactly this: $a$ and $b$ are associates if and only if $a \mid b$ *and* $b \mid a$ — they sit at the same height in the divisibility order. In ideal language, $a \sim b \iff (a) = (b)$: associates are the elements that generate the very same principal ideal. Unique factorisation can only ever be unique "up to order and associates", because associates are invisible to the ideal structure.

**Irreducible.** An atom is something that cannot be split. The naive definition "$a$ has no proper factorisation $a = xy$" is wrong, because it forgets units: $6 = 1 \cdot 6$ is a factorisation, and so is $5 = (-1)(-5)$, and we do not want these to disqualify $5$ from being an atom. The fix is to declare a factorisation *trivial* when one factor is a unit (the other is then merely an associate of $a$). So $a$ is **irreducible** if it is non-zero, not a unit, and *every* factorisation $a = xy$ has $x$ or $y$ a unit. Each clause is load-bearing. Exclude $0$: in an integral domain $0 = 0 \cdot 0$, and more importantly $0$ is the additive identity, not a multiplicative atom; including it would wreck every counting argument. Exclude units: a unit $u$ satisfies $u = u \cdot 1$ but is "below everything" in divisibility, it divides all of $R$, so calling it an atom would make the notion of "smallest building block" meaningless — and a product of irreducibles could then absorb arbitrarily many unit factors, destroying uniqueness. The non-trivial-factorisation clause is the actual content: it says $a$ genuinely does not break apart.

**Prime.** Here is the subtle one, and the reason this page is the heart of the chapter. There are *two* distinct intuitions for a "prime number", and they happen to coincide in $\mathbb{Z}$ but not in general. The first intuition — "cannot be factored" — is irreducibility, which we just defined. The second intuition is **Euclid's lemma**: a prime $p$ has the property that if $p$ divides a product $xy$, then $p$ divides one of the factors. This second property is *not* about factoring $p$ itself; it is about how $p$ interacts with *other* products. We take it as the definition: $p$ is **prime** if it is non-zero, not a unit, and $p \mid xy \Rightarrow p \mid x$ or $p \mid y$. Why is this the right multiplicative axiom and not irreducibility? Because uniqueness of factorisation is precisely a cancellation-and-rearrangement argument, and that argument runs on Euclid's lemma — when you have $p_1 \cdots p_n = q_1 \cdots q_m$ and need to match a $p$ to a $q$, you ask "$p_1$ divides the product of the $q$'s, so which $q$ does it divide?", and only primality answers that. Irreducibility tells you an element does not split; primality tells you it *propagates through products*, and propagation is what uniqueness needs.

The deepest motivation is the relationship between the last two. Primality is the *stronger* notion: **every prime is irreducible** (proved below — if a prime $p$ factors as $p = ab$ then $p \mid ab$, so $p$ divides a factor, and cancelling in the integral domain forces the other factor to be a unit). The converse fails: in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the element $2$ is irreducible (it does not split) yet not prime (it divides the product $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}) = 6$ without dividing either factor). So the two intuitions for "prime number" really are different axioms, and the entire arc of this chapter is the question *when do they coincide?* — the answer being: in any [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]], and in particular in any [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]]. Defining both notions separately, and keeping them separate, is what makes that question askable.

# The Definition

Let $R$ be an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]].

**Divisibility.** For $a, b \in R$, we say $a$ **divides** $b$, written $a \mid b$, if there exists $c \in R$ with
$$b = ac.$$
Equivalently, $a \mid b$ if and only if $(b) \subseteq (a)$, where $(a) = aR$ is the [[Def - Ideal|principal ideal]] generated by $a$.

**Associates.** Two elements $a, b \in R$ are **associates** if there exists a [[Def - Unit and Field|unit]] $c \in R^\times$ with
$$a = bc.$$
The following are equivalent: (i) $a$ and $b$ are associates; (ii) $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$; (iii) $(a) = (b)$. Being associates is an equivalence relation on $R$.

**Irreducible.** An element $a \in R$ is **irreducible** if

1. $a \neq 0$;
2. $a$ is not a unit; and
3. whenever $a = xy$ with $x, y \in R$, then $x$ is a unit or $y$ is a unit.

An element that is non-zero, not a unit, and not irreducible is called **reducible**.

**Prime.** An element $p \in R$ is **prime** if

1. $p \neq 0$;
2. $p$ is not a unit; and
3. whenever $p \mid xy$ with $x, y \in R$, then $p \mid x$ or $p \mid y$.

By induction, condition 3 extends to any finite product: if $p$ is prime and $p \mid x_1 x_2 \cdots x_n$, then $p \mid x_i$ for some $i$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The cleanest compression of this page is the translation into the language of [[Def - Ideal|ideals]], where each of the four notions becomes a statement about [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime ideals]] or about inclusions of principal ideals.

Divisibility *is* reverse inclusion of principal ideals: $a \mid b \iff (b) \subseteq (a)$. Associates *are* equality of principal ideals: $a \sim b \iff (a) = (b)$. So the set of principal ideals, ordered by inclusion, is exactly the set of elements ordered by divisibility, with associate elements identified — the map $a \mapsto (a)$ is the quotient of $(R, \mid)$ by the associate relation, and it reverses order. This is why people freely switch between "the gcd of $a$ and $b$" and "the smallest principal ideal containing $(a)$ and $(b)$": they are the same object viewed through this dictionary.

Primality has an even sharper ideal-theoretic shadow, and it is the key lemma of the section: **the principal ideal $(r)$ is a [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime ideal]] of $R$ if and only if $r = 0$ or $r$ is a prime element.** Recall a prime ideal $P$ is a proper ideal with the property that $xy \in P \Rightarrow x \in P$ or $y \in P$. Unwind the definitions: $xy \in (r)$ means $r \mid xy$, and $x \in (r)$ means $r \mid x$. So the prime-ideal condition on $(r)$ is *verbatim* the prime-element condition on $r$ — modulo the edge cases. The element definition of "prime" is precisely the ideal definition of "prime", pulled back along $r \mapsto (r)$. (The case $r = 0$ is genuine: $(0) = \{0\}$ is a prime ideal exactly because $R$ is an integral domain — that is the very definition of integral domain rephrased — yet $0$ is not a prime *element*, since we exclude $0$ by fiat. This mismatch is the only blemish in the dictionary, and it is the price of wanting $0$ to be excluded from factorisations.)

Against the world of number theory: divisibility, irreducibility, and primality are the literal abstractions of the integer notions, with $\mathbb{Z}$ as the motivating model in which all three behave perfectly. The novelty — the thing with no integer analogue — is that **irreducible and prime can come apart**. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ the two words name the same set of objects, which is why elementary number theory never distinguishes them; the general theory must, and the gap between them is measured precisely by the failure of unique factorisation.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — ordinary primes in $\mathbb{Z}$.** The integer $7$ is both irreducible and prime in $\mathbb{Z}$. It is irreducible because its only factorisations are $7 = 1 \cdot 7 = (-1)(-7)$, each with a unit factor. It is prime because if $7 \mid xy$ then by the fundamental theorem of arithmetic $7$ appears in the prime factorisation of $xy$, hence in that of $x$ or of $y$. In $\mathbb{Z}$ the two notions coincide for *every* element — this is exactly the statement that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]].

**Is an instance — irreducible polynomials.** In $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ the polynomial $X^2 + 1$ is irreducible: a non-trivial factorisation would be into two degree-one factors, giving a rational root, and $X^2 + 1$ has none. It is also prime, because $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] and there irreducible implies prime. By contrast $X^2 - 1 = (X-1)(X+1)$ is reducible. Degree is the typical irreducibility certificate for polynomials: a polynomial of degree $\le 3$ over a field is reducible if and only if it has a root.

**Is an instance — every prime is irreducible.** This is a corollary that doubles as a calibration check; it holds in *every* integral domain.

> [!note]- Proof that every prime is irreducible
> Let $p \in R$ be prime and suppose $p = ab$. We must show $a$ or $b$ is a unit. Since $p = ab$ we certainly have $p \mid ab$. Primality gives $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$; without loss of generality $p \mid a$, say $a = pc$ for some $c \in R$. Substituting,
> $$p = ab = (pc)b = p(cb).$$
> Now $R$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] and $p \neq 0$, so we may cancel $p$ from $p \cdot 1 = p \cdot (cb)$ to obtain $1 = cb$. Hence $b$ is a unit. Together with $p \neq 0$ and $p$ not a unit (both inherited from primality), this shows $p$ is irreducible. $\blacksquare$
>
> The single place the argument uses the integral domain hypothesis is the cancellation of $p$ — without it the implication can fail.

**Is NOT an instance — an irreducible that is not prime.** This is the central non-example of the section, and the reason "prime" and "irreducible" must be defined separately. Let $R = \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, and equip it with the **norm** $N(a + b\sqrt{-5}) = a^2 + 5b^2$, which is multiplicative: $N(zw) = N(z)N(w)$.

> [!note]- Why $2$ is irreducible but not prime in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$
> *Units.* If $z$ is a unit then $N(z) N(z^{-1}) = N(1) = 1$, so $N(z) = 1$; solving $a^2 + 5b^2 = 1$ over $\mathbb{Z}$ gives only $a = \pm 1, b = 0$. So $R^\times = \{\pm 1\}$.
>
> *Irreducibility of $2$.* Suppose $2 = zw$. Taking norms, $4 = N(2) = N(z)N(w)$. The possible norm values dividing $4$ are $1, 2, 4$, but $a^2 + 5b^2 = 2$ has no integer solution — nothing in $R$ has norm $2$. So $\{N(z), N(w)\} = \{1, 4\}$, meaning one of $z, w$ has norm $1$ and is therefore a unit. Hence $2$ is irreducible. The same norm argument shows $3$, $1 + \sqrt{-5}$ and $1 - \sqrt{-5}$ are all irreducible (there is also no element of norm $3$).
>
> *Failure of primality.* Observe
> $$(1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}) = 1 - (-5) = 6 = 2 \cdot 3,$$
> so $2 \mid (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$. If $2$ were prime it would divide one factor. But $2 \mid (1 \pm \sqrt{-5})$ would force $N(2) \mid N(1 \pm \sqrt{-5})$, i.e. $4 \mid 6$, which is false. So $2$ divides the product without dividing either factor: $2$ is **not prime**.
>
> The same example shows factorisation into irreducibles is not unique here: $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ are two genuinely different factorisations of $6$ into irreducibles, and the factors are not associates of one another (they have different norms). So $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is an integral domain that is **not** a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]].

**Corollary — $(r)$ is a prime ideal if and only if $r = 0$ or $r$ is a prime element.** This is the bridge between prime elements and [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime ideals]], and verifying it is the best test of whether the definitions have landed.

> [!note]- Proof of the prime-element / prime-ideal correspondence
> *($\Rightarrow$)* Suppose $(r)$ is a prime ideal. If $r = 0$ we are done. Otherwise, prime ideals are proper, so $(r) \neq R$, which means $r$ is not a unit. Suppose $r \mid ab$; then $ab \in (r)$, and primality of the ideal gives $a \in (r)$ or $b \in (r)$, i.e. $r \mid a$ or $r \mid b$. Hence $r$ is a prime element.
>
> *($\Leftarrow$)* If $r = 0$ then $(0) = \{0\}$, which is a prime ideal precisely because $R$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]]: $ab \in \{0\}$ means $ab = 0$, forcing $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. If instead $r \neq 0$ is a prime element, it is not a unit, so $(r) \neq R$ is proper; and if $ab \in (r)$ then $r \mid ab$, so $r \mid a$ or $r \mid b$, giving $a \in (r)$ or $b \in (r)$. Hence $(r)$ is a prime ideal. $\blacksquare$

**Calibration check.** Confirm that the units of $R$ are exactly the elements that divide $1$ (equivalently, the elements all of whose multiples form the whole [[Def - Ring|ring]], $(u) = R$); that $a$ and $b$ are associates if and only if they are mutual divisors; that $0$ is divisible by every element but divides only itself; and that no unit is ever irreducible or prime (the definitions exclude units by hand). If you can also explain *why* the cancellation step in "prime $\Rightarrow$ irreducible" needs $R$ to be an integral domain — and produce a ring with zero divisors where a prime-like element fails to be irreducible — the four definitions have fully landed.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Unique Factorization Domain *(from this topic)*
> Once "irreducible" and "prime" are in hand, the definition of a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] is the demand that every non-zero non-unit factor into irreducibles, uniquely up to order and associates. The key structural fact is that in a UFD the two notions on this page *coincide*: irreducible $=$ prime.

> [!tip] Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains *(from this topic)*
> The implication [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|every PID is a UFD]] runs precisely on the upgrade "irreducible $\Rightarrow$ prime", which holds in any [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] and is what makes the uniqueness half of the factorisation argument go through.

> [!tip] Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple *(from this topic)*
> The divisibility relation defined here is the order with respect to which the [[Def - Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple|greatest common divisor]] is a greatest lower bound and the least common multiple a least upper bound — and "defined only up to associates" is a direct consequence of associates being divisibility-indistinguishable.
