# Problem Statement

Let $R$ be a **principal ideal domain**. Prove:
$$\text{if }p\in R\text{ is irreducible, then }p\text{ is prime.}$$

Concretely: assume $p$ is irreducible, $p\mid ab$ for some $a,b\in R$, and $p\nmid a$; show that $p\mid b$.

The intended route: consider the ideal $(p,a)\trianglelefteq R$ generated by $p$ and $a$. Since $R$ is a PID, $(p,a)=(d)$ for some $d\in R$. Then $d\mid p$, and irreducibility of $p$ forces $d$ to be a unit. Hence $(p,a)=R$, so $1=rp+sa$ for some $r,s\in R$. Multiply by $b$ and read off $p\mid b$.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are a principal ideal domain, irreducible and prime elements, the ideal generated by two elements, and units.

![[Def - Integral Domain#The Definition]]

![[Def - Principal Ideal Domain#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] (PID) is an integral domain in which *every* ideal is principal: for each ideal $I$ there is a single $d$ with $I=(d)=\{dr:r\in R\}$.

![[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements#The Definition]]

An element $p$ is **irreducible** if it is non-zero, not a unit, and every factorisation $p=xy$ forces $x$ or $y$ to be a unit. It is **prime** if it is non-zero, not a unit, and whenever $p\mid xy$ then $p\mid x$ or $p\mid y$. Throughout, $d\mid a$ ("$d$ [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|divides]] $a$") means $a=dc$ for some $c\in R$ — equivalently $a\in(d)$, equivalently $(a)\subseteq(d)$.

![[Def - Ideal#The Definition]]

For $a_1,\dots,a_k\in R$ the ideal they **generate** is $(a_1,\dots,a_k)=\{a_1r_1+\cdots+a_kr_k:r_i\in R\}$ — the set of all $R$-linear combinations. In particular $(p,a)=\{rp+sa:r,s\in R\}$, and it contains both $p$ (take $r=1,s=0$) and $a$ (take $r=0,s=1$).

![[Def - Unit and Field#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Unit and Field|unit]] $u$ has a multiplicative inverse; the principal ideal it generates is the whole [[Def - Ring|ring]], $(u)=R$, because $r=u(u^{-1}r)$ for every $r$.

The bridge that makes the proof run — *the ideal–divisibility dictionary*:
$$d\mid a\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a\in(d)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (a)\subseteq(d).$$

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *prove-an-implication* problem of the most consequential kind: it is the **key lemma** that turns a principal ideal domain into a unique factorization domain. As the [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4]] strategy records, the gap between "irreducible" and "prime" is exactly the gap between "factorisations exist" and "factorisations are unique"; closing it for PIDs is what makes [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]] true.

**Assumption pattern.** The hypothesis "$R$ is a PID" is *only ever used in one way*: it converts a multi-generator ideal into a single-generator ideal. The recognisable trigger is that we have a *non-divisibility hypothesis* ($p\nmid a$) and want a *Bézout-type identity* ($1=rp+sa$). The link between these is the ideal $(p,a)$: a Bézout identity is precisely the statement $1\in(p,a)$, i.e. $(p,a)=R$.

**Theorem routing.** The route is: form $(p,a)$; use *PID* to write $(p,a)=(d)$; use the *divisibility dictionary* to get $d\mid p$; use *irreducibility of $p$* to split $p=q_1 d$ into "$q_1$ is a unit" or "$d$ is a unit". The first case is killed by the hypothesis $p\nmid a$ (it would make $p\mid a$). The second case gives $d$ a unit, hence $(d)=R$, hence $1\in(p,a)$ — a Bézout identity $1=rp+sa$. Multiplying this identity by $b$ and using $p\mid ab$ delivers $p\mid b$.

**Key decision point.** Two non-obvious moves. First, *the choice to study the ideal $(p,a)$ at all* — nothing in the statement mentions it; it is conjured precisely because it is the object on which "PID" can act. Second, *the case split from irreducibility*: $d\mid p$ gives $p=q_1d$, and irreducibility says one of $q_1,d$ is a unit; the genuine insight is recognising that the hypothesis $p\nmid a$ *exists solely to eliminate the wrong branch* (if $q_1$ were a unit, $d$ would be an associate of $p$, and $d\mid a$ would force $p\mid a$). This is the integer-coprimality argument — "$p\nmid a\Rightarrow\gcd(p,a)=1\Rightarrow 1=rp+sa$" — transplanted into a general PID.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Form the two-generator ideal $(p,a)$ as a probe.** Build the ideal generated by $p$ and the element $a$ that $p$ fails to divide; this is the object the PID hypothesis will simplify.

2. **Collapse a multi-generator ideal to one generator using the PID hypothesis.** Since $R$ is a PID, write $(p,a)=(d)$ for a single $d\in R$.

3. **Translate ideal containment into divisibility (the dictionary).** From $p\in(p,a)=(d)$ read off $d\mid p$; likewise $a\in(d)$ gives $d\mid a$.

4. **Split a factorisation using irreducibility.** From $d\mid p$, write $p=q_1d$; irreducibility of $p$ forces $q_1$ or $d$ to be a unit.

5. **Eliminate a case with a standing hypothesis.** The branch "$q_1$ is a unit" would make $d$ an associate of $p$ and yield $p\mid a$, contradicting $p\nmid a$; discard it.

6. **Use that a unit generates the whole ring to extract a Bézout identity.** $d$ a unit $\Rightarrow(d)=R\Rightarrow 1\in(p,a)\Rightarrow 1=rp+sa$ for some $r,s\in R$.

7. **Multiply an identity through and read off divisibility.** Multiply $1=rp+sa$ by $b$: $b=rpb+sab$; both terms are divisible by $p$ (the second because $p\mid ab$), so $p\mid b$.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> You are handed a *non-divisibility* fact, $p\nmid a$, and you want a *Bézout identity* $1=rp+sa$. The object that mediates between "an element is/ is not in a span" and "an element is a combination" is an *ideal*. Which ideal naturally has $p$, $a$ and (you hope) $1$ in it?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Consider $(p,a)=\{rp+sa:r,s\in R\}$. The hypothesis "$R$ is a PID" is there to be used — and it can only do one thing: rewrite $(p,a)$ as a single principal ideal $(d)$. Do that. Now $p\in(d)$ and $a\in(d)$. What does membership in $(d)$ say in the language of divisibility?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> From $p\in(d)$ you get $d\mid p$, so $p=q_1 d$. Now $p$ is *irreducible*: in any factorisation of $p$, one factor is a unit. So $q_1$ is a unit or $d$ is a unit. Rule out "$q_1$ is a unit": if it were, then $d=q_1^{-1}p$, and since $d\mid a$ this would give $p\mid a$ — contradicting $p\nmid a$. Therefore $d$ is a unit.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> $d$ is a unit, so $(d)=R$, hence $(p,a)=R$, hence $1\in(p,a)$: there are $r,s\in R$ with $1=rp+sa$. Multiply both sides by $b$ to get $b=rpb+sab$. Now stare at the right-hand side: the first term is $p\cdot(rb)$, and the second is $(sb)\cdot(ab)$ — but you assumed $p\mid ab$. So $p$ divides both terms, hence their sum $b$.

# Solution

The proof is the abstract form of the integer argument "if a prime $p$ does not divide $a$ then $p$ and $a$ are coprime, so $1=rp+sa$". The principal-ideal-domain hypothesis supplies the coprimality: it collapses the probe ideal $(p,a)$ to a single generator $d$, irreducibility of $p$ forces $d$ to be a unit, and a unit generator means $(p,a)$ is everything — which *is* the Bézout identity.

**Step 1: Form the probe ideal $(p,a)$ and collapse it with the PID hypothesis.**

The ideal $(p,a)\trianglelefteq R$ is principal: there is a single $d\in R$ with $(p,a)=(d)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Suppose $p$ is irreducible, $p\mid ab$, and $p\nmid a$; we must show $p\mid b$.
>
> Consider the ideal generated by $p$ and $a$,
> $$(p,a)=\{\,rp+sa:r,s\in R\,\}\;\trianglelefteq\;R.$$
> It is a genuine ideal: closed under addition and under multiplication by any ring element. By hypothesis $R$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] — *every* ideal of $R$ is principal. Applying this to the ideal $(p,a)$ produces a single element $d\in R$ with
> $$(p,a)=(d).$$
> This is the only place the PID hypothesis is used: it converts a *two-generator* ideal into a *one-generator* ideal.

**Step 2: $d$ divides both $p$ and $a$.**

From $(p,a)=(d)$, the divisibility dictionary gives $d\mid p$ and $d\mid a$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The ideal $(p,a)$ contains its generators: $p=1\cdot p+0\cdot a\in(p,a)$ and $a=0\cdot p+1\cdot a\in(p,a)$. Under the identification $(p,a)=(d)$ from Step 1,
> $$p\in(d)\qquad\text{and}\qquad a\in(d).$$
> By the **ideal–divisibility dictionary**, membership $x\in(d)$ is exactly $d\mid x$: since $(d)=\{dc:c\in R\}$, an element $x$ lies in $(d)$ if and only if $x=dc$ for some $c$, which is the definition of $d\mid x$. Hence
> $$d\mid p\qquad\text{and}\qquad d\mid a.$$
> In particular write $p=q_1 d$ for some $q_1\in R$.

**Step 3: $d$ is a unit.**

The factorisation $p=q_1 d$ together with irreducibility of $p$, and the hypothesis $p\nmid a$, forces $d$ to be a unit.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By Step 2, $p=q_1 d$. The element $p$ is [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|irreducible]]: in *any* factorisation of $p$ into two factors, one factor must be a unit. Hence
> $$q_1\text{ is a unit}\qquad\text{or}\qquad d\text{ is a unit}.$$
> *Eliminate the first case.* Suppose $q_1$ is a unit, with inverse $q_1^{-1}\in R$. From $p=q_1 d$ we get $d=q_1^{-1}p$. By Step 2, $d\mid a$, say $a=dc$; substituting, $a=q_1^{-1}p\,c=p\,(q_1^{-1}c)$, so $p\mid a$. This contradicts the standing hypothesis $p\nmid a$. So $q_1$ is *not* a unit.
>
> Irreducibility leaves exactly one surviving alternative: **$d$ is a unit.**

**Step 4: $(p,a)=R$, hence a Bézout identity $1=rp+sa$ exists.**

Since $d$ is a unit, $(d)=R$, so $(p,a)=R$; in particular $1\in(p,a)$, which means $1=rp+sa$ for some $r,s\in R$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> A [[Def - Unit and Field|unit]] $d$ generates the whole ring as a principal ideal: for any $x\in R$, $x=d\,(d^{-1}x)\in(d)$, so $(d)=R$. By Step 1, $(p,a)=(d)$, therefore
> $$(p,a)=R.$$
> In particular $1\in R=(p,a)$. By the description of the generated ideal, $1\in(p,a)$ means $1$ is an $R$-linear combination of $p$ and $a$: there exist $r,s\in R$ with
> $$1=rp+sa.$$
> This is the **Bézout identity** — the abstract analogue of "$\gcd(p,a)=1$" — and it is the engine of the final step.

**Step 5: Multiply by $b$ and conclude $p\mid b$.**

Multiplying $1=rp+sa$ by $b$ exhibits $b$ as a sum of two multiples of $p$. Hence $p\mid b$, so $p$ is prime.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Multiply the Bézout identity $1=rp+sa$ by $b$:
> $$b=1\cdot b=(rp+sa)\,b=rpb+sab=p\,(rb)+(sb)\,(ab).$$
> Examine the two summands.
> - $p\,(rb)$ is visibly a multiple of $p$, so $p\mid p(rb)$.
> - For the second summand, the hypothesis $p\mid ab$ gives $ab=pk$ for some $k\in R$; then $(sb)(ab)=(sb)(pk)=p\,(sbk)$, so $p\mid(sb)(ab)$.
>
> Both terms are divisible by $p$, and divisibility by $p$ is closed under addition (if $p\mid u$ and $p\mid v$ then $u=p\alpha$, $v=p\beta$, $u+v=p(\alpha+\beta)$). Therefore $p$ divides their sum:
> $$p\mid b.$$
> This is exactly what was required. Since $p$ is non-zero and not a unit (it is irreducible) and we have shown $p\mid ab\Rightarrow(p\mid a\text{ or }p\mid b)$, the element $p$ satisfies the definition of a prime. Hence every irreducible in a PID is prime. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** In a principal ideal domain $R$, every irreducible element is prime.
>
> Let $p$ be irreducible. Suppose $p\mid ab$ and $p\nmid a$; we show $p\mid b$.
>
> Form the ideal $(p,a)=\{rp+sa:r,s\in R\}$. Since $R$ is a PID, $(p,a)=(d)$ for some $d\in R$.
>
> As $p,a\in(p,a)=(d)$, we have $d\mid p$ and $d\mid a$; write $p=q_1 d$. Since $p$ is irreducible, $q_1$ or $d$ is a unit. If $q_1$ were a unit, then $d=q_1^{-1}p$; combined with $d\mid a$ this gives $a=dc=p(q_1^{-1}c)$, so $p\mid a$ — contradicting $p\nmid a$. Hence $d$ is a unit.
>
> A unit generates the whole ring, so $(p,a)=(d)=R$. Thus $1\in(p,a)$, i.e. $1=rp+sa$ for some $r,s\in R$. Multiplying by $b$:
> $$b=rpb+sab.$$
> The term $rpb$ is divisible by $p$; and $sab$ is divisible by $p$ because $p\mid ab$. Hence $p\mid b$.
>
> Therefore $p$ is non-zero, not a unit, and satisfies $p\mid ab\Rightarrow p\mid a\ \text{or}\ p\mid b$: $p$ is prime. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**To manufacture a Bézout identity, build the ideal whose elements *are* the candidate identities and prove it is the whole ring.** The conceptual core: a relation "$1=rp+sa$" is *the same statement as* "$1\in(p,a)$", which is *the same statement as* "$(p,a)=R$". So whenever a proof needs a Bézout-style combination, the move is to form the ideal generated by the relevant elements and show it equals $R$. This reframes an existence problem (do coefficients $r,s$ exist?) as a structural problem (is this ideal improper?). In a PID the structural problem is tractable because $(p,a)$ becomes a single $(d)$, and "$(d)=R$" reduces to "$d$ is a unit". The pattern — *replace "an identity exists" with "an ideal is everything"* — recurs across commutative algebra: it is how one proves coprime [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] satisfy the Chinese Remainder Theorem, and how Bézout's identity itself is established in $\mathbb{Z}$ and in $F[X]$.

**The PID hypothesis is a single-purpose tool: its only job is to collapse a many-generator ideal to one generator.** Notice that "$R$ is a PID" enters the proof exactly once — to rewrite $(p,a)$ as $(d)$ — and is never used again. This is characteristic. The strength of a principal ideal domain is *not* that it has special elements but that it has *no complicated ideals*: every ideal, however many generators you write it with, is secretly principal. So in any PID proof, the strategic question is "which ideal should I collapse?" The art of the proof is choosing the *right* probe ideal. Here it is $(p,a)$, assembled from the element $p$ we care about and the element $a$ that $p$ fails to divide — chosen so that collapsing it produces a generator $d$ whose only options (via irreducibility) are "unit" or "associate of $p$". When working in a PID, treat the hypothesis as a license to turn any finite list of elements into one element, and design the list so that the resulting generator is forced into a useful corner.

**"Irreducible $\Rightarrow$ prime" is the load-bearing lemma of unique factorisation, and it is exactly what general integral domains lack.** Prime always implies irreducible in any integral domain — that direction is cheap. The hard, structure-dependent direction is the converse, and *this exercise is its proof for PIDs*. Why does it matter? Existence of factorisations into irreducibles is comparatively easy (it follows from the ascending chain condition, which a PID satisfies). *Uniqueness* is the subtle half, and uniqueness is precisely a cancellation argument that needs "$p\mid q_1\cdots q_m\Rightarrow p\mid q_j$ for some $j$" — i.e. it needs irreducibles to be *prime*. So the implication ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD has this lemma as the gear connecting PID to UFD: see [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]]. The contrapositive is just as instructive: $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ has an irreducible ($2$) that is not prime (see [[Ex - Failure of unique factorization]]), and *that single failure* is the entire reason it is neither a UFD nor a PID. The gap between irreducible and prime is the precise measure of how badly factorisation can fail.

**The case split from irreducibility, and the hypothesis that kills the wrong branch.** Irreducibility is a *dichotomy*: any factorisation $p=q_1 d$ yields "$q_1$ is a unit OR $d$ is a unit". A proof using irreducibility almost always proceeds by extracting such a factorisation and then *eliminating one branch using a separate hypothesis*. Here the separate hypothesis is $p\nmid a$: if $q_1$ were the unit, then $d$ and $p$ would be associates and $d\mid a$ would propagate to $p\mid a$, contradiction. Recognising this is the key to reading the proof's logic — the hypothesis $p\nmid a$ is not decorative, it is the exact device that forces the *other* branch ($d$ a unit). The general lesson for spaced practice: when a proof invokes irreducibility, expect a two-case fork, and look for which standing assumption is engineered to close the unwanted case. If you return to this proof after months, the reconstruction hinges on remembering "$p\nmid a$ exists to kill the branch where $d$ is an associate of $p$."
