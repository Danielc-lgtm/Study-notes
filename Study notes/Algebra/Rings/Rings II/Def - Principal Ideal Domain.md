# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] — a non-zero commutative [[Def - Ring|ring]] with $1$ and no zero divisors. For $a \in R$, the **principal ideal** generated by $a$ is $(a) = aR = \{ar : r \in R\}$, and for $a_1, \dots, a_k \in R$ the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] they generate is $(a_1, \dots, a_k) = \{a_1 r_1 + \cdots + a_k r_k : r_i \in R\}$. We abbreviate "principal ideal domain" as PID where the long form would be cumbersome in prose, but spell it out in all definitions and statements. For a field $F$, $F[X]$ is the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]]; $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is the Gaussian integers. The chapter symbol registry is on [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4]].

# Axiom Motivation

We have a dictionary, established with the notion of [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|divisibility]], between *elements* of a ring and *principal [[Def - Ideal|ideals]]*: $a \mid b$ is the same statement as $(b) \subseteq (a)$, and $a, b$ are associates exactly when $(a) = (b)$. Under this dictionary, divisibility theory is the theory of *principal* ideals. But a ring has ideals that are not principal — the ideal $(2, X)$ of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ needs two generators — and on those the dictionary has nothing to say. A **principal ideal domain** is, by definition, a ring where this gap disappears: *every* ideal is principal, so the dictionary becomes a perfect translation between the ideal lattice and divisibility, with no ideal left over.

Why is "every ideal is principal" the *right* hypothesis to isolate, rather than some other constraint on the ideal structure? The motivation is entirely the consequences, and they are exactly the theorems of elementary number theory.

The first consequence concerns greatest common divisors. In $\mathbb{Z}$, the gcd of $a$ and $b$ is not merely a number dividing both; Bézout's identity says it is an *integer combination* $\gcd(a,b) = ra + sb$. Where does that come from? The set $\{ra + sb : r, s \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is the ideal $(a, b)$, and the reason a gcd with the Bézout property exists is that this ideal *is principal*, $(a, b) = (d)$, and then $d$ is the gcd. In a ring where $(a, b)$ failed to be principal, there would be no Bézout-style gcd. So "every ideal is principal" is precisely the hypothesis under which gcds behave as they do in $\mathbb{Z}$.

The second, deeper consequence is **factorisation**. In a general [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] an [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|irreducible element need not be prime]] — $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ has irreducibles that fail Euclid's lemma — and that failure is exactly what destroys unique factorisation. In a principal ideal domain, irreducible *does* imply prime, and the proof is a one-line use of the defining axiom: to show an irreducible $p$ divides $a$ or $b$ when $p \mid ab$ and $p \nmid a$, form the ideal $(p, a)$; it is principal, $(p,a) = (d)$, irreducibility of $p$ forces $d$ to be a unit, so $(p, a) = R$, giving a Bézout expression $1 = rp + sa$, and multiplying by $b$ finishes it. Every step needs $(p,a)$ to be principal. So "every ideal is principal" is also the precise hypothesis that repairs the irreducible/prime gap — and hence (with one more ingredient, the ascending chain condition) gives unique factorisation.

Now, what should the hypothesis *not* be? It would be too weak to ask only that finitely generated ideals be principal, or only that the ideal $(a,b)$ be principal for the particular pair you care about — you want the conclusion for all pairs, so you ask it for all ideals. It would be too strong to ask that every ideal be generated by a *single specified* element in some canonical way, or to demand a Euclidean function: a Euclidean function certainly forces every ideal to be principal (pick the ideal element of minimal size), but there exist principal ideal domains carrying *no* Euclidean function at all — the ring $\mathbb{Z}\bigl[\tfrac{1 + \sqrt{-19}}{2}\bigr]$ is the standard example. So "Euclidean" is strictly stronger than "PID". The principal-ideal-domain axiom is the Goldilocks choice: weak enough to include [[Def - Ring|rings]] with no Euclidean algorithm, strong enough to force Bézout gcds, irreducible-implies-prime, and unique factorisation. We bundle in "integral domain" because the whole point is divisibility theory, and divisibility behaves well only without zero divisors — the cancellation $ab = ac, a \neq 0 \Rightarrow b = c$ is used constantly.

# The Definition

A **principal ideal domain** is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] $R$ in which every ideal is principal: for every [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $I \trianglelefteq R$ there exists an element $a \in R$ with
$$I = (a) = aR.$$

Equivalently, $R$ is a non-zero commutative ring with $1$, having no zero divisors, such that every ideal is generated by a single element. The zero ideal is principal, $\{0\} = (0)$, and the whole ring is principal, $R = (1)$; the substance of the definition is that *every ideal in between* is principal too.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A principal ideal domain is the integral domain in which the **element/ideal dictionary is a bijection**. Recall the dictionary from [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|divisibility]]: the map $a \mapsto (a)$ sends elements to principal ideals, identifies associates, and turns divisibility into reverse inclusion. In a general ring this map lands only in the *principal* ideals, a proper subset of all ideals. The principal-ideal-domain axiom says the image is everything — so the lattice of ideals, ordered by inclusion, is order-isomorphic to the set of elements modulo associates, ordered by divisibility *upside down*. Every ideal-theoretic statement becomes a divisibility statement and vice versa. This is the cleanest compression: a PID is where "ideal theory" and "divisibility theory" are literally the same theory.

The concept sits inside a chain of strictly increasing generality, and locating it there is the best way to hold it:
$$\text{fields} \ \subsetneq \ \text{Euclidean domains} \ \subsetneq \ \text{principal ideal domains} \ \subsetneq \ \text{unique factorization domains} \ \subsetneq \ \text{integral domains}.$$
Each inclusion is a theorem and each is strict. A field is a PID for the trivial reason that its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and itself. A [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] is a PID because the Euclidean function lets you pick a minimal-size generator (see [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]]); the inclusion is strict because $\mathbb{Z}\bigl[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{-19}}{2}\bigr]$ is a PID with no Euclidean function. A PID is a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] (see [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]]); the inclusion is strict because $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a UFD but not a PID. And a UFD is an integral domain, strictly, since $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is an integral domain that is not a UFD.

There is a sharp comparison with linear algebra. An ideal of a ring is the ring-theoretic analogue of a subspace of a vector space; "principal" — generated by one element — is the analogue of "one-dimensional", i.e. a line. A *field* is a ring all of whose ideals are principal *and* the structure is so rigid there are only two of them. A principal ideal domain relaxes this: there can be many ideals, arranged in an intricate lattice, but each is still "one-generator". So a PID is, loosely, a ring whose ideal geometry is built entirely from "lines" — and this is exactly the structural simplicity that powers the classification of finitely generated modules over a PID, the theorem behind both the Jordan normal form and the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — $\mathbb{Z}$.** The integers form a principal ideal domain: every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ has the form $n\mathbb{Z} = (n)$. The proof is the Euclidean algorithm — given a non-zero ideal $I$, its smallest positive element $n$ generates it — and this is the historical prototype of the whole notion.

**Is an instance — $F[X]$ for a field $F$.** For any field $F$, the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] $F[X]$ is a principal ideal domain: it is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] under $\varphi = \deg$, and Euclidean domains are PIDs. A concrete payoff: for a matrix $A$ over $F$, the set $I = \{f \in F[X] : f(A) = 0\}$ of polynomials annihilating $A$ is an ideal, hence principal, $I = (m)$; the generator $m$ is the **minimal polynomial** of $A$, and the principal-ideal-domain property is precisely the statement that a minimal polynomial *exists* and *divides every other annihilating polynomial*.

**Is an instance — the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$.** Because $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] under the norm $\varphi(z) = |z|^2$, it is a principal ideal domain. This is the structural fact underlying the classification of which primes are sums of two squares.

**Is NOT an instance — $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.** The polynomial ring over the integers is an integral domain but **not** a principal ideal domain. The witness is the ideal $(2, X)$.

> [!note]- Why $(2, X)$ is not principal in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$
> Suppose $(2, X) = (f)$ for some $f \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. Since $2 \in (2, X) = (f)$, we have $2 = fg$ for some $g$; comparing degrees forces $\deg f = 0$, so $f$ is a constant integer, and $f \mid 2$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ leaves only $f \in \{\pm 1, \pm 2\}$.
>
> If $f = \pm 1$ then $(f) = \mathbb{Z}[X]$, the whole ring. But $(2, X) \neq \mathbb{Z}[X]$: every element of $(2, X)$ has even constant term (it is $2 \cdot p + X \cdot q$, whose constant term is $2 \cdot p(0)$), so $1 \notin (2, X)$.
>
> If $f = \pm 2$ then, since $X \in (2, X) = (f)$, we would need $\pm 2 \mid X$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, i.e. $X = \pm 2 \cdot h$ for some $h \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ — impossible, as the coefficients of $X$ are not all even.
>
> Every case is contradictory, so $(2, X)$ is not principal, and $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not a principal ideal domain. $\blacksquare$

Note that $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ *is* a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] — so this example also shows the inclusion (PID) $\subsetneq$ (UFD) is strict: being a UFD does not make a ring a PID.

**Is NOT an instance — $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$.** This integral domain is not a principal ideal domain. Indeed it is not even a unique factorization domain ($6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ are two distinct irreducible factorisations), and every PID is a UFD, so failure of unique factorisation rules out the PID property. Concretely, the ideal $(2, 1 + \sqrt{-5})$ is not principal.

**Corollary — every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain.** See [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]]: the Euclidean function supplies, in any non-zero ideal, an element of minimal value, and division with remainder forces that element to generate the ideal. The converse fails — there are principal ideal domains with no Euclidean function — so "Euclidean" is *strictly* stronger than "PID".

**Corollary — every principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain.** See [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]]. Two facts feed this: in a PID irreducible elements are prime (proved via Bézout on the ideal $(p, a)$), and a PID satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals (any increasing chain of ideals stabilises, because its union is a principal ideal whose generator lies in some term). Existence of factorisations comes from the chain condition; uniqueness comes from irreducible-equals-prime.

**Corollary — in a principal ideal domain, irreducible $\iff$ prime.** One direction holds in every integral domain (prime $\Rightarrow$ irreducible); the principal-ideal-domain axiom buys the converse. This collapse of the two notions is the single most useful structural feature of a PID.

**Calibration check.** Verify that a field is a principal ideal domain (its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and itself, both principal). Verify that in $\mathbb{Z}$ the ideal $(4, 6)$ is principal and equals $(2)$ — and that this is just $\gcd(4,6) = 2$ in disguise. Verify that the ascending chain $(2) \subseteq (2) \subseteq \cdots$ stabilises trivially, while in $\mathbb{Z}$ no *strictly* ascending chain of ideals can be infinite. If you can also explain why $(2, X) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}[X]$ — every element has even constant term — the definition and its boundary have landed.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains *(from this topic)*
> The principal-ideal-domain axiom is exactly what is needed to prove [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|every PID is a UFD]]: it delivers both the ascending chain condition (for existence of factorisations) and the upgrade irreducible $\Rightarrow$ prime (for uniqueness).

> [!tip] Structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID *(from module theory)*
> Modules over a principal ideal domain admit a complete classification: every finitely generated module is a direct sum of cyclic modules. This single theorem specialises to the structure theorem for finitely generated abelian groups (take $R = \mathbb{Z}$) and to the Jordan / rational canonical form for a linear operator (take $R = F[X]$). The PID hypothesis is indispensable — the classification fails over $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

> [!tip] Greatest common divisors and Bézout's identity *(from this topic)*
> In a principal ideal domain the [[Def - Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple|greatest common divisor]] of $a$ and $b$ always exists and satisfies a Bézout identity $\gcd(a,b) = ra + sb$, because the ideal $(a, b)$ is principal — generated precisely by a gcd.
