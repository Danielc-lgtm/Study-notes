# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] — a nonzero commutative [[Def - Ring|ring]] with $1_R \neq 0_R$ and no zero divisors. A **unit** is an element with a multiplicative inverse. For $a, b \in R$, "$a$ **divides** $b$", written $a \mid b$, means $b = ac$ for some $c \in R$; equivalently $(b) \subseteq (a)$. Elements $a, b$ are **associates** if $a = bu$ for a unit $u$; equivalently $(a) = (b)$. An element $p$ is **[[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|irreducible]]** if it is nonzero, not a unit, and $p = xy$ forces $x$ or $y$ to be a unit; it is **[[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|prime]]** if it is nonzero, not a unit, and $p \mid xy$ forces $p \mid x$ or $p \mid y$. $R$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] (PID) if every [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] is of the form $(b)$. $R$ is a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] (UFD) if every non-unit is a product of irreducibles, and such factorizations are unique up to reordering and associates. The symbol $(a_1, \dots, a_n)$ denotes the ideal *generated by* the listed elements, $\trianglelefteq$ means "is an ideal of". The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4]].

# Statement

> **Principal ideal domains are unique factorization domains.** Let $R$ be a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]]. Then $R$ is a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]]:
>
> 1. **(Existence.)** Every non-zero non-unit of $R$ can be written as a finite product of [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|irreducible]] elements.
> 2. **(Uniqueness.)** If $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n = q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m$ with all $p_i, q_j$ irreducible, then $n = m$ and, after reordering, $p_i$ is an [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|associate]] of $q_i$ for every $i$.
>
> The proof rests on two structural facts about a PID: every [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|irreducible]] element is [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|prime]]; and $R$ satisfies the **ascending chain condition** on [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] (every increasing chain of ideals stabilises).

---

# Motivation

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic says every integer factors into primes, and that the factorisation is essentially unique. We so thoroughly take this for granted that it is a shock to learn it can *fail*. In the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the number $6$ has two genuinely different factorisations into irreducible elements,
$$6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5}),$$
and $2$ is not an associate of either $1 \pm \sqrt{-5}$. Unique factorisation is a *privilege*, not a birthright, and the question this theorem answers is: **which [[Def - Ring|rings]] have it?** A useful, checkable sufficient condition would let us know, of a given ring, whether $\mathbb{Z}$-style arithmetic is available.

The theorem supplies exactly such a condition: being a *principal ideal domain* — every ideal generated by a single element — is enough. This is valuable because it converts an unwieldy question about *elements and their factorisations* into a tidy question about *ideals*. "Does every element factor uniquely?" is hard to attack head-on; "is every ideal principal?" is a structural property one can often verify in one stroke, and via [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains|Euclidean domains are PIDs]] it is automatic the moment a Euclidean function exists. So the chain Euclidean domain $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD lets a single division-with-remainder check certify the entire edifice of unique factorisation.

The diagnosis of *why* factorisation fails is the real content. Look again at $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. The element $2$ is irreducible — it cannot be split into smaller non-units — yet it is *not prime*: it divides the product $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5}) = 6$ without dividing either factor. **Irreducible and prime are different conditions in general**, and unique factorisation is precisely the demand that they coincide. Irreducibility is about not being *decomposable*; primality is about *detecting which factor of a product you sit inside*. Uniqueness of factorisation needs the second property, but the building blocks are defined by the first. The theorem's deep claim is that *in a PID the two notions merge* — every irreducible is prime — and that this merging, plus a finiteness condition that stops factorisation from going on forever, is exactly what unique factorisation requires. It tells you both *that* PIDs factor uniquely and *what* went wrong in the rings that do not.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition is "$R$ is a principal ideal domain". The skill is recognising the PID property without it being announced.

The first disguised source is **a ring already shown to be Euclidean**. The property $B$ is "$R$ carries a Euclidean function". By [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains|Euclidean domains are PIDs]], $R$ is then a PID, and this theorem makes it a UFD. The non-obvious part is that an algorithmic property (division with remainder) ultimately certifies a factorisation property (unique factorisation) two implications downstream. *Example problem:* prove $F[X]$ has unique factorisation of polynomials — it is Euclidean via $\deg$, hence a PID, hence a UFD.

The second disguised source is **a ring whose every ideal is visibly generated by one element**, even when "PID" is not stated. The property $B$ is "an explicit description of all ideals shows each is principal". The bridge is the definition itself. The non-obviousness lies in *recognising* that an ad hoc ideal description amounts to the PID hypothesis. *Example problem:* the formal power series ring $F[[X]]$, whose only ideals are $(X^n)$ and $\{0\}$, is a PID, hence a UFD.

The third disguised source is **a ring with the ascending chain condition together with the property that irreducibles are prime** — the two structural facts the proof actually uses. The property $B$ is "$R$ is Noetherian and every irreducible is prime". Strictly, the theorem is proved *through* these two facts, so any domain enjoying both is a UFD by the very same argument, even if it is not a PID. The non-obvious recognition is that the PID hypothesis is used *only* to derive these two intermediate facts. *Example problem:* a polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a UFD though it is not a PID — it is reached by a separate argument supplying the same two facts.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$R$ is a UFD — unique factorisation into irreducibles holds".

Combine the conclusion with **the demand for greatest common divisors**. In a UFD, gcds and lcms exist and are unique up to associates, constructed by taking the minimum (resp. maximum) exponent of each irreducible across the factorisations. The further result $E$ is the entire arithmetic of divisibility — coprimality, gcd, lcm — made available, exactly as in $\mathbb{Z}$. This is non-obvious because the *definition* of UFD mentions only factorisation, not gcds, yet gcds fall out of it directly.

Combine the conclusion with **a polynomial ring built on top**. Gauss's lemma states that if $R$ is a UFD then so is the polynomial ring $R[X]$. The further result $E$: starting from a UFD, all of $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ inherits unique factorisation, so multivariate polynomial rings over $\mathbb{Z}$ or over a field are UFDs. The non-obvious leverage is that the UFD property *propagates upward through polynomial extensions*, even though $R[X]$ is typically *not* a PID — so this theorem seeds a class of UFDs far larger than the class of PIDs.

Combine "every irreducible is prime" (the key intermediate fact) with **a single divisibility puzzle**. Once irreducibles are prime, "an irreducible $p$ divides a product" immediately yields "$p$ divides one of the factors". The further result $E$ is Euclid-style argumentation in $R$: the classic proofs about coprimality and cancellation transfer verbatim. The non-obvious point is that the *uniqueness* half of the theorem is essentially this one implication, applied repeatedly.

# Why Is It True

There are two things to believe — that factorisations *exist*, and that they are *unique* — and the intuitions are different, so take them in turn.

**Why factorisations exist.** Try to factor an element $r$ and imagine it *failing forever*. If $r$ is irreducible, you are done immediately, so suppose it is not: then $r = r_1 s_1$ with both factors non-units. If *both* $r_1$ and $s_1$ factored into irreducibles, so would $r$ — so at least one of them, say $r_1$, must itself resist factorisation. Repeat the argument on $r_1$: it splits as $r_2 s_2$, and again one factor, $r_2$, resists. You generate an infinite sequence $r, r_1, r_2, \dots$ in which each term is a *proper* factor of the previous one. Now translate "proper factor" into the language of ideals: $r_{k+1} \mid r_k$ means $(r_k) \subseteq (r_{k+1})$, and because the factor pulled out is a non-unit, the inclusion is *strict*. So a never-ending failure to factor produces a *strictly increasing infinite chain of ideals*
$$(r) \subsetneq (r_1) \subsetneq (r_2) \subsetneq \cdots.$$
But a PID *cannot have such a chain*. Why not? Take the union of all the ideals in the chain — it is itself an ideal, and in a PID it must be principal, $(a)$ for some single element $a$. That generator $a$ lives in the union, so it already lies in *one* of the ideals of the chain, say $(r_N)$. From that point on, the chain is squeezed: $(r_N) \subseteq (a) \subseteq (r_N)$, forcing the chain to be *constant* beyond stage $N$ — contradicting strict increase. The ascending chains *terminate*; the factorisation process therefore *cannot* run forever; so every element does break down into irreducibles. The intuition: in a PID there is no infinite descent through proper factors, because an infinite descent of elements would be an infinite ascent of ideals, and ideals in a PID have nowhere infinite to ascend to.

**Why factorisations are unique.** This is where irreducibility being too weak, and primality being just right, becomes the whole story. Suppose $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n = q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m$, two factorisations into irreducibles. Look at $p_1$. It certainly divides the left side, hence the right side: $p_1 \mid q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m$. Now — *if you only knew $p_1$ were irreducible* — you would be stuck, because irreducibility tells you $p_1$ cannot be split, but says nothing about *which* of the $q_j$ it might divide. This is exactly the failure in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: $2$ is irreducible and divides $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ but divides neither factor, and uniqueness collapses. The rescue is that in a PID *every irreducible is prime*. Primality is the missing detection power: $p_1 \mid q_1 \cdots q_m$ now forces $p_1$ to divide some single $q_j$. Reorder so it is $q_1$. But $q_1$ is itself irreducible, and $p_1 \mid q_1$ with $p_1$ a non-unit means $q_1 = p_1 \cdot(\text{something})$, and that something must be a unit — so $p_1$ and $q_1$ are *associates*. Cancel them (legal: $R$ is a domain, so nonzero elements cancel) and you face a shorter identity $p_2 \cdots p_n = (\text{unit})\,q_2 \cdots q_m$. Induct. The two lists are matched off one irreducible at a time, associate by associate, and the process can only end with both lists exhausted simultaneously — so $n = m$. Uniqueness is just the repeated use of one implication: *an irreducible divides a product only if it divides a factor.*

**Why is every irreducible prime in a PID?** This is the linchpin, and the idea is "manufacture coprimality". Let $p$ be irreducible and suppose $p \mid ab$ but $p \nmid a$; we want $p \mid b$. Form the ideal $(p, a)$. Since $R$ is a PID it is principal, $(p,a) = (d)$ for one element $d$. Then $d \mid p$, so writing $p = q_1 d$ and using that $p$ is *irreducible*, either $q_1$ or $d$ is a unit. If $q_1$ were a unit, then $d$ would be an associate of $p$, and $d \mid a$ would give $p \mid a$ — contradicting $p \nmid a$. So $d$ is a unit, which means $(p,a) = (d) = R$. In particular $1 \in (p,a)$, so $1 = rp + sa$ for some $r, s \in R$ — a Bézout relation expressing that $p$ and $a$ are "coprime". Multiply through by $b$: $b = rpb + sab$. The first term is divisible by $p$ trivially; the second is divisible by $p$ because $p \mid ab$. So $p \mid b$. The PID property entered exactly once, to make $(p, a)$ principal, which is what produced the Bézout relation — and the Bézout relation is precisely the algebraic form of "$p$ and $a$ share no common factor".

Standing back: a PID has two gifts. The principal-ideal property forbids infinite ascending chains, which *bounds* factorisation and gives *existence*; and it forces irreducibles to be prime, which gives the detection power that delivers *uniqueness*. Unique factorisation is the conjunction of "factorisation terminates" and "irreducible = prime", and a PID hands you both.

# What Makes This Hard

The genuinely non-obvious step is proving *irreducible implies prime*: the trick is to form the ideal $(p, a)$, use the PID hypothesis to make it principal, and extract a Bézout relation $1 = rp + sa$ — recognising that "$p$ does not divide $a$" must be converted into manufactured coprimality is where most people stall. For existence, the subtle move is translating an unending factorisation process into a strictly ascending chain of *ideals* and then killing that chain via "the union is principal, so its generator already appears at a finite stage". The most common error is to attempt uniqueness using only irreducibility — which is insufficient, exactly as $6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ shows — instead of first upgrading irreducible to prime.

---

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Prove two structural facts about the PID $R$ first: (A) every irreducible is prime, and (B) $R$ has no infinite strictly ascending chain of ideals (the ACC). Then existence of factorisations follows from (B) — an element with no factorisation would generate an infinite ascending chain — and uniqueness follows from (A) — peel off matching associates one at a time.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Irreducible $\Rightarrow$ prime.** Show every irreducible $p \in R$ is prime.
   - *Hint:* Assume $p \mid ab$, $p \nmid a$. Make $(p,a) = (d)$ principal; from $d \mid p$ and $p$ irreducible deduce $d$ is a unit, so $(p,a) = R$; get $1 = rp + sa$; multiply by $b$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the engine of the uniqueness proof — it gives an irreducible the power to detect which factor of a product it divides.

2. **Ascending chain condition.** Show every chain $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ of ideals of $R$ stabilises.
   - *Hint:* The union $I = \bigcup I_n$ is an ideal; in a PID, $I = (a)$; the generator $a$ lies in some $I_N$, forcing $I_n = I_N$ for all $n \geq N$.
   - *Why needed:* It guarantees the factorisation process terminates, giving existence.

3. **Existence of factorisations.** Show every non-zero non-unit is a product of irreducibles.
   - *Hint:* If $r$ has no such factorisation, build $r = r_1 s_1$ with $r_1$ also unfactorisable; iterate to get $(r) \subsetneq (r_1) \subsetneq (r_2) \subsetneq \cdots$, contradicting the ACC.
   - *Why needed:* It is half of the UFD definition.

4. **Uniqueness of factorisations.** Show two irreducible factorisations of the same element match up.
   - *Hint:* From $p_1 \cdots p_n = q_1 \cdots q_m$, $p_1$ divides the product of $q_j$; as $p_1$ is prime (step 1) it divides some $q_j$; reorder, deduce $p_1, q_1$ are associates; cancel (domain) and induct.
   - *Why needed:* It is the other half of the UFD definition.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: In any integral domain, every prime element is irreducible
> **Statement:** Let $R$ be an integral domain and $r \in R$ prime. Then $r$ is irreducible.
>
> **Hint:** Write $r = ab$; since $r \mid r = ab$ and $r$ is prime, $r$ divides a factor; cancel in the domain.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the "easy direction" — together with Lemma 2 (its PID-only converse) it establishes that in a PID irreducible and prime coincide, which is exactly what uniqueness needs.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $r$ be prime, so $r$ is nonzero, not a unit. Suppose $r = ab$. Then $r \mid ab$, and since $r$ is prime, $r \mid a$ or $r \mid b$; say, without loss of generality, $r \mid a$, so $a = rc$ for some $c \in R$. Substituting, $r = ab = rcb$, hence $r \cdot 1_R = r \cdot (cb)$. Since $R$ is an integral domain and $r \neq 0$, we may cancel $r$: $1_R = cb$. So $b$ is a unit. Thus in $r = ab$ one of the factors is a unit, and $r$ is irreducible. (This direction needs only "integral domain", not "PID".)

> [!note]- Lemma 2: In a PID, every irreducible element is prime
> **Statement:** Let $R$ be a principal ideal domain and $p \in R$ irreducible. Then $p$ is prime.
>
> **Hint:** Suppose $p \mid ab$ and $p \nmid a$. The ideal $(p, a)$ is principal, $(d)$; use irreducibility of $p$ to force $d$ to be a unit, so $(p,a) = R$ and $1 = rp + sa$; multiply by $b$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the *linchpin*: it upgrades irreducibles to primes, which is the entire mechanism of the uniqueness proof. The general inequivalence (e.g. $2 \in \mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is irreducible but not prime) shows the PID hypothesis is doing essential work.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $p$ be irreducible; then $p$ is nonzero and not a unit. Suppose $p \mid a \cdot b$; we must show $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$. Assume $p \nmid a$ and aim to prove $p \mid b$.
> >
> > Consider the ideal $(p, a) \trianglelefteq R$. Since $R$ is a principal ideal domain, $(p, a) = (d)$ for some $d \in R$. Because $p \in (p,a) = (d)$, we have $d \mid p$, so $p = q_1 d$ for some $q_1 \in R$. As $p$ is irreducible, in this factorisation either $q_1$ is a unit or $d$ is a unit.
> >
> > *Case $q_1$ a unit.* Then $d = q_1^{-1} p$, so $d$ is an associate of $p$. Also $a \in (p, a) = (d)$, so $d \mid a$, hence $p \mid a$ (since $p$ and $d$ are associates). This contradicts the assumption $p \nmid a$.
> >
> > *Therefore $d$ is a unit.* Then $(p, a) = (d) = R$. In particular $1_R \in (p, a)$, so there exist $r, s \in R$ with
> > $$1_R = r p + s a.$$
> > Multiply this equation through by $b$:
> > $$b = r p b + s a b.$$
> > Now $p \mid rpb$ obviously, and $p \mid sab$ because $p \mid ab$ by hypothesis. Hence $p$ divides the sum, $p \mid b$. So $p$ is prime.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: A PID satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals
> **Statement:** Let $R$ be a principal ideal domain and $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ an ascending chain of ideals. Then there is $N$ with $I_n = I_N$ for all $n \geq N$.
>
> **Hint:** The union of an ascending chain of ideals is an ideal; in a PID it is principal; its generator already lies at a finite stage.
>
> **Why needed:** It forbids an infinite chain of strictly larger ideals, which is exactly what makes the factorisation process terminate — the existence half of the theorem.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $I = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} I_n$. First, $I$ is an ideal: if $x, y \in I$ then $x \in I_j$ and $y \in I_k$ for some $j, k$; taking $\ell = \max(j,k)$, both $x, y \in I_\ell$ (the chain is ascending), so $x - y \in I_\ell \subseteq I$; and for $x \in I_j$ and $t \in R$, $tx \in I_j \subseteq I$. So $I$ is closed under subtraction and absorbs multiplication, hence is an ideal.
> >
> > Since $R$ is a principal ideal domain, $I = (a)$ for some $a \in R$. As $a \in I = \bigcup_n I_n$, the element $a$ lies in some $I_N$. Then for every $n \geq N$:
> > $$(a) \subseteq I_N \subseteq I_n \subseteq I = (a),$$
> > where the first inclusion holds because $a \in I_N$, the next two because the chain is ascending and $I$ is the union. All terms are squeezed equal, so $I_n = I_N$ (indeed $= I = (a)$) for all $n \geq N$. The chain stabilises. (Rings with this property are called Noetherian.)

> [!note]- Lemma 4: Existence — every non-zero non-unit factors into irreducibles
> **Statement:** In a principal ideal domain $R$, every non-zero non-unit $r$ is a finite product of irreducible elements.
>
> **Hint:** If not, repeatedly extract an unfactorisable proper factor, building a strictly ascending chain of principal ideals; contradict the ACC (Lemma 3).
>
> **Why needed:** It is part (1) of the UFD definition.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Suppose, for contradiction, that some non-zero non-unit $r \in R$ is *not* a product of irreducibles. In particular $r$ is not itself irreducible, so we can write $r = r_1 s_1$ with $r_1, s_1$ both non-units. If both $r_1$ and $s_1$ were products of irreducibles, then so would $r$ be; hence at least one of them is not a product of irreducibles — without loss of generality, $r_1$. Note $r_1$ is a non-zero non-unit (a factor of the nonzero $r$ in a domain is nonzero, and it was chosen a non-unit).
> >
> > Apply the same reasoning to $r_1$: write $r_1 = r_2 s_2$ with $r_2, s_2$ non-units and $r_2$ not a product of irreducibles. Iterating produces an infinite sequence $r, r_1, r_2, \dots$ in which $r_{k+1}$ is a proper factor of $r_k$ — "proper" because the cofactor $s_{k+1}$ is a non-unit, so $r_k$ and $r_{k+1}$ are *not* associates. Translating divisibility into ideals, $r_{k+1} \mid r_k$ gives $(r_k) \subseteq (r_{k+1})$, and non-associateness makes the inclusion strict:
> > $$(r) \subsetneq (r_1) \subsetneq (r_2) \subsetneq \cdots.$$
> > This is an infinite *strictly* ascending chain of ideals, contradicting the ascending chain condition (Lemma 3), which says any ascending chain — in particular this one — must eventually be constant. The contradiction shows no such $r$ exists: every non-zero non-unit is a product of irreducibles.

> [!note]- Lemma 5: Uniqueness — irreducible factorisations agree up to order and associates
> **Statement:** In a principal ideal domain $R$, if $p_1 \cdots p_n = q_1 \cdots q_m$ with all $p_i, q_j$ irreducible, then $n = m$ and, after reordering, $p_i$ and $q_i$ are associates for each $i$.
>
> **Hint:** $p_1$ is prime (Lemma 2); it divides $\prod q_j$, hence some $q_j$; reorder, conclude $p_1, q_1$ associate, cancel in the domain, induct.
>
> **Why needed:** It is part (2) of the UFD definition, and it is where "irreducible $\Rightarrow$ prime" is indispensable.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Argue by induction on $n$, the number of irreducible factors on the left.
> >
> > Suppose $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n = q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m$ with all factors irreducible. The element $p_1$ divides the left-hand side, hence divides the right-hand side: $p_1 \mid q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m$. By Lemma 2, $p_1$ is *prime*; applying the prime property repeatedly, $p_1$ divides one of the factors $q_j$. Reorder the $q$'s so that $p_1 \mid q_1$. Write $q_1 = p_1 \cdot a$ for some $a \in R$. Since $q_1$ is irreducible and $p_1$ is not a unit, the cofactor $a$ must be a unit; hence $p_1$ and $q_1$ are associates.
> >
> > Substitute $q_1 = a p_1$ into the equation:
> > $$p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n = (a p_1) q_2 \cdots q_m = p_1 (a q_2) q_3 \cdots q_m.$$
> > Since $R$ is an integral domain and $p_1 \neq 0$, cancel $p_1$:
> > $$p_2 \cdots p_n = (a q_2) q_3 \cdots q_m.$$
> > The element $a q_2$ is an associate of the irreducible $q_2$, hence itself irreducible; rename it $q_2$. We now have an equality of two irreducible factorisations with $n - 1$ factors on the left. By the induction hypothesis, $n - 1 = m - 1$ (so $n = m$) and, after reordering, $p_i$ is an associate of $q_i$ for $i = 2, \dots, n$. Together with $p_1$ associate to $q_1$, this gives the claim. (Base case $n = 1$: $p_1 = q_1 \cdots q_m$ with $p_1$ irreducible forces $m = 1$, since any further irreducible factor would make $p_1$ a product of two non-units.) The counting also rules out $n \neq m$: were $n = m + k$ with $k > 0$, cancelling all $m$ matched pairs would leave $p_{m+1} \cdots p_n$ equal to a unit, impossible for irreducibles.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a principal ideal domain. We show $R$ is a unique factorization domain by establishing existence and uniqueness of factorisations into irreducibles. The proof uses two structural facts, each proved first.
>
> ---
> **Fact A — every irreducible is prime (Lemma 2).** Let $p$ be irreducible and suppose $p \mid ab$ with $p \nmid a$. Since $R$ is a PID, the ideal $(p, a)$ is principal, $(p,a) = (d)$. From $p \in (d)$ we get $d \mid p$, so $p = q_1 d$; irreducibility of $p$ forces $q_1$ or $d$ to be a unit. If $q_1$ is a unit then $d$ is an associate of $p$, and $a \in (d)$ gives $d \mid a$, hence $p \mid a$ — contradiction. So $d$ is a unit and $(p,a) = R$, whence $1 = rp + sa$ for some $r, s \in R$. Multiplying by $b$, $b = rpb + sab$; both terms are divisible by $p$ (the first trivially, the second since $p \mid ab$), so $p \mid b$. Thus $p$ is prime. (Conversely, every prime is irreducible in any domain, Lemma 1; so in a PID the two notions coincide.)
>
> ---
> **Fact B — the ascending chain condition (Lemma 3).** Let $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ be an ascending chain of ideals and $I = \bigcup_n I_n$. Then $I$ is an ideal (closure under subtraction and absorption follow since any two elements lie in a common $I_\ell$). As $R$ is a PID, $I = (a)$; the generator $a$ lies in some $I_N$, so for $n \geq N$, $(a) \subseteq I_N \subseteq I_n \subseteq I = (a)$, forcing $I_n = I_N$. Every ascending chain stabilises.
>
> ---
> **Existence (Lemma 4).** Suppose some non-zero non-unit $r$ is not a product of irreducibles. Then $r$ is not irreducible, so $r = r_1 s_1$ with $r_1, s_1$ non-units; since $r$ is not a product of irreducibles, at least one factor is not — say $r_1$. Iterating, $r_k = r_{k+1} s_{k+1}$ with each $s_{k+1}$ a non-unit and each $r_{k+1}$ not a product of irreducibles. Each $r_{k+1}$ is a *proper* factor of $r_k$, so $(r_k) \subsetneq (r_{k+1})$ (strict, because $s_{k+1}$ is a non-unit, so $r_k, r_{k+1}$ are not associates). This yields an infinite strictly ascending chain
> $$(r) \subsetneq (r_1) \subsetneq (r_2) \subsetneq \cdots,$$
> contradicting Fact B. Hence every non-zero non-unit of $R$ is a finite product of irreducibles.
>
> ---
> **Uniqueness (Lemma 5).** Suppose $p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n = q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m$ with all $p_i, q_j$ irreducible; induct on $n$. The factor $p_1$ divides the left side, hence $p_1 \mid q_1 \cdots q_m$. By Fact A, $p_1$ is prime, so $p_1$ divides some $q_j$; reorder so $p_1 \mid q_1$, and write $q_1 = p_1 a$. Since $q_1$ is irreducible and $p_1$ is not a unit, $a$ is a unit, so $p_1$ and $q_1$ are associates. Substituting and cancelling $p_1$ (valid: $R$ is a domain, $p_1 \neq 0$):
> $$p_2 \cdots p_n = (a q_2) q_3 \cdots q_m,$$
> and $a q_2$ is an associate of the irreducible $q_2$, hence irreducible; rename it $q_2$. By the induction hypothesis applied to this $(n-1)$-factor identity, $n - 1 = m - 1$ and, after reordering, $p_i$ is an associate of $q_i$ for $i \geq 2$. With $p_1$ associate to $q_1$, the factorisations agree up to order and associates; in particular $n = m$ (a leftover product of irreducibles can never equal a unit).
>
> ---
> **Conclusion.** Every non-zero non-unit of $R$ factors into irreducibles, and the factorisation is unique up to reordering and associates. Hence $R$ is a unique factorization domain. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Unique factorisation of polynomials over a field.** For a field $F$, the ring $F[X]$ is Euclidean (via $\deg$), hence a PID, hence — by this theorem — a UFD. So every polynomial over a field factors uniquely into irreducible polynomials. The application is nonobvious because the *unique factorisation of polynomials*, normally treated as a basic fact, is here revealed as a downstream consequence of the PID structure of $F[X]$, and "irreducible polynomial = prime polynomial" is the in-disguise instance of Fact A.

**The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, structurally.** Applying the theorem to $R = \mathbb{Z}$ — a PID, since every ideal is $n\mathbb{Z}$ — recovers the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: every integer factors uniquely into primes. The application is nonobvious in that the elementary theorem is reproved at a structural level, with the *uniqueness* half identified as nothing but "every irreducible integer is prime" — the abstract content of Euclid's lemma.

**Gaussian integers and the two-squares theorem.** The ring $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is Euclidean (norm function), hence a PID, hence a UFD. Unique factorisation in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is the structural backbone of the classification of integers expressible as a sum of two squares: an odd prime $p$ is a sum of two squares precisely when $p$ *factors* in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, and the UFD property controls how. The application is nonobvious because a question about *integers* is resolved through unique factorisation in a *larger* ring, certified by this theorem.

**Why factorisation fails — diagnosing $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$.** Used in contrapositive, the theorem explains the failure $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$: since unique factorisation fails, $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ cannot be a PID, and indeed the ideal $(2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ is non-principal. The application is nonobvious because the theorem is run *backwards* — non-uniqueness of element factorisation is converted into the existence of a non-principal ideal, which is the seed of ideal-theoretic number theory (factor *ideals* instead of elements).

# Bridges

- **[[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|Irreducible and Prime Elements]]** — this theorem is the definitive statement on their relationship. In general (Lemma 1) prime $\Rightarrow$ irreducible always, but irreducible $\not\Rightarrow$ prime; in a PID (Lemma 2) the implication closes and the two coincide. Unique factorisation is precisely the demand that they coincide.

- **[[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains|Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]]** — the preceding link in the chain. Composing the two gives Euclidean domain $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD, so a single Euclidean function certifies unique factorisation. Both implications are strict: there are PIDs that are not Euclidean, and UFDs that are not PIDs.

- **[[Def - Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple|Greatest Common Divisor and Least Common Multiple]]** — a downstream consequence. In a UFD, gcds and lcms exist and are unique up to associates, built by taking minima and maxima of the exponents in the irreducible factorisations. So this theorem is what makes the gcd–lcm calculus available in a PID.

- **Gauss's Lemma and polynomial rings** — the propagation result. Gauss's lemma states $R$ a UFD implies $R[X]$ a UFD. Since $R[X]$ is generally *not* a PID (e.g. $\mathbb{Z}[X]$), this produces a class of UFDs strictly larger than the PIDs, all seeded by this theorem applied to the base ring.

- **Noetherian rings and the Hilbert Basis Theorem** — the ascending chain condition (Lemma 3) is the definition of a Noetherian ring. The PID is the simplest Noetherian domain; the Hilbert Basis Theorem extends "Noetherian" to polynomial rings, and the ACC is the general tool guaranteeing that factorisation-like processes terminate.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Structure Theorem for Modules over a PID *(from Module Theory)*
> A PID being a UFD underpins the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID: every such module is a direct sum of cyclic modules $R/(d_i)$. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ this classifies finitely generated abelian groups; over $F[X]$ it yields the Jordan and rational canonical forms.

> [!tip] Dedekind Domains and Unique Factorisation of Ideals *(from Algebraic Number Theory)*
> Rings of integers in number fields are generally *not* UFDs — elements need not factor uniquely. The repair is to factor *ideals* instead: in a Dedekind domain every ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals. The PID case, where unique factorisation of elements does hold, is the well-behaved extreme that motivates the general theory.
