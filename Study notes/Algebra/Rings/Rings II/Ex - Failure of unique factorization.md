# Problem Statement

Work in the ring
$$R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]=\{\,a+b\sqrt{-5}:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\,\}\;\leq\;\mathbb{C},$$
the subring of $\mathbb{C}$ generated by $\sqrt{-5}$. Equip it with the **norm**
$$N:R\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\qquad N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2,$$
which is the function $z\mapsto z\bar z=|z|^2$ restricted to $R$.

1. Show that the **units** of $R$ are exactly $\pm 1$.
2. Show that $2$, $3$, $1+\sqrt{-5}$ and $1-\sqrt{-5}$ are all **irreducible** in $R$ — using the fact that no element of $R$ has norm $2$ or $3$.
3. Exhibit the two factorisations
$$6=2\cdot 3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$$
and show they are **genuinely different** factorisations of $6$ into irreducibles — no irreducible on one side is an associate of one on the other.
4. Conclude that $2$ is **not prime**, and hence that $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is **not a unique factorization domain**.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are an integral domain, the multiplicative norm on it, the units, irreducible and prime elements, and the definition of a unique factorization domain.

![[Def - Integral Domain#The Definition]]

$R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] because it is a subring of the field $\mathbb{C}$, and any subring of a field inherits the absence of zero divisors.

![[Def - Unit and Field#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Unit and Field|unit]] of $R$ is an element $u$ with a two-sided multiplicative inverse $u^{-1}\in R$, i.e. $uu^{-1}=1$.

![[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements#The Definition]]

An element $a$ is **irreducible** if it is non-zero, not a unit, and every factorisation $a=xy$ forces $x$ or $y$ to be a unit. An element $p$ is **prime** if it is non-zero, not a unit, and whenever $p\mid xy$ then $p\mid x$ or $p\mid y$. Here $p\mid x$ ("$p$ [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|divides]] $x$") means $x=pc$ for some $c\in R$.

![[Def - Unique Factorization Domain#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] (UFD) is an integral domain in which every non-unit is a product of irreducibles, and that product is unique up to reordering and replacing factors by associates. Two elements are **associates** if each is a unit multiple of the other; equivalently they generate the same principal ideal.

The single tool driving every part of this problem is **multiplicativity of the norm**:
$$N(zw)=N(z)\,N(w)\qquad\text{for all }z,w\in R.$$
This holds because $N(z)=z\bar z$ and complex conjugation is a ring homomorphism, so $N(zw)=(zw)\overline{(zw)}=z\bar z\,w\bar w=N(z)N(w)$. The norm converts a multiplicative question in $R$ — divisibility, factorisation — into an *additive/multiplicative question among ordinary non-negative integers*, where we have full control.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *structural non-example* problem: exhibit a concrete ring failing a desirable property (unique factorisation), and prove the failure rigorously. As the [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4]] strategy records, the way to refute "$R$ is a UFD" is to produce one element with two factorisations into irreducibles that are *not* matched up to associates. The whole proof is then bookkeeping: certify that each factor is genuinely irreducible, and certify that the two lists do not match.

**Assumption pattern.** The ring is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$ carrying a *multiplicative norm valued in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$*. That single feature is the entire engine. A multiplicative norm transports the three notions we must control — being a unit, being irreducible, being an associate — down to arithmetic of integers: units are exactly the norm-$1$ elements, an irreducible cannot be split because its norm cannot be split into a product of two norms each $>1$, and associates have equal norm.

**Theorem routing.** No named theorem; the route is *norm multiplicativity* applied three times. (a) $u$ a unit $\Rightarrow N(u)\mid N(1)=1\Rightarrow N(u)=1$, and $a^2+5b^2=1$ has only $a=\pm1,b=0$. (b) If $2=xy$ then $4=N(2)=N(x)N(y)$; the only factorisations of $4$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ are $1\cdot 4$, $2\cdot 2$, $4\cdot 1$, and *no element has norm $2$*, so the middle case is impossible, forcing $N(x)=1$ or $N(y)=1$ — a unit. The same count handles $3$ ($N=9$, no norm $3$) and $1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ ($N=6$, factorisations $1\cdot6,2\cdot3,3\cdot2,6\cdot1$, and norms $2,3$ are both unavailable). (c) Associate elements have equal norm, so an element of norm $4$ cannot be an associate of one of norm $6$.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious move is *which integer to forbid*. The proof never needs to enumerate elements of $R$; it needs only the negative fact that the *values* $2$ and $3$ are not attained by the form $a^2+5b^2$. Checking this is a finite, two-line case analysis ($b=0$ forces a perfect square; $|b|\geq1$ already gives $a^2+5b^2\geq5$). Forbidding the *intermediate* norms is exactly what blocks the intermediate factorisations and makes four genuinely composite-looking numbers irreducible.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Push a multiplicative question through the norm.** Apply $N(zw)=N(z)N(w)$ to convert "$z$ is a unit / divides / factorises" into a statement about the integers $N(z)$.

2. **Classify units as norm-$1$ elements.** From $uu^{-1}=1$ deduce $N(u)N(u^{-1})=1$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, so $N(u)=1$; then solve $a^2+5b^2=1$ over $\mathbb{Z}$.

3. **Certify irreducibility by a forbidden-norm count.** To show $a$ (with $N(a)=n$) is irreducible, list the factorisations $n=d\cdot e$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ and rule out every "balanced" split $d,e>1$ by showing $R$ has no element of norm $d$.

4. **Detect non-associates by comparing norms.** Associates satisfy $b=ua$ with $u$ a unit, so $N(b)=N(u)N(a)=N(a)$. Unequal norms therefore certify non-associate.

5. **Refute primality with a divisibility obstruction read off the norm.** To show $2\nmid 1+\sqrt{-5}$, note $2\mid w$ would force $N(2)\mid N(w)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$; since $4\nmid 6$, the divisibility fails.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Everything in this problem is controlled by one fact: the norm $N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2$ is *multiplicative*, $N(zw)=N(z)N(w)$. Whenever you see a multiplicative statement in $R$ — "$u$ is a unit", "$a=xy$", "$a\mid b$" — apply $N$ to both sides and read off a statement about ordinary integers.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> For the units: if $u$ is a unit with inverse $u^{-1}$, then $N(u)N(u^{-1})=N(1)=1$. Two non-negative integers multiplying to $1$ are both $1$. So solve $a^2+5b^2=1$ for integers $a,b$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For irreducibility of $2$: suppose $2=xy$. Then $4=N(2)=N(x)N(y)$. The factorisations of $4$ are $1\cdot 4$, $2\cdot 2$, $4\cdot 1$. The case $N(x)=N(y)=2$ would need an element of norm $2$. Is there one? Check whether $a^2+5b^2=2$ has integer solutions — and likewise $a^2+5b^2=3$. The absence of norms $2$ and $3$ is the entire content.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Two elements that are associates differ by a unit factor, and units have norm $1$, so associates have *equal norm*. Compute $N(2)=4$, $N(3)=9$, $N(1\pm\sqrt{-5})=6$. Since $4,9,6$ are all different, no factor of $2\cdot 3$ can be an associate of a factor of $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$. For "$2$ is not prime", note $2\mid 6=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ but ask whether $2$ divides either factor — and use $2\mid w\Rightarrow N(2)\mid N(w)$.

# Solution

The norm $N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2$ is multiplicative, and this single fact does all the work. It pins the units to norm $1$, makes four elements irreducible by forbidding the intermediate norms $2$ and $3$, and separates the two factorisations of $6$ by their norms $4,9$ versus $6,6$.

**Step 1: The norm is multiplicative, and no element has norm $2$ or $3$.**

For all $z,w\in R$, $N(zw)=N(z)N(w)$. Moreover the equations $a^2+5b^2=2$ and $a^2+5b^2=3$ have no solutions with $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Multiplicativity.* Write $N(z)=z\bar z$ where $\bar{\;\cdot\;}$ is complex conjugation. Conjugation is a ring homomorphism $\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$, and it maps $R$ into $R$ since $\overline{a+b\sqrt{-5}}=a-b\sqrt{-5}\in R$. Hence
> $$N(zw)=(zw)\overline{(zw)}=(zw)(\bar z\bar w)=(z\bar z)(w\bar w)=N(z)\,N(w).$$
> Explicitly, with $z=a+b\sqrt{-5}$ and $w=c+d\sqrt{-5}$, one can also verify
> $$(a^2+5b^2)(c^2+5d^2)=(ac-5bd)^2+5(ad+bc)^2,$$
> which is the Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity for the form $x^2+5y^2$.
>
> *Forbidden norms.* Suppose $a^2+5b^2=n$ with $n\in\{2,3\}$. If $|b|\geq 1$ then $5b^2\geq 5>n$, impossible. So $b=0$ and $a^2=n$; but neither $2$ nor $3$ is a perfect square. Hence $a^2+5b^2\in\{2,3\}$ has no integer solution. (The values *attained* by the form start $0,1,4,5,6,9,\dots$ — note the gaps at $2,3,7,8$.)

**Step 2: The units of $R$ are exactly $\pm 1$.**

An element $u\in R$ is a unit if and only if $N(u)=1$, and the only such elements are $u=\pm 1$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> ($\Rightarrow$) If $u$ is a unit with inverse $u^{-1}\in R$, then applying $N$ to $u\cdot u^{-1}=1$ and using Step 1,
> $$N(u)\,N(u^{-1})=N(1)=1^2+5\cdot 0^2=1.$$
> This is an equation in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$: two non-negative integers whose product is $1$. The only possibility is $N(u)=N(u^{-1})=1$.
>
> Now solve $N(u)=1$, i.e. $a^2+5b^2=1$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$. If $|b|\geq 1$ then $5b^2\geq 5>1$, so $b=0$ and $a^2=1$, giving $a=\pm 1$. Hence $u\in\{1,-1\}$.
>
> ($\Leftarrow$) Conversely $1$ and $-1$ are visibly units: $1\cdot 1=1$ and $(-1)(-1)=1$. So the unit group of $R$ is exactly $\{+1,-1\}$.

**Step 3: $2$, $3$, $1+\sqrt{-5}$, $1-\sqrt{-5}$ are all irreducible.**

Each of these elements is non-zero and not a unit, and any factorisation forces one factor to be a unit. So each is irreducible.

> [!note]- Derivation
> First note $N(2)=4$, $N(3)=9$, $N(1\pm\sqrt{-5})=1^2+5\cdot 1^2=6$. All four norms exceed $1$, so by Step 2 none of the four elements is a unit; none is $0$.
>
> *$2$ is irreducible.* Suppose $2=xy$ with $x,y\in R$. Applying $N$: $\;4=N(2)=N(x)N(y)$. The factorisations of $4$ as a product of two non-negative integers are $1\cdot 4,\;2\cdot 2,\;4\cdot 1$. The middle case $N(x)=N(y)=2$ is *impossible* — Step 1 showed no element of $R$ has norm $2$. So $N(x)=1$ or $N(y)=1$; by Step 2 that factor is a unit. Hence $2$ is irreducible.
>
> *$3$ is irreducible.* If $3=xy$ then $9=N(x)N(y)$, with factorisations $1\cdot 9,\;3\cdot 3,\;9\cdot 1$. The case $N(x)=N(y)=3$ needs an element of norm $3$, which Step 1 forbids. So one factor has norm $1$, hence is a unit. Thus $3$ is irreducible.
>
> *$1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ are irreducible.* If $1+\sqrt{-5}=xy$ then $6=N(x)N(y)$, with factorisations $1\cdot 6,\;2\cdot 3,\;3\cdot 2,\;6\cdot 1$. Both "balanced" cases — $\{N(x),N(y)\}=\{2,3\}$ — require an element of norm $2$ *or* of norm $3$, and Step 1 forbids both. So one of $N(x),N(y)$ equals $1$, i.e. one factor is a unit. Hence $1+\sqrt{-5}$ is irreducible; the identical argument (or conjugation) handles $1-\sqrt{-5}$.

**Step 4: $6=2\cdot 3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ are two genuinely different factorisations.**

Both products equal $6$, both express $6$ as a product of irreducibles, and no irreducible from the first product is an associate of one from the second. So unique factorisation fails for $6$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Both equal $6$.* Directly, $2\cdot 3=6$, and
> $$(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})=1^2-(\sqrt{-5})^2=1-(-5)=6.$$
> By Step 3 all four factors $2,3,1+\sqrt{-5},1-\sqrt{-5}$ are irreducible, so these are two factorisations of the non-unit $6$ into irreducibles.
>
> *They are not the same up to associates.* Associate elements have equal norm: if $b=ua$ with $u$ a unit, then $N(b)=N(u)N(a)=1\cdot N(a)=N(a)$ by Step 2. The norms here are
> $$N(2)=4,\qquad N(3)=9,\qquad N(1+\sqrt{-5})=N(1-\sqrt{-5})=6.$$
> The first factorisation uses factors of norm $\{4,9\}$; the second uses factors of norm $\{6,6\}$. Since $4,9\notin\{6\}$, *no* factor of $2\cdot 3$ has the same norm as *any* factor of $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$, hence none is an associate of any. The two lists of irreducibles cannot be reordered into associate pairs. Unique factorisation therefore fails.

**Step 5: $2$ is not prime; $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD.**

The element $2$ divides the product $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ but divides neither factor. So $2$ is irreducible without being prime, and $R$ is not a unique factorization domain.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By Step 4, $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})=6=2\cdot 3$, so $2\mid (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$.
>
> Suppose $2\mid 1+\sqrt{-5}$, say $1+\sqrt{-5}=2w$ with $w\in R$. Applying the norm and using multiplicativity, $N(2)\mid N(1+\sqrt{-5})$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, i.e. $4\mid 6$ — false. Hence $2\nmid 1+\sqrt{-5}$. The same computation with $N(1-\sqrt{-5})=6$ gives $2\nmid 1-\sqrt{-5}$.
>
> So $2$ divides the product but neither factor: $2$ fails the defining property of a prime. Yet $2$ is irreducible by Step 3. This exhibits an irreducible element that is not prime.
>
> Finally, $6$ has two factorisations into irreducibles that are not matched up to associates (Step 4). A unique factorization domain by definition forbids exactly this. Therefore $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is **not** a unique factorization domain. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** In $R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the units are $\pm 1$; the elements $2,3,1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ are irreducible; $6=2\cdot 3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ are inequivalent factorisations into irreducibles; $2$ is not prime; and $R$ is not a UFD.
>
> The norm $N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2=|a+b\sqrt{-5}|^2$ satisfies $N(zw)=N(z)N(w)$ (conjugation is multiplicative). The form $a^2+5b^2$ never equals $2$ or $3$: if $|b|\geq 1$ then $a^2+5b^2\geq 5$, and if $b=0$ then $a^2\in\{2,3\}$ is not a perfect square.
>
> *Units.* If $u$ is a unit then $N(u)N(u^{-1})=N(1)=1$, so $N(u)=1$; the equation $a^2+5b^2=1$ forces $b=0,a=\pm1$. Both $\pm1$ are units, so the unit group is $\{\pm1\}$.
>
> *Irreducibles.* $N(2)=4$, $N(3)=9$, $N(1\pm\sqrt{-5})=6$; all $>1$, so none is a unit or zero. If $2=xy$ then $N(x)N(y)=4$; the split $2\cdot 2$ needs norm $2$, impossible, so $N(x)=1$ or $N(y)=1$ — a unit. If $3=xy$ then $N(x)N(y)=9$; the split $3\cdot 3$ needs norm $3$, impossible. If $1\pm\sqrt{-5}=xy$ then $N(x)N(y)=6$; the splits $2\cdot 3$ need norm $2$ or $3$, impossible. Hence all four are irreducible.
>
> *Two factorisations.* $2\cdot 3=6$ and $(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})=1-(-5)=6$. Associates have equal norm (a unit factor has norm $1$). The first factorisation has factors of norm $\{4,9\}$, the second of norm $\{6,6\}$; since $\{4,9\}\cap\{6\}=\varnothing$ no factor of one is an associate of a factor of the other. The factorisations are genuinely distinct.
>
> *$2$ not prime.* $2\mid 6=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$. If $2\mid 1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ then $N(2)\mid N(1\pm\sqrt{-5})$, i.e. $4\mid 6$ — false. So $2$ divides the product but neither factor; $2$ is irreducible but not prime.
>
> Since $6$ admits inequivalent factorisations into irreducibles, $R=\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a unique factorization domain. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**A multiplicative norm is a homomorphism from the multiplicative monoid of the ring to $(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\times)$ — and it transports every divisibility question to the integers.** The decisive structural fact is $N(zw)=N(z)N(w)$. This is exactly the statement that $N$ is a monoid homomorphism, and it lets you replace an *unknown* element of an exotic ring by a *known* non-negative integer. Three classical notions descend along it. Units map to $1$ (the only invertible element of $(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\times)$). Irreducibility of $a$ becomes "$N(a)$ cannot be written as a product of two attained norms each $>1$." Associates map to *equal* norms. The trigger for this technique is any ring presented as a subring of $\mathbb{C}$ (or of a field with a conjugation/trace/determinant): rings of integers $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]$, the Gaussian integers $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, the Eisenstein integers $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$, quaternion orders. Whenever you must reason about factorisation in such a ring, the first move is "write down the norm and check it is multiplicative." For $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ this *same* norm even satisfies a Euclidean division inequality — see [[Ex - The Gaussian integers form a Euclidean domain]] — which is what makes $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ a UFD; here the norm controls factorisation but provides no division algorithm, and the ring fails to be a UFD.

**Irreducibility is certified by the gaps in the value set of the norm, not by the elements themselves.** The proof never lists a single element of $R$ of a given norm. It needs only the *negative* fact that the integers $2$ and $3$ are not values of $a^2+5b^2$. This is the general pattern for proving an element of small norm is irreducible: factor its norm $n$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, and for each balanced split $n=d\cdot e$ with $d,e>1$, show $R$ has no element of norm $d$ — then the split is impossible and one factor must be a unit. The art is entirely in *which integers fail to be norms*. For $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ the form $a^2+5b^2$ skips $2,3,7,8$; those omissions are precisely why $2,3,1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ — numbers that "look composite" — cannot be broken. The same idea proves $2$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-3}]$, or that small primes stay irreducible in any $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{d}]$ with $|d|$ large; conversely, when a prime $p$ *is* a value of the norm form, it typically *splits* (this is the seed of the theory of how rational primes factor in rings of integers).

**Unique factorisation can fail, and the diagnostic is two factorisations of one element whose factor-norms do not match.** A UFD is not "a ring where everything factors" — every non-unit of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ does factor into irreducibles — it is a ring where the factorisation is *unique up to associates*. To refute the UFD property you need one witness: an element with two irreducible factorisations that cannot be paired into associates. The cleanest separating invariant is the norm, since associates share a norm: here $\{4,9\}\neq\{6,6\}$ ends the argument in one line. The general technique — "find an invariant preserved by associates, then show two factorisations disagree on it" — recurs whenever you must prove non-uniqueness of a decomposition. The deeper repair, that ideals factor uniquely even when elements do not, is the historical origin of ideal theory: Kummer and Dedekind invented "ideal numbers" precisely to restore unique factorisation in rings like $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$.

**Irreducible and prime are different conditions, and "prime" is the strong one.** In $\mathbb{Z}$ the two notions coincide, which hides a genuine distinction. *Prime* is about products: $p\mid xy\Rightarrow p\mid x$ or $p\mid y$. *Irreducible* is about factorisations: $a=xy\Rightarrow x$ or $y$ is a unit. Prime always implies irreducible (in any integral domain), but the converse needs extra structure. The witness here is $2$: irreducible by the norm count, yet $2\mid(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ while $2\nmid 1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ because $4\nmid 6$. The conceptual punchline is that *prime = irreducible* is not automatic — it is equivalent to unique factorisation, and it is exactly what holds in a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] (see [[Ex - In a principal ideal domain irreducibles are prime]]). So this exercise is the negative companion of that lemma: $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ has irreducibles that are not prime, which is *why* it has no unique factorisation, which is *why* it cannot be a principal ideal domain. The chain ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD has $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ sitting outside its rightmost box.
