# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a commutative [[Def - Ring|ring]] with $1 \neq 0$, and all ideals are two-sided. We write $I \trianglelefteq R$ for "$I$ is an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] of $R$", $R/I$ for the [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient ring]], and $a + I$ for the coset of $a$ — the zero element of $R/I$ is $0 + I = I$, so "$a + I = 0_{R/I}$" means exactly "$a \in I$". An ideal is **proper** if $I \neq R$. For a prime number we use $p$; the ideal $p\mathbb{Z} = \{pk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is the set of multiples of $p$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, and $(X)$ denotes the principal ideal generated by $X$ in a polynomial ring. The full symbol registry is on [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4]].

# Axiom Motivation

We already know, from the [[Def - Ideal|definition of an ideal]], that an ideal $I$ has a *strong closure* property: take anything in $I$ and multiply by anything in $R$, and you stay in $I$. The two definitions on this page identify the two most important *special kinds* of ideal. The honest way to discover them is not to pull the axioms out of a hat, but to ask a single organising question: **given an ideal $I$, what does the quotient ring $R/I$ look like?** The quotient $R/I$ is a new ring built by "setting $I$ to zero", and the philosophy of the section is that *good properties of the ideal $I$ should correspond to good properties of the quotient ring $R/I$*. We have two favourite kinds of ring — [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domains]] and [[Def - Unit and Field|fields]] — and each one, demanded of $R/I$, will *define* a kind of ideal.

**Discovering the prime ideal.** Suppose we want $R/I$ to be an integral domain — the mildest civilising condition, "no zero divisors". Unwind what that says about $I$. The quotient $R/I$ has no zero divisors means: whenever $(a + I)(b + I) = 0_{R/I}$, one of $a + I$, $b + I$ is zero. Now $(a+I)(b+I) = ab + I$ by the definition of quotient multiplication, and "$ab + I = 0_{R/I}$" means "$ab \in I$"; likewise "$a + I = 0_{R/I}$" means "$a \in I$". Substituting, the condition becomes a statement purely about $I$ inside $R$:
$$ab \in I \quad \Longrightarrow \quad a \in I \ \text{ or } \ b \in I.$$
This is the **prime ideal** axiom, and we did not invent it — we *derived* it as the exact translation of "$R/I$ is an integral domain". (We also need $R/I \neq 0$, i.e. $1 \notin I$, i.e. $I \neq R$; hence prime ideals are required to be proper.) The name "prime" is well chosen: in $\mathbb{Z}$, the condition "$p \mid ab \Rightarrow p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$" is precisely Euclid's lemma, the defining property of a prime number, and $p \mid x$ is the same as $x \in p\mathbb{Z}$.

There is a second, equally illuminating way to read the axiom. An *ideal* says: in-$I$ times out-of-$I$ lands in $I$ (strong closure). A *prime ideal* says the converse-flavoured statement: if a product lands in $I$, some factor was already in $I$ — equivalently, **the complement $R \setminus I$ is closed under multiplication**. A product of two elements *outside* $I$ stays outside $I$. So a prime ideal is an ideal whose complement is a multiplicatively closed set. Hold both readings: "quotient is a domain" and "complement is multiplicatively closed".

**Discovering the maximal ideal.** Now demand more: we want $R/I$ to be not merely a domain but a full [[Def - Unit and Field|field]]. What does *that* say about $I$? Here we use a structural fact about fields — a non-zero ring is a field if and only if its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and the whole ring (a field has the simplest possible ideal structure, because every non-zero element is a unit and any ideal containing a unit is everything). So "$R/I$ is a field" says "$R/I$ has exactly two ideals". By the ideal correspondence — ideals of $R/I$ are in bijection with ideals of $R$ that contain $I$ — "$R/I$ has only two ideals" translates to "the only ideals of $R$ containing $I$ are $I$ itself and $R$". That is the **maximal ideal** axiom:
$$I \neq R, \quad \text{and no ideal } J \text{ satisfies } I \subsetneq J \subsetneq R.$$
$I$ is *maximal* in the literal poset sense — a top element among proper ideals, with nothing strictly between it and $R$. Again the axiom was forced: it is the exact translation of "$R/I$ is a field".

**Why these two and not others; what weakening/strengthening costs.** The reason these are *the* two definitions worth isolating is that integral domain and field are the two most important quotient behaviours, and each is now available as a property of the ideal. If we *weakened* the prime axiom — dropped it entirely — the quotient could have zero divisors and we lose cancellation, factorisation, the embedding into a field; the quotient is a generic ring with no leverage. If we *strengthened* prime to maximal we get a field, the best possible quotient — but at a cost: maximal is a genuinely stronger demand, so maximal ideals are rarer. The relationship is forced by the inclusion of *quotient* properties: every field is an integral domain, so **every maximal ideal is automatically prime**. The converse fails, and it must fail, because "integral domain" is strictly weaker than "field": the ideal $(0) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$ has quotient $\mathbb{Z}/(0) \cong \mathbb{Z}$, a domain that is not a field, so $(0)$ is prime but not maximal. The gap between the two definitions is exactly the gap between the two kinds of ring. Finally, why must both ideals be *proper* ($I \neq R$)? Because $R/R$ is the zero ring, which we have deliberately excluded from being a domain or a field — admitting $R$ as a prime or maximal ideal would clutter every later theorem with a useless exception, just as admitting $1$ as a prime number would.

# The Definition

Let $R$ be a commutative [[Def - Ring|ring]].

## Prime ideal

An [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $P \trianglelefteq R$ is a **prime ideal** if $P \neq R$ and, for all $a, b \in R$,
$$a \cdot b \in P \quad \Longrightarrow \quad a \in P \ \text{ or } \ b \in P.$$

Three formulations are equivalent, and each is useful:

1. **(Primary)** $P \neq R$ and $ab \in P \Rightarrow a \in P$ or $b \in P$.
2. **(Complement)** $P \neq R$ and the complement $R \setminus P$ is **multiplicatively closed**: $1 \in R \setminus P$, and $a, b \notin P \Rightarrow ab \notin P$.
3. **(Quotient)** The quotient ring $R/P$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]]; see [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]].

Formulation 2 is the contrapositive of formulation 1 together with $1 \notin P$ (which holds because $P \neq R$); formulation 3 is the central characterisation, proved by checking that "$(a+P)(b+P) = 0_{R/P}$" unwinds to "$ab \in P$".

## Maximal ideal

An [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $I \trianglelefteq R$ is a **maximal ideal** if $I \neq R$ and there is no ideal strictly between $I$ and $R$ — that is, for every ideal $J$ with
$$I \subseteq J \subseteq R, \qquad \text{either } J = I \ \text{ or } \ J = R.$$
Equivalently, $I$ is a maximal element of the partially ordered set of *proper* ideals of $R$, ordered by inclusion.

Two formulations are equivalent:

1. **(Primary)** $I \neq R$, and no ideal $J$ satisfies $I \subsetneq J \subsetneq R$.
2. **(Quotient)** The quotient ring $R/I$ is a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]]; see [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]].

The equivalence rests on two facts: a non-zero ring is a field if and only if its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and itself, and the ideal correspondence puts ideals of $R/I$ in bijection with ideals of $R$ containing $I$.

## Relationship

**Every maximal ideal is prime.** If $I$ is maximal then $R/I$ is a field, every field is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]], so $R/I$ is an integral domain, hence $I$ is prime. The converse is false in general (see the examples).

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The cleanest compression is the **ideal–quotient dictionary**: an ideal is a "constraint", the quotient is "the ring under that constraint", and the two definitions on this page are simply the constraints that force the constrained ring to be one of the two kinds of ring we like. Prime $\leftrightarrow$ quotient is an integral domain; maximal $\leftrightarrow$ quotient is a field. The whole section is the discovery that a property of a *sub*object ($I \trianglelefteq R$) and a property of the corresponding *quotient* object ($R/I$) are two faces of one coin — the same translation principle that, in group theory, matches a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] $N \trianglelefteq G$ to the quotient group $G/N$ and reads off, for instance, "$N$ maximal among proper normal subgroups $\iff$ $G/N$ is simple".

For prime ideals there is a sharp number-theoretic compression: **a prime ideal is the abstraction of a prime number, with divisibility rewritten as containment**. In $\mathbb{Z}$, "$d$ divides $x$" is literally "$x \in d\mathbb{Z}$", so the ideal $p\mathbb{Z}$ *is* the relation "divisible by $p$", and the prime-ideal axiom "$ab \in p\mathbb{Z} \Rightarrow a \in p\mathbb{Z}$ or $b \in p\mathbb{Z}$" *is* Euclid's lemma "$p \mid ab \Rightarrow p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$". The genius of Dedekind's reformulation is that even in rings where elements fail to factor uniquely, *ideals* still do — prime ideals are the objects that restore unique factorisation, now of ideals rather than elements.

Geometrically — the algebraic-geometry reading — the set of all prime ideals of $R$ is the **spectrum** $\operatorname{Spec} R$, the points of an algebraic space, and the maximal ideals are its "closed points", corresponding to actual geometric points. Containment of ideals reverses to specialisation of points. A prime ideal $P$ has $R/P$ an integral domain — the coordinate ring of an *irreducible* subvariety — while a maximal ideal $I$ has $R/I$ a field — the residue field at a point. Thus "maximal $\Rightarrow$ prime" is the geometric truism that a single point is an irreducible set, and the failure of the converse is the existence of positive-dimensional irreducible subvarieties (the prime $(0)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ corresponds to the whole "line" $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb{Z}$, which is irreducible but is not a point).

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance (both) — $p\mathbb{Z} \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$ for $p$ prime.** In the integers, the non-zero ideal $n\mathbb{Z}$ is prime if and only if $n$ is a prime number. If $n = p$ is prime and $ab \in p\mathbb{Z}$, then $p \mid ab$, so by Euclid's lemma $p \mid a$ or $p \mid b$, i.e. $a \in p\mathbb{Z}$ or $b \in p\mathbb{Z}$ — so $p\mathbb{Z}$ is prime. It is moreover *maximal*: the quotient $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}/p$ is the field of $p$ elements, and "quotient is a field" means the ideal is maximal. So in $\mathbb{Z}$ every non-zero prime ideal is also maximal — a special feature of $\mathbb{Z}$ that holds in any [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], not in general rings.

**Is NOT an instance (prime fails) — $n\mathbb{Z}$ for composite $n$.** If $n = pq$ with $p, q \neq 1$, then $n\mathbb{Z}$ is *not* a prime ideal: $n = pq \in n\mathbb{Z}$, but $p \notin n\mathbb{Z}$ and $q \notin n\mathbb{Z}$ since $0 < p, q < n$. A product lands in the ideal while neither factor does. Consistently, $\mathbb{Z}/n$ has zero divisors (it is not an integral domain), so $n\mathbb{Z}$ is not prime by the quotient criterion either. This probes the prime axiom directly: $6\mathbb{Z}$ fails because $2 \cdot 3 = 6 \in 6\mathbb{Z}$ with $2, 3 \notin 6\mathbb{Z}$.

**Is an instance (prime) but NOT (maximal) — the zero ideal $(0) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$.** The ideal $\{0\}$ is prime: $ab \in \{0\}$ means $ab = 0$, and since $\mathbb{Z}$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] this forces $a = 0$ or $b = 0$. (In general, $(0)$ is a prime ideal of $R$ exactly when $R$ is an integral domain.) But $(0)$ is *not* maximal in $\mathbb{Z}$: it sits strictly inside the proper ideal $2\mathbb{Z}$, since $(0) \subsetneq 2\mathbb{Z} \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}$. By the quotient test, $\mathbb{Z}/(0) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain but not a field — exactly the gap "domain but not field". This is the cleanest witness that **prime does not imply maximal**.

**Is an instance (prime) but NOT (maximal) — $(X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$.** A less trivial separating example. In the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, the principal ideal $(X)$ of polynomials with zero constant term is prime but not maximal. It is prime because the quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ — evaluation at $X = 0$ — is an integral domain. It is not maximal because that quotient $\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field; concretely $(X)$ is strictly contained in the proper ideal $(2, X)$, since $(X) \subsetneq (2, X) \subsetneq \mathbb{Z}[X]$. (By contrast $(2, X)$ *is* maximal in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$: the quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ is a field.) This shows the prime-not-maximal phenomenon is not a degenerate accident of the zero ideal — it pervades rings of dimension greater than one.

**Corollary — an ideal can be tested by passing to the quotient.** Both definitions reduce to a property of $R/I$: to check primality, check whether $R/I$ has zero divisors; to check maximality, check whether $R/I$ is a field. This is often dramatically easier than working with the ideal directly — recognising $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ instantly settles that $(X)$ is prime and not maximal. The quotient criteria are the workhorses; see [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]].

**Corollary — in a field, $(0)$ is the unique maximal ideal and the unique prime ideal.** A [[Def - Unit and Field|field]] $F$ has only the two ideals $(0)$ and $F$. The ideal $(0)$ is maximal — nothing proper lies strictly above it — and hence prime; and $F$ itself is excluded for being improper. So a field has exactly one prime ideal and exactly one maximal ideal, namely $(0)$. This is the calibration check that a field has the simplest possible ideal structure.

**Calibration check.** Verify that $(0)$ is a prime ideal of $R$ if and only if $R$ is an integral domain, and a maximal ideal of $R$ if and only if $R$ is a field. Confirm that $6\mathbb{Z}$ is neither prime nor maximal in $\mathbb{Z}$, that $5\mathbb{Z}$ is both, and that $(X)$ is prime but not maximal in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ while $(2, X)$ is maximal. If you can also explain — using "every field is an integral domain" — *why* maximal must imply prime, and produce the standard counterexample to the converse from memory, you have understood both definitions and the relationship between them.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The Spectrum of a Ring *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The set $\operatorname{Spec} R$ of all prime ideals of $R$, topologised by the Zariski topology, is the fundamental geometric object attached to a ring. Maximal ideals are its closed points; prime ideals are its irreducible closed subsets. Commutative algebra becomes geometry.

> [!tip] Existence of Maximal Ideals *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> Using Zorn's lemma, every proper ideal of a non-zero ring is contained in some maximal ideal. In particular every non-zero ring has at least one maximal ideal, hence at least one prime ideal, hence a field quotient.

> [!tip] Unique Factorisation of Ideals *(from Number Theory)*
> In a ring of algebraic integers, elements need not factor uniquely, but *ideals* factor uniquely into prime ideals (Dedekind). Prime ideals are the objects that rescue unique factorisation when prime elements fail.
