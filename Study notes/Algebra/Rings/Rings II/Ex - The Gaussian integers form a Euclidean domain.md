# Problem Statement

The **Gaussian integers** are the ring
$$\mathbb{Z}[i]=\{\,a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\,\}\;\leq\;\mathbb{C},$$
the subring of $\mathbb{C}$ generated by $i=\sqrt{-1}$. Equip it with the function
$$\varphi:\mathbb{Z}[i]\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\qquad \varphi(z)=N(z)=|z|^2=a^2+b^2\quad(z=a+bi).$$

1. Show that $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ together with $\varphi$ is a **Euclidean domain**: verify the multiplicativity bound $\varphi(ab)\geq\varphi(b)$, and prove the **division-with-remainder** property — for $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$ with $b\neq 0$ there exist $q,r\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$ with
$$a=bq+r,\qquad r=0\ \text{ or }\ \varphi(r)<\varphi(b).$$
The construction: form the complex number $a/b\in\mathbb{C}$ and **round each coordinate to the nearest integer** to get $q\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$.
2. Conclude that $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a **principal ideal domain** and a **unique factorization domain**.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the integral domain $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, its norm, the definition of a Euclidean domain, and the two implications ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD.

![[Def - Integral Domain#The Definition]]

$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]]: it is a subring of the field $\mathbb{C}$, so it has no zero divisors.

![[Def - Euclidean Domain#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] is an integral domain $R$ with a function $\varphi:R\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ (a *Euclidean function*) such that (i) $\varphi(ab)\geq\varphi(b)$ for all non-zero $a,b$, and (ii) for any $a,b\in R$ with $b\neq 0$ there are $q,r\in R$ with $a=bq+r$ and either $r=0$ or $\varphi(r)<\varphi(b)$. The point of (ii) is that one can run the Euclidean algorithm.

The norm here is $N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2=|a+bi|^2$, and it is **multiplicative**: $N(zw)=N(z)N(w)$, because $N(z)=z\bar z$ and conjugation is a ring homomorphism. We will use $\varphi=N$ throughout.

![[Def - Principal Ideal Domain#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] (PID) is an integral domain in which every ideal is principal — generated by a single element.

![[Def - Unique Factorization Domain#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] (UFD) is an integral domain in which every non-unit factors into irreducibles uniquely up to order and associates.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *verify-a-structure* problem: confirm that a concrete ring with a concrete candidate function satisfies the Euclidean-domain axioms, then chain two general theorems to extract the consequences. As the [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4]] strategy records, "show $R$ is a ED/PID/UFD" problems are won by checking the *defining axioms* of the strongest applicable class and then *quoting the implications downward*.

**Assumption pattern.** The ring sits inside $\mathbb{C}$ as a *lattice* — the integer points of the plane — and the candidate $\varphi$ is the squared distance to the origin. The recognisable feature is geometric: $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a unit grid, so every point of $\mathbb{C}$ lies within a controlled distance of a lattice point. That "covering radius" being strictly less than $1$ is exactly what a Euclidean function needs.

**Theorem routing.** Axiom (i), $\varphi(ab)\geq\varphi(b)$, is immediate from multiplicativity: $\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)\geq\varphi(b)$ since $\varphi(a)\geq 1$ for $a\neq 0$. Axiom (ii) is the geometric heart: divide in the *field* $\mathbb{C}$ to get $a/b$, round to the nearest Gaussian integer $q$, and the rounding error $c=a/b-q$ has each coordinate in $[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]$, so $|c|^2\leq\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12<1$; setting $r=a-bq$ gives $\varphi(r)=\varphi(b)|c|^2<\varphi(b)$. Once $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a Euclidean domain, [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] makes it a PID and [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]] makes it a UFD.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious move is *to leave the ring*. There is no division algorithm internal to $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ to appeal to; the quotient $q$ is *manufactured* by performing genuine division in the larger field $\mathbb{C}$ and then snapping the answer back onto the lattice. The single inequality that makes the method work is that the worst-case rounding error has norm $\tfrac12<1$ — and the reason this is strict is that $\sqrt{-1}$ produces a *square* lattice with covering radius $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}<1$. The contrast with $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, where the analogous lattice is too sparse and the covering radius exceeds $1$, is exactly why that ring fails to be Euclidean (see [[Ex - Failure of unique factorization]]).

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Reduce a Euclidean-axiom check to multiplicativity of the norm.** Axiom (i) $\varphi(ab)\geq\varphi(b)$ follows from $\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)$ and $\varphi(a)\geq 1$.

2. **Divide in the ambient field, then round back into the ring.** Compute $a/b\in\mathbb{C}$ exactly, then take $q\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$ by rounding each of the real and imaginary parts to a nearest integer.

3. **Bound the rounding error by the geometry of the lattice.** Each coordinate of $a/b-q$ lies in $[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]$, so the error norm is at most $\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12<1$.

4. **Transfer a strict norm bound from the field to the ring.** From $\varphi(r)=\varphi(b)\cdot|a/b-q|^2$ and the error bound $<1$, deduce $\varphi(r)<\varphi(b)$.

5. **Quote a chain of structural implications.** Apply [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] then [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]] to upgrade "Euclidean" to "PID" to "UFD".

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Axiom (i) is short. The norm is multiplicative: $\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)$. Since $a\neq 0$, the integer $\varphi(a)=|a|^2$ is at least $1$. What does multiplying a non-negative integer by something $\geq 1$ do to it?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> For axiom (ii) you must produce a quotient $q\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$ and remainder $r$. There is no division *inside* $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — but $\mathbb{C}$ is a field, so $a/b$ makes sense as a complex number. Compute it. The catch: $a/b$ is generally not a Gaussian integer. How do you get the nearest Gaussian integer to a given complex number?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Write $a/b=x+yi$ with $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$. Round: let $q=m+ni$ where $m$ is an integer nearest to $x$ and $n$ an integer nearest to $y$. Then $|x-m|\leq\tfrac12$ and $|y-n|\leq\tfrac12$. The error is $c=a/b-q$; compute the maximum possible value of $|c|^2=(x-m)^2+(y-n)^2$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Set $r=a-bq$. Then $r\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (it is a difference of elements of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$), and $r=a-bq=b(a/b-q)=bc$. Apply the norm: $\varphi(r)=\varphi(b)\varphi(c)=\varphi(b)|c|^2$. You showed $|c|^2\leq\tfrac12<1$. Conclude. Then $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is Euclidean — now just cite the ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD theorems.

# Solution

The norm $\varphi(z)=|z|^2$ is multiplicative, which disposes of the size axiom in one line. The division axiom is geometric: $a/b$ is a genuine complex number, and because the Gaussian integers form a *unit square lattice*, the nearest lattice point $q$ misses $a/b$ by squared distance at most $\tfrac12<1$ — and that strict gap is the whole theorem.

**Step 1: Axiom (i) — $\varphi(ab)\geq\varphi(b)$.**

For non-zero $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$, $\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)\geq\varphi(b)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The norm $N(z)=z\bar z=|z|^2$ is multiplicative: $N(zw)=(zw)\overline{(zw)}=(z\bar z)(w\bar w)=N(z)N(w)$, since complex conjugation is a ring homomorphism fixing $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ ($\overline{a+bi}=a-bi\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$). With $\varphi=N$,
> $$\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\,\varphi(b).$$
> For a *non-zero* Gaussian integer $a=a_1+a_2i$, the value $\varphi(a)=a_1^2+a_2^2$ is a strictly positive integer, hence $\varphi(a)\geq 1$. Multiplying the non-negative integer $\varphi(b)$ by something $\geq 1$ cannot decrease it:
> $$\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\,\varphi(b)\geq 1\cdot\varphi(b)=\varphi(b).$$
> This verifies axiom (i). (It also records the useful fact that the only norm-$1$ elements — the units — are $\pm1,\pm i$.)

**Step 2: Axiom (ii), the construction — divide in $\mathbb{C}$ and round to the nearest Gaussian integer.**

Given $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$ with $b\neq 0$, form $a/b\in\mathbb{C}$, round each coordinate to a nearest integer to obtain $q\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$, and set $r=a-bq\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$. Then $a=bq+r$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Since $\mathbb{C}$ is a field and $b\neq 0$, the quotient $a/b$ is a well-defined complex number; write
> $$\frac{a}{b}=x+yi,\qquad x,y\in\mathbb{R}.$$
> (Concretely $a/b=a\bar b/(b\bar b)=a\bar b/\varphi(b)$, so $x$ and $y$ are rational.) Choose integers $m,n$ with $|x-m|\leq\tfrac12$ and $|y-n|\leq\tfrac12$ — every real number lies within $\tfrac12$ of some integer — and put
> $$q=m+ni\in\mathbb{Z}[i].$$
> Define $r=a-bq$. Both $a$ and $bq$ lie in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ (the ring is closed under multiplication and subtraction), so $r\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$, and trivially $a=bq+r$. It remains only to control the size of $r$ — Step 3.

**Step 3: Axiom (ii), the bound — the remainder is strictly smaller.**

The rounding error $c=a/b-q$ satisfies $\varphi(c)=|c|^2\leq\tfrac12<1$, and therefore $\varphi(r)=\varphi(b)\,\varphi(c)<\varphi(b)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> With $a/b=x+yi$ and $q=m+ni$ as in Step 2, the error is
> $$c=\frac{a}{b}-q=(x-m)+(y-n)i.$$
> Each coordinate satisfies $|x-m|\leq\tfrac12$ and $|y-n|\leq\tfrac12$, so
> $$\varphi(c)=|c|^2=(x-m)^2+(y-n)^2\;\leq\;\Big(\tfrac12\Big)^2+\Big(\tfrac12\Big)^2=\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12.$$
> The key point is that this bound is **strictly less than $1$**: $\tfrac12<1$. Now relate $r$ to $c$. From $r=a-bq$ and $c=a/b-q$,
> $$r=a-bq=b\Big(\frac{a}{b}-q\Big)=bc.$$
> Apply the norm — multiplicative even though $c$ is only a complex number, since $N(zw)=N(z)N(w)$ holds for all $z,w\in\mathbb{C}$:
> $$\varphi(r)=N(bc)=N(b)\,N(c)=\varphi(b)\,\varphi(c)\;\leq\;\tfrac12\,\varphi(b)\;<\;\varphi(b).$$
> So either $r=0$, or $r\neq 0$ and $\varphi(r)<\varphi(b)$. Together with $a=bq+r$ this is exactly axiom (ii). Steps 1–3 show $(\mathbb{Z}[i],\varphi)$ is a Euclidean domain.

**Step 4: $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a principal ideal domain and a unique factorization domain.**

By the structural implications ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD, the Gaussian integers form a PID and a UFD.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Steps 1–3 established that $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, with $\varphi(z)=|z|^2$, satisfies both axioms of a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]].
>
> [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] states that every Euclidean domain is a principal ideal domain: in a non-zero ideal $I$, pick $b\in I\setminus\{0\}$ with $\varphi(b)$ minimal; division-with-remainder writes any $a\in I$ as $a=bq+r$ with $r=a-bq\in I$, and minimality of $\varphi(b)$ forbids $\varphi(r)<\varphi(b)$, forcing $r=0$, so $a\in(b)$ and $I=(b)$. Hence $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a **principal ideal domain**.
>
> [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]] states that every PID is a UFD: the ascending chain condition (a PID is Noetherian) guarantees existence of factorisations into irreducibles, and the lemma "in a PID every irreducible is prime" (see [[Ex - In a principal ideal domain irreducibles are prime]]) guarantees uniqueness. Hence $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a **unique factorization domain**. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $(\mathbb{Z}[i],\varphi)$ with $\varphi(z)=|z|^2=N(z)$ is a Euclidean domain; consequently $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a PID and a UFD.
>
> $\mathbb{Z}[i]\leq\mathbb{C}$ is an integral domain. The norm $N(z)=z\bar z$ is multiplicative, $N(zw)=N(z)N(w)$, for all $z,w\in\mathbb{C}$.
>
> *Axiom (i).* For non-zero $a,b$, $\varphi(a)=|a|^2$ is a positive integer, so $\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)\geq\varphi(b)$.
>
> *Axiom (ii).* Let $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$, $b\neq 0$. In $\mathbb{C}$, write $a/b=x+yi$ with $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$. Pick integers $m,n$ with $|x-m|\leq\tfrac12$, $|y-n|\leq\tfrac12$, and set $q=m+ni\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$, $r=a-bq\in\mathbb{Z}[i]$. Then $a=bq+r$, and with $c=a/b-q=(x-m)+(y-n)i$ we have $r=bc$ and
> $$\varphi(c)=(x-m)^2+(y-n)^2\leq\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12<1,$$
> so $\varphi(r)=\varphi(b)\varphi(c)\leq\tfrac12\varphi(b)<\varphi(b)$. Thus $r=0$ or $\varphi(r)<\varphi(b)$.
>
> Hence $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a Euclidean domain. By [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] it is a PID, and by [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]] it is a UFD. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Verifying a Euclidean domain is a two-axiom checklist, and a multiplicative norm settles the easy axiom instantly.** Axiom (i), $\varphi(ab)\geq\varphi(b)$, is a "size does not shrink under multiplication" condition; whenever the candidate $\varphi$ is *multiplicative* and takes values $\geq 1$ on non-zero elements, axiom (i) is automatic — $\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)\geq\varphi(b)$. So the entire content of "$R$ is Euclidean" concentrates in axiom (ii), the division-with-remainder property. The trigger to reach for a norm as the Euclidean function: the ring is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$ (or of a number field), where $z\mapsto|z|^2$ — or more generally the field norm — is built in and multiplicative. This is the same norm that controls factorisation in [[Ex - Failure of unique factorization]]; the difference is whether it *also* supports division.

**To divide inside a ring with no division algorithm, divide in a larger field and project the answer back.** This is the central reusable trick. $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ has no internal division operation, but it is embedded in the field $\mathbb{C}$ where $a/b$ is a genuine, exact object. The quotient $q$ is then *defined* to be the nearest ring element to the field-quotient, and the remainder $r=a-bq$ is whatever is left. The method works for any ring realised as a *lattice* in a normed space, provided the rounding is good enough. The same "compute exactly in the completion, then round" pattern appears far beyond ring theory — it is the structure of nearest-lattice-point algorithms, of continued-fraction expansions, and of the proof that $F[X]$ is Euclidean (there the "field" is the field of rational functions and "rounding" is discarding the negative-degree part). Recognise the pattern: an exact operation in a big structure, followed by a controlled projection into the small one.

**The Euclidean property of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is geometry: the covering radius of the lattice must be strictly below $1$.** The decisive inequality is $|c|^2\leq\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12<1$, and every symbol in it is geometric. The Gaussian integers are the *unit square lattice* in the plane; any complex number is within squared-distance $\tfrac12$ of a lattice point because the farthest you can be is the centre of a unit square, at distance $\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$. That this distance is *strictly less than $1$* is what forces $\varphi(r)<\varphi(b)$. The same calculation shows $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-2}]$ is Euclidean (rectangle lattice, covering radius still $<1$) but fails for $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$: there the lattice is stretched, the covering radius exceeds $1$, the rounding error can have norm $>1$, and the method collapses — which is the geometric shadow of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ not being a UFD. "Is this ring of integers Euclidean for the norm?" is, for imaginary quadratic rings, *literally* the question "is the covering radius of this lattice less than $1$?".

**ED, PID, and UFD form a one-directional chain, and the payoff of checking the strongest is that everything weaker comes free.** The classes satisfy ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD, and neither arrow reverses. The strategic consequence: if you can verify the *Euclidean* axioms — often the most concrete and computational of the three — you get "every ideal is principal" and "factorisation is unique" as immediate corollaries via [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]] and [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]], with no further work. This is why "$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is Euclidean" is such a load-bearing fact: it is the cheapest possible entry point that unlocks the entire toolkit of unique factorisation — gcds exist, irreducibles are prime, the fundamental-theorem-of-arithmetic analogue holds. When you meet a new ring and want its arithmetic to behave, the move is always: try to prove it Euclidean first, and only fall back to checking PID or UFD directly if no Euclidean function presents itself.
