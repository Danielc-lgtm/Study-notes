# Problem Statement

Work in the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — polynomials in one indeterminate $X$ with integer coefficients. Consider two [[Def - Ideal|ideals]]:
$$(X) = \{\,Xf : f\in\mathbb{Z}[X]\,\}, \qquad (2,X) = \{\,2f + Xg : f,g\in\mathbb{Z}[X]\,\}.$$

Prove that:

1. The ideal $(X)$ is **prime** but **not maximal**.
2. The ideal $(2,X)$ is **maximal** (hence also prime).

Conclude that in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ the inclusion "maximal $\Rightarrow$ prime" is strict — there is a prime ideal, namely $(X)$, that is not maximal.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, two of its ideals, the notions of prime and maximal ideal, and the two characterisations of these notions through the quotient ring.

An [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] $I\trianglelefteq R$ of a commutative ring is an additive [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]] closed under multiplication by *every* element of $R$: $r\in R, x\in I \Rightarrow rx\in I$. For elements $a_1,\dots,a_k\in R$, the ideal they **generate** is $(a_1,\dots,a_k)=\{a_1r_1+\cdots+a_kr_k : r_i\in R\}$, the smallest ideal containing all of them.

A [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|prime ideal]] $P\trianglelefteq R$ is an ideal with $P\neq R$ such that whenever a product $ab$ lies in $P$, at least one factor does: $ab\in P\Rightarrow a\in P$ or $b\in P$. A [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|maximal ideal]] $M\trianglelefteq R$ is an ideal with $M\neq R$ that is *maximal* among proper ideals: the only ideals $J$ with $M\subseteq J\subseteq R$ are $J=M$ and $J=R$.

An [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] is a non-zero commutative ring with no zero divisors: if $ab=0$ then $a=0$ or $b=0$. A [[Def - Unit and Field|field]] is a non-zero commutative ring in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse. Every field is an integral domain, but not conversely — $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain that is not a field.

The bridge between ideals and these properties is a pair of theorems characterising primality and maximality through the [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient ring]] $R/I$:

> **Maximal and prime ideals via quotients.** For an ideal $I\trianglelefteq R$ of a commutative ring:
> - $I$ is **prime** if and only if $R/I$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]];
> - $I$ is **maximal** if and only if $R/I$ is a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]].

See [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]]. Because every field is an integral domain, the second statement implies the first specialised: every maximal ideal is prime.

Finally, the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]]: a ring homomorphism $\varphi:R\to S$ factors as $R/\ker\varphi\cong\operatorname{im}\varphi$. We will use it to *identify* the two quotient [[Def - Ring|rings]] $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)$ and $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)$ with familiar rings.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *classify an ideal* problem: given a concrete ideal, decide whether it is prime, maximal, both, or neither. As the [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] records, the universal move for such problems is *do not reason about the ideal directly — identify its quotient ring and read the answer off the quotient*. Primality and maximality of $I$ are not facts you check element-by-element; they are facts about the *nature of $R/I$*. The whole problem is therefore converted into "identify $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)$ and $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)$".

**Assumption pattern.** Both ideals are generated by simple polynomials, and both quotients are *evaluation quotients*: killing $X$ means "set $X=0$", and additionally killing $2$ means "set $X=0$ and reduce coefficients mod $2$". The recognisable pattern is: *a polynomial-ring ideal generated by indeterminates and integers, whose quotient is computed by substitution*. Whenever an ideal of $R[X]$ contains $X$ itself, quotienting collapses every polynomial to its constant term — the quotient is (a quotient of) the coefficient ring.

**Theorem routing.** The route runs in two stages. First, the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] converts each quotient into a familiar ring: build a surjection $\mathbb{Z}[X]\to T$ with kernel exactly the ideal, and conclude $\mathbb{Z}[X]/I\cong T$. For $(X)$ the surjection is evaluation at $0$, $\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}$, giving $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$. For $(2,X)$ it is evaluation at $0$ followed by reduction mod $2$, $\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{F}_2$, giving $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$. Second, [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients|the quotient characterisation]] reads off the verdict: $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain but not a field, so $(X)$ is prime but not maximal; $\mathbb{F}_2$ is a field, so $(2,X)$ is maximal.

**Key decision point.** The interesting subtlety is the *gap between integral domain and field*. It is tempting to think "prime and maximal are nearly the same". They are not, and $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ exhibits the difference cleanly: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the universal example of an integral domain that fails to be a field — every non-zero non-unit integer ($2$, $3$, $\dots$) has no inverse. So the quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ lands *exactly* in the gap, certifying $(X)$ as prime-but-not-maximal. The other genuine decision is *which surjection to build* for $(2,X)$: one must check the kernel is the full ideal $(2,X)$ and not something larger or smaller, which is where the structure "constant term reduced mod $2$" must be pinned down precisely.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Identify a quotient ring by the first isomorphism theorem** (operation: *build a surjection out of $R$ with the right kernel*). To compute $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)$, construct the surjective evaluation homomorphism $\varepsilon_0:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}$, $f\mapsto f(0)$, verify $\ker\varepsilon_0=(X)$, and conclude $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$. Likewise for $(2,X)$ with target $\mathbb{F}_2$.

2. **Compute the kernel of an evaluation map as the ideal of polynomials vanishing there** (operation: *a polynomial lies in the kernel of $\varepsilon_a$ if and only if it vanishes at $a$*). The kernel of $f\mapsto f(0)$ is the polynomials with zero constant term, which is exactly $(X)$ — every such polynomial is $X$ times something.

3. **Characterise primality via the quotient being an integral domain** (the prime $\Leftrightarrow$ domain operation; see [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients]]). Since $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain, $(X)$ is prime.

4. **Characterise maximality via the quotient being a field** (the maximal $\Leftrightarrow$ field operation). Since $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$ is a field, $(2,X)$ is maximal; and since $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ is *not* a field, $(X)$ is not maximal.

5. **Compose ring [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphisms]]** (operation: *the composite of two ring homomorphisms is a ring homomorphism*). The surjection $\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{F}_2$ is the composite of evaluation at $0$, $\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}$, with reduction mod $2$, $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{F}_2$.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Do not try to verify primality or maximality directly from the definitions — that is the hard way. Both notions have a quotient-ring characterisation: an ideal $I$ is prime exactly when $R/I$ is an integral domain, and maximal exactly when $R/I$ is a field. So the problem is really: *what are the rings $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)$ and $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)$?*

> [!note]- Hint 2
> To identify $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)$, find a surjective ring homomorphism out of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ whose kernel is exactly $(X)$, then apply the first isomorphism theorem. Quotienting by $(X)$ should feel like "setting $X=0$". Which evaluation map does that? What is its image, and what is its kernel?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Evaluation at $0$, $\varepsilon_0:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}$ with $f\mapsto f(0)$, is surjective (constants hit everything) and has kernel $\{f : f(0)=0\}=(X)$. So $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$. Now: is $\mathbb{Z}$ an integral domain? Is $\mathbb{Z}$ a field? The answers settle whether $(X)$ is prime and whether it is maximal.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For $(2,X)$, compose evaluation at $0$ with reduction mod $2$: the map $\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{F}_2$, $f\mapsto f(0)\bmod 2$. It is surjective onto the two-element field $\mathbb{F}_2=\{0,1\}$. Its kernel is the polynomials whose constant term is even — check this is exactly $(2,X)$. Then $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$, a field, so $(2,X)$ is maximal.

# Solution

The whole solution is the slogan "to classify an ideal, identify its quotient ring." We compute $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$ using the first isomorphism theorem, then read off primality and maximality from the quotient characterisation. The integral-domain-but-not-field ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is what makes $(X)$ land strictly between prime and maximal.

**Step 1: $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ via evaluation at $0$.**

The evaluation homomorphism $\varepsilon_0:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}$, $f\mapsto f(0)$, is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel exactly $(X)$. By the first isomorphism theorem, $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Define $\varepsilon_0:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}$ by $\varepsilon_0(f)=f(0)$ — concretely, $\varepsilon_0\big(\sum_j a_jX^j\big)=a_0$, the constant term. This is a [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]]: it is the evaluation map guaranteed by the universal property of the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] (a homomorphism out of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is determined by where it sends $X$ — here $X\mapsto 0$ — together with the inclusion of $\mathbb{Z}$), and one checks directly that constant terms add and multiply correctly: the constant term of $f+g$ is $a_0+b_0$, and the constant term of $fg$ is $a_0b_0$.
>
> **Surjective.** Every integer $n$ is the value $\varepsilon_0(n)$ of the constant polynomial $n$. So $\operatorname{im}\varepsilon_0=\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> **Kernel is $(X)$.** A polynomial $f=\sum_j a_jX^j$ lies in $\ker\varepsilon_0$ if and only if $a_0=0$, i.e. if and only if $f$ has zero constant term. Any such polynomial factors as $f=X\cdot(a_1+a_2X+\cdots)=X g$ for $g\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$, so $f\in(X)$. Conversely every element $Xg$ of $(X)$ has constant term $0$, so lies in $\ker\varepsilon_0$. Hence
> $$\ker\varepsilon_0=(X).$$
>
> By the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]], $\mathbb{Z}[X]/\ker\varepsilon_0\cong\operatorname{im}\varepsilon_0$, that is
> $$\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}.$$

**Step 2: $(X)$ is prime — because $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain.**

The quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain, so by the quotient characterisation of primality, $(X)$ is a prime ideal of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]]: it is non-zero and commutative, and the product of two non-zero integers is non-zero. The theorem [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients|maximal and prime ideals via quotients]] states that an ideal $I\trianglelefteq R$ is **prime** if and only if $R/I$ is an integral domain. Applying this with $R=\mathbb{Z}[X]$, $I=(X)$, and $R/I\cong\mathbb{Z}$:
> $$\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\text{ is an integral domain}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (X)\text{ is prime.}$$
> (One can also see this directly: $(X)$ is the set of polynomials with zero constant term, and if a product $fg$ has zero constant term then $a_0b_0=0$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, forcing $a_0=0$ or $b_0=0$; but routing through the quotient is the systematic argument.)

**Step 3: $(X)$ is not maximal — because $\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field.**

The quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ is *not* a field, so by the quotient characterisation of maximality, $(X)$ is *not* a maximal ideal.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is **not** a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]]: the element $2\in\mathbb{Z}$ is non-zero but has no multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{Z}$ (there is no integer $b$ with $2b=1$). The theorem [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients|maximal and prime ideals via quotients]] states that $I$ is **maximal** if and only if $R/I$ is a field. Since $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field,
> $$(X)\text{ is not maximal.}$$
>
> Concretely, what witnesses non-maximality is an ideal strictly between $(X)$ and $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. The ideal $(2,X)$ is exactly such a witness: $(X)\subsetneq(2,X)$ because $2\in(2,X)$ but $2\notin(X)$ (the constant polynomial $2$ has non-zero constant term), and $(2,X)\subsetneq\mathbb{Z}[X]$ because $(2,X)$ is proper (it does not contain $1$ — see Step 4). So $(X)\subsetneq(2,X)\subsetneq\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a proper ideal sitting strictly above $(X)$, directly violating the definition of maximality.

**Step 4: $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$ via evaluation at $0$ then reduction mod $2$.**

The composite homomorphism $\psi:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{F}_2$, $f\mapsto f(0)\bmod 2$, is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel exactly $(2,X)$. By the first isomorphism theorem, $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $\mathbb{F}_2=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}=\{0,1\}$, the field with two elements. Define
> $$\psi:\mathbb{Z}[X]\xrightarrow{\ \varepsilon_0\ }\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{\ \pi\ }\mathbb{F}_2,\qquad \psi(f)=f(0)+2\mathbb{Z},$$
> the composite of evaluation at $0$ (Step 1) with the reduction-mod-$2$ quotient map $\pi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{F}_2$. A composite of [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphisms]] is a ring homomorphism, so $\psi$ is one. Concretely $\psi\big(\sum_j a_jX^j\big)=a_0\bmod 2$ — the constant term, reduced modulo $2$.
>
> **Surjective.** $\psi(0)=0$ and $\psi(1)=1$, so $\psi$ hits both elements of $\mathbb{F}_2$. Hence $\operatorname{im}\psi=\mathbb{F}_2$.
>
> **Kernel is $(2,X)$.** First, $(2,X)\subseteq\ker\psi$: the generators satisfy $\psi(2)=2\bmod 2=0$ and $\psi(X)=0\bmod 2=0$, and a general element $2f+Xg$ maps to $\psi(2)\psi(f)+\psi(X)\psi(g)=0$. Conversely, suppose $f=\sum_j a_jX^j\in\ker\psi$, so $a_0$ is even, say $a_0=2k$. Then
> $$f = a_0 + (a_1X+a_2X^2+\cdots) = 2k + X(a_1+a_2X+\cdots),$$
> which is visibly of the form $2\cdot(\text{const}) + X\cdot(\text{poly})$, hence $f\in(2,X)$. So $\ker\psi\subseteq(2,X)$, and therefore
> $$\ker\psi=(2,X).$$
>
> By the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]],
> $$\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{F}_2.$$

**Step 5: $(2,X)$ is maximal — because $\mathbb{F}_2$ is a field.**

The quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$ is a field, so by the quotient characterisation, $(2,X)$ is maximal; being maximal, it is also prime.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The two-element ring $\mathbb{F}_2$ is a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]]: it is non-zero and commutative, and its only non-zero element $1$ is its own inverse, $1\cdot 1=1$. By [[Thm - Maximal and Prime Ideals via Quotients|maximal and prime ideals via quotients]], $I$ is maximal if and only if $R/I$ is a field, so
> $$\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\text{ is a field}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (2,X)\text{ is maximal.}$$
> Every field is an integral domain, so every maximal ideal is prime; hence $(2,X)$ is also prime. This recovers the result of [[Ex - The ideal (2, X) is not principal|the (2,X) exercise]] from a different angle: there, $(2,X)$ was shown to be a proper ideal by exhibiting the homomorphism $\psi$; here the *same* homomorphism, pushed through the first isomorphism theorem, shows the much stronger fact that $(2,X)$ is maximal.

**Step 6: Conclusion — prime does not imply maximal.**

We have produced, inside the single ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, a prime ideal that is not maximal.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Steps 2 and 3 show $(X)$ is prime but not maximal. Steps 4 and 5 show $(2,X)$ is maximal (hence prime). So the implication "maximal $\Rightarrow$ prime" — which always holds, because every field is an integral domain — is *strict* in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$: its converse fails, witnessed by $(X)$.
>
> The structural reason is transparent through the quotients. Maximality of $I$ means $R/I$ is a field; primality means $R/I$ is only an integral domain. The gap between the two is exactly the gap between "field" and "integral domain", and $\mathbb{Z}=\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)$ sits squarely in that gap — an integral domain whose non-zero non-units ($2,3,5,\dots$) have no inverses. The chain of strict inclusions $(X)\subsetneq(2,X)\subsetneq\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is the ideal-theoretic image of the same fact: $(X)$ is prime but has a proper ideal strictly above it, so it cannot be maximal. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** In $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, the ideal $(X)$ is prime but not maximal, and $(2,X)$ is maximal.
>
> *Identifying $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)$.* The evaluation map $\varepsilon_0:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}$, $f\mapsto f(0)$, is a ring homomorphism. It is surjective, since every integer $n$ is $\varepsilon_0(n)$. Its kernel is the set of polynomials with zero constant term: such an $f$ equals $Xg$ for some $g$, so $\ker\varepsilon_0=(X)$. By the first isomorphism theorem, $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$.
>
> Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain, $(X)$ is prime; since $\mathbb{Z}$ is not a field (e.g. $2$ has no inverse), $(X)$ is not maximal. Explicitly $(X)\subsetneq(2,X)\subsetneq\mathbb{Z}[X]$ exhibits a proper ideal strictly above $(X)$.
>
> *Identifying $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)$.* The map $\psi:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{F}_2$, $f\mapsto f(0)\bmod 2$, is the composite of $\varepsilon_0$ with reduction mod $2$, hence a ring homomorphism. It is surjective onto $\mathbb{F}_2=\{0,1\}$. Its kernel consists of the $f$ with even constant term; writing $f=a_0 + X(a_1+a_2X+\cdots)$ with $a_0=2k$ shows $f\in(2,X)$, and conversely every element of $(2,X)$ has even constant term, so $\ker\psi=(2,X)$. By the first isomorphism theorem, $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$.
>
> Since $\mathbb{F}_2$ is a field, $(2,X)$ is maximal, and therefore also prime.
>
> *Conclusion.* $(X)$ is prime but not maximal, while $(2,X)$ is maximal. Hence in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ the implication maximal $\Rightarrow$ prime is strict. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**To classify an ideal as prime or maximal, identify its quotient ring — never argue from the definition directly.** The single organising principle here is the dictionary $I$ *prime* $\Leftrightarrow$ $R/I$ *integral domain* and $I$ *maximal* $\Leftrightarrow$ $R/I$ *field*. This converts a question about an ideal — a subset, awkward to reason about — into a question about a *ring*, where one has the full vocabulary of ring theory. The procedure is mechanical and reusable: (i) build a surjective homomorphism $\varphi:R\to T$ onto a ring you recognise, (ii) check $\ker\varphi=I$, (iii) invoke the first isomorphism theorem to get $R/I\cong T$, (iv) ask whether $T$ is a field, an integral domain, or neither. The trigger is *any* concrete ideal whose primality or maximality is in question; the reaction is "find the quotient". This is exactly how one shows $(p)\trianglelefteq\mathbb{Z}$ is maximal ($\mathbb{Z}/(p)$ is the field $\mathbb{F}_p$), how one shows $(X^2+1)\trianglelefteq\mathbb{R}[X]$ is maximal ($\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)\cong\mathbb{C}$), and how one shows $(X)\trianglelefteq k[X,Y]$ is prime but not maximal ($k[X,Y]/(X)\cong k[Y]$).

**Quotienting by an ideal that contains an indeterminate is *substitution*: the quotient is a quotient of the coefficient ring.** When an ideal $I\trianglelefteq R[X]$ contains $X$ itself, passing to $R[X]/I$ kills $X$ — every polynomial collapses to its constant term — and the quotient is computed by "setting $X=0$". If $I=(X)$ the quotient is the coefficient ring $R$ exactly; if $I$ also contains further relations among constants, the quotient is $R$ modulo those relations. This is why $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{Z}/(2)=\mathbb{F}_2$: the first sets $X=0$, the second sets $X=0$ *and* $2=0$. The general slogan, which iterates, is $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]/(X_1,\dots,X_n)\cong R$, and more generally adding generators to the ideal corresponds to adding relations to the quotient. Recognising "this ideal contains an indeterminate" instantly tells you the quotient is a substitution quotient and saves all the work of manipulating [[Def - Coset|cosets]].

**Prime and maximal are genuinely different, and the gap is precisely the gap between "integral domain" and "field".** It is a common and costly error to treat prime and maximal as interchangeable. They coincide in some rings — in a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] every non-zero prime is maximal, and in any *finite* commutative ring every prime is maximal, because a [[Thm - Finite Integral Domains are Fields|finite integral domain is a field]] — but not in general. The honest picture is the implication chain "maximal $\Rightarrow$ prime", coming from "field $\Rightarrow$ integral domain", with a *strict* converse failure whenever the ambient ring has a quotient that is a domain but not a field. The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is the prototype of "integral domain, not a field", so any quotient isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ — here $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(X)$ — automatically certifies a prime-but-not-maximal ideal. When you meet a new prime ideal, ask: is its quotient a field? If the quotient is something like $\mathbb{Z}$ or $k[Y]$ (a domain with non-invertible elements), the ideal is prime but *not* maximal, and there is a proper ideal strictly above it.

**A single homomorphism can certify "proper", and pushed through the first isomorphism theorem certifies "maximal" — the stronger statement comes almost free.** The reduction map $\psi:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{F}_2$ already appeared in [[Ex - The ideal (2, X) is not principal|the non-principality exercise]], where it was used only to show $(2,X)\neq\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — that $(2,X)$ is *proper*. The lesson here is that the same homomorphism, once you also identify its *image* and apply the first isomorphism theorem, yields far more: $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$, and the nature of $\mathbb{F}_2$ (a field) upgrades "proper" all the way to "maximal". This is a general efficiency principle: when you have built a surjection $R\to T$ to prove an ideal proper, do not discard it — read off $R/I\cong T$ and let the structure of $T$ tell you whether $I$ is merely proper, prime, or maximal. The richer the target ring $T$ you can land on, the more the first isomorphism theorem hands you for the same construction.
