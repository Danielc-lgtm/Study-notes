# Problem Statement

Let $R=\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots]$ be the polynomial ring over $\mathbb{Z}$ in **countably many** indeterminates $X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots$. Show that $R$ is **not Noetherian**.

Concretely, exhibit the failure in both of the two equivalent ways: produce an ideal of $R$ that is not finitely generated, and produce an infinite strictly ascending chain of [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] that never stabilises. The natural candidate for both is the ideal generated by all the variables,
$$\mathfrak{m} = (X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots),$$
together with the chain
$$(X_1)\subsetneq(X_1,X_2)\subsetneq(X_1,X_2,X_3)\subsetneq\cdots.$$

**Recall:**

The objects in play are an infinite-variable polynomial ring, an ideal of it that requires infinitely many generators, and an ascending chain of ideals.

A [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,\dots]$ in infinitely many variables is built so that *each individual element is still a finite expression*: a polynomial is a finite $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination of monomials, and each monomial involves only finitely many of the variables to non-negative integer powers. There is no element of $R$ that "uses all the variables at once". Formally one may take $R$ to be the union (direct limit) $\bigcup_{n\ge 1}\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ of the finite-variable polynomial [[Def - Ring|rings]], each included in the next; every element therefore lives in $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ for some finite $n$.

![[Def - Ideal#The Definition]]

The ideal **generated by a set** $S\subseteq R$, written $(S)$, is the smallest ideal containing $S$; explicitly it is the set of all *finite* sums $\sum_i r_i s_i$ with $r_i\in R$ and $s_i\in S$. So $\mathfrak{m}=(X_1,X_2,\dots)$ is the set of all finite $R$-linear combinations of the variables — equivalently, the polynomials with **zero constant term**.

![[Def - Noetherian Ring#The Definition]]

The two faces of "Noetherian" are tied together by the following result, which is what licenses attacking the problem from either side.

![[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals#Statement]]

An ideal $I$ is **finitely generated** if $I=(r_1,\dots,r_n)$ for some finite list $r_1,\dots,r_n\in R$. So "not finitely generated" means: for *every* finite list of elements of $I$, the ideal they generate is strictly smaller than $I$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *disprove a structural property of a ring* problem — specifically, exhibit a ring failing the ascending chain condition. As the [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] explains, the Noetherian condition is a *finiteness* condition, and the way to break a finiteness condition is to construct an explicit infinite object that the condition forbids. Every "$R$ is not Noetherian" proof is, at heart, the production of one non-finitely-generated ideal or, equivalently, one non-stabilising chain.

**Assumption pattern.** The hypothesis is the single structural feature "$R$ has infinitely many independent indeterminates, but each element uses only finitely many." This is the precise tension we exploit: the ring is *globally* infinite-dimensional, yet *every element is finite*. A finite generating set is a finite collection of elements, so collectively it still only mentions finitely many variables — and then it cannot reach a variable it never mentions. The assumption "infinitely many variables, finitely-supported elements" routes directly to "no finite set generates the variable ideal."

**Theorem routing.** The bridge is [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]]: Noetherian $\iff$ every ideal finitely generated $\iff$ ACC holds. Contrapositively, *exhibiting one non-finitely-generated ideal* suffices to conclude "not Noetherian," and so does *exhibiting one strictly ascending chain*. We do both, since they illuminate each other: the chain is literally the sequence of finitely-generated approximations to the bad ideal $\mathfrak{m}$, and the bad ideal is their union.

**Key decision point.** The crux is the choice of test ideal: $\mathfrak{m}=(X_1,X_2,\dots)$, the ideal of all polynomials with zero constant term. The genuinely clever observation is the *support argument* — given any finite list of polynomials $f_1,\dots,f_k\in\mathfrak{m}$, all of them together involve only finitely many variables, say none beyond $X_N$; then every element of $(f_1,\dots,f_k)$ also involves no variable beyond $X_N$, so $X_{N+1}$ — which lies in $\mathfrak{m}$ — is not in $(f_1,\dots,f_k)$. The finite list can never catch up with the infinite supply of variables.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Disprove "Noetherian" by exhibiting one non-finitely-generated ideal.** Rather than checking all chains, we use [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] to reduce "not Noetherian" to the single task of producing an ideal with no finite generating set. This is the operation that converts a universally-quantified failure ("some chain does not stabilise") into a concrete construction.

2. **Pass to the support of a polynomial / a finite set of polynomials.** Each polynomial mentions only finitely many variables; the *support* of a finite collection $f_1,\dots,f_k$ is the finite set of variables appearing in any of them. This finite invariant is the obstruction: it is preserved under the ideal operations, so it bounds what $(f_1,\dots,f_k)$ can contain.

3. **Track which variables an element of an ideal can involve.** Multiplying by an arbitrary $r\in R$ and adding can *introduce* new variables coming from the multipliers — so this needs care. We use the *evaluation/specialisation* refinement below to pin the argument down rigorously.

4. **Specialise variables by an evaluation homomorphism.** To prove rigorously that $X_{N+1}\notin(f_1,\dots,f_k)$, apply the ring homomorphism that sets every variable to $0$ except $X_{N+1}$. It kills $f_1,\dots,f_k$ (they have zero constant term and no $X_{N+1}$) hence their whole ideal, but sends $X_{N+1}$ to $X_{N+1}\neq 0$ — a contradiction if $X_{N+1}$ were in the ideal.

5. **Realise a non-finitely-generated ideal as the union of its finite truncations.** The ideal $\mathfrak{m}=\bigcup_n(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ is the increasing union of the chain; this exhibits the chain and the bad ideal as two views of the same phenomenon.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> "Noetherian" is a *finiteness* condition (ascending chain condition, equivalently: every ideal is finitely generated). To *disprove* it you only need to break it *once*: by [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] a single non-finitely-generated ideal, or a single non-stabilising ascending chain, is enough. Which ideal of $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,\dots]$ "obviously" needs infinitely much data to describe?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Try the ideal $\mathfrak{m}=(X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots)$ — all polynomials with zero constant term. Suppose for contradiction it were generated by a *finite* list $f_1,\dots,f_k$. Each $f_i$ is a finite polynomial: it involves only finitely many variables. So the whole list involves only finitely many — say none with index above $N$. Now look at the variable $X_{N+1}$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Every element of $(f_1,\dots,f_k)$ is a sum $\sum_i r_i f_i$. The multipliers $r_i$ may bring in *new* variables, so "elements of the ideal involve no variable above $X_N$" is **false** in general. Fix the argument with a homomorphism: let $\varphi:R\to\mathbb{Z}[X_{N+1}]$ send $X_{N+1}\mapsto X_{N+1}$ and every other variable to $0$. Apply $\varphi$ to the supposed equation $X_{N+1}=\sum_i r_i f_i$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Continuing Hint 3: $\varphi(f_i)=0$ for each $i$, because $f_i$ has zero constant term and involves no $X_{N+1}$ — setting all its variables to $0$ leaves $0$. Hence $\varphi\bigl(\sum_i r_i f_i\bigr)=\sum_i\varphi(r_i)\,\varphi(f_i)=0$. But $\varphi(X_{N+1})=X_{N+1}\neq 0$. Contradiction. For the chain version: $X_{n+1}\in(X_1,\dots,X_{n+1})$ but the same homomorphism argument shows $X_{n+1}\notin(X_1,\dots,X_n)$, so every inclusion $(X_1,\dots,X_n)\subsetneq(X_1,\dots,X_{n+1})$ is strict.

# Solution

The strategy is to pick the ideal $\mathfrak{m}=(X_1,X_2,\dots)$ of polynomials with zero constant term, assume it has a finite generating set, observe that finitely many polynomials mention only finitely many variables, and then use an evaluation homomorphism to show a never-mentioned variable escapes the ideal. The same homomorphism shows the variable chain is strictly increasing.

**Step 1: Reduce "not Noetherian" to producing one bad ideal.**

By [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]], a ring is Noetherian if and only if every ideal is finitely generated. So to prove $R$ is *not* Noetherian it suffices to exhibit a *single* ideal of $R$ that is not finitely generated.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The theorem [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] states that for any ring $R$ the following are equivalent: (a) $R$ satisfies the ascending chain condition on ideals; (b) every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated. "Noetherian" *is* condition (a). Negating the equivalence, $R$ fails (a) if and only if $R$ fails (b), i.e. if and only if *some* ideal of $R$ is not finitely generated. Thus one counterexample ideal proves non-Noetherianity — we need not inspect all chains.

**Step 2: Choose the test ideal $\mathfrak{m}=(X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots)$.**

Let $\mathfrak{m}$ be the ideal generated by all the variables. Equivalently, $\mathfrak{m}$ is the set of all polynomials in $R$ with zero constant term. This is a genuine ideal of $R$, and it is the candidate we will show is not finitely generated.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Define $\mathfrak{m}=(X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots)$, the smallest ideal containing every variable. An element of $\mathfrak{m}$ is a finite sum $\sum_i r_i X_{j_i}$ with $r_i\in R$; every such sum has zero constant term (each summand is a multiple of a variable, hence has no constant term). Conversely, any polynomial $f$ with zero constant term is a finite $\mathbb{Z}$-combination of monomials, each of which is divisible by at least one variable, so $f$ is a finite $R$-combination of variables and lies in $\mathfrak{m}$. Hence
> $$\mathfrak{m}=\{\,f\in R:\ f\text{ has zero constant term}\,\}.$$
> That this is an ideal is also visible from the homomorphism $\varepsilon:R\to\mathbb{Z}$ sending every variable to $0$ (evaluation of the constant term): $\mathfrak{m}=\ker\varepsilon$, and a kernel is always an ideal.

**Step 3: A finite generating set mentions only finitely many variables.**

Suppose, for contradiction, that $\mathfrak{m}=(f_1,\dots,f_k)$ for some finite list $f_1,\dots,f_k\in R$. Each $f_i$ is a polynomial, so it involves only finitely many variables; hence the whole list involves only finitely many. Choose $N$ so large that no variable $X_j$ with $j>N$ appears in any of $f_1,\dots,f_k$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By definition of the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,\dots]$, every element is a *finite* $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combination of monomials, and every monomial is a finite product of variables. So each $f_i$ involves only finitely many distinct variables — call that finite set $\operatorname{supp}(f_i)$, the *support* of $f_i$. The union $\operatorname{supp}(f_1)\cup\cdots\cup\operatorname{supp}(f_k)$ is a finite union of finite sets, hence finite. A finite set of variables has a largest index; let $N$ be (at least) that largest index. Then for every $j>N$, the variable $X_j$ appears in none of $f_1,\dots,f_k$. The point is purely that $k<\infty$ and each $f_i$ is finite, while the supply of variables is infinite: the list can be "outrun."

**Step 4: The variable $X_{N+1}$ escapes the ideal $(f_1,\dots,f_k)$.**

The variable $X_{N+1}$ lies in $\mathfrak{m}$ but does **not** lie in $(f_1,\dots,f_k)$. This contradicts $\mathfrak{m}=(f_1,\dots,f_k)$, so $\mathfrak{m}$ is not finitely generated.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Certainly $X_{N+1}\in\mathfrak{m}$ — it is one of the generators (it has zero constant term).
>
> Suppose toward a contradiction that $X_{N+1}\in(f_1,\dots,f_k)$. Then there are multipliers $r_1,\dots,r_k\in R$ with
> $$X_{N+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k} r_i\,f_i. \tag{$\ast$}$$
> One must be careful: the $r_i$ may themselves involve high-index variables, so we cannot argue merely from "supports." Instead, **specialise**. Define a ring homomorphism
> $$\varphi:R\longrightarrow \mathbb{Z}[X_{N+1}],\qquad \varphi(X_{N+1})=X_{N+1},\qquad \varphi(X_j)=0\ \text{ for all }j\neq N+1.$$
> This is well-defined: a homomorphism out of a polynomial ring is determined freely by where it sends the variables (the universal property of [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial rings]]), and any assignment of the variables to elements of a commutative ring extends uniquely to a ring homomorphism.
>
> Apply $\varphi$ to each $f_i$. Recall (Step 3) that $f_i$ involves no variable of index $>N$, so in particular $f_i$ does not involve $X_{N+1}$; and $f_i\in\mathfrak{m}$ has zero constant term. Therefore $\varphi(f_i)$ is obtained from $f_i$ by setting *every* variable occurring in $f_i$ to $0$, which leaves only the constant term — namely $0$. Hence
> $$\varphi(f_i)=0\qquad\text{for every }i=1,\dots,k.$$
> Now apply $\varphi$ to both sides of $(\ast)$. Since $\varphi$ is a ring homomorphism,
> $$\varphi(X_{N+1})=\varphi\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} r_i f_i\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(r_i)\,\varphi(f_i)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varphi(r_i)\cdot 0=0.$$
> But $\varphi(X_{N+1})=X_{N+1}$, which is a *non-zero* element of $\mathbb{Z}[X_{N+1}]$. This is the contradiction $X_{N+1}=0$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X_{N+1}]$, which is false.
>
> Hence $X_{N+1}\notin(f_1,\dots,f_k)$. But $X_{N+1}\in\mathfrak{m}$, so $(f_1,\dots,f_k)\neq\mathfrak{m}$ — contradicting the assumption $\mathfrak{m}=(f_1,\dots,f_k)$. As $f_1,\dots,f_k$ was an arbitrary finite list, no finite list generates $\mathfrak{m}$: the ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ is **not finitely generated**. By Step 1, $R$ is not Noetherian.

**Step 5: The chain version — the same failure as a non-stabilising ascending chain.**

Equivalently, the ascending chain
$$(X_1)\subsetneq(X_1,X_2)\subsetneq(X_1,X_2,X_3)\subsetneq\cdots$$
has every inclusion strict, so it never stabilises; this directly violates the ascending chain condition.

> [!note]- Derivation
> For each $n\ge 1$ put $I_n=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$. Plainly $I_n\subseteq I_{n+1}$, since the generators of $I_n$ are among those of $I_{n+1}$; so $I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq I_3\subseteq\cdots$ is an ascending chain of ideals.
>
> Each inclusion is *strict*. Indeed $X_{n+1}\in I_{n+1}$, while $X_{n+1}\notin I_n$ by exactly the Step 4 argument with $N=n$ and the finite list $f_1,\dots,f_k$ taken to be $X_1,\dots,X_n$: the homomorphism $\varphi:R\to\mathbb{Z}[X_{n+1}]$ sending $X_{n+1}\mapsto X_{n+1}$ and all other variables to $0$ kills each generator $X_1,\dots,X_n$, hence kills $I_n$, but sends $X_{n+1}$ to $X_{n+1}\neq 0$. So $X_{n+1}\in I_{n+1}\setminus I_n$ and $I_n\subsetneq I_{n+1}$.
>
> A chain in which *every* inclusion is strict cannot have a stage $N$ with $I_N=I_{N+1}=\cdots$. This is precisely the negation of the ascending chain condition, so $R$ is not Noetherian.
>
> Finally, the chain and the bad ideal are the *same phenomenon viewed twice*: $\mathfrak{m}=\bigcup_{n\ge 1}I_n$, the increasing union of the chain. The proposition behind [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] turns "the chain $\{I_n\}$ does not stabilise" into "the ideal $\bigcup_n I_n$ is not finitely generated," and conversely — so the two halves of this solution are not independent proofs but two readings of one counterexample.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $R=\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots]$ is not Noetherian.
>
> By [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]], it suffices to exhibit one ideal of $R$ that is not finitely generated.
>
> Let $\mathfrak{m}=(X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots)$, the set of polynomials with zero constant term; it is an ideal (it is $\ker\varepsilon$ for the constant-term homomorphism $\varepsilon:R\to\mathbb{Z}$, $X_j\mapsto 0$).
>
> Suppose for contradiction $\mathfrak{m}=(f_1,\dots,f_k)$ with $f_i\in R$. Each $f_i$ is a finite polynomial, so the finite set of variables occurring in $f_1,\dots,f_k$ has a largest index $N$; thus no $f_i$ involves any $X_j$ with $j>N$.
>
> If $X_{N+1}\in\mathfrak{m}=(f_1,\dots,f_k)$, write $X_{N+1}=\sum_{i=1}^k r_i f_i$ with $r_i\in R$. Let $\varphi:R\to\mathbb{Z}[X_{N+1}]$ be the ring homomorphism with $\varphi(X_{N+1})=X_{N+1}$ and $\varphi(X_j)=0$ for $j\neq N+1$ (well-defined by the universal property of the polynomial ring). Each $f_i$ has zero constant term and involves no $X_{N+1}$, so $\varphi(f_i)=0$. Hence $X_{N+1}=\varphi(X_{N+1})=\sum_i\varphi(r_i)\varphi(f_i)=0$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X_{N+1}]$ — false.
>
> Therefore $X_{N+1}\notin(f_1,\dots,f_k)$, yet $X_{N+1}\in\mathfrak{m}$; so $\mathfrak{m}\neq(f_1,\dots,f_k)$, contradicting the choice of $f_1,\dots,f_k$. Hence $\mathfrak{m}$ is not finitely generated, and $R$ is not Noetherian.
>
> *Equivalently*, the chain $I_n=(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ satisfies $I_n\subsetneq I_{n+1}$ for every $n$, because $X_{n+1}\in I_{n+1}$ while the homomorphism $R\to\mathbb{Z}[X_{n+1}]$ above (with $N=n$) shows $X_{n+1}\notin I_n$. This ascending chain never stabilises, directly violating the ascending chain condition. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**To break a finiteness condition, build one explicit infinite object the condition forbids.** "Noetherian" is a finiteness hypothesis, and the universal recipe for *disproving* a finiteness hypothesis is to construct a single witnessing infinity — here, an ideal needing infinitely many generators, equivalently an infinite strictly ascending chain. The first move is always to invoke the characterisation theorem ([[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]]) to turn the negation of a universally-quantified condition ("*every* chain stabilises") into a concrete existence task ("*find one* non-finitely-generated ideal"). This trigger-reaction — "prove $R$ is not Noetherian" $\rightsquigarrow$ "exhibit one bad ideal or one bad chain" — recurs whenever a property is defined by a chain condition or a finite-generation condition: the same shape proves a [[Def - Module|module]] is not finitely generated, a [[Def - Group|group]] is not finitely presented, or a topological space is not compact (an open cover with no finite subcover). Finiteness conditions are *disproved by exhibits*, never by case analysis.

**Infinitely many generators, finitely-supported elements: the canonical non-Noetherian tension.** The ring $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,\dots]$ is the prototype non-Noetherian ring precisely because it isolates one mechanism: the ring is *globally* infinite (infinitely many independent variables) while *every individual element is finite* (each polynomial mentions finitely many variables). A finite generating set is a finite bundle of finite elements, so it has a finite "support," and a finite support can never reach an unmentioned generator. Recognising this tension lets you spot or build non-Noetherian rings everywhere: the ring of all algebraic integers, the ring $k[X_1,X_2,\dots]$ over any field, the ring of continuous functions on $[0,1]$ (the ideal of functions vanishing on a neighbourhood of $0$ is not finitely generated), or a non-Noetherian valuation ring. Conversely it clarifies *why* Hilbert's basis theorem must restrict to **finitely many** variables: adjoining one variable at a time keeps you Noetherian, but the infinite limit escapes — the theorem is sharp exactly at this boundary.

**Specialise by an evaluation homomorphism to certify that an element is not in an ideal.** The naive support argument — "elements of $(f_1,\dots,f_k)$ involve only the variables that $f_1,\dots,f_k$ involve" — is *false*, because the multipliers $r_i$ in $\sum r_i f_i$ can smuggle in new variables. The fix is the most reusable trick for *non-membership* in an ideal: find a ring homomorphism $\varphi$ that **kills the ideal but not the element**. Here $\varphi$ sets all variables to $0$ except $X_{N+1}$; it annihilates every $f_i$ (zero constant term, no $X_{N+1}$) hence the whole ideal $(f_1,\dots,f_k)$, yet sends $X_{N+1}$ to something non-zero. The general principle: an ideal $I$ is contained in $\ker\varphi$ for every homomorphism $\varphi$ vanishing on a generating set, so to show $x\notin I$ it suffices to produce one homomorphism with $\varphi(I)=0$ but $\varphi(x)\neq 0$. This is the affine-geometry instinct — *evaluate at a cleverly chosen point* — and it powers proofs that $(2,X)$ is not principal in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, that certain ideals are proper, and the whole Nullstellensatz circle of ideas relating ideals to their zero sets.

**A non-stabilising chain and a non-finitely-generated ideal are one counterexample, not two.** It is tempting to view "exhibit a bad chain" and "exhibit a bad ideal" as separate tasks, but the proposition underlying [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] welds them: given an ascending chain $\{I_n\}$, its union $\bigcup_n I_n$ is an ideal, and that union is finitely generated *if and only if* the chain stabilises (a finite generating set lies in some $I_N$, forcing $I_N=I_{N+1}=\cdots$). So the chain $(X_1)\subsetneq(X_1,X_2)\subsetneq\cdots$ and the ideal $\mathfrak{m}=\bigcup_n(X_1,\dots,X_n)$ are the same object seen two ways. The lesson for problem-solving: pick whichever formulation is easier to compute with — chains make *strictness* a local check ($X_{n+1}\notin I_n$), while the union makes the *global* obstruction ("no finite generating set") explicit — and translate freely between them. When you have produced one, you have for free produced the other.

**This exercise is the boundary marker for Hilbert's basis theorem; keep it beside the positive results.** The value of this counterexample is calibration: it stands exactly where the good behaviour stops. [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]] says $R$ Noetherian $\implies R[X]$ Noetherian, and by induction $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian for *every finite* $n$ — so $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ and $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ are Noetherian (see [[Ex - Hilbert's basis theorem and polynomial rings in several variables]]). This exercise shows that the conclusion genuinely *fails* in the infinite-variable limit: induction gives you each finite stage but does not survive the union. Keeping the positive theorem and this negative companion side by side pins down the precise content of "finitely generated" in "finitely generated algebra over a Noetherian ring" — it is the hypothesis without which the entire edifice of Noetherian commutative algebra (and hence much of algebraic geometry, which needs coordinate rings to be Noetherian) would collapse.
