# Problem Statement

Show that the ring
$$\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X^2-Y^3)$$
is **Noetherian**.

Here $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ is the polynomial ring over $\mathbb{Z}$ in two indeterminates, $(X^2-Y^3)$ is the ideal generated by the single polynomial $X^2-Y^3$, and the quotient is the coordinate ring of the cuspidal cubic. The intended route is purely structural: $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian; Hilbert's basis theorem, applied twice, lifts this to $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$; and a quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the integers, a two-variable polynomial ring over them, a principal ideal, and the quotient ring it cuts out.

![[Def - Noetherian Ring#The Definition]]

The two equivalent faces of this condition — the ascending chain condition, and "every ideal finitely generated" — are tied together by:

![[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals#Statement]]

The engine for *building* Noetherian rings out of old ones is Hilbert's basis theorem:

![[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem#Statement]]

By a [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring in two variables]] we mean $R[X,Y]$, which is defined iteratively as $(R[X])[Y]$: first adjoin $X$, then adjoin $Y$ to the result. This iterative reading is exactly what lets Hilbert's theorem be applied twice.

![[Def - Quotient Ring#The Definition]]

Finally, $\mathbb{Z}$ itself is Noetherian: $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] (every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $(n)$ — see [[Ex - Every ideal of the integers is principal]]), and in a principal ideal domain every ideal is generated by *one* element, hence finitely generated, hence the ring is Noetherian by the proposition above.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *prove a ring is Noetherian* problem, of the *structural / closure* kind: the ring in question is not attacked directly but is recognised as **built from a known Noetherian ring by Noether-preserving operations**. As the [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] notes, almost no Noetherian ring is proved Noetherian by inspecting its ideals directly; instead one identifies a base case and a chain of constructions known to preserve the property.

**Assumption pattern.** The ring $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X^2-Y^3)$ wears its construction on its sleeve. Reading it inside-out: start with $\mathbb{Z}$; form the polynomial ring in $X$; form the polynomial ring in $Y$ over that; quotient by an ideal. The assumption pattern is "the ring is presented as a finite tower of polynomial extensions and quotients over $\mathbb{Z}$" — and every operation in that tower is known to preserve Noetherianity.

**Theorem routing.** Three results route the base case to the conclusion. (1) $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian (it is a PID, so every ideal is principal, so finitely generated — [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]]). (2) [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]]: if $R$ is Noetherian then $R[X]$ is Noetherian — apply once to get $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ Noetherian, again to get $\mathbb{Z}[X][Y]=\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ Noetherian. (3) The quotient proposition: a quotient $R/I$ of a Noetherian ring $R$ is Noetherian. Composing: $\mathbb{Z}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Z}[X]\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X^2-Y^3)$.

**Key decision point.** The only thing that could trip a reader: the polynomial $X^2-Y^3$ is a *red herring* for the Noetherian question. One does not need to factor it, decide irreducibility, or understand the geometry of the cusp. The single judgement to make is "$(X^2-Y^3)$ is *some* ideal, and a quotient by *any* ideal of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian." Recognising that the specific generator is irrelevant — that it is the *closure properties*, not the arithmetic of the cubic, doing the work — is the whole insight.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Identify the base ring as Noetherian via "PID $\Rightarrow$ Noetherian."** $\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain, so every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by one element; by [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] this makes $\mathbb{Z}$ Noetherian. This is the standard way to certify a base case.

2. **Adjoin an indeterminate by Hilbert's basis theorem.** Given that $R$ is Noetherian, [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]] hands you that $R[X]$ is Noetherian. This is the operation that "grows" a Noetherian ring while keeping the property.

3. **Iterate the polynomial-ring construction.** Read $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ as $(\mathbb{Z}[X])[Y]$ and apply operation 2 twice. The multivariate case is *defined* as repeated single-variable adjunction, so Hilbert's theorem extends to it by induction with no extra work.

4. **Pass to a quotient by an ideal, preserving Noetherianity.** A quotient $R/I$ of a Noetherian ring $R$ is again Noetherian; the proof routes ideals of $R/I$ back to ideals of $R$ via the ideal correspondence and pushes finite generating sets forward.

5. **Use the ideal correspondence to transport finite generation across a quotient.** Ideals of $R/I$ correspond bijectively (via [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence]]) to ideals of $R$ containing $I$; a finite generating set of the upstairs ideal projects to a finite generating set downstairs.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Do **not** try to understand the ring $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X^2-Y^3)$ element by element, and do not analyse the polynomial $X^2-Y^3$. Read the ring's *name* as a recipe: it is built from $\mathbb{Z}$ by three operations. What are they, and is each one known to preserve the Noetherian property?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> The base is $\mathbb{Z}$. Why is $\mathbb{Z}$ Noetherian? (Recall every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $(n)$ — it is a principal ideal domain — and a ring in which every ideal is finitely generated is Noetherian.) The two "growing" operations are: adjoin a variable, then take a quotient. Which named theorem handles "adjoin a variable"?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]]: $R$ Noetherian $\implies R[X]$ Noetherian. Apply it to $R=\mathbb{Z}$ to get $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ Noetherian; apply it *again* to $R=\mathbb{Z}[X]$, since $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]=(\mathbb{Z}[X])[Y]$, to get $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ Noetherian. Now only the quotient step remains: why is $R/I$ Noetherian when $R$ is?

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For the quotient step, take any ideal $J\trianglelefteq R/I$. By the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] it equals $J'/I$ for a unique ideal $J'$ of $R$ with $I\subseteq J'$. Since $R$ is Noetherian, $J'=(r_1,\dots,r_n)$ is finitely generated. Project: the cosets $r_1+I,\dots,r_n+I$ generate $J$. So every ideal of $R/I$ is finitely generated — that is the Noetherian property. The polynomial $X^2-Y^3$ is never used; *any* ideal would do.

# Solution

The strategy is to read the ring as a tower over $\mathbb{Z}$ and walk up it: $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian because it is a PID; Hilbert's basis theorem applied twice makes $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ Noetherian; and a quotient by any ideal — in particular $(X^2-Y^3)$ — keeps the property. The specific generator $X^2-Y^3$ plays no role.

**Step 1: The base ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian.**

$\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain, so every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by a single element, hence finitely generated; therefore $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By [[Ex - Every ideal of the integers is principal]], every ideal $I\trianglelefteq\mathbb{Z}$ has the form $I=(n)$ for some integer $n\ge 0$ — that is, $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]]. A principal ideal $(n)$ is generated by the *one* element $n$, so it is finitely generated. Thus *every* ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is finitely generated. By [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] — a ring is Noetherian if and only if every ideal is finitely generated — $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian. More generally this shows *every* PID is Noetherian; we only need the instance $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Step 2: $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ is Noetherian — Hilbert's basis theorem, applied twice.**

Adjoining one indeterminate to a Noetherian ring keeps it Noetherian; applying this to $\mathbb{Z}$ and then again to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ yields that $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ is Noetherian.

> [!note]- Derivation
> [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]] states: *if $R$ is a Noetherian ring, then the polynomial ring $R[X]$ is Noetherian.*
>
> *First application.* Take $R=\mathbb{Z}$. By Step 1, $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian. The theorem gives: $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is Noetherian.
>
> *Second application.* The two-variable polynomial ring is, by definition, the result of adjoining $Y$ to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$:
> $$\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]=(\mathbb{Z}[X])[Y].$$
> Take $R=\mathbb{Z}[X]$, which we have just shown is Noetherian. The theorem gives: $(\mathbb{Z}[X])[Y]=\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ is Noetherian.
>
> This is the inductive content of the corollary "$\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian for every $n$": one starts at the Noetherian ring $\mathbb{Z}$ and applies Hilbert's theorem once per variable. Here $n=2$ takes two applications.

**Step 3: A quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian.**

For any ideal $I$ of a Noetherian ring $R$, the quotient ring $R/I$ is Noetherian.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $R$ be Noetherian and $I\trianglelefteq R$ an ideal; we show every ideal of $R/I$ is finitely generated, which by [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] makes $R/I$ Noetherian.
>
> Let $J\trianglelefteq R/I$ be an arbitrary ideal of the quotient. By the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] — the bijection, induced by the quotient map $\pi:R\to R/I$, between ideals of $R/I$ and ideals of $R$ that contain $I$ — there is a unique ideal $J'\trianglelefteq R$ with
> $$I\subseteq J'\qquad\text{and}\qquad J=J'/I=\pi(J')=\{\,r+I:r\in J'\,\}.$$
> ($J'=\pi^{-1}(J)$ is the preimage; it is an ideal of $R$ because preimages of ideals under ring homomorphisms are ideals, and it contains $I=\ker\pi$.)
>
> Since $R$ is Noetherian, the ideal $J'$ is finitely generated: $J'=(r_1,\dots,r_n)$ for some $r_1,\dots,r_n\in R$.
>
> Now project. I claim the cosets $r_1+I,\dots,r_n+I$ generate $J$ inside $R/I$. Indeed, take any element of $J=\pi(J')$; it is $\pi(x)=x+I$ for some $x\in J'$. Write $x=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i r_i$ with $a_i\in R$ (possible since the $r_i$ generate $J'$). Applying the ring homomorphism $\pi$,
> $$x+I=\pi(x)=\pi\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i r_i\right)=\sum_{i=1}^n\pi(a_i)\,\pi(r_i)=\sum_{i=1}^n(a_i+I)(r_i+I).$$
> So $x+I$ is an $(R/I)$-linear combination of $r_1+I,\dots,r_n+I$. Hence $J=(r_1+I,\dots,r_n+I)$ is finitely generated.
>
> As $J$ was an arbitrary ideal of $R/I$, every ideal of $R/I$ is finitely generated, so $R/I$ is Noetherian.

**Step 4: Conclude.**

Apply Step 3 with $R=\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ (Noetherian by Step 2) and $I=(X^2-Y^3)$: the quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X^2-Y^3)$ is Noetherian.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By Step 2, $R=\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ is a Noetherian ring. The set $I=(X^2-Y^3)$ — the principal ideal generated by the polynomial $X^2-Y^3$ — is an ideal of $R$. Step 3 says that for *any* ideal $I$ of a Noetherian ring $R$, the quotient $R/I$ is Noetherian. Taking this particular $I$,
> $$\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X^2-Y^3)\quad\text{is Noetherian.}$$
> Observe that nothing about $X^2-Y^3$ entered the argument: not its degree, not whether it is irreducible, not the singular geometry of the cuspidal cubic it defines. Step 3 would deliver the same conclusion for $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/I$ with $I$ *any* ideal whatsoever. The hypothesis "$X^2-Y^3$" is decoration; the load is borne entirely by "$\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ is Noetherian" plus "quotients preserve Noetherian." $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X^2-Y^3)$ is Noetherian.
>
> *$\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian.* Every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $(n)$ for some $n\ge 0$ (it is a PID), hence finitely generated; by [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]], $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian.
>
> *$\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ is Noetherian.* By [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]], $R$ Noetherian $\implies R[X]$ Noetherian. Applying it to $R=\mathbb{Z}$ gives $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ Noetherian; applying it to $R=\mathbb{Z}[X]$ gives $(\mathbb{Z}[X])[Y]=\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ Noetherian.
>
> *Quotients of Noetherian rings are Noetherian.* Let $R$ be Noetherian, $I\trianglelefteq R$. Given any ideal $J\trianglelefteq R/I$, the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] gives an ideal $J'=\pi^{-1}(J)\trianglelefteq R$ with $I\subseteq J'$ and $J=J'/I$. Since $R$ is Noetherian, $J'=(r_1,\dots,r_n)$. For any $x\in J'$, writing $x=\sum a_i r_i$ and applying the quotient map $\pi$ gives $x+I=\sum(a_i+I)(r_i+I)$; hence $J=(r_1+I,\dots,r_n+I)$ is finitely generated. So every ideal of $R/I$ is finitely generated, and $R/I$ is Noetherian.
>
> *Conclusion.* Apply the last paragraph with $R=\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]$ and $I=(X^2-Y^3)$: the ring $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X^2-Y^3)$ is Noetherian. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Prove a ring is Noetherian by recognising it as a tower of Noether-preserving operations over a Noetherian base.** Almost no Noetherian ring met in practice is shown Noetherian by inspecting its ideals; the property is established *compositionally*. The toolkit is a small base (fields, $\mathbb{Z}$, any PID — all Noetherian) and a list of operations that preserve "Noetherian": adjoining finitely many indeterminates ([[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]]), passing to a quotient, localising, and forming finitely generated algebras. Faced with "is $A$ Noetherian?", the reaction is to *parse the name of $A$ as a construction recipe* and check that the base is Noetherian and every step in the recipe preserves the property. This routine certifies that every finitely generated algebra over a field or over $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian — and that is the class of rings underlying all of classical algebraic geometry (coordinate rings of affine varieties) and much of algebraic number theory (rings of integers, their quotients). The skill is *reading the construction*, not analysing the ideals.

**The specific generator of the quotient ideal is almost always irrelevant to "Noetherian."** The instinct on seeing $\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]/(X^2-Y^3)$ is to engage with $X^2-Y^3$ — to factor it, test irreducibility, picture the cusp. For the Noetherian question this is wasted effort: the quotient proposition says $R/I$ is Noetherian for *every* ideal $I$ of a Noetherian $R$, so the generator is a free parameter. Learning to spot which features of a problem are load-bearing and which are decoration is a central problem-solving skill. The trigger "quotient of a Noetherian ring" should fire the reaction "Noetherian, regardless of what we quotient by," and you should *resist* the pull to study the ideal. (Contrast: questions about whether the quotient is a *domain*, or a *field*, or *reduced*, do depend intimately on the generator — there the polynomial matters. Knowing which questions are generator-sensitive and which are not is the real lesson.)

**Hilbert's basis theorem is single-variable; the multivariate case is induction, applied once per variable.** [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]] is stated for one adjoined indeterminate, $R\rightsquigarrow R[X]$. The multivariate ring is *defined* iteratively, $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]=(R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}])[X_n]$, so applying the theorem $n$ times in succession proves $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ Noetherian for any *finite* $n$. This induction is the content of the corollary "$\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ and $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ are Noetherian" (worked in [[Ex - Hilbert's basis theorem and polynomial rings in several variables]]). The crucial caveat: the induction runs only over *finitely many* variables — each finite stage is Noetherian, but the infinite-variable union $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,\dots]$ is **not** Noetherian (see [[Ex - A ring that is not Noetherian]]). When you "apply Hilbert's theorem twice," internalise that you are running two steps of a finite induction, and that the finiteness is essential.

**The quotient-preserves-Noetherian proof is the universal "push a property through a surjection" template.** The Step 3 argument has a shape worth extracting. To show $R/I$ inherits a finiteness property from $R$: take an ideal $J$ of the quotient, *pull it back* to an ideal $J'=\pi^{-1}(J)$ of $R$ via the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]], use the property *upstairs* in $R$ (here: $J'$ is finitely generated), then *push the witness forward* through the surjection $\pi$ (the cosets $r_i+I$ generate $J$, because $\pi$ is a homomorphism and turns $\sum a_i r_i$ into $\sum(a_i+I)(r_i+I)$). Pull back — apply upstairs — push forward. This three-beat pattern proves not only that quotients of Noetherian rings are Noetherian, but that quotients of finitely generated rings are finitely generated, that homomorphic images of Noetherian modules are Noetherian, and more broadly that *any* property witnessed by a finite generating set descends along surjections. Recognising the template lets you reconstruct the quotient step from scratch whenever you need it.
