# Notation

Fix an [[Def - Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial|algebraic integer]] $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$. The **evaluation map** is the [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]] $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C}$, $\;g \mapsto g(\alpha)$, and its kernel is the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]]
$$I = \ker\varphi = \{\, g \in \mathbb{Z}[X] : g(\alpha) = 0 \,\}.$$
We write $(f)$ for the principal ideal of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ generated by a polynomial $f$, and $\deg g$ for degree. A polynomial is **monic** if its leading coefficient is $1$; it is [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|**primitive**]] if the greatest common divisor of all its coefficients is $1$ (a unit). For $g \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$, the **content** $c(g)$ is that greatest common divisor, so $g = c(g)\,g_1$ with $g_1$ primitive. We work in both $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ and the larger ring $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, with $\mathbb{Z}[X] \subseteq \mathbb{Q}[X]$; the latter, being a polynomial ring over a field, is a Euclidean domain and admits division with remainder. The polynomial $f_\alpha$ is the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$. The full chapter symbol registry is on [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8]].

# Statement

> **Proposition.** Let $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ be an [[Def - Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial|algebraic integer]], and let $I = \ker(\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C},\; g \mapsto g(\alpha))$ be the kernel of evaluation at $\alpha$. Then $I$ is a **principal ideal**:
> $$I = (f_\alpha)$$
> for some **monic, irreducible** polynomial $f_\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. This $f_\alpha$ is the **minimal polynomial** of $\alpha$.

The point worth flagging at once: this is *not* a routine fact. The ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is **not a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]]** — for instance the ideal $(2, X)$ requires two generators and is not principal. So there is no general theorem guaranteeing that an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is generated by a single element. The kernel $I$ is principal for a *special reason*, particular to kernels of evaluation at an algebraic integer, and exhibiting that reason is the content of the proof.

---

# Motivation

The definition of the minimal polynomial wants to name "the" canonical polynomial relation satisfied by $\alpha$. But $\alpha$ satisfies infinitely many integer-polynomial relations at once: if $g(\alpha) = 0$ then $(Xg)(\alpha) = 0$, $(g + g)(\alpha) = 0$, and $h \cdot g$ vanishes at $\alpha$ for every $h$. The collection of all such $g$ is the kernel $I$ of the evaluation homomorphism, and it is an ideal precisely because of these closure properties. To extract a *single* canonical relation, we need the ideal to be **principal** — generated by one polynomial $f_\alpha$, so that every relation $\alpha$ satisfies is a multiple of that one. Only then does "the minimal polynomial" refer to something definite.

So the proposition is the load-bearing fact under the definition. Without it, the phrase "the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$" would be ill-posed: there would be no distinguished generator to point at. The reason the result is interesting, rather than a one-line citation, is the obstacle named in the Statement. In a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] every ideal is principal by fiat, and if $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ were a PID we would simply invoke that. But $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not a PID. We cannot lean on the ambient ring; we must use something about $I$ itself.

What we *can* lean on is the larger ring $\mathbb{Q}[X]$. Replacing the coefficient ring $\mathbb{Z}$ by the field $\mathbb{Q}$ turns $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ into a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] — division with remainder works, so $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ *is* a PID. The strategy almost writes itself: do the division-and-minimality argument upstairs in the friendly ring $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, where ideals are automatically principal, and then descend the conclusion back to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. The descent is not free — it costs a denominator-clearing step and a content computation — and managing that cost is the real work. The proposition is, in spirit, a small instance of the same philosophy that runs [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]]: $\mathbb{Z}[X]$-questions are answered by working in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ and controlling the passage back with content.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The theorem's precondition $A$ is "$\alpha$ is an algebraic integer" — equivalently, "$I = \ker\varphi$ contains a monic polynomial" — and the engine of the proof needs, in addition, division with remainder in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$. Recognising when the proposition is in play means recognising these in disguise.

A first disguised source is **"$\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ satisfies any monic relation with integer coefficients"**, however that relation arises. Property $B$ might be "$\alpha = \zeta$ is a root of unity", or "$\alpha$ is an eigenvalue of an integer matrix", or "$\alpha$ is a sum or product of things already known to be algebraic integers". Each of these supplies a monic $f \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ with $f(\alpha)=0$, hence $f \in I$ and $I \neq \{0\}$, which is the hypothesis. The bridge $B \Rightarrow A$ is non-obvious in the matrix case: the characteristic polynomial of an integer matrix is monic with integer coefficients, so eigenvalues of integer matrices are automatically algebraic integers — and the proposition then hands you a well-defined minimal polynomial for that eigenvalue. *Example problem:* given that $2\cos(2\pi/7)$ is an eigenvalue of an explicit integer matrix, conclude it has a minimal polynomial and compute it.

A second disguised source is **"the coefficient ring is replaced by another ring whose field of fractions is Euclidean"**. The proof never uses $\mathbb{Z}$ specifically — it uses that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a unique factorisation domain (so content is defined) and that $\mathbb{Q} = \operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb{Z})$ gives a Euclidean $\mathbb{Q}[X]$. Property $B$ is "$R$ is a UFD and $\alpha$ is integral over $R$"; the same argument shows the kernel of $R[X] \to \operatorname{Frac}(R)(\alpha)$ is principal. The non-obviousness is that the result is *not* about $\mathbb{Z}$ at all; it is about the UFD/field-of-fractions pair. *Example problem:* show that an element $\alpha$ of a function field, integral over the polynomial ring $k[t]$, has a well-defined minimal polynomial over $k[t]$.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion $C$ is "$I = (f_\alpha)$ with $f_\alpha$ monic and irreducible, and so $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha) \cong \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$".

Combine $C$ with **the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]]** to get a concrete model of the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$. The further result $E$ is the isomorphism $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \cong \mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$ — a presentation by one generator and one relation. This is non-obvious leverage because it converts a subring of $\mathbb{C}$, defined by an opaque "smallest subring containing $\alpha$", into a wholly explicit quotient ring whose elements are polynomials modulo $f_\alpha$; arithmetic in $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ becomes polynomial arithmetic reduced mod $f_\alpha$.

Combine $C$ with **the divisibility criterion it secretly encodes**: a polynomial $g$ vanishes at $\alpha$ if and only if $f_\alpha \mid g$. The further result $E$ is a *test* — to check whether $g(\alpha) = 0$, divide $g$ by $f_\alpha$ and see if the remainder is zero. Non-obvious because it replaces an analytic question (does this complex number annihilate $g$?) with a purely algebraic division. This is precisely the step used in [[Thm - Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers|the theorem that rational algebraic integers are integers]], where one argues that $X - \alpha$ must divide $f_\alpha$.

Combine $C$ with **irreducibility tests for integer polynomials** (Eisenstein, reduction mod a prime). The proposition guarantees $f_\alpha$ *is* irreducible; conversely, if you have a monic $f \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ with $f(\alpha) = 0$ and you can *prove* $f$ irreducible by an external test, then $f$ must equal $f_\alpha$. The further result $E$ is an identification: the irreducibility test certifies that your candidate polynomial is the genuine minimal polynomial. Non-obvious because it lets a one-off irreducibility check pin down the canonical object, sparing the kernel computation entirely.

# Why Is It True

The result should feel believable once you see what is really being claimed and where the friction is.

The kernel $I$ is the set of all integer-polynomial relations satisfied by $\alpha$. We want one relation $f_\alpha$ to generate them all — every other relation a polynomial multiple of $f_\alpha$. Imagine we are inside $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ instead of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. There, the story is completely standard: $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ has division with remainder, so it is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]], hence a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], and *every* ideal — in particular the ideal generated by $I$ inside $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ — is principal. The generator is found by the universal trick: take an element of *least degree* in the ideal, and divide everything else by it; the remainder has smaller degree yet still lies in the ideal, so by minimality it is zero, so everything is a multiple. This is the same "pick the smallest element" argument that proves $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID. Upstairs, principality is free.

So the only question is whether the upstairs conclusion *survives the trip back down* to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Here is the friction. The least-degree generator we found may have rational, non-integer coefficients. When we divide an integer polynomial $h \in I$ by it, $h = f_\alpha q + r$, the quotient $q$ comes out with rational coefficients — the division happened over $\mathbb{Q}$, not $\mathbb{Z}$. We have learned $h = f_\alpha q$ in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ (the remainder vanishes by minimality, as upstairs), but this is a factorisation over the *rationals*, and we wanted one over the *integers*. The denominators in $q$ are the enemy.

Two ideas defuse the denominators. First, we may take the least-degree generator $f_\alpha$ to be **primitive** — having stripped any common integer factor from its coefficients, since scaling does not change which relation it is. Second — and this is the crux — we *clear denominators by hand*: multiply $h = f_\alpha q$ through by a single non-zero integer $a$ large enough to make $aq$ have integer coefficients, getting an honest equation $ah = f_\alpha\,(aq)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Now take **contents** of both sides. Content is multiplicative on integer polynomials, and $f_\alpha$ is primitive — its content is $1$ — so the content of the right side is just the content of $aq$. The content of the left side is $a$ times the content of $h$. Equating: $a$ divides the content of $aq$. But that says every coefficient of $aq$ is divisible by $a$ — so we can divide $aq$ by $a$ and stay inside $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Dividing the whole equation $ah = f_\alpha(aq)$ by $a$ leaves $h = f_\alpha q$ with $q$ now *genuinely* in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. The denominators were never essential; the primitivity of $f_\alpha$ guaranteed they could be refunded. The integer factorisation we wanted has appeared.

That is the whole structure: principality is automatic in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$; the descent to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ costs a denominator-clearing, and a content argument shows the cost is always recoverable because $f_\alpha$ is primitive. The morally identical bookkeeping — primitive polynomial, clear denominators, take contents, find the scalar refunds exactly — is the engine of [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]].

Why is $f_\alpha$ irreducible? Once $I = (f_\alpha)$, the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] gives $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha) \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$, and $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ is a subring of the field $\mathbb{C}$, so it is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]] — no zero divisors. A quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$ being a domain is exactly the statement that $(f_\alpha)$ is a *prime* ideal, i.e. $f_\alpha$ is a prime element, and a prime is [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|irreducible]]. Irreducibility is not extra labour — it is handed over for free by the fact that $\alpha$ lives in a field.

# What Makes This Hard

The single hardest step is the **descent from $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$**: after the Euclidean division gives $h = f_\alpha q$ with $q$ having rational coefficients, most people cannot see why $q$ may be taken to have *integer* coefficients. The non-obvious move is to clear denominators with one integer $a$ and then take contents — using that $f_\alpha$ is primitive — to conclude $a \mid c(aq)$, which forces $q \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. The most common error is to forget to arrange $f_\alpha$ primitive at the start (without primitivity the content equation $c(ah) = c(aq)$ fails and the descent collapses), or to do the Euclidean division in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — which is illegal, since $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ has no division with remainder.

---

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Pick $f_\alpha \in I$ of *minimal degree*, arranged to be primitive. To show $I = (f_\alpha)$, take an arbitrary $h \in I$, divide $h$ by $f_\alpha$ *in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$* (legal there), and use minimality of degree plus evaluation at $\alpha$ to kill the remainder. This gives $h = f_\alpha q$ over $\mathbb{Q}$; clear denominators and take contents to push $q$ into $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Finally, get irreducibility for free from $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha) \cong \mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ being a domain.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **$I \neq \{0\}$ and a minimal-degree primitive element exists.** Show $I$ contains a non-zero polynomial, and pick $f_\alpha \in I \setminus \{0\}$ of least degree, taken primitive.
   - *Hint:* "$\alpha$ algebraic integer" hands you a monic $f \in I$ directly; degrees are non-negative integers so a least one exists; dividing out the content does not change membership in $I$ or the degree.
   - *Why needed:* Minimality of degree is the lever that kills remainders; primitivity is what makes the later content argument work.

2. **Divide in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ and kill the remainder.** For $h \in I$, write $h = f_\alpha q + r$ in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ with $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg f_\alpha$, and show $r = 0$.
   - *Hint:* Clear denominators to $ah = f_\alpha(aq) + (ar)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, then evaluate at $\alpha$: since $f_\alpha(\alpha) = h(\alpha) = 0$, deduce $(ar)(\alpha) = 0$, so $ar \in I$; but $\deg(ar) = \deg r < \deg f_\alpha$ contradicts minimality unless $r = 0$.
   - *Why needed:* Reduces every $h$ to $h = f_\alpha q$ — the factorisation, still only over $\mathbb{Q}$.

3. **Descend $q$ to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.** From $ah = f_\alpha(aq)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, show $q$ actually has integer coefficients.
   - *Hint:* Take contents: $a\,c(h) = c(ah) = c(f_\alpha(aq)) = c(aq)$ since $f_\alpha$ is primitive (content multiplicative). So $a \mid c(aq)$, meaning every coefficient of $aq$ is a multiple of $a$; cancel $a$ to land $q \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$.
   - *Why needed:* Upgrades $h = f_\alpha q$ to an honest equation in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, giving $h \in (f_\alpha)$, hence $I \subseteq (f_\alpha)$; the reverse inclusion is immediate.

4. **Irreducibility of $f_\alpha$.** Show $f_\alpha$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.
   - *Hint:* By the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]], $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha) \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \subseteq \mathbb{C}$; a subring of a field is a domain, so $(f_\alpha)$ is prime, so $f_\alpha$ is prime, hence irreducible.
   - *Why needed:* Completes the statement — $f_\alpha$ is monic *and* irreducible.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: A minimal-degree primitive element of $I$ exists
> **Statement:** The kernel $I = \ker\varphi$ is non-zero, and contains a primitive polynomial $f_\alpha$ of least degree among non-zero elements of $I$.
>
> **Hint:** "Algebraic integer" gives a monic polynomial in $I$; degrees live in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, which is well-ordered, so a least degree is attained; pulling out the content keeps the polynomial in $I$ and fixes its degree.
>
> **Why needed:** Supplies the candidate generator. Both of its features are used later — least degree powers the remainder-killing step, primitivity powers the content descent.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > By definition of [[Def - Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial|algebraic integer]], there is a monic $f \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ with $f(\alpha) = 0$, i.e. $f \in I$ and $f \neq 0$. So $I \neq \{0\}$.
> >
> > The set $\{\deg g : g \in I,\ g \neq 0\}$ is a non-empty subset of $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, which is well-ordered, so it has a least element; choose $g_0 \in I \setminus \{0\}$ realising it. Write $g_0 = c(g_0)\,f_\alpha$ where $c(g_0) \in \mathbb{Z}$ is the content and $f_\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ is [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|primitive]]. Since $g_0(\alpha) = 0$ and $c(g_0) \neq 0$ is a non-zero-divisor in $\mathbb{C}$, evaluating $0 = g_0(\alpha) = c(g_0)\,f_\alpha(\alpha)$ forces $f_\alpha(\alpha) = 0$, so $f_\alpha \in I$. And $\deg f_\alpha = \deg g_0$ is the minimal degree. Thus $f_\alpha$ is a primitive element of $I$ of least degree. $\square$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Division in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ produces zero remainder against $f_\alpha$
> **Statement:** For any $h \in I$, the Euclidean division $h = f_\alpha q + r$ in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ (with $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg f_\alpha$) has $r = 0$.
>
> **Hint:** Clear denominators with an integer $a$, evaluate the resulting integer-polynomial identity at $\alpha$, and use that $f_\alpha$ and $h$ both annihilate $\alpha$ to show the remainder annihilates $\alpha$ too — then minimal degree forces it to vanish.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the step that turns "$h \in I$" into the factorisation $h = f_\alpha q$ (over $\mathbb{Q}$ for now). Without it there is no factorisation to descend.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $h \in I$. Since $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ is a polynomial ring over a field, it is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]]: there exist $q, r \in \mathbb{Q}[X]$ with
> > $$h = f_\alpha q + r, \qquad r = 0 \ \text{ or } \ \deg r < \deg f_\alpha.$$
> > Note this division takes place in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, **not** $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — the quotient $q$ and remainder $r$ have rational coefficients.
> >
> > Choose a non-zero integer $a \in \mathbb{Z}$ that clears all denominators appearing in $q$ and $r$, so that $aq$ and $ar$ lie in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Multiplying the division identity by $a$:
> > $$a h = f_\alpha\,(aq) + (ar),$$
> > an equation in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Evaluate every polynomial at $\alpha$:
> > $$a\,h(\alpha) = f_\alpha(\alpha)\,(aq)(\alpha) + (ar)(\alpha).$$
> > Both $f_\alpha$ and $h$ lie in $I = \ker\varphi$, so $f_\alpha(\alpha) = 0$ and $h(\alpha) = 0$. The identity collapses to $0 = 0 + (ar)(\alpha)$, that is $(ar)(\alpha) = 0$, so $ar \in I$.
> >
> > Suppose $r \neq 0$. Then $ar \neq 0$ and $\deg(ar) = \deg r < \deg f_\alpha$, exhibiting a non-zero element of $I$ of strictly smaller degree than $f_\alpha$ — contradicting the minimality of $\deg f_\alpha$ (Lemma 1). Hence $r = 0$. $\square$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The rational quotient descends to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$
> **Statement:** With $r = 0$ from Lemma 2, the equation $ah = f_\alpha\,(aq)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ forces $q \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$, so $h = f_\alpha q$ holds in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.
>
> **Hint:** Take contents of $ah = f_\alpha(aq)$; content is multiplicative and $f_\alpha$ is primitive, so $c(ah) = c(aq)$; expand $c(ah) = a\,c(h)$ to get $a \mid c(aq)$, which says $a$ divides every coefficient of $aq$.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the descent. It is what makes the conclusion a statement about $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, where the proposition lives, rather than $\mathbb{Q}[X]$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > With $r = 0$, Lemma 2's identity reads
> > $$a h = f_\alpha\,(aq) \qquad \text{in } \mathbb{Z}[X].$$
> > Take **contents** of both sides. Content is multiplicative on $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — $c(uv) \sim c(u)c(v)$ up to a unit — a consequence of [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] (the product of primitive polynomials is primitive). The polynomial $f_\alpha$ is **primitive** by Lemma 1, so $c(f_\alpha)$ is a unit, and
> > $$c(a h) = c\big(f_\alpha\,(aq)\big) = c(f_\alpha)\,c(aq) \sim c(aq).$$
> > On the left, $ah$ is the polynomial $h$ scaled by the integer $a$, so $c(ah) = a\,c(h)$. Therefore
> > $$a\,c(h) \sim c(aq),$$
> > and in particular $a \mid c(aq)$. By the definition of content, $c(aq)$ divides every coefficient of $aq$; since $a \mid c(aq)$, the integer $a$ divides every coefficient of $aq$. Hence $aq = a\,\bar q$ for some $\bar q \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$.
> >
> > Now $aq = a\bar q$ as polynomials with rational coefficients, and $a \neq 0$, so cancelling $a$ gives $q = \bar q \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. Substituting into $ah = f_\alpha(aq) = f_\alpha\,(a\bar q) = a\,(f_\alpha \bar q)$ and cancelling the non-zero $a$ (legal: $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]]) yields
> > $$h = f_\alpha\,\bar q = f_\alpha\,q \qquad \text{in } \mathbb{Z}[X].$$
> > So $h \in (f_\alpha)$. $\square$

> [!note]- Lemma 4: $f_\alpha$ is irreducible
> **Statement:** The generator $f_\alpha$ is an irreducible element of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.
>
> **Hint:** Identify $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$ with $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ via the first isomorphism theorem; it is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$, hence a domain, hence $(f_\alpha)$ is a prime ideal.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the second half of the conclusion: the proposition asserts $f_\alpha$ is monic *and* irreducible.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > By Lemmas 1–3, $I = (f_\alpha)$. The [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem for rings]] applied to $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C}$ gives
> > $$\frac{\mathbb{Z}[X]}{(f_\alpha)} = \frac{\mathbb{Z}[X]}{\ker\varphi} \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}\varphi = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha].$$
> > Now $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{C}$ is a field, hence an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]]; a subring of an integral domain has no zero divisors and contains $1$, so $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ is itself an integral domain. Therefore the quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$ is an integral domain.
> >
> > A quotient ring $R/J$ is an integral domain if and only if $J$ is a prime ideal. So $(f_\alpha)$ is a prime ideal of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, which is to say $f_\alpha$ is a [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|prime element]]. In any integral domain a prime element is irreducible. Hence $f_\alpha$ is irreducible. $\square$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ be an algebraic integer and $I = \ker(\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C},\ g \mapsto g(\alpha))$.
>
> ---
> **Step 1 — a minimal-degree primitive element.**
> Since $\alpha$ is an [[Def - Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial|algebraic integer]], there is a monic $f \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ with $f(\alpha) = 0$; thus $f \in I$ and $I \neq \{0\}$. The set of degrees of non-zero elements of $I$ is a non-empty subset of $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, so it has a least element. Choose $g_0 \in I \setminus \{0\}$ of that least degree and write $g_0 = c(g_0)\,f_\alpha$ with $f_\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ **primitive**. From $0 = g_0(\alpha) = c(g_0)\,f_\alpha(\alpha)$ and $c(g_0) \neq 0$ we get $f_\alpha(\alpha) = 0$, so $f_\alpha \in I$, and $\deg f_\alpha = \deg g_0$ is minimal among non-zero elements of $I$. We will show $I = (f_\alpha)$ and that $f_\alpha$ is irreducible.
>
> ---
> **Step 2 — every $h \in I$ is divisible by $f_\alpha$ in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, with zero remainder.**
> Fix $h \in I$. The ring $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, a polynomial ring over the field $\mathbb{Q}$, is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]], so we may divide:
> $$h = f_\alpha\,q + r, \qquad q, r \in \mathbb{Q}[X], \qquad r = 0 \text{ or } \deg r < \deg f_\alpha.$$
> This division is performed in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, not $\mathbb{Z}[X]$; $q$ and $r$ have rational coefficients in general. Pick a non-zero $a \in \mathbb{Z}$ clearing the denominators of $q$ and $r$, so $aq, ar \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. Multiplying by $a$:
> $$a h = f_\alpha\,(aq) + (ar) \qquad \text{in } \mathbb{Z}[X].$$
> Evaluate at $\alpha$. Since $f_\alpha, h \in I$ we have $f_\alpha(\alpha) = 0$ and $h(\alpha) = 0$, so
> $$0 = a\,h(\alpha) = f_\alpha(\alpha)\,(aq)(\alpha) + (ar)(\alpha) = (ar)(\alpha).$$
> Hence $ar \in I$. If $r \neq 0$ then $ar \neq 0$ and $\deg(ar) = \deg r < \deg f_\alpha$, contradicting the minimality of $\deg f_\alpha$ from Step 1. Therefore $r = 0$, and
> $$a h = f_\alpha\,(aq) \qquad \text{in } \mathbb{Z}[X]. \tag{$\ast$}$$
>
> ---
> **Step 3 — the quotient descends to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.**
> Take contents in $(\ast)$. Content is multiplicative on $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — for $u, v \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$, $c(uv) \sim c(u)\,c(v)$ up to a unit, which is the content form of [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] (the product of primitive polynomials is primitive). As $f_\alpha$ is primitive, $c(f_\alpha)$ is a unit, so
> $$c(a h) = c\big(f_\alpha (aq)\big) \sim c(f_\alpha)\,c(aq) \sim c(aq).$$
> The left side is $c(ah) = a\,c(h)$, the content of $h$ scaled by $a$. Thus $a\,c(h) \sim c(aq)$, and in particular
> $$a \,\big|\, c(aq).$$
> By the definition of content, $c(aq)$ divides every coefficient of $aq$; combined with $a \mid c(aq)$, the integer $a$ divides every coefficient of $aq$. Hence $aq = a\,q'$ for some $q' \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. Since $a \neq 0$, cancel $a$ to obtain $q = q' \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. Now $(\ast)$ becomes $a h = a\,(f_\alpha q')$, and cancelling the non-zero $a$ in the integral domain $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ gives
> $$h = f_\alpha\,q' \in (f_\alpha).$$
> As $h \in I$ was arbitrary, $I \subseteq (f_\alpha)$. The reverse inclusion $(f_\alpha) \subseteq I$ is immediate, since $f_\alpha \in I$ and $I$ is an ideal. Therefore
> $$I = (f_\alpha).$$
>
> ---
> **Step 4 — $f_\alpha$ is irreducible.**
> By the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] applied to $\varphi$,
> $$\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha) = \mathbb{Z}[X]/\ker\varphi \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}\varphi = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha].$$
> The ring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ is a subring of the field $\mathbb{C}$, hence an [[Def - Integral Domain|integral domain]]. So $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$ is an integral domain, which holds if and only if $(f_\alpha)$ is a **prime** ideal. Thus $f_\alpha$ is a [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|prime element]] of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, and since every prime element of an integral domain is irreducible, $f_\alpha$ is irreducible.
>
> ---
> **Conclusion.** The kernel $I$ is the principal ideal $(f_\alpha)$, generated by the monic (rescale the primitive $f_\alpha$ so its leading coefficient is $1$; it is already a least-degree element of $I$, and the leading coefficient of a least-degree element must be a unit for it to generate the monic $f \in I$) irreducible polynomial $f_\alpha$ — the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Eigenvalues of integer matrices have minimal polynomials.** Let $M$ be an $n \times n$ matrix with integer entries and $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$ an eigenvalue. The characteristic polynomial $\det(XI - M)$ is monic with integer coefficients and vanishes at $\lambda$, so $\lambda$ is an algebraic integer and the proposition supplies a well-defined minimal polynomial $f_\lambda$. The application is non-obvious because the *source* of the monic relation — a determinant — looks nothing like an algebraic-integer hypothesis; recognising "characteristic polynomial of an integer matrix" as a certificate of integrality is the move. This also explains why traces and determinants of such matrices, being integer combinations of eigenvalues, behave integrally.

**Recurrence sequences and their growth rates.** A linear recurrence with integer coefficients, say $a_{n+1} = c_{k-1}a_{n} + \cdots + c_0 a_{n-k+1}$, has characteristic polynomial $X^k - c_{k-1}X^{k-1} - \cdots - c_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ — monic. Its roots are the algebraic integers governing the asymptotic growth of $a_n$ (the golden ratio for Fibonacci). The proposition guarantees each such growth rate carries a minimal polynomial, and irreducibility of that minimal polynomial controls whether the growth rate is "genuinely new" or lies in a smaller number field. Non-obvious because a question about the long-term behaviour of an integer sequence is being routed through the principal-ideal structure of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

**Integral elements over a polynomial ring $k[t]$.** Replace $\mathbb{Z}$ by the unique factorisation domain $R = k[t]$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ by $\operatorname{Frac}(R) = k(t)$. If $\alpha$ in some extension is a root of a monic polynomial in $k[t][X]$, the identical argument — minimal-degree primitive element, division in $k(t)[X]$, content descent — shows the kernel of $k[t][X] \to k(t)(\alpha)$ is principal, generated by an irreducible minimal polynomial over $k[t]$. The application is out-of-distribution because "integer" now means "polynomial in $t$" and the proof's reliance on $\mathbb{Z}$ is revealed to be a reliance only on the UFD/field-of-fractions pair, which is the genuine hypothesis.

**Periods of dynamical systems with integer data.** A point of period $n$ for a polynomial map $T : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ with integer coefficients satisfies $T^{n}(z) - z = 0$, and $T^n(X) - X$ is, after normalising, monic with integer coefficients. So periodic points of integer-coefficient maps are algebraic integers with minimal polynomials. The application is non-obvious because dynamics frames the object as a fixed point of an iterate, not as a root of a polynomial; unwinding the iterate exhibits the monic integer relation, after which the proposition applies verbatim.

# Bridges

- **[[Def - Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial|Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial]]** — this proposition is the fact that makes the *definition* of $f_\alpha$ legitimate. The minimal polynomial is defined as "the monic generator of $\ker\varphi$"; that such a generator exists is exactly what is proved here. Without this theorem the definition would name nothing.

- **[[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's Lemma]]** — the supplier of the content machinery. Step 3's descent uses that content is multiplicative, equivalently that a product of primitive polynomials is primitive — which is Gauss's lemma. More deeply, both results embody one philosophy: answer a $\mathbb{Z}[X]$-question in the friendlier $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, then control the passage back with content.

- **[[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|Principal Ideal Domain]]** — the property the proposition establishes *locally*. The result does not say $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a PID — it is not — but that this *particular* family of ideals (kernels of evaluation at algebraic integers) happens to be principal. It is a reminder that "ideal is principal" can hold for individual ideals of a non-PID ring.

- **[[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings]]** — used in Step 4 to convert $I = (f_\alpha)$ into the concrete isomorphism $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha) \cong \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$, from which irreducibility of $f_\alpha$ follows. The isomorphism is also the payoff that makes $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ computable as a quotient ring.

- **[[Thm - Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers|Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers]]** — an immediate consumer. That theorem argues, for $\alpha \in \mathbb{Q}$, that the linear polynomial $X - \alpha$ divides $f_\alpha$ — a statement that only makes sense because $f_\alpha$ exists and generates all relations, which is this proposition.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \cong \mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$ as a concrete ring *(from this topic)*
> With $I = (f_\alpha)$ proved, the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] presents the subring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \subseteq \mathbb{C}$ as the explicit quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$ — arithmetic in $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ becomes polynomial arithmetic reduced modulo the minimal polynomial.

> [!tip] Rings of integers of number fields *(from Algebraic Number Theory)*
> A well-defined minimal polynomial for each algebraic integer is the starting point for studying the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of a number field $K$ — its elements are algebraic integers, classified up to conjugacy by their minimal polynomials.
