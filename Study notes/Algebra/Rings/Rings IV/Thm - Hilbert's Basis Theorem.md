# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a [[Def - Ring|ring]] — commutative with $1$, by the standing convention. We write $I \trianglelefteq R$ for "$I$ is an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] of $R$". The [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] $R[X]$ consists of polynomials $f = a_0 + a_1 X + \cdots + a_d X^d$ with coefficients $a_i \in R$; the **degree** $\deg f$ is the largest $d$ with $a_d \neq 0$, and the corresponding $a_d$ is the **leading coefficient** of $f$. A ring is **[[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian]]** if it satisfies the **ascending chain condition (ACC)** — every chain $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ of ideals stabilises — equivalently, by [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals|the previous theorem]], if every ideal is **finitely generated**, $I = (r_1, \dots, r_n)$ for finitely many $r_i$. We write $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ for the polynomial ring in $n$ indeterminates, built up as $R[X_1][X_2]\cdots[X_n]$, and $R/I$ for the [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient ring]]. The symbol $\trianglelefteq$ means "is an ideal of". The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8]].

# Statement

> **Hilbert's Basis Theorem.** Let $R$ be a [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian]] ring. Then the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] $R[X]$ is also Noetherian.
>
> Equivalently, in the finite-generation language: if every [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] of $R$ is finitely generated, then every ideal of $R[X]$ is finitely generated.
>
> **Corollaries.** Iterating the theorem:
>
> 1. For any field $F$, the polynomial ring $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian; and $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian.
> 2. If $R$ is Noetherian and $I \trianglelefteq R$, the [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient ring]] $R/I$ is Noetherian.
> 3. Every finitely generated ring is Noetherian.

---

# Motivation

A polynomial ideal is, on the face of it, a frightening object. The ideal $(X^2 - Y, Y^3 - Z) \trianglelefteq F[X, Y, Z]$ is an infinite set of polynomials, and a priori there is no reason an arbitrary ideal of $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ should be describable by any finite amount of data. The single-variable case is reassuring — $F[X]$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]], hence a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], so every ideal of $F[X]$ is generated by *one* polynomial. But that argument is special to one variable: it runs on polynomial long division, and division breaks the moment you have two indeterminates. The ring $F[X, Y]$ is *not* a principal ideal domain — the ideal $(X, Y)$ already needs two generators — and there is no Euclidean function to fall back on. So the question is genuinely open: **can every ideal of a multivariate polynomial ring be described by finitely many generators, or are there [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] requiring infinitely many?**

Hilbert's Basis Theorem answers: finitely many always suffice. It does not say "principal" — $(X, Y)$ shows that is false — it says "finitely generated", which is exactly the [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian]] condition. And it delivers this not by a clever direct construction of generators, but by the most economical route imaginable: a *closure* statement. Adjoining one polynomial variable to a Noetherian ring keeps it Noetherian. Apply this once to $\mathbb{Z}$ or to a field $F$ — both trivially Noetherian — and you get $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ and $F[X]$ Noetherian; apply it again to get $\mathbb{Z}[X, Y]$ and $F[X, Y]$; iterate $n$ times and the entire ring $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian. The single closure step, used inductively, tames every polynomial ring in finitely many variables at once.

The significance is hard to overstate, and it is geometric. A system of polynomial equations $f_1 = f_2 = \cdots = 0$ — possibly an *infinite* system — cuts out a subset of affine space called an **affine variety**, the common zero locus. The polynomials that vanish on a given set form an ideal. Hilbert's Basis Theorem says that ideal is finitely generated: $(f_1, f_2, \dots) = (g_1, \dots, g_k)$ for some *finite* list. But two systems of equations with the same ideal of consequences have the same solution set. So **every affine variety, however it is presented, is the zero set of finitely many polynomial equations.** An infinite system is never genuinely needed; some finite subsystem already pins down the same geometry. This is the foundational finiteness theorem of algebraic geometry — without it, varieties would not be objects one could write down — and it is also why computational algebra is possible at all: Gröbner basis algorithms compute with the *finite* generating set the theorem guarantees to exist. When Hilbert first proved it (non-constructively, to the dismay of contemporaries who wanted explicit invariants), Gordan reportedly exclaimed "this is not mathematics, this is theology" — but the theorem stood, and it is now the cornerstone on which the whole subject rests.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition is "$R$ is Noetherian". The skill is recognising Noetherianity of the *coefficient* ring so that the theorem can be applied to the polynomial ring on top of it.

The first disguised source is **a field**. The property $B$ is "$R = F$ is a field". A field has only the two ideals $\{0\}$ and $F$, so the ascending chain condition holds for the most trivial reason, and $F$ is Noetherian. The theorem then gives $F[X]$ Noetherian, and — applied $n$ times — $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ Noetherian. The non-obvious part is purely that one *bothers* to invoke a theorem to handle multivariate polynomials, when the single-variable case looked elementary; the point is that the single-variable elementary argument (principal ideal domain) does *not* iterate, whereas this theorem does. *Example problem:* show every ideal of $\mathbb{C}[X, Y, Z]$ is finitely generated.

The second disguised source is **the ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}$**, or any principal ideal domain. The property $B$ is "$R$ is a principal ideal domain". Every ideal of a principal ideal domain is generated by one element, hence finitely generated, so $R$ is Noetherian. The theorem then makes $R[X]$ Noetherian — crucially, $R[X]$ is itself *not* generally a principal ideal domain (e.g. $\mathbb{Z}[X]$), so the theorem is supplying genuinely new information that the principal-ideal-domain machinery cannot. The non-obvious recognition is that "principal ideal domain" feeds in as a *source of Noetherianity*, even though the *conclusion* is the strictly weaker "finitely generated". *Example problem:* show $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, though not a principal ideal domain, has all ideals finitely generated.

The third disguised source is **a ring already obtained from a Noetherian ring by a Noetherian-preserving operation** — a quotient, a localisation, or a finite polynomial extension. The property $B$ is "$R$ is a quotient $S/I$ of a Noetherian ring $S$", or "$R = S[X_1, \dots, X_m]$ for $S$ Noetherian". By Corollary 2 (quotients) or the theorem itself (polynomial extensions), such an $R$ is Noetherian, and the theorem applies to $R[X]$ in turn. The non-obviousness is that Noetherianity, once established, *compounds*: each Noetherian-preserving construction can be stacked, and the theorem is the engine of the most important such construction. *Example problem:* show the coordinate ring $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]/I$ of any affine variety is Noetherian, then that polynomial rings over it are too.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$R[X]$ is Noetherian — every ideal of $R[X]$ is finitely generated".

Combine the conclusion with **iteration over several variables**. The output ring $R[X]$ is itself a Noetherian ring, so the theorem may be re-applied to it: $R[X][Y] = R[X, Y]$ is Noetherian, then $R[X, Y, Z]$, and so on. The further result $E$ is that $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian for *every* $n$ — the full multivariate finiteness theorem. The non-obvious leverage is that a one-variable closure statement, applied to its own output, climbs to arbitrarily many variables; this self-feeding is exactly what makes Corollary 1 free.

Combine the conclusion with **passage to a quotient**. By the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]], ideals of $R[X]/J$ correspond to ideals of $R[X]$ above $J$, and a finite generating set downstairs pushes forward to a finite generating set upstairs. The further result $E$ is that every quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian (Corollary 2), and hence — since a finitely generated ring is a quotient of some $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ — that **every finitely generated ring is Noetherian** (Corollary 3). The non-obvious point: the theorem about *polynomial extensions* delivers, via the correspondence, a theorem about *all* finitely generated rings, an enormously larger class.

Combine the conclusion with **the geometry of zero sets**. An affine variety is the common zero locus of a (possibly infinite) family of polynomials; the polynomials vanishing on it form an ideal of $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$, which by the theorem is finitely generated. The further result $E$ is that **every affine variety is cut out by finitely many equations** — the foundational finiteness statement of algebraic geometry. The non-obviousness is the translation: an *algebraic* finiteness ("ideals are finitely generated") becomes a *geometric* finiteness ("varieties are defined by finite systems"), because two equation systems with the same ideal have the same solution set.

# Why Is It True

The honest summary is: **$R[X]$ inherits the ascending chain condition from $R$ because a polynomial ideal is controlled by the ideals of *leading coefficients* it produces, and those live downstairs in $R$, where chains are already known to terminate.** The theorem is true because the obstruction to finitely generating a polynomial ideal can always be pushed down into the coefficient ring, and the coefficient ring has no such obstruction.

To see why one should *expect* this, think about how you would try to build an ideal $J \trianglelefteq R[X]$ out of a small number of polynomials, and where the difficulty lies. The greedy strategy is the obvious one: pick a polynomial $f_1 \in J$ of *minimal degree*; if $f_1$ does not already generate $J$, pick $f_2 \in J \setminus (f_1)$ of minimal degree among the leftovers; if $(f_1, f_2) \neq J$, pick $f_3$ likewise; and so on. If this process *stops*, $J$ is finitely generated and we are done. The whole theorem is the claim that the process *must* stop. Why might one fear it does not? Because $J$ might contain polynomials of higher and higher degree that genuinely cannot be reached from the ones already chosen. The question is what could let a polynomial $f$ "escape" the ideal generated by lower-degree polynomials.

Here is the key idea, and it is the heart of the proof. Suppose $f$ has the same degree as some polynomial $f'$ you already have, and the *same leading coefficient*. Then $f - f'$ has strictly smaller degree — the top terms cancel. So the *only* feature of a polynomial that can make it irreducibly new, beyond what lower-degree polynomials already give you, is its **leading coefficient**. If you could match the leading coefficient of any new polynomial using polynomials you already control, you could shave off its top term, reduce its degree, and recurse. Matching leading coefficients is therefore the entire game.

Now leading coefficients are *elements of $R$*, not of $R[X]$. And the leading coefficients of polynomials in $J$ of a given degree form an *ideal of $R$* — they are closed under the ring operations because $J$ is. As the greedy process picks $f_1, f_2, f_3, \dots$, their leading coefficients $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$ generate an ascending chain of ideals of $R$:
$$(a_1) \subseteq (a_1, a_2) \subseteq (a_1, a_2, a_3) \subseteq \cdots.$$
Because each new $f_i$ was chosen of minimal degree among the not-yet-captured polynomials, its degree is $\geq$ all the earlier degrees, and this is exactly the room you need to multiply the earlier polynomials by powers of $X$ to bring their leading terms up to degree $\deg f_i$. So a relation $a_{m+1} = \sum b_i a_i$ among leading coefficients in $R$ can be lifted to a relation among *polynomials* in $R[X]$: the combination $g = \sum b_i f_i X^{\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i}$ has the same degree and the same leading coefficient as $f_{m+1}$.

And now the punchline. The coefficient ring $R$ is Noetherian, so the chain $(a_1) \subseteq (a_1, a_2) \subseteq \cdots$ *cannot ascend forever* — it stabilises at some stage $m$, meaning $a_{m+1} \in (a_1, \dots, a_m)$. By the lifting just described, the next polynomial $f_{m+1}$ has its leading term matched by a $g \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$. Then $f_{m+1} - g$ has *strictly smaller degree* than $f_{m+1}$ — yet $f_{m+1} - g$ still lies in $J$ and, since $g \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$ while $f_{m+1} \notin (f_1, \dots, f_m)$, it still lies *outside* $(f_1, \dots, f_m)$. So we have produced an element of $J \setminus (f_1, \dots, f_m)$ of degree *strictly less* than $f_{m+1}$. But $f_{m+1}$ was chosen to have *minimal* degree among exactly such polynomials. Contradiction. The greedy process therefore could not have reached an $f_{m+1}$ — it had already terminated, and $J$ is finitely generated.

So the intuition is a two-storey one. *Upstairs*, in $R[X]$, the only thing that can make a polynomial new is its leading coefficient, and degree gives you the leverage to manipulate leading coefficients freely. *Downstairs*, in $R$, the leading coefficients form an ascending chain of ideals, and Noetherianity of $R$ forbids that chain from being infinite. The finiteness of $R$'s ideal structure is transmitted upward through the leading-coefficient bridge: $R[X]$ cannot have an infinitely complicated ideal because that would require an infinitely ascending chain of leading-coefficient ideals in $R$, and $R$ has none. One should expect the theorem to hold because adjoining a single variable adds only "degree", a $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$-graded — hence well-founded — bookkeeping layer, and a well-founded layer on top of a Noetherian ring cannot manufacture non-Noetherian behaviour.

# What Makes This Hard

The non-obvious construction is the polynomial $g = \sum_{i=1}^m b_i f_i X^{\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i}$ — the precise combination of the earlier generators, each scaled by a power of $X$, engineered to have the *same degree and leading coefficient* as $f_{m+1}$ so that $f_{m+1} - g$ drops in degree; conjuring this $g$ from the relation $a_{m+1} = \sum b_i a_i$ among leading coefficients is where most people stall. The structural insight that must come first is that **only the leading coefficient can make a polynomial irreducibly new**, so the whole problem reduces to the ideal generated by leading coefficients down in $R$ — and that the exponent $\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i$ is non-negative *exactly because* each $f_i$ was chosen of minimal degree, so $\deg f_{m+1} \geq \deg f_i$. The most common error is to track only the *degrees* of the chosen polynomials and forget that it is the *leading coefficients*, assembled into a chain of ideals of $R$, on which the Noetherian hypothesis of $R$ is actually spent.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Take an ideal $J \trianglelefteq R[X]$ and run a greedy process: repeatedly pick a polynomial in $J$ of minimal degree not yet in the ideal generated by those already chosen. The leading coefficients $a_1, a_2, \dots$ of the chosen polynomials generate an ascending chain of ideals of $R$; since $R$ is Noetherian this chain stabilises. At the stabilisation stage, the next leading coefficient is a combination of earlier ones, which lifts to a polynomial $g$ matching the top of the next chosen polynomial — producing a lower-degree element of $J$ outside the current ideal, contradicting minimality. So the process terminates and $J$ is finitely generated.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Set up the greedy choice of generators.** Build, if possible, a sequence $f_1, f_2, f_3, \dots$ in $J$ with $f_{i+1}$ of minimal degree in $J \setminus (f_1, \dots, f_i)$.
   - *Hint:* If at some stage $J = (f_1, \dots, f_i)$, then $J$ is finitely generated and you are done; so assume the process does not stop, and aim for a contradiction.
   - *Why needed:* It exposes the only candidate obstruction to finite generation, and produces the leading coefficients to analyse.

2. **Form the leading-coefficient chain in $R$ and stabilise it.** Let $a_i$ be the leading coefficient of $f_i$; show $(a_1) \subseteq (a_1, a_2) \subseteq \cdots$ stabilises at some $m$.
   - *Hint:* This is an ascending chain of ideals of $R$; apply the ascending chain condition, which holds because $R$ is Noetherian. Stabilisation means $a_{m+1} \in (a_1, \dots, a_m)$.
   - *Why needed:* This is the *only* place the hypothesis "$R$ is Noetherian" is used; it pins down the stage where the contradiction will be sprung.

3. **Lift the leading-coefficient relation to a polynomial $g$.** From $a_{m+1} = \sum_{i=1}^m b_i a_i$, build $g = \sum_{i=1}^m b_i f_i X^{\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i}$.
   - *Hint:* Check $g \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$, that $\deg g = \deg f_{m+1}$, and that $g$ has leading coefficient $a_{m+1}$ — so $g$ matches the entire top term of $f_{m+1}$. The exponents are $\geq 0$ since $\deg f_{m+1} \geq \deg f_i$ by minimal-degree choice.
   - *Why needed:* $g$ is the tool that shaves the leading term off $f_{m+1}$.

4. **Derive the contradiction.** Show $f_{m+1} - g \in J \setminus (f_1, \dots, f_m)$ has degree $< \deg f_{m+1}$.
   - *Hint:* $f_{m+1} - g$ has strictly smaller degree (top terms cancel); it lies in $J$; and it lies *outside* $(f_1, \dots, f_m)$ since $g$ is inside but $f_{m+1}$ is not. This contradicts the minimal-degree choice of $f_{m+1}$.
   - *Why needed:* The contradiction shows the greedy process cannot reach stage $m+1$, so it terminated earlier — meaning $J = (f_1, \dots, f_m)$ is finitely generated.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Matching the leading term lowers the degree
> **Statement:** Let $f, g \in R[X]$ be non-zero with $\deg f = \deg g$ and the same leading coefficient. Then $f - g = 0$ or $\deg(f - g) < \deg f$.
>
> **Hint:** Subtract; the top-degree terms cancel because both the degree and the leading coefficient agree.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the elementary fact that makes the leading coefficient the *only* obstruction: once you match it, the degree must drop. The polynomial $g$ in the main proof is built precisely to trigger this lemma against $f_{m+1}$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Write $d = \deg f = \deg g$ and let $c$ be the common leading coefficient. Then
> > $$f = c X^d + (\text{terms of degree} < d), \qquad g = c X^d + (\text{terms of degree} < d).$$
> > Subtracting, the $c X^d$ terms cancel exactly:
> > $$f - g = (\text{terms of degree} < d).$$
> > Hence $f - g$ is either the zero polynomial or has degree strictly less than $d = \deg f$. (No hypothesis on $R$ is needed beyond it being a ring; the cancellation is in the additive group of coefficients.)

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The polynomial $g$ matches the top term of $f_{m+1}$
> **Statement:** Suppose $f_1, \dots, f_{m+1} \in R[X]$ with leading coefficients $a_1, \dots, a_{m+1}$ and degrees satisfying $\deg f_{m+1} \geq \deg f_i$ for $i = 1, \dots, m$. If $a_{m+1} = \sum_{i=1}^m b_i a_i$ for some $b_i \in R$, then
> $$g := \sum_{i=1}^m b_i\, f_i\, X^{\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i}$$
> lies in $(f_1, \dots, f_m)$, and either $g = 0$ or $g$ has degree $\deg f_{m+1}$ and leading coefficient $a_{m+1}$.
>
> **Hint:** Each summand $b_i f_i X^{\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i}$ has degree exactly $\deg f_{m+1}$, with degree-$\deg f_{m+1}$ coefficient $b_i a_i$; sum the top coefficients.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the lifting step — it turns a relation among *leading coefficients in $R$* into a *polynomial in $(f_1, \dots, f_m)$* that has the same top term as $f_{m+1}$, ready for Lemma 1.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > First, $g \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$: each summand $b_i f_i X^{\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i}$ is an $R[X]$-multiple of $f_i$ (the exponent $\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i$ is a non-negative integer, precisely because $\deg f_{m+1} \geq \deg f_i$ by hypothesis), and $(f_1, \dots, f_m)$ is an ideal, closed under $R[X]$-multiples and sums.
> >
> > Next, the degree and leading coefficient. Each $f_i$ has degree $\deg f_i$ and leading coefficient $a_i$, so
> > $$f_i\, X^{\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i} = a_i X^{\deg f_{m+1}} + (\text{lower-degree terms}),$$
> > a polynomial of degree exactly $\deg f_{m+1}$ with coefficient $a_i$ in degree $\deg f_{m+1}$. Multiplying by the constant $b_i \in R$ and summing over $i = 1, \dots, m$, the coefficient of $X^{\deg f_{m+1}}$ in $g$ is
> > $$\sum_{i=1}^m b_i a_i = a_{m+1},$$
> > by the assumed relation, while every term of $g$ has degree $\leq \deg f_{m+1}$. So if $a_{m+1} \neq 0$, then $g$ has degree exactly $\deg f_{m+1}$ and leading coefficient $a_{m+1}$. (If the lower-degree terms conspire and $a_{m+1} = 0$ then $g$ could have smaller degree or be zero; but in the main proof $a_{m+1}$ is the leading coefficient of the non-zero polynomial $f_{m+1}$, hence $a_{m+1} \neq 0$, and this case does not arise.)

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a Noetherian ring. We show every ideal $J \trianglelefteq R[X]$ is finitely generated; by [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals|the equivalence of the ascending chain condition with finite generation]], this proves $R[X]$ is Noetherian.
>
> If $J = \{0\}$ it is generated by the empty set, so assume $J \neq \{0\}$.
>
> ---
> **The greedy construction.** We attempt to choose polynomials $f_1, f_2, f_3, \dots \in J$ as follows. Let $f_1 \in J$ be a polynomial of *minimal degree* (such exists: degrees lie in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, well-ordered). If $J = (f_1)$, then $J$ is finitely generated and we are done. Otherwise pick $f_2 \in J \setminus (f_1)$ of minimal degree among polynomials in $J \setminus (f_1)$. If $J = (f_1, f_2)$, we are done. Otherwise pick $f_3 \in J \setminus (f_1, f_2)$ of minimal degree, and so on. In general, if $J = (f_1, \dots, f_i)$ at some stage we are finished; so **suppose, for contradiction, that the process never stops**, producing an infinite sequence $f_1, f_2, f_3, \dots$ with
> $$f_{i+1} \in J \setminus (f_1, \dots, f_i), \qquad f_{i+1} \text{ of minimal degree in } J \setminus (f_1, \dots, f_i).$$
>
> Note that the degrees are non-decreasing: $\deg f_{i+1} \geq \deg f_i$. Indeed $f_i$ already had minimal degree in $J \setminus (f_1, \dots, f_{i-1})$, and $J \setminus (f_1, \dots, f_i) \subseteq J \setminus (f_1, \dots, f_{i-1})$, so the minimal degree over the smaller set is at least that over the larger. In particular $\deg f_{m+1} \geq \deg f_i$ for all $i \leq m$, for every $m$.
>
> ---
> **The leading-coefficient chain in $R$.** For each $i$, let $a_i \in R$ be the leading coefficient of $f_i$ (non-zero, as $f_i \neq 0$). Consider the ascending chain of ideals of $R$
> $$(a_1) \subseteq (a_1, a_2) \subseteq (a_1, a_2, a_3) \subseteq \cdots.$$
> Since $R$ is Noetherian, this chain satisfies the ascending chain condition, so it **stabilises**: there is an index $m$ with
> $$(a_1, \dots, a_m) = (a_1, \dots, a_m, a_{m+1}) = \cdots.$$
> In particular $a_{m+1} \in (a_1, \dots, a_m)$, so there exist $b_1, \dots, b_m \in R$ with
> $$a_{m+1} = \sum_{i=1}^m b_i\, a_i.$$
> *(This is the only step using the Noetherian hypothesis on $R$.)*
>
> ---
> **Lifting to a polynomial.** Define
> $$g = \sum_{i=1}^m b_i\, f_i\, X^{\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i}.$$
> Each exponent $\deg f_{m+1} - \deg f_i$ is a non-negative integer, because $\deg f_{m+1} \geq \deg f_i$ for $i \leq m$. By Lemma 2:
>
> - $g \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$, since each summand is an $R[X]$-multiple of some $f_i$;
> - $g$ has degree $\deg f_{m+1}$ and leading coefficient $a_{m+1}$ — the same degree and leading coefficient as $f_{m+1}$ (here $a_{m+1} \neq 0$, being the leading coefficient of the non-zero $f_{m+1}$).
>
> ---
> **The contradiction.** Since $f_{m+1}$ and $g$ have the same degree and the same leading coefficient, Lemma 1 gives
> $$f_{m+1} - g = 0 \quad\text{or}\quad \deg(f_{m+1} - g) < \deg f_{m+1}.$$
> Consider where $f_{m+1} - g$ lies. Both $f_{m+1} \in J$ and $g \in (f_1, \dots, f_m) \subseteq J$, so $f_{m+1} - g \in J$. Moreover $g \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$ while $f_{m+1} \notin (f_1, \dots, f_m)$, so
> $$f_{m+1} - g \notin (f_1, \dots, f_m)$$
> — were $f_{m+1} - g \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$, adding $g$ would force $f_{m+1} \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$, false. In particular $f_{m+1} - g \neq 0$ (the zero polynomial lies in every ideal), so by Lemma 1
> $$\deg(f_{m+1} - g) < \deg f_{m+1}.$$
> Thus $f_{m+1} - g$ is an element of $J \setminus (f_1, \dots, f_m)$ of degree *strictly smaller* than $\deg f_{m+1}$. But $f_{m+1}$ was chosen to have **minimal degree** among all polynomials in $J \setminus (f_1, \dots, f_m)$ — contradiction.
>
> ---
> **Conclusion.** The assumption that the greedy process never stops is untenable. Hence at some finite stage $J = (f_1, \dots, f_m)$, so $J$ is finitely generated. As $J$ was arbitrary, every ideal of $R[X]$ is finitely generated, and therefore $R[X]$ is Noetherian. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Corollary 1: $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ are Noetherian
> A field $F$ is Noetherian: its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and $F$, so every ascending chain of ideals stabilises immediately. The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian: it is a principal ideal domain, so every ideal is generated by one element, hence finitely generated. Now build up the polynomial ring one variable at a time, using the identification $S[X_1, \dots, X_k] = S[X_1, \dots, X_{k-1}][X_k]$:
> $$F \ \text{Noetherian} \ \xRightarrow{\text{HBT}}\ F[X_1] \ \text{Noetherian} \ \xRightarrow{\text{HBT}}\ F[X_1, X_2] \ \text{Noetherian} \ \xRightarrow{\text{HBT}}\ \cdots,$$
> and after $n$ applications of Hilbert's Basis Theorem, $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian. The identical induction starting from $\mathbb{Z}$ shows $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Corollary 2: A quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian
> Let $R$ be a Noetherian ring and $I \trianglelefteq R$ an ideal; we show $R/I$ is Noetherian. Let $J \trianglelefteq R/I$ be an arbitrary ideal of the quotient. By the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]], $J$ corresponds to an ideal $J' \trianglelefteq R$ with $I \subseteq J'$, related by $J = J'/I = \{r + I : r \in J'\}$. Now $J'$ is an ideal of the Noetherian ring $R$, hence finitely generated: $J' = (r_1, \dots, r_n)$ for some $r_1, \dots, r_n \in R$. Their images then generate $J$:
> $$J = J'/I = (r_1 + I, \ \dots, \ r_n + I) \quad\text{in } R/I,$$
> because every element of $J$ has the form $r + I$ with $r = \sum s_i r_i \in J'$, and reducing modulo $I$ gives $r + I = \sum (s_i + I)(r_i + I)$. So $J$ is finitely generated. As $J$ was arbitrary, every ideal of $R/I$ is finitely generated, and $R/I$ is Noetherian. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Corollary 3: Every finitely generated ring is Noetherian
> A **finitely generated ring** is a ring $R$ generated, as a ring, by finitely many elements $x_1, \dots, x_n$ — equivalently, $R$ is a quotient of a polynomial ring over $\mathbb{Z}$. Concretely, the evaluation map
> $$\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n] \longrightarrow R, \qquad X_i \longmapsto x_i,$$
> is a surjective ring homomorphism (its image is a [[Def - Subring|subring]] containing $1$ and all the $x_i$, hence all of $R$), so by the first isomorphism theorem
> $$R \cong \mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n] \,/\, I$$
> for $I$ the kernel. By Corollary 1, $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian; by Corollary 2, a quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian. Hence $R$ is Noetherian. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Every affine variety is cut out by finitely many equations.** Let $S$ be any set of polynomials in $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$, possibly infinite, and let $V$ be its common zero set in affine $n$-space — the variety it defines. The polynomials vanishing on $V$ form an ideal $I(V)$, and $S \subseteq I(V)$, so $V$ is also the zero set of the ideal $(S)$. By Hilbert's Basis Theorem $(S)$ is finitely generated, $(S) = (g_1, \dots, g_k)$, and $V$ is then exactly the zero set of the finite system $g_1 = \cdots = g_k = 0$. The application is foundational rather than exotic, but it is the canonical illustration: an *algebraic* finiteness theorem (ideals are finitely generated) becomes the *geometric* statement that no variety needs infinitely many defining equations.

**Termination of Buchberger's algorithm.** The Gröbner basis algorithm computes a finite, canonical generating set for a polynomial ideal by repeatedly forming new polynomials (S-polynomials) and adjoining those not reducible by the current set. This is a greedy ideal-enlargement of exactly the kind whose termination the ascending chain condition guarantees: the chain of ideals generated by the partial bases strictly ascends until it cannot, and Hilbert's Basis Theorem (via Noetherianity of $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$) forbids an infinite ascent. The application is nonobvious because the *halting* of a concrete computer algorithm is being underwritten by an abstract finiteness theorem about ideals.

**Noetherianity of coordinate [[Def - Ring|rings]] and the inductive structure of geometry.** The coordinate ring of an affine variety is $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]/I$ for some ideal $I$; by Corollary 1 the polynomial ring is Noetherian and by Corollary 2 the quotient is too. So every coordinate ring is Noetherian, and one may then form polynomial rings over it, take further quotients, and so on, staying Noetherian at every step. The application is nonobvious in that it shows the Noetherian property is the *stable* finiteness condition of algebraic geometry — preserved by exactly the operations (polynomial extension, quotient) used to build new varieties from old.

**[[Def - Group|Group]] rings and finitely generated algebras.** For a finite group $G$ and a Noetherian ring $R$, the group ring $R[G]$ is finitely generated as an $R$-algebra (by the finitely many group elements), and is a quotient of a polynomial ring over $R$; combining the theorem with the quotient corollary shows $R[G]$ is Noetherian. The application is out-of-distribution because group rings sit in representation theory rather than geometry, yet the same Hilbert-Basis-plus-quotient argument applies — any finitely generated algebra over a Noetherian ring inherits the Noetherian property.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals|Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]]** — the theorem this proof is phrased through. Hilbert's Basis Theorem proves "$J \trianglelefteq R[X]$ is finitely generated"; that equivalence is what licenses reading the conclusion as "$R[X]$ satisfies the ascending chain condition", i.e. "$R[X]$ is Noetherian". The hypothesis "$R$ Noetherian" is likewise unpacked, via the same equivalence, into the ascending-chain fact about the leading-coefficient ideals that the proof actually consumes.

- **[[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian Ring]]** — the theorem is the central *closure* property of the Noetherian class: it is closed under adjoining a polynomial variable. The non-example $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, X_2, \dots]$ from that definition shows the closure is one variable at a time only — adjoining infinitely many variables at once destroys Noetherianity, even though each finite stage is fine.

- **[[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|Ideal Correspondence]]** — the engine of Corollary 2. The correspondence between ideals of $R/I$ and ideals of $R$ above $I$ is what transports a finite generating set from $R$ up to the quotient $R/I$, yielding "quotients of Noetherian rings are Noetherian", and thence "every finitely generated ring is Noetherian".

- **[[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains|Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains]]** — the one-variable special case, by a different and stronger route. For a field $F$, $F[X]$ is Euclidean, hence a principal ideal domain, so every ideal is generated by *one* polynomial — far more than Hilbert's Basis Theorem claims. But that argument runs on polynomial long division and does *not* survive a second variable; Hilbert's Basis Theorem is the result that *does* iterate, at the cost of concluding only "finitely generated" rather than "principal".

- **The Hilbert Nullstellensatz** — the companion foundational theorem of algebraic geometry. Where the Basis Theorem says every variety is cut out by *finitely many* equations, the Nullstellensatz identifies *which* ideal a variety determines (its radical), completing the dictionary between ideals of $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ and geometric subsets of affine space. The two together make affine algebraic geometry a theory of finitely generated ideals.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Affine Varieties and the Zariski Topology *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Because $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian, every affine variety is the zero set of finitely many polynomials, and finite generation of ideals makes the Zariski-closed sets behave well — in particular every descending chain of closed subvarieties stabilises, so affine space is a Noetherian topological space and every variety has finitely many irreducible components.

> [!tip] Noetherian Schemes and Coherent Sheaves *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The Noetherian hypothesis on a scheme — locally the spectrum of a Noetherian ring — is the standing finiteness assumption of modern algebraic geometry. Hilbert's Basis Theorem is what guarantees the schemes built from polynomial rings (affine and projective space, and everything cut out inside them) are Noetherian, so that dimension theory and the theory of coherent sheaves apply.

> [!tip] Primary Decomposition and the Lasker–Noether Theorem *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> In a Noetherian ring every ideal is a finite intersection of primary ideals — the Lasker–Noether theorem, the ideal-theoretic generalisation of the prime factorisation of integers. Hilbert's Basis Theorem supplies the Noetherian polynomial rings to which this decomposition applies, making it the structural backbone of the theory of polynomial ideals.
