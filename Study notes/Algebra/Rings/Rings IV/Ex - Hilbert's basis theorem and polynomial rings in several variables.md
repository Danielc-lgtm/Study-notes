# Problem Statement

Let $F$ be a field and $n\ge 1$ an integer. Deduce from Hilbert's basis theorem that the polynomial rings
$$F[X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,\dots,X_n]$$
are **Noetherian**.

Conclude that every ideal of each of these rings is finitely generated — and interpret this: every system of polynomial equations over a field (or over $\mathbb{Z}$), no matter how many equations it contains, is equivalent to a *finite* subsystem.

**Recall:**

The objects are a multivariate polynomial ring over a field or over $\mathbb{Z}$, its ideals, and systems of polynomial equations.

![[Def - Noetherian Ring#The Definition]]

The two equivalent descriptions of this condition are linked by:

![[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals#Statement]]

The one input we are told to *use* is Hilbert's basis theorem:

![[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem#Statement]]

The [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring in n variables]] is defined **iteratively**: $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ means $\bigl(\cdots((R[X_1])[X_2])\cdots\bigr)[X_n]$ — adjoin one variable at a time. So $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]=(R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}])[X_n]$. This recursive structure is precisely the hook for an induction.

Two base facts: a field $F$ is Noetherian (its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and $F$, generated by $0$ and $1$ respectively — both finitely generated), and $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian (it is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], so every ideal is principal, hence finitely generated; see [[Ex - Every ideal of the integers is principal]]).

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *derive a corollary from a theorem* problem: a routine ⭐ exercise whose entire content is recognising that the multivariate statement is the single-variable theorem fed back into itself. As the [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] notes, when a theorem is stated for "one step" and the target object is built by "$n$ repetitions of that step," the proof is an induction with the theorem as the inductive step.

**Assumption pattern.** Hilbert's basis theorem is a *preservation* statement: it says the property "Noetherian" survives the operation "adjoin one indeterminate." The target rings are built by exactly that operation, iterated $n$ times, starting from a base ($F$ or $\mathbb{Z}$) that we can check is Noetherian by hand. The assumption pattern is "Noetherian base + the construction is a finite iteration of a Noether-preserving step."

**Theorem routing.** The base case routes through [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]]: a field has only two ideals (both finitely generated) and $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID (every ideal principal, hence finitely generated), so each is Noetherian. The inductive step is [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]] verbatim: $R$ Noetherian $\implies R[X]$ Noetherian. Induction on $n$ then carries the base case through all $n$ adjunctions. Finally, "Noetherian $\Rightarrow$ every ideal finitely generated" is the other direction of [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]], which delivers the stated conclusion and its interpretation.

**Key decision point.** There is essentially one judgement: see that "$n$ variables" is not a new theorem to prove but a *for-loop* over the one-variable theorem, and set up the induction on the *iterative definition* $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]=(R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}])[X_n]$. The interpretive payoff — translating "every ideal is finitely generated" into "every system of equations reduces to a finite subsystem" — requires noticing that the *solution set* of a family of polynomials depends only on the *ideal* the family generates.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Certify a base ring as Noetherian directly.** For a field, list its ideals ($\{0\}$ and the whole ring) and note both are finitely generated. For $\mathbb{Z}$, invoke "PID $\Rightarrow$ every ideal principal $\Rightarrow$ Noetherian." Either way, [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] converts "every ideal finitely generated" into "Noetherian."

2. **Adjoin an indeterminate by Hilbert's basis theorem.** [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]] is used as the inductive step: from "$R$ Noetherian" it produces "$R[X]$ Noetherian."

3. **Induct on the number of variables along the iterative definition of the polynomial ring.** Using $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]=(R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}])[X_n]$, an induction on $n$ applies operation 2 once per variable.

4. **Read off "every ideal is finitely generated" from "Noetherian."** The reverse direction of [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] turns the structural conclusion into the concrete statement about generating sets.

5. **Replace a family of polynomials by the ideal it generates.** The common zero set of a family of polynomials depends only on the ideal they generate; this is what lets "the ideal is finitely generated" be reinterpreted as "the system of equations has a finite equivalent subsystem."

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Hilbert's basis theorem is about adjoining **one** variable: $R$ Noetherian $\implies R[X]$ Noetherian. You are asked about $n$ variables. How is $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ built from $F$ — all at once, or one variable at a time?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is defined iteratively: $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]=(F[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}])[X_n]$. So this is an **induction on $n$**. What is the base case $n=0$ (or $n=1$), and is the base ring Noetherian?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Base case: a field $F$ has only the ideals $\{0\}$ and $F$, both finitely generated, so $F$ is Noetherian; $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID, so every ideal is principal, so $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian. Inductive step: assume $R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}]$ is Noetherian; apply [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]] to the ring $R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}]$ to conclude $(R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}])[X_n]=R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For the interpretation: a Noetherian ring has *every* ideal finitely generated. Given any (possibly infinite) family $\{f_\alpha\}$ of polynomials, the ideal $(f_\alpha)$ they generate is finitely generated, say by $g_1,\dots,g_m$. Why do the equations $\{f_\alpha=0\}$ and $\{g_1=0,\dots,g_m=0\}$ have the *same* solutions? Because each $g_j$ is a polynomial combination of the $f_\alpha$, and conversely.

# Solution

The strategy is a one-line induction: the base rings $F$ and $\mathbb{Z}$ are Noetherian, and Hilbert's basis theorem is exactly the inductive step "adjoin a variable, stay Noetherian," so iterating it $n$ times gives the multivariate result. Finitely generated ideals then translate into finite subsystems of equations.

**Step 1: The base rings $F$ and $\mathbb{Z}$ are Noetherian.**

A field $F$ is Noetherian, and the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *A field $F$.* The only ideals of a field are $\{0\}$ and $F$ itself. (If an ideal $I$ contains a non-zero element $a$, then since $a$ is a unit, $1=a^{-1}a\in I$, whence $I=F$.) Both ideals are finitely generated: $\{0\}=(0)$ and $F=(1)$. So every ideal of $F$ is finitely generated, and by [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]] — a ring is Noetherian if and only if every ideal is finitely generated — $F$ is Noetherian.
>
> *The ring $\mathbb{Z}$.* By [[Ex - Every ideal of the integers is principal]], every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $(n)$ for some $n\ge 0$; that is, $\mathbb{Z}$ is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]]. A principal ideal $(n)$ is generated by one element, hence finitely generated. So every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is finitely generated, and again by [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]], $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian. (The argument shows every PID is Noetherian; we only need $\mathbb{Z}$.)

**Step 2: Induction on $n$ — each polynomial ring $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian.**

If $R$ is a Noetherian ring, then $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian for every $n\ge 1$. Taking $R=F$ and $R=\mathbb{Z}$ gives the two claims.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Fix a Noetherian ring $R$ (we will use $R=F$ and $R=\mathbb{Z}$, both Noetherian by Step 1). Induct on the number of variables $n$.
>
> *Base case $n=1$.* The ring is $R[X_1]$. By [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]] — *if $R$ is Noetherian then $R[X]$ is Noetherian* — applied to the Noetherian ring $R$, the ring $R[X_1]$ is Noetherian.
>
> *Inductive step.* Suppose, as inductive hypothesis, that $R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}]$ is Noetherian for some $n\ge 2$. The polynomial ring in $n$ variables is, by definition, the result of adjoining one more indeterminate to the $(n-1)$-variable ring:
> $$R[X_1,\dots,X_n]=\bigl(R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}]\bigr)[X_n].$$
> Apply [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]] to the ring $R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}]$ — which is Noetherian by the inductive hypothesis. The theorem yields that $\bigl(R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}]\bigr)[X_n]=R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian.
>
> By induction, $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian for every $n\ge 1$. Specialising $R=F$ shows $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian; specialising $R=\mathbb{Z}$ shows $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian.

**Step 3: Every ideal of these rings is finitely generated.**

Because $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ are Noetherian, every ideal of either ring is finitely generated.

> [!note]- Derivation
> This is the reverse direction of [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]]: *a ring is Noetherian if and only if every ideal of it is finitely generated.* Step 2 established that $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ are Noetherian. Reading the equivalence right-to-left, every ideal of each of these rings is finitely generated: for any ideal $I$ there is a finite list $g_1,\dots,g_m$ of elements with $I=(g_1,\dots,g_m)$.

**Step 4: Interpretation — every system of polynomial equations has a finite equivalent subsystem.**

Any family of polynomial equations over $F$ (or over $\mathbb{Z}$), however large, is equivalent — has exactly the same set of common solutions — to a finite subsystem.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $\{f_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}$ be an arbitrary (possibly infinite) family of polynomials in $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$, and consider the system of equations
> $$f_\alpha(x_1,\dots,x_n)=0\qquad\text{for all }\alpha\in A.$$
> Let $I=(\{f_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A})$ be the ideal they generate. By Step 3, $I$ is finitely generated:
> $$I=(g_1,\dots,g_m)\qquad\text{for finitely many }g_1,\dots,g_m\in I.$$
> I claim the finite system $\{g_1=0,\dots,g_m=0\}$ has *exactly the same* solutions as the original infinite system. The key fact is that **the common zero set of a family of polynomials depends only on the ideal they generate**: if $h=\sum_k p_k\,h_k$ is a polynomial combination of polynomials $h_k$, then any point at which every $h_k$ vanishes is also a point at which $h$ vanishes.
>
> *Every solution of the original system solves the finite one.* Each $g_j$ lies in $I=(\{f_\alpha\})$, so $g_j$ is a finite polynomial combination $g_j=\sum_\alpha p_{j\alpha}f_\alpha$ of the $f_\alpha$. At any point where all $f_\alpha$ vanish, every such combination vanishes, so every $g_j$ vanishes.
>
> *Every solution of the finite system solves the original one.* Each $f_\alpha$ lies in $I=(g_1,\dots,g_m)$, so $f_\alpha=\sum_j q_{\alpha j}g_j$ is a polynomial combination of $g_1,\dots,g_m$. At any point where all $g_j$ vanish, every $f_\alpha$ vanishes.
>
> Hence the two systems have identical solution sets — the (possibly infinite) system $\{f_\alpha=0\}$ is *equivalent* to the finite system $\{g_1=0,\dots,g_m=0\}$. (The same argument runs verbatim over $\mathbb{Z}$.) In geometric language: every affine variety, defined a priori by infinitely many polynomial equations, is in fact cut out by finitely many. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** For a field $F$ and any $n\ge 1$, the rings $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ are Noetherian; consequently every ideal of either is finitely generated.
>
> *Base rings.* A field $F$ has only the ideals $(0)$ and $(1)$, both finitely generated, so $F$ is Noetherian by [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]]. The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID, so every ideal is principal, hence finitely generated, so $\mathbb{Z}$ is Noetherian.
>
> *Induction on $n$.* Let $R$ be Noetherian. For $n=1$: $R[X_1]$ is Noetherian by [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]]. For $n\ge 2$: assume $R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}]$ is Noetherian; since $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]=(R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}])[X_n]$, Hilbert's basis theorem applied to the Noetherian ring $R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}]$ gives that $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian. By induction $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian for all $n\ge 1$. Taking $R=F$ and $R=\mathbb{Z}$ proves both claims.
>
> *Finite generation.* By the reverse direction of [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]], every ideal of a Noetherian ring is finitely generated; so every ideal of $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ and of $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ has a finite generating set.
>
> *Interpretation.* Given a family $\{f_\alpha\}$ of polynomials, let $I=(\{f_\alpha\})$; it is finitely generated, $I=(g_1,\dots,g_m)$. Since each $g_j\in I$ is a polynomial combination of the $f_\alpha$ and each $f_\alpha\in I$ is a polynomial combination of $g_1,\dots,g_m$, the systems $\{f_\alpha=0\}$ and $\{g_1=0,\dots,g_m=0\}$ have identical common zero sets. Thus every system of polynomial equations is equivalent to a finite subsystem. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**A theorem about "one step" extends to "$n$ steps" by induction along the recursive definition of the object.** Hilbert's basis theorem is stated for a single adjoined variable, $R\rightsquigarrow R[X]$, yet the result one actually wants is about $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$. The bridge is the *iterative definition* $R[X_1,\dots,X_n]=(R[X_1,\dots,X_{n-1}])[X_n]$: the multivariate ring is literally the single-variable construction performed $n$ times, so $n$ applications of the one-variable theorem — packaged as an induction on $n$ — prove the multivariate corollary. This is a pervasive pattern: whenever an object is defined recursively ("build it one piece at a time") and you have a theorem for "adding one piece," the general statement is an induction with that theorem as its inductive step. The same move upgrades "the product of two Noetherian-related things behaves well" to finite products, "a single field extension has property $P$" to towers of extensions, and so on. Recognising "is this object built by iterating a step I have a theorem for?" turns many apparently new theorems into one-line corollaries.

**Finitely generated ideal $=$ finite equivalent subsystem: the algebra-geometry dictionary in miniature.** The statement "every ideal of $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is finitely generated" sounds like dry commutative algebra, but it has a vivid geometric meaning, and the translation rests on one fact worth isolating: *the common zero set of a family of polynomials depends only on the ideal that family generates*. Because a polynomial combination of vanishing polynomials vanishes, replacing a family $\{f_\alpha\}$ by any other generating set of the ideal $(\{f_\alpha\})$ — in particular a finite one — leaves the solution set untouched. So "Noetherian" becomes the geometric assertion that *every affine variety is cut out by finitely many equations*, even when presented by infinitely many. This is the first entry in the algebra-geometry dictionary (ideals $\leftrightarrow$ varieties) that the Nullstellensatz later makes precise, and it is the reason algebraic geometers may always assume their varieties are defined by finite systems.

**Hilbert's basis theorem is what makes computational algebraic geometry possible.** That every ideal of $F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is finitely generated is the *enabling hypothesis* for the entire theory of Gröbner bases and effective elimination. An infinite system of equations cannot be handed to an algorithm; a finite generating set can. Buchberger's algorithm, ideal-membership testing, and the solution of polynomial systems by computer all *begin* by relying on this corollary — without it there would be no finite data structure to compute with. The same finiteness underlies the proof that algebraic varieties have well-behaved decompositions into irreducible components (the ascending chain condition prevents infinite descent). When you prove "$F[X_1,\dots,X_n]$ is Noetherian," you are not proving a curiosity: you are certifying that the objects of polynomial algebra are finitely describable, which is the precondition for computing with them at all.

**Finitely many variables is essential — the corollary is sharp.** The induction in this exercise runs over a *finite* number of variables: each step adds one variable and stays Noetherian, and after $n$ steps you are done. It is tempting to imagine the conclusion persists "in the limit," but it does not: the infinite-variable ring $\mathbb{Z}[X_1,X_2,X_3,\dots]$ is **not** Noetherian — the ideal $(X_1,X_2,\dots)$ has no finite generating set (see [[Ex - A ring that is not Noetherian]]). An induction proves a statement for every finite $n$; it says nothing about an infinite union, and here the infinite union genuinely fails. This is the precise boundary of Hilbert's basis theorem, and keeping the positive corollary and the negative infinite-variable example side by side fixes in mind that "finitely generated algebra over a Noetherian ring" is a hypothesis whose word *finitely* cannot be dropped.
