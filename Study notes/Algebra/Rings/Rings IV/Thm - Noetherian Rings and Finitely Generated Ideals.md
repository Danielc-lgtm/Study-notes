# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a [[Def - Ring|ring]] — commutative with $1$, by the standing convention. We write $I \trianglelefteq R$ for "$I$ is an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] of $R$". An ideal $I$ is **finitely generated** if $I = (r_1, \dots, r_n)$ for finitely many $r_1, \dots, r_n \in R$, where $(r_1, \dots, r_n) = \{r_1 s_1 + \cdots + r_n s_n : s_i \in R\}$ is the set of $R$-linear combinations of the generators. A ring $R$ is **[[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian]]** if it satisfies the **ascending chain condition (ACC)**: every chain of ideals $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ stabilises, meaning $I_N = I_{N+1} = I_{N+2} = \cdots$ for some index $N$. The symbol $\subseteq$ allows equality; $\subsetneq$ denotes strict containment. The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8]].

---

# Statement

> **Noetherian [[Def - Ring|rings]] are exactly the rings with all [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] finitely generated.** Let $R$ be a [[Def - Ring|ring]]. Then $R$ satisfies the **ascending chain condition** on ideals — that is, $R$ is [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian]] — if and only if **every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated**.
>
> Explicitly: every ascending chain $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ of ideals stabilises if and only if for every ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$ there are finitely many elements $r_1, \dots, r_n \in R$ with $I = (r_1, \dots, r_n)$.

---

# Motivation

The [[Def - Noetherian Ring|definition]] of a Noetherian ring is given in terms of *chains*: every ascending chain of ideals stabilises. This is the form in which the condition is most easily *recognised in a proof* — whenever an argument enlarges an ideal step by step, the ascending chain condition is the guarantee that it terminates. But it is an awkward form in which to *verify* the condition or to *use* it constructively. To check the ascending chain condition directly you would have to reason about all possible infinite chains; to exploit it you have only the assertion "some chain stabilises", which is not yet a usable object.

This theorem supplies the second face of the condition, the one you actually compute with: **every ideal is finitely generated.** This is a statement about a *single ideal at a time*, and it hands you a concrete finite object — a list of generators $r_1, \dots, r_n$ — in place of an infinite set. A finitely generated ideal can be written down, manipulated, fed into algorithms. The whole computational theory of polynomial ideals (Gröbner bases, elimination, the solution of polynomial systems) lives on the finite-generation side, and the existence proofs of commutative algebra (primary decomposition, the structure of [[Def - Module|modules]]) feed on it too. Without this theorem the Noetherian condition would be a statement about chains and nothing more; with it, "Noetherian" *means* "ideals are finite lists of generators".

There is a deeper reason the equivalence is worth stating as a theorem rather than smuggling into the definition. The two formulations point in opposite directions and are each natural in their own context. The chain form is the *negative*, infinitary statement — "no infinite strict ascent" — and it is the form that makes the maximal-element principle and termination arguments transparent. The finite-generation form is the *positive*, finitary statement — "here are the generators" — and it is the form the [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert Basis Theorem]] is phrased to prove and to use. A working algebraist switches between the two constantly: prove Noetherianity by exhibiting finite generation, then *use* Noetherianity as the ascending chain condition. This theorem is the hinge that makes the switch legal, and recognising that the two are the same condition is itself the content.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The theorem is an equivalence, so it has two preconditions and either may be the recognised one; the skill is spotting whichever form is on offer.

The first disguised source is **a ring whose ideals have been explicitly described and each turns out to be a finite list of generators**. The property $B$ is "an explicit catalogue of all ideals shows each is generated by finitely many elements". The bridge is the finite-generation direction of this theorem, which converts that catalogue directly into the ascending chain condition. The non-obvious part: an ad hoc description of ideals — for instance "the ideals of $R$ are exactly $(0)$ and the $(X^n)$" — *is* a proof of Noetherianity, once routed through this equivalence. *Example problem:* show the formal power series ring $F[[X]]$ is Noetherian by exhibiting all its ideals.

The second disguised source is **a principal ideal domain**, or any ring in which every ideal is principal. The property $B$ is "every ideal is generated by a single element". A single generator is in particular *finitely* many generators, so $B$ instantly meets the finite-generation precondition, and the theorem delivers the ascending chain condition. The non-obviousness is mild but real: "principal" is a stronger, more familiar condition, and recognising that it is a *special case* of finite generation is what routes a principal ideal domain into this theorem. *Example problem:* deduce that $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[X]$ satisfy the ascending chain condition without arguing about chains at all.

The third disguised source is **a quotient or a finite ring presented without ideals listed**. The property $B$ is "$R$ has only finitely many ideals" (automatic for a finite ring, or for a ring with a small ideal lattice). Finitely many ideals means each is finitely generated trivially — take all its elements as generators if need be, or note a finite ideal lattice cannot support an infinite strict chain. The non-obvious recognition is that *cardinality* facts about the ideal lattice feed this theorem. *Example problem:* every finite ring, and every Boolean ring with finitely many idempotents, is Noetherian.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is the *equivalence itself*, so each direction is a target.

Combine the **finite-generation conclusion** with **a process that enlarges an ideal**. Once you know every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated, any construction of the form "build $J_1 \subseteq J_2 \subseteq \cdots$ by repeatedly adjoining elements" is guaranteed to halt, because the ascending chain condition holds. The further result $E$ is *termination of inductive constructions on ideals* — the existence half of [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|unique factorisation]], the finiteness of primary decomposition, the conclusion of the Hilbert Basis Theorem's own proof. The non-obvious leverage: a statement about *single ideals* (finite generation) controls the behaviour of *infinite sequences* of ideals.

Combine the **ascending chain conclusion** with **the search for a maximal element**. The ascending chain condition is equivalent to "every non-empty family of ideals has a maximal element". The further result $E$ is the legitimacy of the proof technique *"suppose not, and take a maximal counterexample"* — pervasive in commutative algebra. The non-obviousness is that an apparently infinitary hypothesis licenses a finite, choose-the-top-element move.

Combine the conclusion with **the Hilbert Basis Theorem**. The Hilbert Basis Theorem is *stated and proved* in the finite-generation language: it takes an ideal $J \trianglelefteq R[X]$ and shows it is finitely generated. This theorem is what lets the conclusion "$J$ is finitely generated" be read back as "$R[X]$ is Noetherian", and the input "$R$ is Noetherian" be read as "every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated", which is the form the proof consumes. The further result $E$ is the entire Noetherian-ness of polynomial rings. The non-obvious point: the Hilbert Basis Theorem could not even be *phrased* cleanly without this equivalence.

# Why Is It True

Both directions are believable once you see that **a chain of ideals and a finite generating set are two ways of packaging the same finite information**, and the union of a chain is the object that mediates between them.

*Why finite generation forces the ascending chain condition.* Suppose every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated, and you are handed an ascending chain $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$. The natural thing to do with an ascending chain is to take its union $I = \bigcup_n I_n$. Because the chain is *ascending*, this union is itself an ideal: any two of its elements already lie together in a single $I_\ell$ (take $\ell$ large enough to contain both), so the union inherits closure under subtraction and absorption. Now apply the hypothesis to this one ideal: $I$ is finitely generated, $I = (r_1, \dots, r_n)$. Here is the key observation — each generator $r_i$, being an element of the union, lives in *some* term $I_{k_i}$ of the chain. There are only finitely many generators, so there are only finitely many indices $k_1, \dots, k_n$, and one of them, $K = \max k_i$, is the largest. By stage $K$ all the generators have appeared: $r_1, \dots, r_n \in I_K$. But the generators generate all of $I$, so $I \subseteq I_K$; and $I_K \subseteq I$ always. Hence $I_K = I$, and from then on the chain is squeezed between $I_K$ and $I$, so it is constant. The intuition: a finitely generated ideal is "completed" by a finite amount of data, and finite data cannot be spread across infinitely many stages — it must all be present by some finite stage, and that stage is where the chain stops.

*Why the ascending chain condition forces finite generation.* This is the contrapositive that does the believing. Suppose some ideal $I$ were *not* finitely generated, and watch how the ascending chain condition is violated. Build a chain greedily. Pick any element $r_1 \in I$. The principal ideal $(r_1)$ is finitely generated — by one element — so it cannot be all of $I$, since $I$ is not finitely generated; therefore $I$ contains an element $r_2 \notin (r_1)$. Now $(r_1, r_2)$ is finitely generated, so again it is not all of $I$, and $I$ contains some $r_3 \notin (r_1, r_2)$. Continue forever: at each stage $(r_1, \dots, r_k)$ is finitely generated, hence a proper subset of $I$, hence misses some element $r_{k+1} \in I$. Each new element was chosen *outside* the previous ideal, so each inclusion is *strict*:
$$(r_1) \subsetneq (r_1, r_2) \subsetneq (r_1, r_2, r_3) \subsetneq \cdots.$$
This is an infinite strictly ascending chain of ideals, and it never stabilises — the ascending chain condition fails. So a ring satisfying the ascending chain condition cannot harbour a non-finitely-generated ideal. The intuition: "not finitely generated" *means* "you can always find one more element the current finite list misses", and "you can always find one more" is precisely the engine that builds a never-ending ascent.

Standing back, the two directions are mirror images. Finite generation says a finite list suffices; the ascending chain condition says you cannot strictly ascend forever. They are equivalent because *an infinite strict ascent is exactly what a non-finitely-generated ideal manufactures, and a finite generating set is exactly what halts an ascent.* The union of the chain is the shared pivot: in one direction you generate the union finitely and the generators force a stopping stage; in the other direction you fail to generate finitely and the failure feeds an unending chain.

# What Makes This Hard

The non-obvious move in the *finite-generation $\Rightarrow$ ACC* direction is to leave the chain entirely and pass to its *union*, then apply the hypothesis to that single ideal — beginners try to argue about the $I_n$ directly and get nowhere. The subtle point afterwards is the pigeonhole step: each of the *finitely many* generators sits in *some* $I_{k_i}$, so the *maximum* index $K = \max k_i$ catches them all at once — it is the finiteness of the generating set, not of anything else, that produces the stopping stage. In the converse direction the only real subtlety is recognising that "not finitely generated" is a *positive* tool: it guarantees that at every stage the current finite ideal $(r_1, \dots, r_k)$ misses an element, which is exactly what you need to keep the chain strictly increasing. The most common error is to forget to *verify that the union $\bigcup_n I_n$ is an ideal* — it is, but only because the chain is ascending, so any two elements lie in a common term; the same union over an *unnested* family of ideals is generally not an ideal.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Prove the two implications separately. Forward ("every ideal finitely generated" $\Rightarrow$ ACC): given a chain, take its union, generate it finitely, and use that the finitely many generators all appear by a maximal finite stage. Backward (ACC $\Rightarrow$ "every ideal finitely generated"): contrapositive — from a non-finitely-generated ideal, greedily adjoin elements to build an infinite strictly ascending chain.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **The union of an ascending chain of ideals is an ideal.** Show $I = \bigcup_n I_n$ is an ideal of $R$.
   - *Hint:* Any two elements $x, y \in I$ lie in $I_j, I_k$; since the chain ascends, both lie in $I_{\max(j,k)}$, so $x - y$ and $tx$ do too.
   - *Why needed:* It is the object the finite-generation hypothesis is applied to in the forward direction.

2. **Forward direction.** Assume every ideal is finitely generated; show an arbitrary chain stabilises.
   - *Hint:* Write $I = \bigcup_n I_n = (r_1, \dots, r_n)$; each $r_i \in I_{k_i}$; let $K = \max_i k_i$; then $r_1, \dots, r_n \in I_K$, so $I \subseteq I_K \subseteq I$, hence $I_K = I = I_{K+1} = \cdots$.
   - *Why needed:* It is one half of the equivalence.

3. **Backward direction.** Assume some ideal $I$ is not finitely generated; build an infinite strictly ascending chain.
   - *Hint:* Pick $r_1 \in I$; since $(r_1) \neq I$, pick $r_2 \in I \setminus (r_1)$; since $(r_1, r_2) \neq I$, pick $r_3 \in I \setminus (r_1, r_2)$; iterate to get $(r_1) \subsetneq (r_1, r_2) \subsetneq \cdots$, so the ACC fails.
   - *Why needed:* It is the contrapositive of the other half — ACC $\Rightarrow$ finite generation.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: The union of an ascending chain of ideals is an ideal
> **Statement:** Let $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ be an ascending chain of ideals of a ring $R$. Then $I = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} I_n$ is an ideal of $R$.
>
> **Hint:** Any two elements of the union already lie together in a single term of the chain, because the chain is ascending.
>
> **Why needed:** The forward direction of the theorem applies the finite-generation hypothesis to exactly this union; without knowing the union is an ideal there is nothing to apply the hypothesis to.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We check the two ideal axioms.
> >
> > *Closed under subtraction (additive [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]]).* Let $x, y \in I$. Then $x \in I_j$ and $y \in I_k$ for some indices $j, k$. Set $\ell = \max(j, k)$. Since the chain is ascending, $I_j \subseteq I_\ell$ and $I_k \subseteq I_\ell$, so both $x$ and $y$ lie in the single ideal $I_\ell$. As $I_\ell$ is an ideal it is closed under subtraction, so $x - y \in I_\ell \subseteq I$. Also $0 \in I_1 \subseteq I$. So $I$ is an additive subgroup of $R$.
> >
> > *Absorbs multiplication.* Let $x \in I$ and $t \in R$. Then $x \in I_j$ for some $j$; since $I_j$ is an ideal, $tx \in I_j \subseteq I$.
> >
> > Both axioms hold, so $I$ is an ideal. The hypothesis that the chain is *ascending* is essential and is used precisely once — in the subtraction step, to place two elements drawn from possibly different terms into one common term. For an unnested family of ideals the union is generally *not* an ideal.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: A finitely generated ideal equal to a union of a chain is reached at a finite stage
> **Statement:** Let $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ be an ascending chain of ideals with union $I = \bigcup_n I_n$, and suppose $I$ is finitely generated, $I = (r_1, \dots, r_n)$. Then there is an index $K$ with $I = I_K$.
>
> **Hint:** Each generator lies in some term of the chain; there are finitely many generators, so take the maximum of those indices.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the crux of the forward direction: it converts "$I$ finitely generated" into "the chain has already reached $I$ by stage $K$", which is stabilisation.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Each generator $r_i$ is an element of $I = \bigcup_n I_n$, so $r_i \in I_{k_i}$ for some index $k_i$. Since there are only finitely many generators $r_1, \dots, r_n$, the set $\{k_1, \dots, k_n\}$ of indices is finite; let
> > $$K = \max_{i = 1, \dots, n} k_i.$$
> > Because the chain is ascending, $I_{k_i} \subseteq I_K$ for every $i$, so *all* the generators lie in $I_K$: $r_1, \dots, r_n \in I_K$. Since $I_K$ is an ideal containing every generator of $I = (r_1, \dots, r_n)$, and $(r_1, \dots, r_n)$ is the *smallest* ideal containing the $r_i$, we get $I = (r_1, \dots, r_n) \subseteq I_K$. The reverse inclusion $I_K \subseteq I$ holds because $I$ is the union of the chain. Hence $I_K = I$. The finiteness of the generating set is what makes $\max_i k_i$ a genuine (finite) index; with infinitely many generators no such maximal stage need exist.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a ring. We prove the two implications of the equivalence separately.
>
> ---
> **($\Leftarrow$) If every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated, then $R$ satisfies the ascending chain condition.**
>
> Suppose every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated. Let
> $$I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$$
> be an arbitrary ascending chain of ideals; we must produce an index beyond which it is constant. Consider the union
> $$I = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} I_n.$$
> By Lemma 1, $I$ is an ideal of $R$ (this is where the chain being *ascending* is used: any two elements of the union lie in a common term). By hypothesis, $I$ is finitely generated, say
> $$I = (r_1, \dots, r_n), \qquad r_1, \dots, r_n \in R.$$
> By Lemma 2, since each generator $r_i$ lies in $I = \bigcup_n I_n$ it lies in some $I_{k_i}$; setting $K = \max_i k_i$, all generators lie in $I_K$, whence
> $$I = (r_1, \dots, r_n) \subseteq I_K \subseteq I,$$
> so $I_K = I$. Now for every $n \geq K$, the chain being ascending gives $I_K \subseteq I_n \subseteq I = I_K$, forcing $I_n = I_K$. Thus
> $$I_K = I_{K+1} = I_{K+2} = \cdots,$$
> and the chain stabilises. Since the chain was arbitrary, $R$ satisfies the ascending chain condition.
>
> ---
> **($\Rightarrow$) If $R$ satisfies the ascending chain condition, then every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated.**
>
> We prove the contrapositive: if some ideal of $R$ is *not* finitely generated, then $R$ fails the ascending chain condition.
>
> Suppose $I \trianglelefteq R$ is not finitely generated. We construct an infinite strictly ascending chain of ideals inside $I$.
>
> Since $I$ is not finitely generated it is in particular non-empty (and not the zero ideal, which is finitely generated by $0$); choose any element $r_1 \in I$. The principal ideal $(r_1)$ is finitely generated — by the single element $r_1$ — so $(r_1) \neq I$, because $I$ is not finitely generated. As $(r_1) \subseteq I$ and $(r_1) \neq I$, the containment is strict and we may choose
> $$r_2 \in I \setminus (r_1).$$
> Now $(r_1, r_2)$ is finitely generated, so again $(r_1, r_2) \neq I$, and since $(r_1, r_2) \subseteq I$ we may choose
> $$r_3 \in I \setminus (r_1, r_2).$$
> Proceeding inductively: having chosen $r_1, \dots, r_k \in I$, the ideal $(r_1, \dots, r_k)$ is finitely generated, hence $\neq I$, hence (being contained in $I$) a *proper* subset of $I$, so there exists
> $$r_{k+1} \in I \setminus (r_1, \dots, r_k).$$
> Because $r_{k+1}$ was chosen *outside* $(r_1, \dots, r_k)$, we have $(r_1, \dots, r_k) \neq (r_1, \dots, r_k, r_{k+1})$, and since clearly $(r_1, \dots, r_k) \subseteq (r_1, \dots, r_k, r_{k+1})$, the inclusion is strict. This produces an infinite chain
> $$(r_1) \subsetneq (r_1, r_2) \subsetneq (r_1, r_2, r_3) \subsetneq \cdots$$
> of ideals, *strictly* ascending at every step. Such a chain never stabilises — no term equals its successor — so $R$ does not satisfy the ascending chain condition.
>
> By contraposition, if $R$ satisfies the ascending chain condition then every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated.
>
> ---
> **Conclusion.** Both implications hold, so $R$ satisfies the ascending chain condition if and only if every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated. $\blacksquare$
>
> *(Remark: the construction in the $(\Rightarrow)$ direction makes infinitely many choices of the elements $r_{k+1}$, so it uses a weak form of the axiom of choice — specifically the axiom of dependent choice, since each choice depends on the previous ones.)*

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Power series rings via their ideal lattice.** The formal power series ring $F[[X]]$ over a field has a remarkably simple ideal lattice: its only ideals are $\{0\}$ and the principal ideals $(X^n)$ for $n \geq 0$. Each is generated by one element, hence finitely generated, so by this theorem $F[[X]]$ satisfies the ascending chain condition. The application is nonobvious because Noetherianity is *defined* by chains, yet here it is established with no chain ever mentioned — a complete catalogue of ideals, each visibly finitely generated, is fed into the equivalence.

**Linear-algebra flags as a sanity check.** Reading the theorem in the special ring-as-module setting: a finite-dimensional vector space has no infinite strictly ascending chain of [[Def - Subspace|subspaces]] (a flag), and indeed every subspace has a finite spanning set. This is the theorem's exact analogue with "ideal" replaced by "subspace", and it makes the abstract equivalence concrete — finite generation of subspaces is automatic from finite [[Def - Dimension|dimension]], and it is precisely what forbids infinite flags. The application is nonobvious in that it identifies a familiar finite-dimensionality fact as an instance of the Noetherian equivalence.

**Diagnosing non-Noetherian rings by exhibiting a bad ideal.** Run the theorem in contrapositive form: to prove a ring is *not* Noetherian, it suffices to display *one* ideal that is not finitely generated. For $R = \mathbb{Z}[X_1, X_2, \dots]$, the ideal $(X_1, X_2, \dots)$ does the job — any finite generating set involves only finitely many variables. The application is nonobvious because it reduces an infinitary statement ("some chain ascends forever") to a finitary one ("this single ideal has no finite generating set"), which is far easier to verify.

**Termination of greedy ideal-enlargement algorithms.** In computational algebra one often builds an ideal by repeatedly testing membership and, on failure, adjoining the missing element — exactly the chain $(r_1) \subsetneq (r_1, r_2) \subsetneq \cdots$ of the proof. This theorem guarantees that *in a Noetherian ring such a procedure terminates*, because an infinite strictly ascending chain is forbidden. The application is nonobvious because the *halting* of an algorithm — an operational, computational property — is being certified by the abstract finite-generation hypothesis on the ring.

# Bridges

- **[[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian Ring]]** — this theorem is the bridge between the two faces of the definition. The ascending chain condition is the *chain* form (infinitary, negative: no infinite strict ascent); finite generation is the *generator* form (finitary, positive: a finite list suffices). The theorem certifies they describe the same rings, and a third equivalent form — every non-empty family of ideals has a maximal element — is recorded in that definition's corollaries.

- **[[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's Basis Theorem]]** — the principal consumer of this equivalence. The Hilbert Basis Theorem is *stated and proved* entirely in the finite-generation language: it takes an ideal $J \trianglelefteq R[X]$ and shows $J$ has a finite generating set. This theorem is what permits the input "$R$ Noetherian" to be unpacked as "every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated" and the output "$J$ finitely generated" to be repackaged as "$R[X]$ is Noetherian".

- **[[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains]]** — the existence half of that theorem used the ascending chain condition of a principal ideal domain to terminate factorisation. A principal ideal domain has every ideal generated by *one* element — a fortiori finitely generated — so this theorem confirms it is Noetherian, and the principal ideal domain is the simplest Noetherian domain.

- **[[Def - Ideal|Ideal]]** — the lemma "the union of an ascending chain of ideals is an ideal" is the technical heart of the forward direction. It is a small but characteristic fact: the ideal axioms are preserved under *nested* unions because any two elements lie in a common term, although they are not preserved under arbitrary unions.

- **Noetherian and Artinian modules** — the same equivalence holds verbatim for modules: an $R$-module satisfies the ascending chain condition on submodules if and only if every submodule is finitely generated. The dual condition, the *descending* chain condition, defines Artinian rings and modules, where the analogous equivalence is more delicate.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The Hilbert Basis Theorem *(from this topic)*
> With this equivalence in hand, "Noetherian" can be freely read as "every ideal is finitely generated". This is exactly the language in which [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's Basis Theorem]] — if $R$ is Noetherian then so is $R[X]$ — is proved: one takes an ideal of $R[X]$ and exhibits finitely many generators.

> [!tip] Maximal Counterexamples and Existence Proofs *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> The chain form of the Noetherian condition is equivalent to "every non-empty family of ideals has a maximal element". This licenses the ubiquitous proof technique *"suppose the conclusion fails, and choose an ideal maximal among the counterexamples"* — used to prove primary decomposition, the existence of associated primes, and many structural theorems of commutative algebra.
