# Notation Registry

- $R, S$ — commutative rings with a multiplicative identity; in §2.5, $R$ is almost always a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]]
- $F$ — a field; in §2.5, almost always the [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] $\operatorname{Frac}(R)$ of the UFD $R$
- $R[X]$ — the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] in one indeterminate over $R$; $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ — the polynomial ring in $n$ indeterminates
- $f, g, h$ — polynomials, typically in $R[X]$ or $F[X]$; $a_i, b_i, c_i$ — their coefficients, elements of $R$ or $F$
- $\deg f$ — the degree of $f$, the largest $m$ with $a_m \neq 0$; $f$ is **monic** if that leading coefficient is $1$
- $c(f)$ — the **content** of $f \in R[X]$, the greatest common divisor $\gcd(a_0, \dots, a_n)$ of its coefficients, an element of $R$ defined up to a unit
- a polynomial is **primitive** when $c(f)$ is a unit, equivalently when its coefficients are coprime
- $u, v, w$ — units of whatever ring is in scope; $a \sim b$ — $a$ and $b$ are **associates**, meaning $a = ub$ for a unit $u$
- "irreducible" and "prime" — the two notions of [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|indecomposable element]]; in a UFD (and in any PID) they coincide for non-zero non-units
- $p$ — an irreducible element, used as the test prime in Eisenstein's criterion and as a prime number in §2.6
- $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ — the usual number systems, regarded as rings
- $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — the **Gaussian integers** $\{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, a subring of $\mathbb{C}$
- $N$ — the **norm** on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2 = z\bar z$, a multiplicative map $\mathbb{Z}[i] \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$
- $\bar z$ — complex conjugate of $z$; for $z = a + bi$, $\bar z = a - bi$, and $N(z) = z\bar z$
- $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ — the field with $p$ elements, for $p$ a prime number; $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ — its multiplicative group of non-zero elements
- $C_n$ — the cyclic group of order $n$
- $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ — the congruence class of an odd prime modulo $4$; the dividing line for Gaussian primality

# Motivation

The two previous topics built a hierarchy of "good" rings. [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings II]] proved the chain [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]] $\Rightarrow$ [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] $\Rightarrow$ [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]], each arrow a theorem, each class a different answer to "how much of $\mathbb{Z}$ does this ring keep?". But that left two large gaps. First, the theory was abstract: it told you a UFD *has* unique factorization but never told you how, in practice, to factor a concrete polynomial or to certify that one cannot be factored. Second, the supply of examples was thin — $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[X]$ for a field $F$, and not much else. This topic, §2.5–2.6, fills both gaps. Section 2.5 is the *practical factorization theory of polynomial rings*; section 2.6 is a *single worked example*, the Gaussian integers, carried far enough to prove a famous theorem of number theory.

Start with §2.5 and the question it answers: **how do you factor a polynomial, and how do you prove one is irreducible?** The naive obstacle is that factorization in $R[X]$ has a boring component you must dispose of first. In $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ the factorization $2X^2 + 2 = 2 \cdot (X^2 + 1)$ is real but uninteresting — it only pulled out the scalar $2$. The genuine factorization question is about the *shape* of the polynomial, not its scalar part, and the first job of §2.5 is to separate the two. The **content** $c(f)$ is the greatest common divisor of the coefficients; it is the scalar part. A **primitive** polynomial is one whose content is a unit — the scalar part has already been stripped away. Every polynomial splits as $f = c(f) \cdot f_1$ with $f_1$ primitive, so factoring $f$ means factoring the scalar $c(f)$ in $R$ (which $R$, being a UFD, already knows how to do) and factoring the primitive part $f_1$ (the interesting problem).

The interesting problem is then solved by one beautiful theorem, [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]]. A primitive polynomial lives in two rings at once: in $R[X]$, and in $F[X]$ where $F = \operatorname{Frac}(R)$ is the field of fractions. Factoring over the field $F$ ought to be *easier* — fields are friendlier than UFDs. Gauss's lemma says you lose nothing by working over the field: **a primitive polynomial that is reducible over $F$ is already reducible over $R$.** Factorization descends along the inclusion $R \hookrightarrow F$. This single fact does two enormous jobs. It powers the structural theorem **"$R$ a UFD $\Rightarrow R[X]$ a UFD"** — so $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, $\mathbb{Z}[X, Y]$, $\mathbb{Q}[X, Y, Z]$ are all UFDs, a vast new stock of examples, and crucially examples that are *not* principal ideal domains. And it underwrites the practical irreducibility test: to show $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, show it is irreducible over $\mathbb{Z}$, where the coefficients are integers and you can argue by divisibility. [[Thm - Eisenstein's Criterion|Eisenstein's criterion]] is exactly such a test — a one-line checklist on the coefficients of $f$ that, when it passes, certifies irreducibility outright.

Section 2.6 then changes register completely: from general theory to one concrete ring, the **Gaussian integers** $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. This is the smallest interesting ring that is not $\mathbb{Z}$, and it was already shown in [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings II]] to be a Euclidean domain — its Euclidean function is the **norm** $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2$ — and hence a PID and a UFD. So $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ has unique factorization into primes, and the obvious question is: *what are its primes?* An ordinary prime number $p$ may or may not survive as a prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: $2 = (1 + i)(1 - i)$ and $5 = (1 + 2i)(1 - 2i)$ factor and so are not prime, but $3$ and $7$ stay prime. The classification — primes congruent to $3 \pmod 4$ stay prime, primes congruent to $1 \pmod 4$ (and $2$) split — is the heart of §2.6. And its payoff is spectacular and unexpected: **Fermat's theorem that a prime is a sum of two squares exactly when it is $2$ or $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$**, an entirely arithmetic statement, falls out of the *ring theory* of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. The factorization of the integer $n$ as a sum of two squares is, in disguise, the factorization of the Gaussian integer $x + iy$ into Gaussian primes. This is the topic's deepest lesson: a hard question about $\mathbb{Z}$ becomes an easy question once you embed $\mathbb{Z}$ in the right larger ring and read off the prime factorization there.

# Concept Map

## §2.5 Factorization in Polynomial Rings

- **[[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial]]**
	- For a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|UFD]] $R$ and a polynomial $f = a_0 + a_1 X + \cdots + a_n X^n \in R[X]$, the **content** $c(f)$ is the greatest common divisor $\gcd(a_0, \dots, a_n)$ of its coefficients, an element of $R$ that — like every gcd in a UFD — is defined only up to multiplication by a unit. The polynomial $f$ is **primitive** when $c(f)$ is a unit, equivalently when its coefficients share no common irreducible factor. One cannot demand $c(f) = 1$ exactly, because the gcd is intrinsically ambiguous up to a unit, so "content is a unit" is the sharpest possible formulation of "the coefficients are coprime". Every $f$ factors as $f = c(f) f_1$ with $f_1$ primitive, which separates the boring scalar part $c(f)$ from the genuine polynomial part $f_1$.

- **[[Thm - Gauss's Lemma]]**
	- Let $R$ be a UFD with [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]] $F$, and let $f \in R[X]$ be **primitive**. Then $f$ is reducible in $R[X]$ if and only if $f$ is reducible in $F[X]$. The forward direction is easy — a factorization $f = gh$ in $R[X]$ with $g, h$ non-units forces $\deg g, \deg h > 0$ by primitivity, so it remains a non-trivial factorization over the field $F$; the reverse direction is the substantial one, and it rests on the lemma that the *product of two primitive polynomials is primitive*, proved by a prime-divisibility argument on the coefficients. The slogan is "factorization descends along the field of fractions": the easier ring $F[X]$ detects no reducibility that the harder ring $R[X]$ has not already exhibited. Primitivity is essential — $2X$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ but reducible as $2 \cdot X$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

- **[[Thm - Polynomial Rings over a UFD]]**
	- If $R$ is a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|UFD]], then the polynomial ring $R[X]$ is a UFD; by induction, $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is a UFD. The proof factors an arbitrary $f$ as $c(f) f_1$, factors the content $c(f)$ inside $R$ (possible because $R$ is a UFD), and factors the primitive part $f_1$ into primitive irreducibles by descending induction on degree; uniqueness is then imported from the known UFD $F[X]$ via [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]], which identifies $R[X]$-irreducibility of primitives with $F[X]$-irreducibility. This is the chapter's great example generator: $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, $\mathbb{Z}[X, Y]$, $\mathbb{Q}[X, Y]$ are all UFDs. Crucially they are UFDs that are **not** PIDs — $(2, X) \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not principal — so this theorem populates the gap between the PID and UFD classes.

- **[[Thm - Eisenstein's Criterion]]**
	- Let $R$ be a UFD and $f = a_0 + a_1 X + \cdots + a_n X^n \in R[X]$ primitive with $a_n \neq 0$. If there is an irreducible $p \in R$ with (i) $p \nmid a_n$, (ii) $p \mid a_i$ for all $0 \le i < n$, and (iii) $p^2 \nmid a_0$, then $f$ is irreducible in $R[X]$, and hence — by [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] — in $F[X]$. The proof supposes $f = gh$ and tracks divisibility by $p$ through the coefficients: condition (iii) forces $p$ to divide the constant term of exactly one factor, and conditions (i)–(ii) then force that factor to absorb the entire degree, leaving the other a unit. It is the practical irreducibility test of the chapter: $X^n - p$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$, and the $p$-th [[Def - Polynomial Ring|cyclotomic polynomial]] $1 + X + \cdots + X^{p-1}$ becomes Eisenstein after the substitution $X \mapsto X + 1$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Cyclotomic Polynomials and Field Extensions *(from Galois Theory)*
> [[Thm - Eisenstein's Criterion|Eisenstein's criterion]] applied to $1 + X + \cdots + X^{p-1}$ (after $X \mapsto X+1$) proves the $p$-th cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_p$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. Irreducibility is precisely what makes $\mathbb{Q}[X]/(\Phi_p)$ a field — the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ — of degree $p - 1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. This is the entry point to the Galois theory of cyclotomic extensions and the constructible-polygon theorems. See [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8]].

## §2.6 Gaussian Integers

- **[[Def - Gaussian Integers]]**
	- The **Gaussian integers** form the subring $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\} \leq \mathbb{C}$, equipped with the **norm** $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2 = z\bar z$, a multiplicative map ($N(zw) = N(z)N(w)$) into $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. As shown in [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings II]], $N$ serves as a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean function]], so $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a Euclidean domain, hence a PID, hence a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|UFD]]; in particular [[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|irreducible and prime]] elements coincide. The units are exactly the four elements of norm $1$, namely $\pm 1, \pm i$, since $N(u) = 1$ is forced for a unit and $N(u)N(u^{-1}) = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. The norm is the device that translates multiplicative questions in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ into multiplicative questions about ordinary integers.

- **[[Thm - The Unit Group of a Finite Field is Cyclic]]**
	- For a prime $p$, the multiplicative group $\mathbb{F}_p^\times = \mathbb{F}_p \setminus \{0\}$ of the field $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is cyclic of order $p - 1$, that is, $\mathbb{F}_p^\times \cong C_{p-1}$. The proof is a counting argument: were $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ non-cyclic, the classification of finite abelian groups would embed a subgroup $C_m \times C_m$ with $m > 1$, producing $m^2$ elements satisfying $x^m = 1$; but $X^m - 1$ has at most $m$ roots in the field $\mathbb{F}_p$, a contradiction. The proof is non-constructive — it exhibits no generator — yet the statement is exactly what §2.6 needs: a cyclic group of even order $4k$ has a unique element of order $2$ and elements of order $4$, which manufactures a square root of $-1$ modulo $p$ whenever $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

- **[[Thm - Classification of Gaussian Primes]]**
	- Up to associates, the primes of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ are of exactly two kinds: (i) the **inert** ordinary primes $p \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, which remain prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$; and (ii) the **split** Gaussian integers $z$ with $N(z) = z\bar z = p$ where $p = 2$ or $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. Equivalently, an ordinary prime $p$ stays prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ if and only if $p$ is *not* a sum of two non-zero squares. The proof combines the norm (a Gaussian prime divides its norm $z\bar z$, an ordinary integer) with the cyclic-group lemma (which supplies, for $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, an $a$ with $p \mid a^2 + 1 = (a+i)(a-i)$, forcing $p$ to split). This is the **prime-splitting law** of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: it says precisely how each rational prime decomposes upon entering the Gaussian integers.

- **[[Thm - Sum of Two Squares]]**
	- A non-negative integer $n$ is a sum of two squares, $n = x^2 + y^2$, if and only if every prime $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ occurring in the prime factorization of $n$ occurs to an *even* power. In the prime case this is Fermat's theorem: a prime is a sum of two squares exactly when it is $2$ or $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$. The proof reads $n = x^2 + y^2$ as the norm equation $n = N(x + iy)$ and factors $x + iy$ into [[Thm - Classification of Gaussian Primes|Gaussian primes]]: each inert prime contributes a factor $p^2$ to the norm (forcing an even exponent), each split prime contributes a single $p$. It is a purely arithmetic statement with a purely ring-theoretic proof, and the same factorization shows constructively *how* to write $n$ as a sum of squares, often in several ways.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Rings of Integers and Prime Splitting *(from Algebraic Number Theory)*
> $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is the ring of integers of the number field $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, and the [[Thm - Classification of Gaussian Primes|inert/split dichotomy]] is the first instance of the general splitting of primes in rings of integers — the central phenomenon studied by algebraic number theory, governed in general by Galois theory and reciprocity laws. The two-squares theorem is the prototype of how class-field-theoretic splitting laws encode classical Diophantine questions.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of this topic pursue a recognizable handful of goals. The dominant target in §2.5 is **proving a specific polynomial irreducible**, almost always over $\mathbb{Q}$ — and, once irreducibility is in hand, the immediate corollary that $\mathbb{Q}[X]/(f)$ is a field, since in $F[X]$ irreducibility is equivalent to the quotient being a field. A closely related target is **proving a number is irrational**: $\sqrt[n]{p}$ is irrational precisely because $X^n - p$ has no rational root, which Eisenstein delivers. A third §2.5 target is **certifying that a ring is a UFD** — typically a multivariable polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ — and, just as often, exhibiting a UFD that is *not* a PID, since the polynomial-ring theorem is the standard source of such examples. In §2.6 the targets shift to number theory: **classifying which integers are sums of two squares**, **deciding whether a given prime stays prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$**, **factoring a Gaussian integer into Gaussian primes**, and **counting or constructing the representations** $n = x^2 + y^2$. These targets recur because each pins down a different facet of factorization theory — whether a polynomial decomposes, whether a ring has unique decomposition, and exactly how the primes of one ring decompose inside a larger one.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The assumptions are equally stereotyped. **The ground ring is a UFD** is the master hypothesis of §2.5: it is what makes the content $c(f)$ well-defined (the gcd exists), what makes [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] and [[Thm - Eisenstein's Criterion|Eisenstein]] available, and what the polynomial-ring theorem both consumes and produces. **A polynomial has integer coefficients**, so that one may argue by divisibility — a luxury unavailable over $\mathbb{Q}$, where every non-zero element is a unit and "divides" carries no information; the recurring discipline is to *descend* a problem from $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ precisely to switch this luxury on. **A polynomial is primitive**, the precondition that licenses Gauss's lemma; when it is not, one first divides out the content. **A prime $p$ divides all but the leading coefficient**, the trigger pattern for Eisenstein. In §2.6 the sources are: **$\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a Euclidean domain**, hence a UFD, so unique factorization into Gaussian primes is available; **the norm is multiplicative**, which converts a factorization in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ into a factorization of ordinary integers; and **a congruence condition on a prime modulo $4$**, which — via the cyclic structure of $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ — controls whether $-1$ has a square root mod $p$ and hence whether $p$ splits. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: primitivity plus a UFD ground ring routes through Gauss's lemma to a $\mathbb{Q}$-irreducibility conclusion; an Eisenstein coefficient pattern routes directly to irreducibility; a congruence $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ routes through the cyclic-group lemma to a splitting of $p$ and thence to a sum-of-squares representation.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything here is self-contained: a reader with no prior factorization theory should be able to follow each operation from the description alone.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Split off the content.** Any $f \in R[X]$ over a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|UFD]] $R$ factors uniquely (up to units) as $f = c(f) \cdot f_1$ with $f_1$ [[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial|primitive]]. This is the first move in essentially every §2.5 problem: it isolates the "boring" scalar part $c(f)$, which lives in $R$ and is factored by $R$'s own UFD structure, from the "interesting" primitive part $f_1$, which is what the polynomial theorems are about. The trigger is any factorization or irreducibility question over $R[X]$; you cannot apply [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] until the polynomial in hand is primitive.

2. **Descend from $F[X]$ to $R[X]$ via Gauss's lemma.** To prove a primitive $f \in R[X]$ is irreducible over the field $F = \operatorname{Frac}(R)$ — the usual goal — instead prove it is irreducible over $R$, where the coefficients are honest UFD elements you can reason about by divisibility. [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] guarantees the two notions agree for primitive polynomials. The trigger is any "$f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$" target: the worked example $X^3 + X + 1$ shows the gain — over $\mathbb{Z}$ the equation $b_0 c_0 = 1$ pins $b_0$ to $\pm 1$, whereas over $\mathbb{Q}$ it says nothing.

3. **Apply Eisenstein's criterion to certify irreducibility.** When a single irreducible $p \in R$ divides every coefficient *except* the leading one, and $p^2$ misses the constant term, [[Thm - Eisenstein's Criterion|Eisenstein]] immediately certifies $f$ irreducible. This is the fastest irreducibility proof available — a checklist, not an argument. The trigger is seeing the coefficient pattern "leading coefficient coprime to $p$, all lower coefficients divisible by $p$, constant term not divisible by $p^2$"; the canonical instance is $X^n - p$, whose only non-leading non-zero coefficient is $-p$.

4. **Substitute $X \mapsto X + a$ to expose an Eisenstein pattern.** Irreducibility is invariant under the ring automorphism $X \mapsto X + a$ of $R[X]$, so if $f$ is not Eisenstein you may try translating the variable to make it so. The trigger is a polynomial whose coefficients have no common prime but which "ought" to be irreducible; the decisive example is the $p$-th [[Def - Polynomial Ring|cyclotomic polynomial]] $1 + X + \cdots + X^{p-1}$, which is not Eisenstein but becomes so under $X \mapsto X + 1$, the binomial coefficients $\binom{p}{i}$ supplying the required divisibility by $p$.

5. **Take norms to convert a Gaussian factorization into an integer factorization.** The norm $N : \mathbb{Z}[i] \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ is multiplicative, so a factorization $z = uv$ in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ yields $N(z) = N(u)N(v)$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ — a factorization of an ordinary integer, which $\mathbb{Z}$ knows how to analyze. This is the workhorse of §2.6. The trigger is any question about [[Def - Gaussian Integers|Gaussian]] divisibility, primality, or units: to show $3$ is a Gaussian prime, note $N(3) = 9$ forces any proper factor to have norm $3$, and $a^2 + b^2 = 3$ has no integer solution.

6. **Identify units and irreducibles by their norm.** In $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, an element is a unit exactly when its norm is $1$ (giving the four units $\pm 1, \pm i$), and an element whose norm is an ordinary prime is automatically irreducible — because any factorization would split that prime norm as $N(u)N(v)$, forcing one factor to have norm $1$. The trigger is needing to decide primality or unit-hood of a specific Gaussian integer: a prime norm is an instant irreducibility certificate, the cheapest one available.

7. **Read off square-root-of-$-1$ from the cyclic structure of $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$.** When $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, the group $\mathbb{F}_p^\times \cong C_{p-1}$ has order divisible by $4$, hence contains an element $a$ of order $4$; then $a^2$ has order $2$, so $a^2 = -1$ in $\mathbb{F}_p$, i.e. $p \mid a^2 + 1$. This is the step that makes $p$ *split* in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, since $a^2 + 1 = (a + i)(a - i)$. The trigger is a prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and a need to factor it in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ or to write it as a sum of two squares; [[Thm - The Unit Group of a Finite Field is Cyclic|cyclicity of $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$]] is what guarantees the element of order $4$ exists.

8. **Clear denominators to move between $R[X]$ and $F[X]$.** A polynomial $g \in F[X]$ over the field of fractions can be multiplied by a suitable $a \in R$ (the product of the denominators of its coefficients) so that $ag \in R[X]$. This is the technical glue inside the proof of Gauss's lemma and the polynomial-ring theorem: it lets a factorization discovered over $F$ be transported into $R[X]$, after which contents are extracted to recover primitive factors. The trigger is having a factorization over the field that you need to realize over the UFD.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Applying Gauss's lemma to a non-primitive polynomial
> [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] equates reducibility over $R[X]$ with reducibility over $F[X]$ — but *only for primitive polynomials*, and forgetting the hypothesis gives false conclusions. The polynomial $2X \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ (it is a non-zero scalar times the irreducible $X$, and scalars are units in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$), yet in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ it factors as $2 \cdot X$ with neither factor a unit, so it is *reducible*. The two notions genuinely disagree here — the content $c(2X) = 2$ is not a unit. The fix is mandatory and easy: always [[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial|split off the content]] first and apply Gauss's lemma to the primitive part $f_1$ alone.

> [!warning] 2. Arguing by divisibility over $\mathbb{Q}$ (or any field)
> When proving $X^3 + X + 1$ irreducible, it is tempting to factor it directly in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ and reason about the coefficients. But over a field every non-zero element is a unit, so a "divisibility" statement like $b_0 c_0 = 1$ constrains $b_0$ not at all — it could be any non-zero rational. Divisibility carries information only in a ring with non-trivial non-units, such as $\mathbb{Z}$. This is the entire reason [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] is valuable: it licenses you to *first descend* to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, where $b_0 c_0 = 1$ forces $b_0 = \pm 1$, and only then conclude. Work in $R[X]$ until the very end, then invoke Gauss's lemma.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming a UFD is a PID
> [[Thm - Polynomial Rings over a UFD|$R$ a UFD $\Rightarrow R[X]$ a UFD]], and after seeing $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[X]$ — which are PIDs — one is tempted to expect every UFD to be a PID. It is false, and $R[X]$ is the standard counterexample-factory. In $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, a UFD by the theorem, the ideal $(2, X)$ of polynomials with even constant term is *not principal*: no single polynomial generates it, since a generator would have to divide both $2$ and $X$ and hence be a unit. The implications run [[Def - Euclidean Domain|ED]] $\Rightarrow$ [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]] $\Rightarrow$ [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|UFD]] one way only; the polynomial-ring theorem is precisely how one populates the strict gap between PID and UFD.

> [!warning] 4. Expecting ordinary primes to remain prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$
> A prime number $p$ is a prime element of $\mathbb{Z}$, and the inclusion $\mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Z}[i]$ tempts one to assume $p$ stays prime in the larger ring. Often it does not: $2 = (1 + i)(1 - i)$ and $5 = (1 + 2i)(1 - 2i)$ factor into Gaussian non-units and so are *not* prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. Primality is relative to the ring — enlarging the ring supplies new potential factors. By the [[Thm - Classification of Gaussian Primes|classification]], exactly the primes $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ remain prime; those with $p = 2$ or $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ split. The same caution applies to irreducibility throughout ring theory: it is never an absolute property of an element.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this topic divide cleanly into the polynomial-factorization problems of §2.5 and the Gaussian-arithmetic problems of §2.6, and each half has a small, learnable repertoire of routes.

If the problem **asks you to prove a polynomial $f$ is irreducible**, almost always over $\mathbb{Q}$, the first reflex is to descend the question into $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ using [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] — but you may only do this once $f$ is [[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial|primitive]], so begin by checking the content and dividing it out if necessary. (A monic polynomial is automatically primitive, which covers most cases.) Once you are working over $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, try [[Thm - Eisenstein's Criterion|Eisenstein's criterion]] first, because it is a checklist rather than an argument: scan for a single prime $p$ dividing every coefficient except the leading one, with $p^2$ missing the constant term. If $f$ does not visibly satisfy Eisenstein, do not abandon the criterion — try the substitution $X \mapsto X + a$, which preserves irreducibility and can convert a non-Eisenstein polynomial into an Eisenstein one; the cyclotomic polynomial $1 + X + \cdots + X^{p-1}$ is the model, irreducible only after $X \mapsto X + 1$. If Eisenstein genuinely fails for all substitutions, fall back on the direct degree argument: suppose $f = gh$ in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ with both factors non-units (hence, by primitivity, of positive degree), enumerate the possible degree splittings, and equate coefficients — the integrality of the coefficients is what makes equations like $b_0 c_0 = 1$ informative, which is exactly the leverage Gauss's lemma bought you.

If the problem **asks you to certify that a ring is a UFD**, look for the ring to be a polynomial ring over a known UFD, and apply [[Thm - Polynomial Rings over a UFD|the polynomial-ring theorem]], iterating it for several indeterminates: $\mathbb{Z}[X, Y, Z]$ is a UFD because $\mathbb{Z}$ is, applied three times. If instead the problem asks for a UFD that is *not* a PID, the same theorem is the source — produce $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ and exhibit the non-principal ideal $(2, X)$. Recognizing that the ED $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD implications are strict, and that polynomial rings live in the strict part, is the key structural fact.

If the problem **concerns the Gaussian integers** — primality of an element, factorization, or units — the universal first move is to **take norms**, because the norm $N$ is multiplicative and converts the question into ordinary integer arithmetic. To decide whether a specific Gaussian integer $z$ is prime, compute $N(z)$: if $N(z)$ is an ordinary prime then $z$ is irreducible outright (any factorization would split a prime norm), and if $N(z)$ is composite, examine which norms its factors could have. To find the units, solve $N(u) = 1$, getting $\pm 1, \pm i$. To decide whether an ordinary prime $p$ stays prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, use the [[Thm - Classification of Gaussian Primes|classification]]: it stays prime exactly when $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, and splits when $p = 2$ or $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ — the underlying reason being that $p$ splits precisely when $-1$ is a square mod $p$, which [[Thm - The Unit Group of a Finite Field is Cyclic|cyclicity of $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$]] makes equivalent to $4 \mid p - 1$.

If the problem **asks whether an integer $n$ is a sum of two squares**, or asks to produce such a representation, route it through the [[Thm - Sum of Two Squares|two-squares theorem]]: $n = x^2 + y^2$ is solvable exactly when every prime $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$ in the factorization of $n$ appears to an even power. To actually construct the representation, factor each prime of $n$ in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — primes $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ and $2$ split as $p = \pi\bar\pi$, primes $\equiv 3 \pmod 4$ stay as $p$ contributing $p^2$ to the norm — multiply the Gaussian factors together into a single $z = x + iy$, and read off $n = N(z) = x^2 + y^2$. The freedom in choosing $\pi$ versus $\bar\pi$ at each split prime is exactly the source of *multiple* representations, as $65 = 1^2 + 8^2 = 7^2 + 4^2$ illustrates.

The meta-strategy uniting both halves is this: **factorization questions become tractable the moment you are in the right ring.** In §2.5 the right ring is the UFD $R$ rather than its field of fractions, because divisibility is informative only where non-units exist, and Gauss's lemma is the passport between the two. In §2.6 the right ring is $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ rather than $\mathbb{Z}$, because a sum of two squares is a *norm*, and a norm factors when the Gaussian integer factors. Identifying the ring in which your question has a clean factorization theory is the central decision in every problem here.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's Lemma]]**: a primitive $f \in R[X]$ is reducible over $R$ iff reducible over $F = \operatorname{Frac}(R)$. This is the chapter's master tool, and its reusability is that it lets you *choose the ring you reason in*. The recognizable setup is "prove $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$" — and the move is always to descend to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, where coefficients are integers and divisibility has teeth. It is the proof engine behind both the polynomial-ring UFD theorem and Eisenstein's criterion, so internalizing it pays compound interest. Recognize its applicability whenever a factorization question is posed over a field that happens to be the field of fractions of a friendlier UFD; the hidden precondition to check first is always primitivity.

- **[[Thm - Polynomial Rings over a UFD|The polynomial-ring UFD theorem]]**: $R$ a UFD $\Rightarrow R[X]$ a UFD. Its reusability is as an *example generator*. Almost every UFD that is not $\mathbb{Z}$ or a field is built by this theorem — $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ for a field $k$, and their subrings and quotients. The standard use is twofold: to certify unique factorization in a multivariable polynomial ring by iterating the theorem, and to manufacture a UFD that is *not* a PID (since $R[X]$ for $R$ a UFD-not-field is never a PID). Reach for it whenever you need a concrete UFD or a counterexample separating the UFD and PID classes.

- **[[Def - Gaussian Integers|The norm on $\mathbb{Z}[i]$]]**: $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2 = z\bar z$, multiplicative. The norm is the single most reused device in §2.6 because it is a *homomorphism from a hard multiplicative structure to an easy one*: it sends $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ to $\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ and turns factorizations into factorizations. Its three standard uses: a prime norm certifies irreducibility ($N(z)$ prime $\Rightarrow z$ irreducible); norm $1$ characterizes units; and $N(z) = z\bar z$ exhibits every Gaussian integer's norm as already factored. Whenever you face a multiplicative question in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — divisibility, primality, counting factorizations — apply the norm first and let ordinary integer arithmetic do the work.

- **[[Thm - The Unit Group of a Finite Field is Cyclic|Cyclicity of $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$]]**: $\mathbb{F}_p^\times \cong C_{p-1}$. Its reusability is as the bridge from a *congruence condition* to an *algebraic existence statement*. The pattern: a cyclic group of order $p - 1$ contains an element of order $d$ exactly when $d \mid p - 1$, so order conditions on group elements translate into divisibility conditions on $p - 1$. In §2.6 this manufactures a square root of $-1$ mod $p$ when $4 \mid p - 1$. More broadly it underlies the existence of primitive roots, the structure of quadratic residues, and the discrete logarithm — reach for it whenever the solvability of a polynomial congruence modulo $p$ is at stake.

- **[[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial|The content–primitive splitting]]**: every $f \in R[X]$ is $c(f) \cdot f_1$ with $f_1$ primitive. This is reused as the universal *normalization* step of polynomial factorization theory. Its role is to cleanly separate the two independent factorization problems hidden inside $f$: factoring the scalar $c(f)$ in $R$, and factoring the primitive part $f_1$ in $R[X]$. Because [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] and [[Thm - Eisenstein's Criterion|Eisenstein]] both demand primitivity, this splitting is the mandatory first line of nearly every §2.5 solution; recognizing it also explains why factorizations like $2X^2 + 2 = 2(X^2+1)$ are "boring" — they touch only the content.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic number theory — $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ as a ring of integers and the splitting of primes.** The Gaussian integers are the prototype of a *ring of integers*: $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ inside the number field $\mathbb{Q}(i)$, the analogue of $\mathbb{Z}$ for that field. The [[Thm - Classification of Gaussian Primes|inert/split classification]] is the first case of the general theory of how a rational prime $p$ decomposes in a ring of integers — it may stay prime (inert), break into distinct prime factors (split), or acquire a repeated factor (ramified; $2 = -i(1+i)^2$ is the ramified prime of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$). The governing principle, that the splitting type of $p$ is controlled by a congruence condition, is the seed of class field theory and the reciprocity laws; the two-squares theorem is the first reciprocity statement, with $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4 \Leftrightarrow -1$ a square mod $p$ being a special case of quadratic reciprocity. General rings of integers are *not* always UFDs — the failure is measured by the ideal class group — which is exactly why $\mathbb{Z}[i]$, being a Euclidean domain, is the clean entry point.

2. **Galois theory — cyclotomic polynomials and irreducibility.** [[Thm - Eisenstein's Criterion|Eisenstein's criterion]] supplies Galois theory's foundational examples. Applied (after $X \mapsto X + 1$) to the $p$-th cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_p = 1 + X + \cdots + X^{p-1}$, it proves $\Phi_p$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ — and irreducibility of $\Phi_p$ is precisely what makes $\mathbb{Q}[X]/(\Phi_p)$ a field, the cyclotomic field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$, of degree $p - 1$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. The whole Galois theory of cyclotomic extensions, including the constructibility of regular polygons, rests on this degree computation, which rests on Eisenstein. More generally, an irreducibility test is what converts a polynomial $f$ into a field extension $\mathbb{Q}[X]/(f)$ whose Galois group can then be studied; §2.5 is the supplier of those extensions, and the cyclic-group lemma of §2.6 reappears as the statement that the Galois group of $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)/\mathbb{Q}$ is $\mathbb{F}_p^\times \cong C_{p-1}$.

3. **Algebraic geometry — polynomial rings over a UFD as coordinate rings.** [[Thm - Polynomial Rings over a UFD|The theorem that $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is a UFD]] for a field $k$ is the algebraic backbone of geometry. The coordinate ring of affine $n$-space is $k[X_1, \dots, X_n]$, and unique factorization there means an irreducible polynomial generates a *prime* ideal — so an irreducible polynomial cuts out an irreducible hypersurface, and the decomposition of a polynomial into irreducible factors is the decomposition of its zero set into irreducible components. Eisenstein-style irreducibility tests therefore double as tests that a hypersurface is irreducible. The content–primitive splitting also has a geometric reading when $R = k[Y]$: a polynomial in $k[Y][X]$ is a family of polynomials in $X$ parameterized by $Y$, and the content tracks the locus in $Y$ where the family degenerates.

4. **Group theory and the classification of finite abelian groups.** The proof that [[Thm - The Unit Group of a Finite Field is Cyclic|$\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ is cyclic]] is a direct consumer of the classification of finite abelian groups: it argues that a non-cyclic $\mathbb{F}_p^\times$ would, by the classification, contain a subgroup $C_m \times C_m$, and then derives a contradiction from the polynomial $X^m - 1$ having too many roots in a field. This is a clean instance of an algebraic structure theorem (classification of abelian groups) combining with a counting fact about fields (a degree-$m$ polynomial has at most $m$ roots) to force a structural conclusion — and the same "too many roots" principle recurs throughout the theory of finite fields, which §2.6's cyclic-group lemma anticipates.

# Insights

**Gauss's lemma is the statement that factorization descends along the field of fractions.** The textbook phrasing — "a primitive polynomial reducible over $F$ is reducible over $R$" — is correct but undersells the idea. The right picture is a comparison of two factorization theories. The ring $F[X]$ is the *friendly* setting: $F$ is a field, so $F[X]$ is Euclidean, a PID, a UFD, and you can clear denominators and invert at will. The ring $R[X]$ is the *honest* setting: $R$ is only a UFD, divisibility is non-trivial, and the content is a genuine obstruction. A priori, asking for a factorization over the friendly ring $F[X]$ is a *weaker* demand than asking over $R[X]$ — more factorizations should exist when units are abundant. Gauss's lemma says that for primitive polynomials this expected gap *collapses*: the friendly ring detects no reducibility the honest ring has not already produced. Factorization "descends" from $F[X]$ down to $R[X]$ along the inclusion $R \hookrightarrow F$. This is why the lemma is the linchpin of the whole section. The UFD theorem needs it to import uniqueness from $F[X]$, where uniqueness is already known, down to $R[X]$. Eisenstein needs it to upgrade an irreducibility proof carried out over $\mathbb{Z}$ — where it is feasible — to a conclusion over $\mathbb{Q}$ — where it is wanted. Once you see Gauss's lemma as *descent*, the architecture of §2.5 becomes a single idea applied twice: do the hard reasoning in the honest ring, then descend the conclusion to the friendly one, or vice versa.

**The norm turns Gaussian arithmetic into ordinary integer arithmetic.** The Gaussian integers look intimidating — a two-dimensional lattice of complex numbers — but §2.6 is easy, and the reason is the norm $N(a + bi) = a^2 + b^2 = z\bar z$. The norm is a multiplicative map $\mathbb{Z}[i] \to \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$, and multiplicativity means it is a *homomorphism of multiplicative monoids from a hard ring to an easy one*. Every multiplicative question about $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ — is $z$ a unit, is $z$ prime, how does $z$ factor — is pushed through the norm into a question about ordinary integers, which you already understand. A unit must have norm $1$, and $a^2 + b^2 = 1$ has four solutions, so there are four units. An element of prime norm cannot factor non-trivially, because its norm cannot be split as a product of two integers both exceeding $1$, so prime norm is an instant irreducibility certificate. A prime number $p$ stays prime in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ exactly when $p$ is *not* a sum of two squares — because a Gaussian factorization $p = z\bar z$ is literally an equation $p = a^2 + b^2$. The norm is doing for $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ what the absolute value does for $\mathbb{Z}$ and what the degree does for $F[X]$: it is the *measure of size* that makes a Euclidean algorithm run and that converts structural questions into arithmetic ones. Whenever you meet a new ring of integers, the first thing to find is its norm.

**The two-squares theorem is the prime-splitting law of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ in disguise.** Fermat's theorem — a prime is a sum of two squares iff it is $2$ or $\equiv 1 \pmod 4$ — looks like a fact about $\mathbb{Z}$, provable by congruences and descent, and historically it was proved that way. The lesson of §2.6 is that it is *secretly a fact about $\mathbb{Z}[i]$*. The equation $n = x^2 + y^2$ is the equation $n = N(x + iy)$, so writing $n$ as a sum of two squares is *factoring $n$ as a norm*, which is *factoring the Gaussian integer $x + iy$*. The question "which $n$ are sums of two squares" is therefore the question "which $n$ are norms", and a multiplicative question about norms is answered by knowing the primes of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ and their norms — the [[Thm - Classification of Gaussian Primes|classification]]. An inert prime $p \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ contributes $N(p) = p^2$, an even power; a split prime $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$ contributes $N(\pi) = p$, a single power. So $n$ is a norm exactly when every inert prime appears to an even power — which is the two-squares theorem, now visibly a corollary of the splitting law. This is the deepest pattern in the topic and the one to carry forward: a Diophantine question about $\mathbb{Z}$ ("is $n$ a sum of two squares") becomes transparent once you embed $\mathbb{Z}$ in the larger ring $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ where the question is a factorization, and read the answer off the prime-splitting law of that ring. This is the prototype of the entire enterprise of algebraic number theory: study integers by passing to a larger ring of integers and understanding how primes decompose there.

**Irreducibility is never absolute — it is a property of an element *relative to a ring*.** This topic hammers a point first met in [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|Rings II]]: whether an element is irreducible depends on the ring you ask in, and enlarging the ring can destroy irreducibility by supplying new factors. The polynomial $X^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ but factors as $(X + i)(X - i)$ over $\mathbb{C}$. The prime number $5$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}$ but factors as $(1 + 2i)(1 - 2i)$ in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. Even the innocuous $2X$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ but reducible in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Two consequences are worth holding onto. First, every irreducibility claim must name its ring, and every irreducibility *proof* must control exactly which factorizations are possible in that ring — which is why [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] is so valuable, since it pins the ambiguity between $R[X]$ and $F[X]$ down to the single, checkable hypothesis of primitivity. Second, the *interesting* mathematical content often lies precisely in *how* irreducibility breaks when you enlarge the ring: the splitting of primes in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is not a defect of $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ but its richest feature, encoding the two-squares theorem. Watching which elements stay irreducible and which decompose, as you move up a tower of rings, is one of the most productive things you can do with a factorization problem.
