# Problem Statement

Prove that every ideal of the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers is **principal**: for each ideal $I\trianglelefteq\mathbb{Z}$ there is an integer $n\geq 0$ with
$$I = (n) = n\mathbb{Z} = \{\,\dots,-2n,\,-n,\,0,\,n,\,2n,\,\dots\,\}.$$
In particular every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is generated by a single element. (A ring with this property — an integral domain in which every ideal is principal — is called a *principal ideal domain*; this exercise shows $\mathbb{Z}$ is one.)

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the ring $\mathbb{Z}$, an arbitrary ideal of it, and the principal ideal generated by a single integer.

![[Def - Ideal#The Definition]]

Unpacking the definition for $R=\mathbb{Z}$: a subset $I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}$ is an ideal precisely when (i) $I$ is a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ — it contains $0$, and is closed under addition and under negation; and (ii) $I$ absorbs multiplication by *every* integer — if $a\in I$ and $b\in\mathbb{Z}$ then $ab\in I$. Property (ii) is the **strong-closure** axiom; it is what distinguishes an ideal from a mere subring.

For an integer $n$, the **principal ideal** generated by $n$ is
$$(n) = n\mathbb{Z} = \{\,n\cdot r : r\in\mathbb{Z}\,\},$$
the set of all integer multiples of $n$. It is the smallest ideal containing $n$.

Two standard facts about $\mathbb{Z}$ are used and should be stated explicitly. First, the **well-ordering principle**: every non-empty set of positive integers has a least element. Second, the **division algorithm**: for integers $m$ and $n>0$ there exist a quotient $q\in\mathbb{Z}$ and a remainder $r\in\mathbb{Z}$ with
$$m = q\,n + r,\qquad 0\leq r < n.$$

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *classify all ideals of a specific ring* problem — and simultaneously a *prove a structural property of a ring* problem (the property "every ideal is principal"). As the [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] notes, to pin down an unknown ideal $I$ you look for a *distinguished element* of $I$ — one canonically attached to $I$ itself — and then show $I$ is exactly what that element generates.

**Assumption pattern.** The only hypothesis is "$I$ is an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$." Two features of $\mathbb{Z}$ make this hypothesis powerful. The strong-closure axiom means $I$ contains *all multiples* of any of its members — so once we find a single good element, the inclusion $(n)\subseteq I$ is free. And $\mathbb{Z}$ is **well-ordered on the positives**: any non-empty set of positive integers has a smallest element. The assumption pattern is therefore "ideal + ambient ring is well-ordered," and that pairing is exactly what the proof exploits.

**Theorem routing.** There is no named theorem; the route is a two-step construction. Step one: produce the *least positive element* $n$ of $I$ — this requires well-ordering and a short argument that $I$ has positive elements at all. Step two: prove $I=(n)$ by *two inclusions*. The inclusion $(n)\subseteq I$ is immediate from strong closure. The inclusion $I\subseteq(n)$ is the substance, and it routes through the **division algorithm**: dividing any $m\in I$ by $n$ produces a remainder $r=m-qn$ that is *also in $I$* (by closure) yet satisfies $0\le r<n$ — and minimality of $n$ then forces $r=0$.

**Key decision point.** The crux is the choice of generator: take $n$ to be the *smallest positive element* of $I$. Nothing else works as cleanly. Having chosen it, the genuinely clever move is to feed the remainder $r=m-qn$ back into the ideal: $r$ is a *new* element of $I$, strictly smaller than $n$ and non-negative, so the only way to avoid contradicting "$n$ is least positive" is $r=0$. The proof is a minimal-counterexample/extremal argument: name the extremal object, then show any deviation manufactures something even more extremal.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Extract a multiple via strong closure** (the ideal-absorption operation). Given any $a\in I$ and any $b\in\mathbb{Z}$, the product $ab$ lies in $I$. This is used twice: to get $(n)\subseteq I$ once $n\in I$ is found, and to certify that $qn\in I$ when forming the remainder $r=m-qn$.

2. **Take a least element of a set of positive integers** (well-ordering). Applied to the set of positive members of $I$ to *name* the generator $n$. This is the operation that converts the abstract hypothesis "$I$ is an ideal" into a concrete, canonically-defined integer.

3. **Divide with remainder to reduce an element modulo a generator** (the Euclidean / division-algorithm operation). Applied to an arbitrary $m\in I$: write $m=qn+r$ with $0\le r<n$, so that $m$ is "$n$-aligned plus a small leftover."

4. **Close an ideal under subtraction** (additive-subgroup closure, part (i) of the ideal axioms). Used to conclude $r=m-qn\in I$, since both $m$ and $qn$ lie in $I$.

5. **Identify a set by proving two inclusions.** The conclusion $I=(n)$ is obtained as $(n)\subseteq I$ and $I\subseteq(n)$ separately — the standard move for proving two ideals (or two sets) equal.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Handle the trivial ideal $I=\{0\}$ separately: it is $(0)=0\mathbb{Z}$, done. So assume $I$ has a non-zero element. The whole game is now to find *one canonical element* of $I$ that generates everything. What is the most natural distinguished element a set of integers can have?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Among the elements of $I$, look at the *positive* ones. Convince yourself $I$ has positive elements at all (if $a\in I$ is non-zero, what about $-a$? — and one of $a,-a$ is positive). Now invoke the well-ordering principle: the non-empty set of positive elements of $I$ has a *least* element. Call it $n$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> One inclusion is free: since $n\in I$, strong closure gives $nr\in I$ for every $r\in\mathbb{Z}$, so $(n)\subseteq I$. For the reverse inclusion, take an arbitrary $m\in I$ and divide it by $n$: the division algorithm gives $m=qn+r$ with $0\le r<n$. You want to show $r=0$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Continuing Hint 3: rearrange to $r=m-qn$. Now $m\in I$, and $qn\in I$ (strong closure, since $n\in I$), so $r=m-qn\in I$ (closure under subtraction). But $0\le r<n$, and $n$ is the *smallest positive* element of $I$. A member of $I$ that is non-negative and strictly below $n$ cannot be positive — so $r=0$. Hence $m=qn\in(n)$.

# Solution

The strategy is to name the smallest positive element $n$ of a non-zero ideal $I$, and then show $I=(n)$ by two inclusions — the forward one free from strong closure, the reverse one from the division algorithm with minimality of $n$ killing the remainder.

**Step 1: Dispose of the zero ideal.**

If $I=\{0\}$ then $I=(0)=0\mathbb{Z}$, which is principal. So assume from now on that $I\neq\{0\}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The principal ideal generated by $0$ is $(0)=\{0\cdot r:r\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\{0\}$. So the ideal consisting of $0$ alone is principal with generator $n=0$. Every ideal contains $0$ (it is an additive subgroup), so the only remaining case is an ideal with a *non-zero* element, which we treat in the steps below.

**Step 2: $I$ has a smallest positive element $n$.**

The set of strictly positive elements of $I$ is non-empty, so by the well-ordering principle it has a least element; call it $n$. Thus $n\in I$, $n>0$, and no element of $I$ lies strictly between $0$ and $n$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Since $I\neq\{0\}$, pick a non-zero $a\in I$. The ideal $I$ is in particular an additive subgroup of $\mathbb{Z}$, so it is closed under negation: $-a\in I$. Exactly one of $a$ and $-a$ is positive, and whichever it is, it is a positive element of $I$. Hence the set
> $$P = \{\,x\in I : x>0\,\}$$
> is non-empty.
>
> $P$ is a non-empty set of positive integers, so the **well-ordering principle** applies: $P$ has a least element. Define $n:=\min P$. By construction $n\in I$, $n>0$, and — because $n$ is the minimum of $P$ — there is no element of $I$ strictly between $0$ and $n$ (such an element would be a positive element of $I$ smaller than $n$, contradicting minimality).

**Step 3: $(n)\subseteq I$ — the forward inclusion, free from strong closure.**

Every multiple of $n$ lies in $I$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> We have $n\in I$. The strong-closure axiom of an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] says: if $a\in I$ and $b\in\mathbb{Z}$, then $ab\in I$. Taking $a=n$ and $b=r$ for an arbitrary integer $r$ gives $nr\in I$.
>
> Therefore $\{nr:r\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subseteq I$. The left-hand set is exactly the principal ideal $(n)=n\mathbb{Z}$. Hence $(n)\subseteq I$.

**Step 4: $I\subseteq(n)$ — the reverse inclusion, via the division algorithm.**

Every element of $I$ is a multiple of $n$: dividing it by $n$ leaves remainder $0$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $m\in I$ be arbitrary. Apply the **division algorithm** to $m$ and $n$ (legitimate since $n>0$): there exist $q,r\in\mathbb{Z}$ with
> $$m = q\,n + r,\qquad 0\le r < n.$$
> We claim the remainder $r$ lies in $I$. Indeed, rearrange to $r = m - q\,n$. Now:
> - $m\in I$ by assumption;
> - $q\,n\in I$, because $n\in I$ and strong closure absorbs multiplication by the integer $q$;
> - hence $r = m - q\,n\in I$, because $I$ is an additive subgroup and so is closed under subtraction.
>
> So $r$ is an element of $I$ satisfying $0\le r<n$. If $r$ were strictly positive it would be a positive element of $I$ smaller than $n$, contradicting that $n$ is the *smallest* positive element of $I$ (Step 2). The only escape is $r=0$.
>
> With $r=0$ the division equation reads $m=q\,n$, so $m\in n\mathbb{Z}=(n)$. As $m\in I$ was arbitrary, $I\subseteq(n)$.

**Step 5: Conclude.**

Combining the two inclusions, $I=(n)$, so $I$ is principal.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Step 3 gives $(n)\subseteq I$ and Step 4 gives $I\subseteq(n)$. Two sets each contained in the other are equal:
> $$I = (n) = n\mathbb{Z}.$$
> Together with Step 1 (the case $I=\{0\}=(0)$), *every* ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ has the form $(n)$ for some integer $n\ge 0$. Hence every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is principal. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** Every ideal $I\trianglelefteq\mathbb{Z}$ is principal.
>
> If $I=\{0\}$, then $I=(0)$ and we are done. Assume $I\neq\{0\}$.
>
> *Naming the generator.* Choose non-zero $a\in I$; since $I$ is an additive subgroup, $-a\in I$, and one of $a,-a$ is positive, so the set $P=\{x\in I:x>0\}$ is non-empty. By the well-ordering principle $P$ has a least element $n=\min P$; thus $n\in I$ and $n>0$.
>
> *$(n)\subseteq I$.* For any $r\in\mathbb{Z}$, strong closure gives $nr\in I$. Hence $(n)=\{nr:r\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subseteq I$.
>
> *$I\subseteq(n)$.* Let $m\in I$. By the division algorithm there are $q,r\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $m=qn+r$ and $0\le r<n$. Then $qn\in I$ (strong closure, $n\in I$), so $r=m-qn\in I$ ($I$ closed under subtraction). Since $0\le r<n$ and $n$ is the least positive element of $I$, the element $r\in I$ cannot be positive; hence $r=0$ and $m=qn\in(n)$. As $m$ was arbitrary, $I\subseteq(n)$.
>
> Therefore $I=(n)=n\mathbb{Z}$. In all cases $I$ is principal. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**To classify the ideals of a ring, attach a canonical element to each ideal and show it generates.** Faced with "describe every ideal of $R$", the productive instinct is not to enumerate ideals but to find, *intrinsically inside each ideal $I$*, a distinguished element — one defined by a universal property of $I$ itself, so that it does not depend on any arbitrary choices. Here that element is "the smallest positive member of $I$." Once such an element $n$ exists, you immediately get $(n)\subseteq I$ for free from strong closure, and the only remaining work is the reverse inclusion. This template — *name the extremal element, then prove it generates* — is exactly how one shows $F[X]$ is a principal ideal domain (take a *non-zero polynomial of least degree* in the ideal) and, more generally, how one proves *every Euclidean domain is a PID*. The trigger is the phrase "every ideal of $R$ is..." or "$R$ is a principal ideal domain"; the reaction is "what canonical smallest element does an ideal of $R$ have?"

**The division algorithm is a remainder-killing machine, and minimality is the trigger that pulls it.** The reverse inclusion $I\subseteq(n)$ is not proved by manipulating $m$ directly; it is proved by *dividing* $m$ by $n$ and arguing the remainder away. The mechanism has three moving parts that always appear together: (i) division produces a remainder $r$ that is *strictly smaller* than the divisor $n$ in some size measure; (ii) closure properties of the ideal force $r$ *back into* the ideal; (iii) the *minimality* of $n$ then says $I$ has no non-zero element that small, so $r=0$. Remove any part and the argument collapses. This is the single most reusable proof pattern in elementary ring theory: it reappears verbatim with "degree" replacing "absolute value" to show ideals of $F[X]$ are principal (see [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials]]), and it is the engine behind computing kernels of evaluation homomorphisms (see [[Ex - Identifying a quotient ring with the first isomorphism theorem]]). The abstraction of "a ring carrying such a size-measure-with-division" is a *Euclidean domain*, and the theorem "every Euclidean domain is a PID" is just this exercise with the words swapped.

**Strong closure makes one good element worth an entire ideal.** The asymmetry between the two inclusions is instructive. Proving $(n)\subseteq I$ took one line, because the strong-closure axiom of an ideal is extravagantly generous: knowing a *single* element $n\in I$ instantly hands you every integer multiple $nr$. This is the defining power of an ideal over a mere subring — a subring only absorbs products of its *own* elements, whereas an ideal absorbs products with *anything* in the ring. The lesson for problem-solving: when you possess even one concrete element of an ideal, do not treat it as one element — treat it as the whole principal ideal it drags in. Conversely, the *hard* direction is always "is the ideal *no bigger* than what that element generates?", and that is where genuine ring-specific structure (a division algorithm, here) must be spent.

**Well-ordering is how an existence hypothesis becomes a constructible object.** The hypothesis "$I$ is a non-zero ideal" is purely existential — it asserts *some* non-zero element exists but names none. The well-ordering principle is the tool that upgrades this into a *specific, canonically-defined* integer: the least positive element. Many ring-theory and number-theory arguments hinge on this upgrade. The pattern "the hypothesis gives me a non-empty set of positive integers; well-ordering hands me its minimum; minimality is then exploited by a descent argument" recurs in proofs of the existence of greatest common divisors, the irrationality of $\sqrt 2$, and the fundamental theorem of arithmetic. Whenever a proof needs to *fix* an object that the hypotheses only guarantee abstractly, ask whether some associated set of positive integers can be minimised — and then plan to derive a contradiction from a smaller one.

**This exercise is the prototype; its failure elsewhere is equally instructive.** Showing $\mathbb{Z}$ is a principal ideal domain is the cleanest instance of the whole circle of ideas, but its real value is as a *baseline against which non-principal rings stand out*. The companion exercise [[Ex - The ideal (2, X) is not principal]] shows that $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — the integers with one indeterminate adjoined — has an ideal $(2,X)$ that is **not** principal. Comparing the two pinpoints exactly what $\mathbb{Z}$ has and $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ lacks: $\mathbb{Z}$ supports a division algorithm (it is Euclidean), so its ideals are forced to be principal by the argument above; $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ supports no such single-step division (you cannot divide $2$ by $X$ or vice versa), the minimality argument has no foothold, and an ideal needing *two* generators can survive. Keeping this exercise and its negative companion side by side is the way to internalise *why* the principal-ideal property holds when it holds.
