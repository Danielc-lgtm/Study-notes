# Problem Statement

Prove that
$$\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1) \;\cong\; \mathbb{C}$$
as [[Def - Ring|rings]]. That is, the quotient of the polynomial ring $\mathbb{R}[X]$ by the principal [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] generated by $X^2+1$ is isomorphic to the field of complex numbers.

You should do this **not** by hand-checking a candidate map, but by constructing the *evaluation homomorphism* $\mathbb{R}[X] \to \mathbb{C}$ that sends $X \mapsto i$, computing its kernel and image, and feeding the result into the first isomorphism theorem.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are a polynomial ring, a ring homomorphism out of it, the ideal that is its kernel, the quotient by that ideal, and the isomorphism theorem that ties them together.

![[Def - Polynomial Ring#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]] $\varphi : R \to S$ is a function that preserves all of the ring structure: it satisfies $\varphi(r_1+r_2)=\varphi(r_1)+\varphi(r_2)$, $\varphi(r_1 r_2)=\varphi(r_1)\varphi(r_2)$, $\varphi(0_R)=0_S$, and — crucially — $\varphi(1_R)=1_S$. Its **kernel** is $\ker(\varphi)=\{r\in R:\varphi(r)=0_S\}$ and its **image** is $\operatorname{im}(\varphi)=\{\varphi(r):r\in R\}$.

![[Def - Ideal#The Definition]]

![[Def - Quotient Ring#The Definition]]

![[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings#Statement]]

The single fact about $\mathbb{R}[X]$ that powers the whole computation is the **universal property of the polynomial ring**: a ring homomorphism out of $\mathbb{R}[X]$ is *completely free* in where it sends the indeterminate $X$. Concretely, for any ring $S$ containing $\mathbb{R}$ as a [[Def - Subring|subring]] and any chosen element $s\in S$, there is a unique ring homomorphism $\mathbb{R}[X]\to S$ that fixes every real constant and sends $X\mapsto s$ — it is the *evaluation-at-$s$* map $f(X)\mapsto f(s)$. Polynomials are exactly "formal expressions you are allowed to plug things into."

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is an *identify a quotient ring* problem: we are handed a quotient $R/I$ described abstractly (as [[Def - Coset|cosets]] of an ideal) and asked to recognise it as a familiar ring. As the [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|problem-solving strategy]] of the topic page records, the canonical tool for this class is the first isomorphism theorem, run *in reverse*: instead of starting from a homomorphism and discovering its quotient, we start from the quotient we want to understand and *manufacture* a homomorphism whose kernel is the given ideal.

**Assumption pattern.** The ideal is *principal and explicit*: $I=(X^2+1)$, generated by a single polynomial. An explicit generator is the signal to look for a homomorphism that kills exactly that generator. The generator $X^2+1$ has a transparent meaning — "the thing that becomes $0$ when $X^2=-1$" — and the ring $\mathbb{C}$ is precisely a ring containing $\mathbb{R}$ in which there is an element squaring to $-1$. The match between "generator forced to vanish" and "target ring where it does vanish" is the entire setup.

**Theorem routing.** The route is: build $\varphi:\mathbb{R}[X]\to\mathbb{C}$ with $X\mapsto i$ (legal by the universal property of [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial rings]]); show $\operatorname{im}(\varphi)=\mathbb{C}$; show $\ker(\varphi)=(X^2+1)$; then [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|the first isomorphism theorem]] delivers $\mathbb{R}[X]/\ker(\varphi)\cong\operatorname{im}(\varphi)$, i.e. $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)\cong\mathbb{C}$ for free — well-definedness, injectivity, surjectivity and the homomorphism property all come packaged.

**Key decision point.** Two non-obvious choices make the proof work. First, *which homomorphism to build*: the indeterminate must go to a root of the generator, so $X\mapsto i$ (a root of $t^2+1$) rather than anything else. Second, and harder, the *kernel computation*: it is immediate that $(X^2+1)\subseteq\ker\varphi$, but the reverse inclusion $\ker\varphi\subseteq(X^2+1)$ is the genuine content. This is where the [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|division algorithm for polynomials]] enters — it forces every kernel element to be a literal multiple of $X^2+1$.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Define a homomorphism out of a polynomial ring by choosing where $X$ goes** (the universal property). The move is to *specify* $\varphi:\mathbb{R}[X]\to\mathbb{C}$ by the single datum $X\mapsto i$ together with "fix $\mathbb{R}$"; the universal property guarantees this extends uniquely to a genuine ring homomorphism, namely $f\mapsto f(i)$. No homomorphism axioms need be verified by hand.

2. **Run the first isomorphism theorem in reverse to identify a quotient** (operation: *recognise $R/I$ via a surjection with kernel $I$*). Rather than analysing cosets directly, we exhibit a surjective homomorphism $\mathbb{R}[X]\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{C}$ and arrange its kernel to be $(X^2+1)$; [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|the theorem]] then converts "surjection with kernel $I$" into "$R/I\cong S$".

3. **Apply polynomial division to pin down a kernel** (operation: *use the [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]] to reduce modulo a generator*). To show $\ker\varphi\subseteq(X^2+1)$ we divide an arbitrary kernel element $f$ by $X^2+1$, getting a remainder of degree $<2$, and show that remainder is forced to be zero.

4. **Compute an image by checking surjectivity on a spanning set** (operation: *verify surjectivity by hitting generators of the target*). Since every complex number is $a+bi$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$, and $\varphi(a+bX)=a+bi$, the image already contains all of $\mathbb{C}$.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Do not try to verify a map $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)\to\mathbb{C}$ directly — that is the tedious by-hand route. Instead, work *upstream*: find a ring homomorphism $\varphi$ out of $\mathbb{R}[X]$ itself (not out of the quotient) whose kernel is exactly $(X^2+1)$. Then a single named theorem finishes the job.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> A homomorphism out of $\mathbb{R}[X]$ is determined by one free choice: where does $X$ go? You want the generator $X^2+1$ to land in the kernel, so $X$ must go to an element whose square is $-1$. In $\mathbb{C}$ there is an obvious such element. Send $X\mapsto i$ and fix every real number; this is evaluation at $i$, $f\mapsto f(i)$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Image: every complex number is $a+bi=\varphi(a+bX)$, so $\varphi$ is surjective. Kernel: clearly $X^2+1\in\ker\varphi$, so $(X^2+1)\subseteq\ker\varphi$. For the reverse inclusion, take $f\in\ker\varphi$ and divide $f$ by $X^2+1$ using the polynomial division algorithm: $f=q\,(X^2+1)+r$ with $\deg r<2$, so $r=b_0+b_1X$. Evaluate at $i$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Continuing Hint 3: from $f=q(X^2+1)+r$, evaluating at $i$ gives $0=\varphi(f)=q(i)\cdot 0 + r(i)=b_0+b_1 i$. Since $b_0,b_1$ are *real*, $b_0+b_1i=0$ forces $b_0=b_1=0$, so $r=0$ and $f=q(X^2+1)\in(X^2+1)$. Now $\ker\varphi=(X^2+1)$ and $\operatorname{im}\varphi=\mathbb{C}$; apply the first isomorphism theorem.

# Solution

The strategy is to manufacture a surjective ring homomorphism $\varphi:\mathbb{R}[X]\to\mathbb{C}$ whose kernel is exactly $(X^2+1)$, and then quote the first isomorphism theorem. Everything reduces to two computations: the image and the kernel.

**Step 1: Build the evaluation homomorphism $\varphi:\mathbb{R}[X]\to\mathbb{C}$, $X\mapsto i$.**

There is a unique ring homomorphism $\varphi:\mathbb{R}[X]\to\mathbb{C}$ that fixes every real number and sends $X$ to $i$. Explicitly it is evaluation at $i$:
$$\varphi\!\left(\sum_{k} a_k X^k\right) \;=\; \sum_k a_k\, i^k.$$

> [!note]- Derivation
> This is the universal property of the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] $\mathbb{R}[X]$. The ring $\mathbb{C}$ contains $\mathbb{R}$ as a subring, and $i\in\mathbb{C}$ is a chosen element. The universal property states: for any ring $S\supseteq\mathbb{R}$ and any $s\in S$, the rule $f(X)\mapsto f(s)$ — substitute $s$ for $X$ — is a ring homomorphism $\mathbb{R}[X]\to S$, and it is the *unique* homomorphism fixing $\mathbb{R}$ with $X\mapsto s$.
>
> Concretely, with $s=i$, define $\varphi(a_0+a_1X+\cdots+a_nX^n)=a_0+a_1 i+\cdots+a_n i^n$. One checks the [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]] axioms directly: it respects addition because evaluating a sum of polynomials at $i$ adds the evaluations coefficient-by-coefficient; it respects multiplication because the coefficients of a product polynomial are the [[Def - Convolution|convolution]] of the input coefficients, and evaluation at $i$ turns that convolution into the product of the evaluated values (this is just the statement that "substitute then multiply" equals "multiply then substitute"); it sends the zero polynomial to $0$ and the constant polynomial $1$ to $1\in\mathbb{C}$. So $\varphi$ is a genuine ring homomorphism, and we never have to revisit these axioms — they are guaranteed.

**Step 2: $\operatorname{im}(\varphi)=\mathbb{C}$ — the homomorphism is surjective.**

Every complex number lies in the image, so $\operatorname{im}(\varphi)=\mathbb{C}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> An arbitrary element of $\mathbb{C}$ has the form $a+bi$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$. Consider the degree-one polynomial $a+bX\in\mathbb{R}[X]$. Then
> $$\varphi(a+bX)=a+b\cdot i = a+bi.$$
> So every complex number is hit by $\varphi$, giving $\mathbb{C}\subseteq\operatorname{im}(\varphi)$. The reverse inclusion $\operatorname{im}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathbb{C}$ is automatic since $\varphi$ maps into $\mathbb{C}$ by construction. Hence $\operatorname{im}(\varphi)=\mathbb{C}$.
>
> Already the linear polynomials suffice — we do not even need higher-degree polynomials to reach all of $\mathbb{C}$, which is a foreshadowing of the kernel result: everything of degree $\geq 2$ is "redundant" modulo $X^2+1$.

**Step 3: $(X^2+1)\subseteq\ker(\varphi)$ — the easy inclusion.**

The generator $X^2+1$ lies in the kernel, and since the kernel is an ideal, the whole ideal it generates lies in the kernel.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Evaluate the generator: $\varphi(X^2+1)=i^2+1=-1+1=0$. So $X^2+1\in\ker(\varphi)$.
>
> By the lemma that the kernel of any ring homomorphism is an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] ($\ker(\varphi)\trianglelefteq\mathbb{R}[X]$), the kernel is closed under multiplication by *arbitrary* elements of $\mathbb{R}[X]$ — the strong-closure axiom of an ideal. Therefore for every $g\in\mathbb{R}[X]$ the product $g\cdot(X^2+1)$ lies in $\ker(\varphi)$. But $\{g\cdot(X^2+1):g\in\mathbb{R}[X]\}$ is by definition the principal ideal $(X^2+1)$. Hence $(X^2+1)\subseteq\ker(\varphi)$.

**Step 4: $\ker(\varphi)\subseteq(X^2+1)$ — the genuine inclusion, via polynomial division.**

Any $f\in\ker(\varphi)$, divided by $X^2+1$, leaves remainder $0$; hence $f$ is a multiple of $X^2+1$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $f\in\ker(\varphi)$, so $f(i)=0$. Apply the [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm for polynomials]] over the field $\mathbb{R}$: dividing $f$ by the polynomial $X^2+1$ produces $q,r\in\mathbb{R}[X]$ with
> $$f = q\cdot(X^2+1) + r,\qquad \deg r < \deg(X^2+1)=2.$$
> A polynomial of degree less than $2$ has the form $r=b_0+b_1 X$ with $b_0,b_1\in\mathbb{R}$.
>
> Now apply $\varphi$ — equivalently, evaluate at $i$:
> $$0 = \varphi(f) = \varphi(q)\cdot\varphi(X^2+1) + \varphi(r) = \varphi(q)\cdot 0 + (b_0 + b_1 i) = b_0 + b_1 i.$$
> So $b_0+b_1 i = 0$ in $\mathbb{C}$. The decisive point is that $b_0$ and $b_1$ are **real** numbers, so $b_0+b_1i=0$ is a statement about the real and imaginary parts: it forces $b_0=0$ and $b_1=0$. Hence $r=0$, and
> $$f = q\cdot(X^2+1) \in (X^2+1).$$
> As $f$ was an arbitrary kernel element, $\ker(\varphi)\subseteq(X^2+1)$.
>
> Combining with Step 3, $\ker(\varphi)=(X^2+1)$.

**Step 5: Apply the first isomorphism theorem.**

With $\operatorname{im}(\varphi)=\mathbb{C}$ and $\ker(\varphi)=(X^2+1)$, the first isomorphism theorem gives $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)\cong\mathbb{C}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem for rings]] states that for any ring homomorphism $\varphi:R\to S$, the kernel $\ker(\varphi)$ is an ideal of $R$ and there is a ring isomorphism
> $$R/\ker(\varphi)\;\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\;\operatorname{im}(\varphi),\qquad r+\ker(\varphi)\mapsto\varphi(r).$$
> Substituting $R=\mathbb{R}[X]$, $S=\mathbb{C}$, $\ker(\varphi)=(X^2+1)$ (Step 4) and $\operatorname{im}(\varphi)=\mathbb{C}$ (Step 2):
> $$\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)\;\cong\;\mathbb{C}.$$
> The isomorphism is the map $a+bX+(X^2+1)\mapsto a+bi$. The theorem hands us, in one stroke, that this map is well-defined (independent of coset representative), bijective, and multiplicative — exactly the four tedious verifications one would otherwise grind through by hand. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)\cong\mathbb{C}$.
>
> By the universal property of the polynomial ring $\mathbb{R}[X]$, there is a unique ring homomorphism
> $$\varphi:\mathbb{R}[X]\to\mathbb{C},\qquad \varphi\Big(\sum_k a_k X^k\Big)=\sum_k a_k i^k,$$
> namely evaluation at $i$, fixing $\mathbb{R}$ and sending $X\mapsto i$.
>
> *Surjective.* For $a+bi\in\mathbb{C}$ (with $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$), $\varphi(a+bX)=a+bi$. Hence $\operatorname{im}(\varphi)=\mathbb{C}$.
>
> *Kernel, $\supseteq$.* $\varphi(X^2+1)=i^2+1=0$, so $X^2+1\in\ker(\varphi)$. Since $\ker(\varphi)$ is an ideal, $(X^2+1)\subseteq\ker(\varphi)$.
>
> *Kernel, $\subseteq$.* Let $f\in\ker(\varphi)$. By the Euclidean algorithm over the field $\mathbb{R}$, write $f=q(X^2+1)+r$ with $\deg r<2$, so $r=b_0+b_1X$, $b_0,b_1\in\mathbb{R}$. Applying $\varphi$: $0=\varphi(f)=\varphi(q)\cdot 0+(b_0+b_1i)=b_0+b_1i$. As $b_0,b_1\in\mathbb{R}$, this forces $b_0=b_1=0$, so $r=0$ and $f=q(X^2+1)\in(X^2+1)$. Hence $\ker(\varphi)\subseteq(X^2+1)$.
>
> Therefore $\ker(\varphi)=(X^2+1)$.
>
> *Conclusion.* By the first isomorphism theorem for rings, $\mathbb{R}[X]/\ker(\varphi)\cong\operatorname{im}(\varphi)$, that is,
> $$\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)\;\cong\;\mathbb{C},$$
> via $a+bX+(X^2+1)\mapsto a+bi$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**To identify a quotient ring, run the first isomorphism theorem backwards: build the surjection, do not analyse the cosets.** The naive approach to "$R/I\cong S$" is to write down a candidate map on cosets and verify it is well-defined, bijective, additive and multiplicative — four separate checks, every one a chance to err, and the well-definedness check in particular is fiddly. The first isomorphism theorem exists precisely to abolish this labour. The reusable algorithm is: (1) *guess the target* $S$; (2) construct a homomorphism $\varphi:R\to S$ defined on $R$ itself, not on the quotient — this sidesteps well-definedness entirely, because there are no cosets to be consistent about yet; (3) show $\varphi$ is surjective, so $\operatorname{im}\varphi=S$; (4) show $\ker\varphi=I$; (5) quote the theorem. The trigger for this pattern is *any* problem phrased as "show $R/I$ is (isomorphic to) something". It recurs constantly: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}_n$ via the reduction-mod-$n$ map, $\mathbb{C}[X]/(X)\cong\mathbb{C}$ via evaluation at $0$, $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2-1)\cong\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ via $f\mapsto(f(1),f(-1))$, $k[X,Y]/(Y)\cong k[X]$ via $Y\mapsto 0$. In every case the work is *building the right $\varphi$ and computing its kernel*, never coset bookkeeping.

**A homomorphism out of a polynomial ring is a single free choice — send the indeterminate to a root of the generator.** Polynomial rings are the "free" objects of ring theory: $\mathbb{R}[X]$ is the universal ring containing $\mathbb{R}$ with one extra element subject to no relations. The operational consequence is that to specify a homomorphism $\mathbb{R}[X]\to S$ you choose *one thing*: the image of $X$. When the goal is to kill an ideal $(p(X))$, the image of $X$ must be a *root of $p$* in the target $S$ — because $\varphi(p(X))=p(\varphi(X))$, and we need this to be $0$. Here $p=X^2+1$, whose root in $\mathbb{C}$ is $i$, so $X\mapsto i$; if the problem were $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2-2)$ the choice would be $X\mapsto\sqrt 2\in\mathbb{R}$ giving image $\mathbb{R}$ (note the kernel is then *not* all of $(X^2-2)$ unless one is careful — $X^2-2$ has two real roots). The general principle: *adjoining a root* and *quotienting by the minimal polynomial of that root* are the same operation, and the universal property is the bridge. This is the seed of field extension theory — $\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)$ is literally "$\mathbb{R}$ with a root of $X^2+1$ adjoined."

**The kernel of a homomorphism is computed with two inclusions, and the division algorithm supplies the hard one.** Showing $\ker\varphi=(g)$ always splits into $(g)\subseteq\ker\varphi$ and $\ker\varphi\subseteq(g)$. The first is cheap and structural: check $\varphi(g)=0$, then invoke that the kernel is an ideal so it swallows the entire principal ideal $(g)$ — you never argue element-by-element for this direction. The second is the real theorem and needs a *reduction principle* that says "every element can be brought into a normal form modulo $g$, and only the zero normal form lies in the kernel." For polynomials over a field that reduction principle is the [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]]: dividing by $g$ leaves a remainder of degree $<\deg g$, a low-dimensional space of possible remainders, and the kernel condition then pins the remainder to $0$. This is exactly the same shape of argument as "every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is $n\mathbb{Z}$" (see [[Ex - Every ideal of the integers is principal]]), where integer division plays the role of polynomial division. The transferable lesson: whenever you must show "$\ker\subseteq(g)$" or "$I\subseteq(g)$", look for a division-with-remainder operation in the ambient ring — a *Euclidean structure* — and use it to force remainders to vanish. Rings carrying such a structure (Euclidean domains) are exactly the rings where this technique always works, and in them every ideal is principal.

**Reality-of-coefficients is doing silent but essential work.** The step "$b_0+b_1i=0$ with $b_0,b_1\in\mathbb{R}$ forces $b_0=b_1=0$" looks trivial, but it is the place where the *field $\mathbb{R}$* and the *degree-$2$ irreducibility of $X^2+1$ over $\mathbb{R}$* enter. The polynomial $X^2+1$ has no real root, which is why the remainder $b_0+b_1X$ — a polynomial that does have $i$ as a root only when it is zero — must vanish identically. Contrast $X^2-1=(X-1)(X+1)$, which factors over $\mathbb{R}$: there the analogous quotient $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2-1)$ is *not* a field and the evaluation-at-a-single-root map fails to have kernel $(X^2-1)$. The lesson for spaced practice: when a quotient $\mathbb{F}[X]/(p)$ turns out to be a field, suspect that $p$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{F}$, and conversely; the "force the remainder to zero" step is exactly where irreducibility is consumed.
