# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a [[Def - Ring|ring]] with additive identity $0$ and multiplicative identity $1$, assumed commutative unless stated otherwise. We write $I \trianglelefteq R$ for "$I$ is an **ideal** of $R$" — the same symbol $\trianglelefteq$ that group theory uses for a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]], and the choice is deliberate (see below). For $a \in R$ the ideal generated by $a$ is $(a) = aR = \{ar : r \in R\}$; for finitely many elements $a_1, \dots, a_k$ the ideal they generate is $(a_1, \dots, a_k)$; for an arbitrary subset $A \subseteq R$ it is $(A)$. The full symbol registry is on [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2]].

# Axiom Motivation

The right way to discover the notion of an ideal is to ask: by *what* substructure of a ring are we allowed to quotient? In group theory the answer is a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]], and the answer was forced by a single demand — that coset multiplication be well-defined, independent of the representatives chosen. We now want to run exactly the same argument for rings, and watch which condition it forces.

Take a [[Def - Ring|ring]] $R$ and a subset $I$. We want to build a quotient ring $R/I$ whose elements are the additive cosets $r + I$. For the *additive* structure we already know what to do: $(R, +, 0)$ is an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]], its quotient by $I$ is a group exactly when $I$ is a subgroup, and since the addition is commutative every subgroup is automatically normal — so the only requirement from the additive side is that $I$ be an additive subgroup. That is the first axiom, and it is the easy one.

The interesting demand comes from multiplication. We must define $(r_1 + I)(r_2 + I)$, and the only candidate formula is "multiply representatives, then take the coset":
$$(r_1 + I)(r_2 + I) := r_1 r_2 + I.$$
The danger is the usual one: a coset has many names, $r + I = r' + I$ whenever $r' - r \in I$, and the *output* must not depend on which name we picked. So re-name both factors: write $r_1' = r_1 + a_1$ and $r_2' = r_2 + a_2$ with $a_1, a_2 \in I$. Then
$$r_1' r_2' = (r_1 + a_1)(r_2 + a_2) = r_1 r_2 + r_1 a_2 + a_1 r_2 + a_1 a_2.$$
For the product coset to be unchanged we need $r_1' r_2' + I = r_1 r_2 + I$, that is, the three extra terms $r_1 a_2 + a_1 r_2 + a_1 a_2$ must lie in $I$. Now $a_1 a_2$ is a product of two elements of $I$ — a *subring* would already absorb that. But $r_1 a_2$ and $a_1 r_2$ are products of an element of $I$ with a *completely arbitrary* element of $R$. For these to land back in $I$ we need precisely:
$$a \in I,\ b \in R \quad \Longrightarrow \quad ab \in I.$$
This is the second axiom, and it is the whole point. The set $I$ must be closed under multiplication not merely by its own elements, but by *everything in the ring*. Once we have it, $r_1 a_2 \in I$ and $a_1 r_2 \in I$ and (taking $b = a_2 \in R$) also $a_1 a_2 \in I$, so all three terms vanish into $I$ and the multiplication is well-defined. An ideal is exactly a subset for which this argument goes through — nothing more, nothing less.

This is why the absorbing axiom is sometimes called **strong closure**. A [[Def - Subring|subring]] is closed under multiplication by its own elements; an ideal is closed under multiplication by *all* of $R$. The relationship is exactly that of subgroup to normal subgroup: a normal subgroup is closed not only under internal multiplication but under conjugation by *external* elements, and an ideal is closed not only under internal multiplication but under multiplication by *external* elements. In both cases the "external" closure is precisely the extra strength that makes a quotient exist.

What goes wrong if we *weaken* the absorbing axiom to mere subring closure — quotient by an arbitrary subring? Take $R = \mathbb{Q}$ and the subring $S = \mathbb{Z}$. The additive cosets $r + \mathbb{Z}$ form a perfectly good abelian group $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$, but multiplication breaks: $\tfrac{1}{2} + \mathbb{Z}$ and $0 + \mathbb{Z}$ are the same coset as $\tfrac{1}{2} + \mathbb{Z}$ and... try to compute $(\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z})(\tfrac{1}{2}+\mathbb{Z})$. We have $\tfrac{1}{2} + \mathbb{Z} = \tfrac{3}{2} + \mathbb{Z}$ since $1 \in \mathbb{Z}$, yet $\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac14$ while $\tfrac{3}{2}\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac34$, and $\tfrac14 + \mathbb{Z} \neq \tfrac34 + \mathbb{Z}$. The product depends on the representative; there is no quotient ring. The subring $\mathbb{Z}$ is not absorbing — $\tfrac12 \in \mathbb{Q}$ times $1 \in \mathbb{Z}$ gives $\tfrac12 \notin \mathbb{Z}$ — and that failure is exactly the failure of the quotient.

What goes wrong if we *strengthen* the axioms — for instance demand that $I$ also contain $1$? Then by strong closure $1 \cdot r = r \in I$ for every $r$, so $I = R$ and the only such "ideal" is the whole ring, whose quotient is the zero ring. We would lose every interesting quotient. So the two axioms are the Goldilocks pair: weaken the second and quotients break, strengthen by demanding $1 \in I$ and only the trivial quotient survives. An ideal is the exact, minimal, necessary-and-sufficient structure by which a ring can be divided.

# The Definition

Let $R$ be a [[Def - Ring|ring]]. A subset $I \subseteq R$ is an **ideal** of $R$, written $I \trianglelefteq R$, if both of the following hold:

1. **Additive closure.** $I$ is a subgroup of the additive group $(R, +, 0)$ — that is, $0 \in I$, and $I$ is closed under addition and under taking additive inverses.

2. **Strong (absorbing) closure.** If $a \in I$ and $b \in R$, then $ab \in I$.

(In a non-commutative ring axiom 2 as stated defines a *right* ideal; a *left* ideal absorbs multiplication on the left, and a *two-sided* ideal absorbs on both sides. In a commutative ring the three notions coincide, and "ideal" means two-sided ideal. It is the two-sided notion that quotients require.)

An ideal $I$ is a **proper ideal** if $I \neq R$.

**Ideal generated by an element.** For a fixed $a \in R$, the **ideal generated by $a$** is
$$(a) := aR = \{ar : r \in R\}.$$
This is the smallest ideal containing $a$.

**Ideal generated by finitely many elements.** For $a_1, \dots, a_k \in R$, the **ideal generated by $a_1, \dots, a_k$** is
$$(a_1, \dots, a_k) := \{a_1 r_1 + \cdots + a_k r_k : r_1, \dots, r_k \in R\},$$
the set of all $R$-linear combinations of the generators. It is the smallest ideal containing all of $a_1, \dots, a_k$.

**Ideal generated by a subset.** For an arbitrary subset $A \subseteq R$, the **ideal generated by $A$** is
$$(A) := \left\{ \sum_{a \in A} r_a\, a \ :\ r_a \in R,\ \text{only finitely many } r_a \text{ nonzero} \right\}.$$
The finiteness restriction is essential — a ring has no notion of an infinite sum, so every element of $(A)$ must be a *finite* $R$-linear combination of elements of $A$.

**Principal ideal.** An ideal $I$ is a **principal ideal** if $I = (a)$ for some single element $a \in R$ — that is, if it can be generated by one element.

# Categorical Definition

An ideal has a clean categorical description as **the kernel of a ring homomorphism**, and this single statement encodes both the additive-closure and the strong-closure axioms.

Spell it out. A *kernel* in a category with a *zero object* (an object that is both initial and terminal, like the zero ring $\{0\}$ in $\mathbf{Ring}$ ... almost — see the subtlety below) is the universal sub-object collapsed to zero by a given morphism. Concretely, for a [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]] $\varphi : R \to S$, the kernel $\ker\varphi = \{r \in R : \varphi(r) = 0_S\}$ is the universal sub-object of $R$ that $\varphi$ kills. The two ideal axioms are forced exactly because $\varphi$ is a ring homomorphism: additive closure follows from $\varphi(r_1 + r_2) = \varphi(r_1) + \varphi(r_2)$, and strong closure $a \in I, b \in R \Rightarrow ab \in I$ follows from $\varphi(ab) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b) = 0_S \cdot \varphi(b) = 0_S$. Conversely, every ideal $I \trianglelefteq R$ arises this way: the quotient map $\pi : R \to R/I$ has kernel exactly $I$. So the equivalence is exact — *ideals are precisely kernels of ring homomorphisms*.

There is a subtlety worth flagging. The category $\mathbf{Ring}$ does not have an actual zero object — $\mathbb{Z}$ is initial and the zero ring $\{0\}$ is terminal, but they are different. So the categorical notion of "kernel" must be understood in a slightly weaker sense than for abelian groups or modules: the kernel of $\varphi : R \to S$ is the *equalizer* of $\varphi$ and the unique homomorphism $R \to S$ factoring through $\{0\}$ (the constant-zero "morphism" — except that this is not a ring homomorphism unless $S$ is the zero ring). The cleanest way to say it is: ideals correspond bijectively to **congruences** on $R$, where a congruence is an equivalence relation $\sim$ compatible with both ring operations. Given an ideal $I$, the corresponding congruence is $r_1 \sim r_2 \iff r_1 - r_2 \in I$; given a congruence, the corresponding ideal is the equivalence class of $0$. This congruence viewpoint is what survives when the categorical "kernel" fails, and it generalises to any algebraic category. From this perspective, the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] is the statement that congruences and quotient objects are in canonical bijection — the universal property of the quotient $R/I$ as a coequalizer of two parallel arrows in $\mathbf{Ring}$.

Finally, ideals form a complete lattice — the intersection of any family of ideals is again an ideal (verified directly: both axioms are preserved by intersection), and the join of a family is the ideal generated by their union. This lattice is the categorical "subobject lattice in the kernel sense", and operations like ideal sum $I + J$, ideal product $IJ$, and ideal quotient $(I : J)$ are all lattice or near-lattice operations that have categorical analogues across algebraic categories.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

An ideal is the ring-theoretic instance of the universal pattern "*the substructures by which you may quotient*", and the precise analogue is the [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] of a group. The dictionary is exact: a normal subgroup is a subgroup closed under conjugation by every element of the ambient group, and an ideal is an additive subgroup closed under multiplication by every element of the ambient ring; in each case the defining axiom is "internal closure plus closure under an external operation", and in each case that external closure is exactly what makes the representative-independence argument for the quotient succeed. Run the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] in either category and you get the same theorem: the kernel of a structure-preserving map is the quotientable substructure, and the map factors as an isomorphism from "domain modulo kernel" onto the image. So "ideal" is simply "normal subgroup, transplanted to rings" — or, more sharply, "kernel in the category of rings".

There is one place where the analogy deliberately *breaks*, and it is worth holding onto. A normal subgroup *is* a subgroup. An ideal is generally *not* a subring. The reason is the unit: a [[Def - Subring|subring]] must contain $1$, but a proper ideal cannot contain $1$ (strong closure would then force $I = R$). So $2\mathbb{Z}$, the even integers, is an ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ but not a subring of $\mathbb{Z}$ — it has no multiplicative identity. This is the first genuine divergence between group theory and ring theory: the structure you quotient by is no longer a structure of the same kind as what you started with.

A second compression. The single-generator construction $(a) = aR$ is the image of the "multiplication by $a$" map $R \to R$, $r \mapsto ar$. Strong closure of $(a)$ is then automatic — it says the image of a multiplication map absorbs further multiplication, which is just associativity. From this angle the question "is every ideal principal?" becomes "is every ideal the image of some single multiplication map?", and the answer (yes for $\mathbb{Z}$, yes for $F[X]$, no in general) is governed by whether the ring supports a [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]].

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — $n\mathbb{Z} \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$, and these are all the ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$.** For any $n$, the set $n\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -2n, -n, 0, n, 2n, \dots\}$ is an ideal: it is an additive subgroup, and if $a \in n\mathbb{Z}$ and $b \in \mathbb{Z}$ then $ab$ is still a multiple of $n$. Remarkably, *every* ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ has this form, so every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is principal, $n\mathbb{Z} = (n)$. The proof is the Euclidean algorithm: given a nonzero ideal $I$, let $n$ be its smallest positive element. Strong closure gives $n\mathbb{Z} \subseteq I$. Conversely, for any $m \in I$ write $m = qn + r$ with $0 \le r < n$; then $qn \in I$ and $m \in I$, so $r = m - qn \in I$, and minimality of $n$ forces $r = 0$, whence $m = qn \in n\mathbb{Z}$. The mechanism — divide, and use that the remainder is "smaller" — is exactly what makes ideals principal, and it generalises to any ring carrying a notion of size compatible with division.

**Is an instance — $(X) \trianglelefteq R[X]$, the polynomials with zero constant term.** In the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] $R[X]$, the subset $\{f \in R[X] : \text{the constant coefficient of } f \text{ is } 0\}$ is an ideal. One can check the axioms by hand, but the slick argument is that this set is the kernel of the evaluation-at-$0$ homomorphism $R[X] \to R$, $f \mapsto f(0)$, and every kernel is an ideal (see [[Def - Ring Homomorphism]]). It equals the principal ideal $(X) = X \cdot R[X]$, since a polynomial has zero constant term exactly when it is divisible by $X$.

**Is NOT an instance — a subring need not be an ideal.** The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ form a [[Def - Subring|subring]] of $\mathbb{Q}$, but $\mathbb{Z}$ is *not* an ideal of $\mathbb{Q}$: take $a = 1 \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $b = \tfrac12 \in \mathbb{Q}$; then $ab = \tfrac12 \notin \mathbb{Z}$, so strong closure fails. This probes the second axiom directly: subrings are closed under multiplication by their own elements, ideals must be closed under multiplication by all of the ring, and these are genuinely different demands.

**Is NOT an instance — an ideal need not be a subring.** Conversely, $2\mathbb{Z} \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}$ is a perfectly good ideal but is *not* a subring of $\mathbb{Z}$, because it does not contain the multiplicative identity $1$. This is the structural lesson of the section: in group theory the thing you quotient by is itself a group, but in ring theory the thing you quotient by is generally not a ring of the same flavour. It probes the interaction between strong closure and the unit.

**Corollary — a proper ideal contains no unit, hence not $1$.** Suppose $I \trianglelefteq R$ contains a [[Def - Unit and Field|unit]] $u$, so $uv = 1$ for some $v \in R$. By strong closure $1 = uv \in I$, and then for every $r \in R$ we get $r = 1 \cdot r \in I$, so $I = R$. Contrapositively, a *proper* ideal contains no unit at all — in particular it never contains $1$. This is the cleanest test for properness, and it also re-proves that a proper ideal is not a subring.

**Corollary — the only ideals of a field are $\{0\}$ and the field itself.** In a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]] every nonzero element is a unit. By the previous corollary any ideal containing a nonzero element contains a unit and is therefore the whole field; the only other possibility is the zero ideal $\{0\}$. So a field has exactly two ideals. This is a calibration check: if you can derive it from the unit corollary in one line, you have understood strong closure. It is also the seed of a deep fact — a ring is a field precisely when it has no nontrivial proper ideals.

**Calibration check.** Verify that $\{0\}$ and $R$ are always ideals of $R$ (the trivial and improper ideals), that the intersection of any family of ideals is again an ideal (both axioms are preserved under intersection — this is *why* "the smallest ideal containing $A$" makes sense, it is the intersection of all ideals containing $A$), and that for $a \in R$ the set $aR$ really is an ideal. If you can also explain why $(a_1, \dots, a_k)$ needs *all* coefficients $r_i$ to range over $R$ — rather than over the ideal — you have understood that the generators are absorbed, not merely added.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Quotient Ring *(from this topic)*
> An ideal is precisely the licence to form the [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient ring]] $R/I$. The entire payoff of this definition is the next one: with $I \trianglelefteq R$ in hand, coset multiplication is well-defined and $R/I$ is a ring.

> [!tip] The Isomorphism Theorems for Rings *(from this topic)*
> Kernels of [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphisms]] are exactly ideals, and this powers the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first]], [[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|second]] and [[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|third]] isomorphism theorems, together with the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] between ideals of $R/I$ and ideals of $R$ containing $I$.

> [!tip] Principal Ideal Domains and Euclidean Domains *(from Rings II and beyond)*
> The question "is every ideal principal?" singles out the [[Rings II — §2.3–2.4|principal ideal domains]]. Rings carrying a [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]] — such as $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[X]$ for a field $F$ — always have this property, and ideals become the right language for divisibility, factorisation, and primality.
