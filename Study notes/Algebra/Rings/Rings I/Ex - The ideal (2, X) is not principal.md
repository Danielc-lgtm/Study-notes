# Problem Statement

Work in the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — polynomials in one indeterminate $X$ with integer coefficients. Consider the ideal
$$(2,X) = \{\,2f + Xg : f,g\in\mathbb{Z}[X]\,\}\;\trianglelefteq\;\mathbb{Z}[X],$$
the ideal generated by the constant polynomial $2$ and the polynomial $X$.

Prove that $(2,X)$ is **not a principal ideal** — there is no single polynomial $d\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$ with $(2,X)=(d)$.

Deduce that $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is **not a principal ideal domain**.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, an ideal of it generated by two elements, the notion of a principal ideal, and the units of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

![[Def - Polynomial Ring#The Definition]]

![[Def - Ideal#The Definition]]

For elements $a_1,\dots,a_k$ of a ring $R$, the ideal they **generate** is $(a_1,\dots,a_k)=\{a_1r_1+\cdots+a_kr_k:r_i\in R\}$ — all $R$-linear combinations. An ideal $I$ is **principal** if $I=(d)=\{dr:r\in R\}$ for some single $d\in R$. A *principal ideal domain* (PID) is an integral domain in which every ideal is principal.

![[Def - Unit and Field#The Definition]]

The units of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — the polynomials with a multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — are exactly the constant polynomials $1$ and $-1$. The reason is degree: in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ the degree of a product is the sum of degrees ($\deg(pq)=\deg p+\deg q$, valid because $\mathbb{Z}$ has no zero divisors), so $pq=1$ forces $\deg p+\deg q=0$, hence $p$ is a constant; and a constant integer is invertible *in $\mathbb{Z}$* only for $\pm 1$.

A central notion is **divisibility**: in a ring $R$, an element $d$ **divides** $a$, written $d\mid a$, if $a=dc$ for some $c\in R$. The link to ideals is the dictionary
$$d\mid a \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a\in(d) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad (a)\subseteq(d).$$

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *prove a negative structural statement* problem: show that no object of a certain kind exists (here, no single generator for $(2,X)$). As the [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic page's strategy]] records, negative statements about ideals are attacked by *assuming the object exists and deriving a contradiction* — and the standard contradiction engine for "$I$ is principal" hypotheses is the **ideal–divisibility dictionary**: $I=(d)$ forces $d$ to divide every element of $I$.

**Assumption pattern.** The ideal is given by *two* generators, $2$ and $X$, that are "arithmetically unrelated" — $2$ is a non-unit constant, $X$ is a non-constant with zero constant term. The assumption to be refuted, "$(2,X)=(d)$", would compress these two into one. The pattern is: *a two-generator ideal whose generators have no common factor except units, sitting in a ring with few units.* That pattern is the fingerprint of a non-principal ideal.

**Theorem routing.** No named theorem; the route is the divisibility dictionary plus a degree count. If $(2,X)=(d)$ then, because $2\in(d)$ and $X\in(d)$, the generator $d$ divides both $2$ and $X$. Divisibility of $2$ caps $\deg d$ at $0$, so $d$ is a constant integer; divisibility of $X$ by a constant forces that constant to be $\pm1$; hence $d$ is a unit. But an ideal containing a unit is the whole ring (see [[Ex - An ideal contains a unit exactly when it is the whole ring]]), so $(d)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$. The contradiction is sealed by showing $(2,X)\neq\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — and the cleanest way to see *that* is a homomorphism argument: $(2,X)$ is the kernel of a surjection $\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, so it is a proper ideal.

**Key decision point.** Two subtle moves. First, the realisation that "$d$ generates the ideal" is the strong statement "$d$ divides *every* element," and in particular both named generators — this is what converts an ideal hypothesis into arithmetic. Second, and the genuine ⭐⭐⭐ step: proving $(2,X)\neq\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is *not* skippable hand-waving. One must exhibit a polynomial provably outside $(2,X)$ — and the slick certificate is the constant-term-mod-$2$ homomorphism, under which every element of $(2,X)$ maps to $0$ but $1$ does not.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Translate "$I$ is principal" into a divisibility statement about the generators** (the ideal–divisibility dictionary). From the assumed $(2,X)=(d)$ and the memberships $2\in(2,X)$, $X\in(2,X)$, conclude $d\mid 2$ and $d\mid X$.

2. **Bound a degree across a product** (the degree-additivity operation in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$). From $2=d\cdot c$ deduce $\deg d+\deg c=\deg 2=0$, so $\deg d=0$: the generator is a constant.

3. **Identify the units of a ring** (the unit-classification operation). Using that the units of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ are exactly $\pm1$, conclude that a constant dividing $X$ must be $\pm1$, hence a unit.

4. **Use that an ideal containing a unit is the whole ring** (operation: *a unit in $I$ collapses $I$ to $R$*; see [[Ex - An ideal contains a unit exactly when it is the whole ring]]). Since $d$ is a unit and $d\in(d)=(2,X)$, get $(2,X)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

5. **Certify an ideal is proper by exhibiting a surjective homomorphism that kills it** (operation: *$I\subseteq\ker\varphi$ with $\varphi\neq 0$ shows $I\neq R$*). The reduction map $\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, $f\mapsto f(0)\bmod 2$, sends every element of $(2,X)$ to $0$ but sends $1$ to $1\neq 0$, so $1\notin(2,X)$ and $(2,X)\neq\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> This is a non-existence claim, so argue by contradiction: *suppose* $(2,X)=(d)$ for some single polynomial $d\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$, and extract an impossibility. The first thing to ask: what does it mean for $d$ to generate an ideal that contains both $2$ and $X$?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> If $(2,X)=(d)$ then $2\in(d)$ and $X\in(d)$. Membership in a principal ideal is *divisibility*: $a\in(d)$ means $d\mid a$. So $d$ divides $2$ **and** $d$ divides $X$. Now use degrees: in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ the degree of a product is the sum of degrees. What does $d\mid 2$ say about $\deg d$?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> From $d\mid 2$, since $\deg 2=0$ and degrees add, $\deg d=0$ — so $d$ is a *constant integer*. From $d\mid X$, that constant must divide the polynomial $X$; comparing leading coefficients, the constant divides $1$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, so $d=\pm1$. Thus $d$ is a unit, and $(d)=(\pm1)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$. You have shown $(2,X)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — now contradict that.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> To show $(2,X)\neq\mathbb{Z}[X]$, prove $1\notin(2,X)$. Consider the ring homomorphism $\varphi:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ sending a polynomial to (its constant term, reduced mod $2$) — equivalently $f\mapsto f(0)+2\mathbb{Z}$. Check $\varphi(2)=0$ and $\varphi(X)=0$, so $(2,X)\subseteq\ker\varphi$; but $\varphi(1)=1\neq 0$. Hence $1\notin(2,X)$, so $(2,X)$ is proper — contradiction.

# Solution

The strategy is a proof by contradiction. Assume $(2,X)=(d)$; the divisibility dictionary forces $d$ to divide both $2$ and $X$; a degree count then forces $d=\pm1$, a unit; so $(2,X)$ would be all of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — but a homomorphism argument shows $(2,X)$ is a proper ideal. The contradiction proves no $d$ exists.

**Step 1: Assume a generator $d$ exists; it divides both $2$ and $X$.**

Suppose for contradiction $(2,X)=(d)$ for some $d\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Then $d\mid 2$ and $d\mid X$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The ideal $(2,X)$ certainly contains its generators $2$ and $X$ (take $f=1,g=0$ and $f=0,g=1$ respectively in $2f+Xg$). Under the assumption $(2,X)=(d)$, this means
> $$2\in(d)\qquad\text{and}\qquad X\in(d).$$
> By the **ideal–divisibility dictionary**, membership $a\in(d)$ is exactly the statement $d\mid a$: indeed $(d)=\{dc:c\in\mathbb{Z}[X]\}$, so $a\in(d)$ if and only if $a=dc$ for some $c$, which is the definition of $d\mid a$. Therefore
> $$d\mid 2\qquad\text{and}\qquad d\mid X.$$
> Concretely, there exist polynomials $c_1,c_2\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$ with $2=d\,c_1$ and $X=d\,c_2$.

**Step 2: $d$ is a constant integer.**

From $d\mid 2$, the degree of $d$ is $0$: $d$ is a constant polynomial, $d=k$ for some $k\in\mathbb{Z}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Write $2=d\,c_1$ with $c_1\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Take degrees of both sides. In the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, the coefficient ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain (it has no zero divisors), so the leading coefficient of a product is the product of the leading coefficients and is non-zero; consequently **degrees add**:
> $$\deg(d) + \deg(c_1) = \deg(d\,c_1) = \deg(2) = 0.$$
> Degrees of non-zero polynomials are non-negative integers, and two non-negative integers summing to $0$ must both be $0$. (Note $d\neq 0$: if $d=0$ then $(d)=\{0\}$, but $(2,X)$ contains the non-zero element $2$, so $(2,X)\neq(0)$.) Hence $\deg d=0$, i.e. $d$ is a non-zero constant polynomial. Write $d=k$ with $k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$.

**Step 3: $d=\pm 1$, hence $d$ is a unit.**

From $d\mid X$, the constant $k$ divides $X$; comparing coefficients forces $k=\pm1$. So $d$ is a unit of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> By Step 1, $d\mid X$, and by Step 2, $d=k$ is a constant integer. So $X = k\cdot c_2$ for some $c_2\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Write $c_2 = \sum_j b_j X^j$ with $b_j\in\mathbb{Z}$. Then $k\,c_2 = \sum_j (k\,b_j)X^j$, and equating this to $X=0+1\cdot X+0\cdot X^2+\cdots$ coefficient-by-coefficient gives in particular
> $$k\,b_1 = 1\quad\text{(coefficient of }X\text{)}.$$
> This is an equation in $\mathbb{Z}$: the integer $k$ has an integer inverse $b_1$. The only integers with an integer multiplicative inverse are $1$ and $-1$. Hence $k=\pm1$, i.e. $d=\pm1$.
>
> Both $1$ and $-1$ are [[Def - Unit and Field|units]] of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ ($1\cdot1=1$ and $(-1)(-1)=1$). So $d$ is a unit.

**Step 4: A principal ideal generated by a unit is the whole ring, so $(2,X)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$.**

Since $d$ is a unit, $(d)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$; under our assumption that means $(2,X)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> If $d$ is a unit with inverse $d^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$, then for any $r\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$ we have $r = 1\cdot r = (d\,d^{-1})r = d\,(d^{-1}r)\in(d)$. So $(d)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$. (This is the content of [[Ex - An ideal contains a unit exactly when it is the whole ring]]: an ideal containing a unit is the entire ring.)
>
> Under the standing assumption $(2,X)=(d)$, we conclude
> $$(2,X)=\mathbb{Z}[X].$$
> So *if* $(2,X)$ were principal, it would have to be all of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. The next step shows that is false.

**Step 5: But $(2,X)$ is a proper ideal — it does not contain $1$.**

The reduction-mod-$2$-of-the-constant-term homomorphism kills every element of $(2,X)$ but not $1$. So $1\notin(2,X)$ and $(2,X)\neq\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Define $\varphi:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ by sending a polynomial to its constant term reduced modulo $2$:
> $$\varphi\Big(\sum_j a_j X^j\Big) = a_0 + 2\mathbb{Z}.$$
> This is the composite of evaluation at $0$ ($f\mapsto f(0)$, a ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}$ by the universal property of the polynomial ring) with the quotient map $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$; a composite of ring homomorphisms is a ring homomorphism. So $\varphi$ is a genuine ring homomorphism.
>
> Now evaluate $\varphi$ on the generators of $(2,X)$:
> - $\varphi(2) = 2+2\mathbb{Z} = 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$;
> - $\varphi(X) = 0+2\mathbb{Z} = 0$ ($X$ has constant term $0$).
>
> Every element of $(2,X)$ has the form $2f+Xg$, and $\varphi(2f+Xg)=\varphi(2)\varphi(f)+\varphi(X)\varphi(g)=0+0=0$. Thus
> $$(2,X)\subseteq\ker\varphi.$$
> On the other hand $\varphi(1) = 1+2\mathbb{Z}\neq 0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. Hence $1\notin\ker\varphi$, and *a fortiori* $1\notin(2,X)$.
>
> An ideal of a ring $R$ equals $R$ if and only if it contains $1$ (again [[Ex - An ideal contains a unit exactly when it is the whole ring]]). Since $1\notin(2,X)$, we conclude $(2,X)\neq\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — the ideal $(2,X)$ is proper.

**Step 6: Contradiction; conclude.**

Steps 4 and 5 are incompatible, so the assumption fails: $(2,X)$ is not principal, and $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not a principal ideal domain.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Step 4 derived $(2,X)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$ from the assumption $(2,X)=(d)$; Step 5 proved outright that $(2,X)\neq\mathbb{Z}[X]$. These contradict each other. The only unproven input was the assumption that a single generator $d$ exists. Therefore that assumption is false:
> $$(2,X)\text{ is not a principal ideal.}$$
> Finally, $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is an integral domain (it is a polynomial ring over the integral domain $\mathbb{Z}$, and degree-additivity shows it has no zero divisors). A principal ideal domain is by definition an integral domain *all* of whose ideals are principal. Since $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ has at least one non-principal ideal, namely $(2,X)$, it is **not** a principal ideal domain. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** $(2,X)\trianglelefteq\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not principal; hence $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not a PID.
>
> Suppose, for contradiction, that $(2,X)=(d)$ for some $d\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$.
>
> Since $2,X\in(2,X)=(d)$, we have $d\mid 2$ and $d\mid X$; write $2=d\,c_1$, $X=d\,c_2$ with $c_1,c_2\in\mathbb{Z}[X]$.
>
> As $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain, degrees are additive in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. From $2=d\,c_1$: $\deg d+\deg c_1=\deg 2=0$, so $\deg d=0$; thus $d=k$ is a (non-zero) constant integer. From $X=k\,c_2$, comparing the coefficient of $X$ gives $k\,b_1=1$ for some integer $b_1$, so $k\in\{1,-1\}$. Hence $d=\pm1$ is a unit, and therefore $(d)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Under the assumption, $(2,X)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$.
>
> But consider $\varphi:\mathbb{Z}[X]\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, $\varphi(\sum a_jX^j)=a_0+2\mathbb{Z}$, the reduction mod $2$ of the constant term — a ring homomorphism (composite of evaluation at $0$ with the quotient $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$). Then $\varphi(2)=0$ and $\varphi(X)=0$, so every $2f+Xg\in(2,X)$ satisfies $\varphi(2f+Xg)=0$; hence $(2,X)\subseteq\ker\varphi$. Since $\varphi(1)=1\neq 0$, we get $1\notin(2,X)$, so $(2,X)\neq\mathbb{Z}[X]$.
>
> This contradicts $(2,X)=\mathbb{Z}[X]$. Hence no such $d$ exists: $(2,X)$ is not principal.
>
> Finally $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is an integral domain but possesses the non-principal ideal $(2,X)$, so $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not a principal ideal domain. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**To refute "$I$ is principal," translate the hypothesis into divisibility and squeeze the generator.** The single most important move is the **ideal–divisibility dictionary**: $a\in(d)\iff d\mid a$. A hypothesis "$I=(d)$" is therefore the very strong assertion "$d$ divides *every* element of $I$" — and in particular $d$ divides each *named generator* of $I$. The proof template for "$I=(a_1,\dots,a_k)$ is not principal" is: assume $I=(d)$; deduce $d\mid a_i$ for every $i$; argue that a *common divisor* of all the $a_i$ is forced to be a unit (because the generators share no non-trivial common factor); conclude $I$ would be the whole ring; then contradict that by showing $I$ is proper. The trigger is a multi-generator ideal whose generators are "coprime" in the relevant sense; the reaction is "a single generator would have to divide all of them, hence be a unit." This is exactly how one shows $(x,y)$ is non-principal in $k[x,y]$, or that non-principal ideals exist in $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}\,]$ — the arithmetic differs, the skeleton is identical.

**Degree is the size function that constrains divisibility in a polynomial ring — but $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ has no full division algorithm.** Two facts about $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ collaborate here. First, *degrees add* across products (because $\mathbb{Z}$ is an integral domain), so $d\mid 2$ — a divisor of a *degree-zero* polynomial — is instantly pinned to degree zero: a constant. This is the polynomial analogue of "a divisor of a small number is small." Second, and this is the deep point, $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ does *not* support a Euclidean division algorithm: you cannot divide $2$ by $X$ with a smaller remainder, nor $X$ by $2$ within $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. The minimality argument that made every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ principal (see [[Ex - Every ideal of the integers is principal]]) and every ideal of $F[X]$ principal therefore has *no foothold* in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — and the gap it leaves is precisely where a genuinely two-generator ideal like $(2,X)$ can live. The lesson: "every ideal is principal" is a privilege of *Euclidean* rings; adjoining an indeterminate to a Euclidean ring ($\mathbb{Z}\rightsquigarrow\mathbb{Z}[X]$) generically destroys that privilege. Watch for the loss of a division algorithm as the signal that non-principal ideals appear.

**Units are the "invisible" elements of ideal theory — generating an ideal with a unit collapses it to everything.** The proof hinges on the chain "$d$ is a unit $\Rightarrow(d)=R$." Units are the elements that divide $1$, hence divide *everything*, hence generate the whole ring as a principal ideal. This makes the unit group the natural source of *triviality*: any structural statement about ideals must be read "up to units," because multiplying a generator by a unit changes nothing ($(d)=(ud)$ for a unit $u$). The reason $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is fertile ground for non-principal ideals is that its unit group is *tiny* — only $\pm1$. With so few units, two generators rarely collapse to one: there is no unit available to "absorb" the discrepancy between $2$ and $X$. Contrast a field, where *every* non-zero element is a unit, so every non-zero ideal is the whole field and the only ideals are $\{0\}$ and the field itself (see [[Ex - An ideal contains a unit exactly when it is the whole ring]]). The size of the unit group is a direct measure of how "rigid" the ideal structure of a ring is.

**Proving an ideal is proper is real content, and a surjective homomorphism is the cleanest certificate.** A tempting gap in this proof is to wave the hand and say "obviously $(2,X)\neq\mathbb{Z}[X]$." It is not obvious — it is the load-bearing half of the contradiction, and it must be *witnessed*. The reusable technique for "$I$ is a proper ideal" is to construct a surjective ring homomorphism $\varphi:R\to S$ onto a *non-trivial* ring with $I\subseteq\ker\varphi$; then $I\neq R$ because $\varphi$ does not kill all of $R$. Here $\varphi$ is reduction of the constant term mod $2$, with $S=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, and the witness that $I$ is proper is the single fact $\varphi(1)\neq 0$. Equivalently — and this is the conceptual punchline — $(2,X)$ is exactly the kernel of $\varphi$, so by the first isomorphism theorem $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(2,X)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, a non-zero ring; an ideal whose quotient is non-zero is automatically proper. Whenever you must show an ideal is proper (or, going further, *maximal*, or *prime*), the move is to *identify its quotient ring*: the nature of $R/I$ certifies the nature of $I$.
