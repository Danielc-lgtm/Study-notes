# Problem Statement

1. Show that the set of **Gaussian integers**
$$\mathbb{Z}[i] := \{\, a + b i : a, b \in \mathbb{Z} \,\}$$
is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$.
2. Show that the set
$$\mathbb{Z}\!\left[\tfrac12\right] := \left\{\, \tfrac{m}{2^k} : m \in \mathbb{Z},\ k \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} \,\right\}$$
of **dyadic rationals** is a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$.
3. More generally, let $R$ be a (commutative) ring and $\alpha \in R$ an element. Describe the **smallest subring of $R$ containing $\alpha$** — that is, identify the subring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ generated by $\alpha$ explicitly, and explain in what sense it is the smallest. Reconcile this general description with the two concrete cases above.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are a ring, a subring, and the notion of "the subring generated by" a set.

A [[Def - Ring|ring]] $R$ is an abelian [[Def - Group|group]] under $+$ with an associative, distributive, unital multiplication; here all [[Def - Ring|rings]] are commutative with identity $1_R$.

![[Def - Subring#The Definition]]

In practice, to check that a subset $S \subseteq R$ is a subring one verifies the **subring test**: $S$ contains $0_R$ and $1_R$, and $S$ is closed under three operations — addition ($s, t \in S \Rightarrow s + t \in S$), additive inverses ($s \in S \Rightarrow -s \in S$), and multiplication ($s, t \in S \Rightarrow s \cdot t \in S$). Associativity, commutativity and distributivity are then inherited automatically from $R$, because they are universally-quantified identities: if they hold for all elements of $R$ they hold in particular for all elements of the subset $S$.

The **smallest subring containing a subset** $T \subseteq R$ is the intersection of all [[Def - Subring|subrings]] of $R$ that contain $T$. This intersection is itself a subring, and it is contained in every subring containing $T$ — hence "smallest". For a single element $\alpha$ it is written $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ and called the subring **generated by** $\alpha$. The notation $R[X]$ for a [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] and the bracket notation $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ are deliberately similar: part 3 explains the link.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** Parts 1 and 2 are *verify a given subset is a subring* problems — pure checklist verification. Part 3 is a *characterise a universally-defined object* problem: "the smallest subring containing $\alpha$" is defined abstractly (as an intersection) and must be given a concrete, constructive description. The topic's [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2#Problem-Solving Strategy|problem-solving strategy]] pairs these: to identify a "smallest closed set", guess the constructive description, then prove the guess is closed (so it is *a* subring containing $\alpha$) and prove it sits inside every competitor (so it is the *smallest*).

**Assumption pattern.** For parts 1–2 the only "assumption" is the explicit description of the set, and the task is to confront that description with the four closure requirements. The recognisable feature is that the defining form ($a + bi$; $m/2^k$) is *stable* under the ring operations — adding or multiplying two elements of the stated form lands in the stated form after a short algebraic rearrangement. For part 3 the recognisable feature is the word "smallest", which always signals a two-inclusion proof.

**Theorem routing.** No named theorem; the route is the **subring test** for parts 1–2 and a **double-inclusion argument** for part 3. For part 3 the constructive guess is "all $\mathbb{Z}$-polynomial expressions in $\alpha$": every element of the form $c_0 + c_1\alpha + c_2\alpha^2 + \cdots + c_n\alpha^n$ with integer coefficients $c_i$. One shows (a) this set is a subring containing $\alpha$ — closed under the operations because sums and products of polynomial expressions are again polynomial expressions — and (b) any subring containing $\alpha$ must contain all such expressions, since a subring contains $1$, hence every integer multiple of $1$, hence (being closed under multiplication and addition) every $\mathbb{Z}$-combination of powers of $\alpha$.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious idea in part 3 is that "smallest subring containing $\alpha$" — defined top-down as an intersection — equals a bottom-up object: the *image of the evaluation map* $\mathbb{Z}[X] \to R$, $X \mapsto \alpha$. The decision is to recognise that the abstract intersection and the concrete polynomial-image are the same set, proved by the double inclusion. Once that is seen, parts 1 and 2 are revealed as the special cases $\alpha = i$ (where $\alpha^2 = -1$ truncates all powers down to $a + b\alpha$) and $\alpha = \tfrac12$ (where powers $\alpha^k = 2^{-k}$ never collapse, producing the full dyadic family).

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Apply the subring test.** To certify a subset is a subring, check membership of $0$ and $1$ and closure under addition, additive inverse, and multiplication — and *invoke inheritance* for the axioms (associativity, commutativity, distributivity) that come free from the ambient ring.

2. **Inherit universally-quantified axioms from a parent structure.** The associative/commutative/distributive laws are statements "for all elements"; restricting the quantifier to a subset preserves their truth, so they need no separate verification in $S$. This is why the subring test is short.

3. **Characterise a "smallest closed object" by a double inclusion.** To prove a set $S$ is the smallest subring containing $\alpha$: show $S$ *is* a subring containing $\alpha$, and show $S \subseteq T$ for every subring $T \ni \alpha$. The two inclusions together pin $S$ as the intersection.

4. **Generate elements by closing the generators under the operations.** Starting from $\alpha$ and $1$, repeatedly apply addition, negation, and multiplication; the elements reachable this way are exactly the integer-coefficient polynomial expressions in $\alpha$ — this is the constructive (bottom-up) description of the generated subring.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> For parts 1 and 2, do not prove the ring axioms from scratch. Use the **subring test**: a subset $S$ of a ring $R$ is a subring exactly when $0, 1 \in S$ and $S$ is closed under addition, negation, and multiplication. Associativity, commutativity and distributivity are inherited from $R$ for free — they are "for all" identities and a subset of a set where they hold is still a set where they hold.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> For $\mathbb{Z}[i]$: take two elements $a + bi$ and $c + di$ with $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{Z}$. Compute their sum, the negation of one, and their product. After collecting real and imaginary parts, is each result again of the form $(\text{integer}) + (\text{integer})\,i$? (Use $i^2 = -1$ for the product.) For $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$: take $m/2^j$ and $n/2^k$; put them over a common power of $2$ for the sum, and multiply for the product.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For part 3, "smallest subring containing $\alpha$" means the intersection of all subrings that contain $\alpha$. Guess what it is: any subring containing $\alpha$ must contain $1$, hence $1 + 1 + \cdots = n$ for every integer $n$, hence $\alpha, \alpha^2, \alpha^3, \dots$, hence every sum $c_0 + c_1\alpha + \cdots + c_n\alpha^n$ with $c_i \in \mathbb{Z}$. So the candidate is the set of all such integer-coefficient polynomial expressions in $\alpha$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Prove the candidate set $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] = \{\,\sum_{i=0}^n c_i \alpha^i : n \ge 0,\ c_i \in \mathbb{Z}\,\}$ is itself a subring (sums and products of polynomial expressions are polynomial expressions) and contains $\alpha$ — so it is *one* subring containing $\alpha$. By Hint 3 it lies inside *every* subring containing $\alpha$. Being both a member of the family and below every member, it is the intersection: the smallest. Then check $i^2 = -1$ collapses $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ to $\{a + bi\}$, and $(\tfrac12)^k = 2^{-k}$ makes $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$ the dyadics.

# Solution

The plan: verify $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ and $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$ pass the subring test by direct computation; then characterise $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ as the set of integer-coefficient polynomial expressions in $\alpha$ via a double inclusion, and recover the two examples as special cases.

**Step 1: $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$.**

The set $\{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ contains $0$ and $1$, and is closed under addition, negation, and multiplication — each operation, applied to elements of this form, returns an element of this form.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Apply the subring test to $S = \mathbb{Z}[i] \subseteq \mathbb{C}$.
>
> *Contains $0$ and $1$.* $0 = 0 + 0i$ and $1 = 1 + 0i$, both with integer coefficients, so $0, 1 \in S$.
>
> *Closed under addition.* Take $a + bi$ and $c + di$ with $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then
> $$(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i.$$
> Since $a + c \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $b + d \in \mathbb{Z}$, the sum is in $S$.
>
> *Closed under negation.* $-(a + bi) = (-a) + (-b)i$, and $-a, -b \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $-(a+bi) \in S$.
>
> *Closed under multiplication.* Using $i^2 = -1$,
> $$(a + bi)(c + di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\, i^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i.$$
> Both $ac - bd \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $ad + bc \in \mathbb{Z}$, so the product is in $S$.
>
> *Inherited axioms.* Associativity and commutativity of $+$ and $\cdot$, and distributivity, hold for all complex numbers, hence in particular for all elements of $S$; they need no separate check. Therefore $S = \mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$. $\blacksquare$

**Step 2: $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$ is a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$.**

The dyadic rationals $\{m/2^k : m \in \mathbb{Z},\ k \ge 0\}$ contain $0$ and $1$ and are closed under the three operations — putting fractions over a common power of $2$ handles the sum, and exponent addition handles the product.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Apply the subring test to $S = \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12] \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$.
>
> *Contains $0$ and $1$.* $0 = 0 / 2^0$ and $1 = 1 / 2^0$, so $0, 1 \in S$.
>
> *Closed under addition.* Take $m / 2^j$ and $n / 2^k$ with $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$, $j, k \ge 0$. Let $\ell = \max(j, k)$. Bring both over the denominator $2^\ell$:
> $$\frac{m}{2^j} + \frac{n}{2^k} = \frac{m \cdot 2^{\ell - j} + n \cdot 2^{\ell - k}}{2^\ell}.$$
> The exponents $\ell - j$ and $\ell - k$ are non-negative, so the numerator $m \cdot 2^{\ell - j} + n \cdot 2^{\ell - k}$ is an integer and $\ell \ge 0$; the sum is in $S$.
>
> *Closed under negation.* $-\,(m / 2^k) = (-m) / 2^k$ with $-m \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $-(m/2^k) \in S$.
>
> *Closed under multiplication.* By exponent addition,
> $$\frac{m}{2^j} \cdot \frac{n}{2^k} = \frac{m n}{2^{\,j + k}},$$
> with $mn \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $j + k \ge 0$; the product is in $S$.
>
> *Inherited axioms.* The ring axioms hold throughout $\mathbb{Q}$, hence on the subset $S$. Therefore $S = \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$ is a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$. $\blacksquare$

**Step 3: the smallest subring of $R$ containing $\alpha$ is the set of integer-coefficient polynomial expressions in $\alpha$.**

Define $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] := \{\, c_0 + c_1\alpha + \cdots + c_n\alpha^n : n \ge 0,\ c_i \in \mathbb{Z}\,\}$. This set is a subring of $R$ containing $\alpha$, and it is contained in every subring of $R$ containing $\alpha$. Hence it equals the intersection of all such subrings — the smallest one.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Write $P := \{\, \sum_{i=0}^n c_i \alpha^i : n \ge 0,\ c_i \in \mathbb{Z}\,\}$, the set of values at $\alpha$ of integer-coefficient polynomials. Here $c\,\alpha^i$ for $c \in \mathbb{Z}$ means the $|c|$-fold sum of $\pm\alpha^i$, and $\alpha^0 := 1_R$.
>
> *Claim A — $P$ is a subring of $R$ containing $\alpha$.* It contains $0$ (the empty/zero combination), $1 = 1 \cdot \alpha^0$, and $\alpha = 1 \cdot \alpha^1$. It is closed under addition: the sum of $\sum c_i \alpha^i$ and $\sum d_i \alpha^i$ is $\sum (c_i + d_i)\alpha^i$, again an integer-coefficient expression. It is closed under negation: $-\sum c_i \alpha^i = \sum (-c_i)\alpha^i$. It is closed under multiplication: by distributivity,
> $$\Big(\sum_i c_i \alpha^i\Big)\Big(\sum_j d_j \alpha^j\Big) = \sum_k \Big(\sum_{i + j = k} c_i d_j\Big)\alpha^k,$$
> and each coefficient $\sum_{i+j=k} c_i d_j$ is an integer, so the product is again in $P$. The ring axioms are inherited from $R$. So $P$ is a subring of $R$, and $\alpha \in P$.
>
> *Claim B — $P$ is contained in every subring $T$ of $R$ with $\alpha \in T$.* Let $T$ be any such subring. Then $1 \in T$ (subring axiom), and closure under addition and negation forces $n \cdot 1 \in T$ for every integer $n$ (repeated addition of $1$, or of $-1$). Closure under multiplication forces $\alpha^i \in T$ for every $i \ge 0$ (repeated multiplication of $\alpha$, with $\alpha^0 = 1 \in T$). Then $c_i \alpha^i = (c_i \cdot 1)(\alpha^i) \in T$ since $T$ is closed under products, and finally $\sum_i c_i \alpha^i \in T$ since $T$ is closed under addition. So every element of $P$ lies in $T$: $P \subseteq T$.
>
> *Conclusion.* By Claim A, $P$ is a member of the family $\mathcal{F}$ of all subrings of $R$ containing $\alpha$. By Claim B, $P \subseteq T$ for every $T \in \mathcal{F}$, so $P \subseteq \bigcap \mathcal{F}$. But $\bigcap \mathcal{F} \subseteq P$ as well, since $P \in \mathcal{F}$ is one of the sets being intersected. Hence
> $$\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \;=\; \bigcap_{T \in \mathcal{F}} T \;=\; P \;=\; \big\{\, c_0 + c_1\alpha + \cdots + c_n\alpha^n : n \ge 0,\ c_i \in \mathbb{Z}\,\big\}.$$
> The intersection $\bigcap\mathcal F$ is the smallest subring containing $\alpha$ — it is a subring (an intersection of subrings is a subring), it contains $\alpha$, and it lies inside every competitor — and we have just identified it concretely.
>
> *Polynomial-ring reading.* The map $\operatorname{ev}_\alpha : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to R$ sending a polynomial $\sum c_i X^i$ to $\sum c_i \alpha^i$ respects $+$ and $\cdot$ (this is exactly the coefficient computation above), i.e. it is a ring homomorphism. Its image is precisely $P = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$. So "the subring generated by $\alpha$" is literally "the image of the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ under evaluation at $\alpha$" — which is why the bracket notation $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ echoes the polynomial-ring notation $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

**Step 4: recover the two examples as special cases.**

For $\alpha = i$, the relation $i^2 = -1$ collapses every power of $i$ to $\pm 1$ or $\pm i$, so $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ reduces to $\{a + bi\}$. For $\alpha = \tfrac12$, no relation collapses the powers, and $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$ unfolds to the full set of dyadic rationals.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Case $\alpha = i$ in $R = \mathbb{C}$.* By Step 3, $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{\sum c_k i^k : c_k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. But the powers of $i$ cycle: $i^0 = 1,\ i^1 = i,\ i^2 = -1,\ i^3 = -i,\ i^4 = 1, \dots$. So every power $i^k$ equals $1, i, -1$, or $-i$, and any integer combination $\sum c_k i^k$ collects into a single term $a + bi$ with $a$ the net integer coefficient of the real powers and $b$ that of the imaginary powers. Hence $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ — exactly the set of Step 1. The relation $i^2 + 1 = 0$ is what truncates the a priori infinite list of powers down to two basis elements $1, i$.
>
> *Case $\alpha = \tfrac12$ in $R = \mathbb{Q}$.* By Step 3, $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12] = \{\sum_{k=0}^n c_k (\tfrac12)^k : c_k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. Now $(\tfrac12)^k = 1/2^k$, and there is *no* relation among these powers — they are all distinct and none is an integer combination of the others. A finite sum $\sum_{k=0}^n c_k / 2^k$, placed over the common denominator $2^n$, equals $(\sum_k c_k 2^{n-k}) / 2^n$, an integer over a power of $2$. Conversely every $m / 2^n$ arises this way (take $c_n = m$, other $c_k = 0$). Hence $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12] = \{m / 2^k : m \in \mathbb{Z},\ k \ge 0\}$ — exactly the set of Step 2.
>
> The contrast is instructive: $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is *finitely generated as an abelian group* (basis $1, i$) because $i$ satisfies a monic integer polynomial relation $i^2 = -1$, whereas $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$ is *not* finitely generated as an abelian group, because $\tfrac12$ satisfies no monic integer relation — it is not an algebraic integer. The single template $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] = \operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ev}_\alpha)$ produces both behaviours; which one occurs is governed entirely by what relations $\alpha$ satisfies. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **$\mathbb{Z}[i] \le \mathbb{C}$.** $0 = 0 + 0i$ and $1 = 1 + 0i$ lie in $\mathbb{Z}[i]$. For $a + bi, c + di$ with integer coefficients: $(a+bi) + (c+di) = (a+c) + (b+d)i \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$; $-(a+bi) = (-a) + (-b)i \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$; and $(a+bi)(c+di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i \in \mathbb{Z}[i]$ using $i^2 = -1$. Ring axioms are inherited from $\mathbb{C}$. By the subring test, $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is a subring of $\mathbb{C}$.
>
> **$\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12] \le \mathbb{Q}$.** $0 = 0/2^0$ and $1 = 1/2^0$ lie in $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$. For $m/2^j, n/2^k$ with $\ell = \max(j,k)$: $m/2^j + n/2^k = (m\,2^{\ell-j} + n\,2^{\ell-k})/2^\ell \in \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$; $-(m/2^k) = (-m)/2^k \in \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$; $\ (m/2^j)(n/2^k) = mn / 2^{j+k} \in \mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$. Ring axioms are inherited from $\mathbb{Q}$. By the subring test, $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12]$ is a subring of $\mathbb{Q}$.
>
> **Smallest subring containing $\alpha$.** Let $P = \{\sum_{i=0}^n c_i \alpha^i : n \ge 0,\ c_i \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. (i) $P$ is a subring containing $\alpha$: it holds $0, 1, \alpha$; it is closed under addition ($\sum c_i\alpha^i + \sum d_i\alpha^i = \sum(c_i+d_i)\alpha^i$), negation ($-\sum c_i\alpha^i = \sum(-c_i)\alpha^i$) and multiplication ($\big(\sum c_i\alpha^i\big)\big(\sum d_j\alpha^j\big) = \sum_k\big(\sum_{i+j=k}c_id_j\big)\alpha^k$); axioms inherited from $R$. (ii) If $T$ is any subring with $\alpha \in T$, then $1 \in T$ forces $n\cdot1 \in T$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, $\alpha \in T$ forces $\alpha^i \in T$ for all $i \ge 0$, and closure gives $\sum c_i\alpha^i \in T$; hence $P \subseteq T$. From (i) $P$ is one of the subrings containing $\alpha$; from (ii) $P$ lies inside all of them. Therefore $P = \bigcap\{T : T \text{ subring},\ \alpha \in T\}$, the smallest subring containing $\alpha$, and $P = \operatorname{im}(\operatorname{ev}_\alpha : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to R)$.
>
> **Examples.** With $\alpha = i$: $i^k \in \{1, i, -1, -i\}$, so $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. With $\alpha = \tfrac12$: $(\tfrac12)^k = 2^{-k}$ with no collapsing relation, so $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac12] = \{m/2^k : m \in \mathbb{Z},\ k \ge 0\}$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**The subring test is short because universally-quantified axioms are inherited — verify only closure, never the laws.** To prove a subset is a subring you check membership of $0$ and $1$ and closure under addition, negation, and multiplication — and you do *not* re-prove associativity, commutativity, or distributivity. The reason is structural and worth internalising: those laws are sentences of the form "for all elements, ...", and the truth of a for-all sentence is preserved under restricting the domain of quantification to a subset. A subset of a place where an identity holds is still a place where the identity holds. This is exactly why "sub-structure" tests across algebra are uniformly short — the [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]] test, the [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] test, the subring test all check only the operations and the distinguished elements, never the equational axioms. The transferable habit: when proving something is a sub-object, partition the defining conditions into *equational axioms* (free, inherited) and *closure/existence conditions* (the only things needing work), and spend all your effort on the second class.

**"Smallest object with a property" is always proved by a double inclusion: exhibit it, then bound it below.** Part 3 defines $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ top-down as an intersection of all subrings containing $\alpha$ — an abstract, non-constructive description. To make it usable you guess a concrete set $P$ and prove two things: $P$ *is* a subring containing $\alpha$ (so $P$ is one of the sets in the intersection, giving $\bigcap \subseteq P$), and $P$ is contained in *every* subring containing $\alpha$ (giving $P \subseteq \bigcap$). Together these force $P = \bigcap$. This "smallest = intersection, identified by sandwiching" pattern is universal: the subgroup generated by a set, the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] generated by a set, the topology generated by a subbasis, the $\sigma$-algebra generated by a collection, the closure of a set — every one is defined as an intersection and made concrete by exactly this two-inclusion argument. Recognising the word "smallest" (or "generated by", or "closure") as a trigger for double inclusion is one of the highest-leverage pattern matches in mathematics.

**"Generated by" objects are images of free objects under evaluation — and relations among the generators control the size.** The subring generated by $\alpha$ turned out to be the *image* of the polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ under the evaluation homomorphism $X \mapsto \alpha$. This is the deepest content of the exercise: $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is the "free" commutative ring on one generator — it imposes no relations — and *any* one-generated subring is a quotient-image of it, obtained by collapsing along whatever relations the specific generator $\alpha$ happens to satisfy. When $\alpha = i$ the relation $i^2 = -1$ collapses the infinitely many powers down to a rank-two object $\{a + bi\}$; when $\alpha = \tfrac12$ no relation holds, the powers stay independent, and the object is the infinitely-generated dyadics. The same template, different relations, different size. This "generated object = free object modulo relations" picture recurs everywhere — group presentations, [[Def - Module|modules]], algebras — and it tells you precisely where to look to understand a generated structure: find the relations satisfied by the generators, because the kernel of the evaluation map *is* the structure's deviation from being free. Whenever you meet a bracket like $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$, $R[\alpha]$, or $k[\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n]$, read it as "polynomials in the generators, evaluated", and immediately ask what relations the generators obey.
