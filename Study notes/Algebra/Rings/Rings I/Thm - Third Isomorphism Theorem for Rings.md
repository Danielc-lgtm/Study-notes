# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a [[Def - Ring|ring]] and $I, J$ are [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] of $R$ with $I \subseteq J$. The [[Def - Quotient Ring|quotient ring]] $R/I$ has elements the cosets $r + I$, with addition and multiplication on representatives. Inside it,
$$J/I = \{j + I : j \in J\}$$
is the set of cosets with a representative in $J$. The double quotient $(R/I)/(J/I)$ is the quotient of the ring $R/I$ by the ideal $J/I$; a typical element is a coset $(r + I) + J/I$. The symbol $\trianglelefteq$ means "is an ideal of", $\subseteq$ is set inclusion, $\twoheadrightarrow$ a surjection, and $\cong$ a ring isomorphism. The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2]].

# Statement

> **[[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem|Third Isomorphism Theorem]] for Rings.** Let $R$ be a ring and let $I \subseteq J$ be [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] of $R$. Then $J/I$ is an ideal of $R/I$, and there is a ring isomorphism
> $$\frac{R/I}{J/I} \;\cong\; \frac{R}{J}.$$

---

# Motivation

You have learned to build quotient [[Def - Ring|rings]], and nothing stops you from building one *out of another quotient ring*. Start with $R$, divide by an ideal $I$ to get $R/I$, then notice that $R/I$ has ideals of its own, and divide again. The result is a double quotient $(R/I)/(J/I)$ — a quotient of a quotient. The third isomorphism theorem answers the obvious worry: have you actually built something new, something hard to understand, by iterating the construction? The answer is reassuringly no. Dividing by $I$ and then dividing the result by $J/I$ gives nothing more than dividing $R$ by $J$ in one step. The two ideals "compose": quotienting is *transitive*.

This is the **cancellation law for ring quotients**, and the slogan is exactly that — the $I$'s cancel:
$$\frac{R/I}{J/I} \;\cong\; \frac{R}{J}.$$
It is the ring-theoretic analogue of the schoolbook identity $\dfrac{a/c}{b/c} = \dfrac{a}{b}$, and it deserves to be remembered in that form. The practical force is that an intimidating iterated construction collapses to a single, familiar quotient. If a problem ever produces a double quotient, you do not study it as a double quotient — you cancel and look at $R/J$ instead.

Why would anyone form a double quotient in the first place? Often because the two stages arise naturally and separately. You might pass to $R/I$ to record one simplification — say, set some relation to zero — and only later wish to impose a further relation, which lives in the already-quotiented ring as the ideal $J/I$. The third isomorphism theorem says you may impose the two relations in either order, or both at once, and land in the same ring. It is the statement that "imposing relations" is a coherent, order-independent operation, which is precisely what you need for the bookkeeping of any extended algebraic computation to be sound.

And, as with the [[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|second isomorphism theorem]], the deepest point is the proof: the third isomorphism theorem is *not* proved from scratch. It is the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] applied to one specific homomorphism — the natural map $R/I \to R/J$ that "forgets the finer distinction". Once you spot that map, the theorem writes itself.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition is "a nested pair of ideals $I \subseteq J$ of a ring $R$". The source question is: when does a problem hand you this nested pair, perhaps in disguise?

The first disguised source is **an iterated quotient construction**. Any time a problem builds a ring in stages — quotient, then quotient again — you have a double quotient, and the inner and outer ideals assemble into a nested pair $I \subseteq J$ in the original ring. The bridge is the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]]: every ideal of $R/I$ has the form $J/I$ for a unique ideal $J$ of $R$ containing $I$, so the outer ideal of your double quotient *automatically* names a $J \supseteq I$. The non-obvious step is to undo the correspondence — to recover the genuine ideal $J$ of $R$ from the ideal $J/I$ of $R/I$ — and only then is the theorem applicable. *Example problem:* identify $\big(\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}\big)\big/\langle \bar 4\rangle$ by recognising $\langle \bar 4 \rangle = 4\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ and cancelling to $\mathbb{Z}/4\mathbb{Z}$.

The second disguised source is **two ideals where one contains the other, presented without the word "nested"**. If a problem gives you ideals $I$ and $J$ and you can *prove* $I \subseteq J$ — for instance because $J$ is generated by a superset of the generators of $I$, or because $J$ is a maximal ideal containing the not-necessarily-maximal $I$ — then the theorem applies. The non-obvious part is establishing the inclusion in the first place; once $I \subseteq J$ is known, the cancellation is automatic. *Example problem:* with $I = (X^2)$ and $J = (X)$ in $k[X]$, note $I \subseteq J$ and cancel.

The third disguised source is **a chain of relations imposed one after another**. When you model "impose relation $\rho_1$, then impose relation $\rho_2$" as quotients, the first quotient is by $I = (\rho_1)$ and the second is by the image of $(\rho_1, \rho_2)$, giving the nested pair $I = (\rho_1) \subseteq J = (\rho_1, \rho_2)$. The theorem then certifies that the order of imposition does not matter. The non-obviousness is recognising "impose another relation" as "quotient by a larger ideal of the same $R$". *Example problem:* show that imposing $X^2 = 0$ and then $X = 0$ on $k[X]$ gives the same ring as imposing $X = 0$ directly.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusion is $(R/I)/(J/I) \cong R/J$. Combined with other facts it does more.

Combine the conclusion with **a property of the single quotient $R/J$**. Whatever you know about $R/J$ transfers, via the isomorphism, to the double quotient — and conversely. If $J$ is a maximal ideal, $R/J$ is a field, so the double quotient $(R/I)/(J/I)$ is a field; if $J$ is prime, the double quotient is an integral domain. The further result $E$ is that you can certify a double quotient as a field or domain *without ever forming it*, by checking maximality or primality of the single ideal $J$ in $R$. This is non-obvious because the double quotient looks structurally complicated, yet its ring type is decided by one ideal upstairs.

Combine the conclusion with **counting in a finite ring**. If $R$ is finite, the isomorphism gives $|(R/I)/(J/I)| = |R/J| = |R|/|J|$, and also $|R/I| = |R|/|I|$, $|J/I| = |J|/|I|$. The further result is the consistency of these counts — $|(R/I)/(J/I)| = |R/I|/|J/I|$ — which is the ring analogue of the index multiplicativity $[G:K] = [G:H][H:K]$. This lets you compute the size of an iterated quotient by cancellation rather than by enumerating cosets of cosets.

Combine the conclusion with **the lattice of ideals between $I$ and $R$**. The third theorem, read alongside the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]], says the ideals of $R/I$ are exactly the $J/I$ for $I \subseteq J \trianglelefteq R$, and each corresponding quotient $(R/I)/(J/I)$ is $R/J$. The further result $E$ is a complete description of *all* quotients of $R/I$: they are precisely the quotients $R/J$ of $R$ by ideals $J \supseteq I$. This is non-obvious and powerful — it says passing to $R/I$ does not create exotic quotients, it merely restricts attention to those quotients of $R$ that already kill $I$.

# Why Is It True

Do not start with the double quotient. Start with the most natural map you can write between the two single quotients $R/I$ and $R/J$. Because $I \subseteq J$, a coset of $I$ is "finer" information than a coset of $J$: knowing $r + I$ pins $r$ down to within $I$, which is more precise than pinning it down to within the larger $J$. So there is an obvious "coarsening" map
$$\psi : R/I \longrightarrow R/J, \qquad r + I \longmapsto r + J,$$
that throws away the extra precision. The entire third isomorphism theorem is the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] applied to $\psi$, and "why is it true" is just "what are the kernel and image of $\psi$".

First, is $\psi$ even well-defined? Yes, and this is exactly where $I \subseteq J$ earns its keep. If $r + I = r' + I$, then $r - r' \in I$; since $I \subseteq J$, also $r - r' \in J$, so $r + J = r' + J$. The coarsening map is well-defined *because* the finer ideal sits inside the coarser one — drop the inclusion hypothesis and $\psi$ is not a function at all. It is plainly a ring homomorphism, since both quotient multiplications are computed on the same representatives in $R$.

Now, what does $\psi$ collapse? An element $r + I$ of $R/I$ is sent to the zero of $R/J$ exactly when $r + J = J$, i.e. $r \in J$. So the kernel of $\psi$ is the set of [[Def - Coset|cosets]] $r + I$ with $r \in J$ — which is precisely $J/I$. This is the whole reason $J/I$ is an ideal of $R/I$: it is a *kernel*, and kernels are ideals. No axiom-checking was needed.

And the image? The map $\psi$ sends $r + I \mapsto r + J$, and as $r$ ranges over all of $R$ this hits every coset of $J$. So $\psi$ is surjective: $\operatorname{im}\psi = R/J$.

The first isomorphism theorem now says $\psi$ induces an isomorphism from $R/I$ modulo its kernel onto its image. Substituting:
$$\frac{R/I}{J/I} \;\cong\; \frac{R}{J}.$$
Every clause — "$J/I$ is an ideal of $R/I$" and the isomorphism itself — is a feature of the *single* coarsening map $\psi$. The intuition for the isomorphism is the intuition for the first theorem: $\psi$ identifies two elements of $R/I$ exactly when they agree modulo $J/I$, so collapsing $J/I$ turns $\psi$ into a perfect bijection. The cancellation law is then visible directly: $\psi$ forgets the difference between "modulo $I$" and "modulo $J$", and quotienting $R/I$ by precisely that forgotten difference recovers $R/J$. The $I$'s cancel because $\psi$ never really used them.

# What Makes This Hard

The proof is immediate once you write down the coarsening map $\psi : R/I \to R/J$, $r + I \mapsto r + J$, so the difficulty is psychological — the theorem is stated as an isomorphism with no map in sight, and one must invent it. The single non-obvious step is realising that the hypothesis $I \subseteq J$ is what makes $\psi$ *well-defined* (an element of $I$ is automatically in $J$, so finer-equal implies coarser-equal); students who omit this check have not actually got a function. The most common error is mishandling the double quotient: treating $(R/I)/(J/I)$ as if its elements were elements of $R$ rather than cosets-of-cosets, instead of letting the first isomorphism theorem deliver the identification mechanically.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Do not analyse the double quotient directly. Write down the coarsening map $R/I \to R/J$, check it is a well-defined ring homomorphism (this is where $I \subseteq J$ is used), then apply the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]]: its kernel is $J/I$ and it is surjective, so the conclusion follows.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Build the map and check it is well-defined.** Define $\psi : R/I \to R/J$ by $\psi(r + I) = r + J$, and show the output does not depend on the representative.
   - *Hint:* If $r + I = r' + I$ then $r - r' \in I \subseteq J$, so $r + J = r' + J$. The inclusion $I \subseteq J$ is exactly what makes this work.
   - *Why needed:* Without well-definedness $\psi$ is not a function, and nothing below is meaningful.

2. **Check $\psi$ is a ring homomorphism.** Show $\psi$ respects addition and multiplication and sends $1$ to $1$.
   - *Hint:* Both $R/I$ and $R/J$ compute their operations on representatives drawn from $R$, so $\psi$ trivially intertwines them.
   - *Why needed:* The first isomorphism theorem requires a ring homomorphism as input.

3. **Compute the kernel.** Show $\ker\psi = J/I$.
   - *Hint:* $\psi(r + I) = 0$ in $R/J$ means $r + J = J$, i.e. $r \in J$; the cosets $r + I$ with $r \in J$ are exactly $J/I$.
   - *Why needed:* The first isomorphism theorem will then declare $J/I$ an ideal of $R/I$ for free and supply the denominator.

4. **Check $\psi$ is surjective.** Show every coset $r + J$ is hit.
   - *Hint:* $r + J = \psi(r + I)$ by definition, for any $r \in R$.
   - *Why needed:* Surjectivity makes the image equal to $R/J$, the right-hand side of the conclusion.

5. **Apply the first isomorphism theorem.** Conclude $(R/I)/(J/I) \cong R/J$.
   - *Hint:* Substitute $\ker\psi = J/I$ and $\operatorname{im}\psi = R/J$ into $(R/I)/\ker\psi \cong \operatorname{im}\psi$.
   - *Why needed:* This is the asserted isomorphism, and the ideal clause is already established.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: The coarsening map is well-defined
> **Statement:** Let $I \subseteq J$ be ideals of $R$. The rule $\psi(r + I) = r + J$ defines a function $\psi : R/I \to R/J$ — the output is independent of the chosen representative.
>
> **Hint:** Equal $I$-cosets differ by an element of $I$; since $I \subseteq J$, that element is also in $J$.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the one place the hypothesis $I \subseteq J$ is used; without it $\psi$ is not a function and the theorem is false.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Suppose $r + I = r' + I$ for $r, r' \in R$. By the coset equality criterion (for the additive [[Def - Group|group]]), $r - r' \in I$. Since $I \subseteq J$ by hypothesis, $r - r' \in J$ as well. Therefore $r + J = r' + J$, that is, $\psi(r + I) = \psi(r' + I)$. So the value of $\psi$ at a coset does not depend on which representative is used, and $\psi$ is a genuine function $R/I \to R/J$. (Note where the inclusion was used: it converted "differ by an element of $I$" into "differ by an element of $J$". If $I \not\subseteq J$, two equal $I$-cosets could map to distinct $J$-cosets, and $\psi$ would fail to be a function.)

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The coarsening map is a ring homomorphism
> **Statement:** The well-defined map $\psi : R/I \to R/J$, $\psi(r + I) = r + J$, is a ring homomorphism.
>
> **Hint:** The operations on $R/I$ and on $R/J$ are both defined by performing the operation on representatives in $R$.
>
> **Why needed:** The first isomorphism theorem takes a ring homomorphism as its input.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > For cosets $r + I$ and $r' + I$, addition and multiplication in $R/I$ are $(r+I)+(r'+I) = (r+r')+I$ and $(r+I)(r'+I) = rr'+I$; the same formulas with $J$ in place of $I$ hold in $R/J$. Hence
> > $$\psi\big((r+I)+(r'+I)\big) = \psi\big((r+r')+I\big) = (r+r')+J = (r+J)+(r'+J) = \psi(r+I)+\psi(r'+I),$$
> > $$\psi\big((r+I)(r'+I)\big) = \psi\big(rr'+I\big) = rr'+J = (r+J)(r'+J) = \psi(r+I)\,\psi(r'+I).$$
> > Finally $\psi(1_R + I) = 1_R + J$, which is the multiplicative identity of $R/J$. So $\psi$ respects addition, multiplication, and the identity: it is a ring homomorphism.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The kernel of the coarsening map is $J/I$, and it is surjective
> **Statement:** For $\psi : R/I \to R/J$, $\psi(r + I) = r + J$, the kernel is $\ker\psi = J/I$ and $\psi$ is surjective, $\operatorname{im}\psi = R/J$.
>
> **Hint:** A coset $r + I$ dies under $\psi$ exactly when $r \in J$; and every coset $r + J$ is plainly $\psi(r+I)$.
>
> **Why needed:** Feeding the kernel into the first isomorphism theorem makes $J/I$ an ideal of $R/I$ automatically; surjectivity makes the image the full ring $R/J$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Kernel.** By definition $\ker\psi = \{r + I \in R/I : \psi(r+I) = 0_{R/J}\}$. The zero of $R/J$ is the coset $0_R + J = J$, so $\psi(r+I) = r + J$ equals it exactly when $r + J = J$, i.e. $r \in J$. Hence
> > $$\ker\psi = \{r + I : r \in J\} = J/I.$$
> > In particular, since the kernel of a ring homomorphism is always an ideal of its domain (by Lemma 1 of [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings]]), $J/I$ is an ideal of $R/I$.
> >
> > **Surjectivity.** Take any element of $R/J$; it is a coset $r + J$ for some $r \in R$. Then $r + I \in R/I$ satisfies $\psi(r+I) = r + J$. So every element of $R/J$ is in the image, and $\operatorname{im}\psi = R/J$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a ring and let $I \subseteq J$ be ideals of $R$.
>
> **Step 1 — define the homomorphism.** Define
> $$\psi : R/I \longrightarrow R/J, \qquad \psi(r + I) = r + J.$$
> By Lemma 1 this is well-defined: if $r + I = r' + I$ then $r - r' \in I \subseteq J$, so $r + J = r' + J$. The use of the hypothesis $I \subseteq J$ is exactly here. By Lemma 2, $\psi$ is a ring homomorphism, since multiplication in both $R/I$ and $R/J$ is computed on representatives drawn from $R$.
>
> **Step 2 — compute the kernel.** By Lemma 3, the zero of $R/J$ is the coset $J$, and $\psi(r + I) = r + J = J$ holds exactly when $r \in J$. Hence
> $$\ker\psi = \{r + I : r \in J\} = J/I.$$
> Because the kernel of any ring homomorphism is an ideal of its domain, $J/I$ is an ideal of $R/I$. *(Ideal clause proved.)*
>
> **Step 3 — $\psi$ is surjective.** By Lemma 3, given any coset $r + J \in R/J$, the element $r + I \in R/I$ satisfies $\psi(r + I) = r + J$. Hence $\operatorname{im}\psi = R/J$.
>
> **Step 4 — apply the first isomorphism theorem.** By the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem for rings]] applied to $\psi$,
> $$(R/I)/\ker\psi \;\cong\; \operatorname{im}\psi.$$
> Substituting $\ker\psi = J/I$ from Step 2 and $\operatorname{im}\psi = R/J$ from Step 3,
> $$\frac{R/I}{J/I} \;\cong\; \frac{R}{J}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Cancellation in modular arithmetic.** Take $R = \mathbb{Z}$, $J = d\mathbb{Z}$, and $I = n\mathbb{Z}$ where $d \mid n$ (so $I \subseteq J$). The theorem gives $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})/(d\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/d\mathbb{Z}$. This is the statement that reducing mod $n$ and then further reducing mod $d$ is the same as reducing mod $d$ outright — the everyday fact "if you know a number mod $12$, you know it mod $4$, and the mod-$4$ information is a clean quotient of the mod-$12$ ring". The application is non-obvious only because the schoolchild's mod-reduction is rarely *named* as the third isomorphism theorem, yet that is exactly what makes the two-stage reduction coherent.

**Truncating power series or jets.** In $k[X]$, the ideals $(X^n) \subseteq (X^m)$ for $m \le n$ are nested, and the theorem gives $\big(k[X]/(X^n)\big)/\big((X^m)/(X^n)\big) \cong k[X]/(X^m)$. The ring $k[X]/(X^m)$ is the ring of $m$-jets — polynomials remembered only up to order $m$. The theorem says: if you have remembered a function to order $n$ (a fine jet), you can forget down to order $m$ (a coarse jet), and the coarse jet ring is a quotient of the fine one. This is the algebraic backbone of Taylor truncation, and the non-obvious recognition is that "truncate further" is "quotient by a larger power ideal".

**Successive quotients in the construction of finite fields.** To build $\mathbb{F}_{p^2}$ one takes $\mathbb{F}_p[X]/(f)$ for an irreducible quadratic $f$. If a computation first passes to an intermediate quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(p)$ and later imposes $f$, the third isomorphism theorem (with $I = (p)$, $J = (p, f)$ inside $\mathbb{Z}[X]$) certifies that $\big(\mathbb{Z}[X]/(p)\big)/\big((p,f)/(p)\big) \cong \mathbb{Z}[X]/(p, f) \cong \mathbb{F}_p[X]/(\bar f)$. The application is non-obvious because the two relations "$p = 0$" and "$f = 0$" are imposed at different times, and the theorem is what guarantees the order does not matter.

**Quotient stacks of relations in presentations.** Given a ring presented by generators and relations, adding a further relation to an already-presented quotient corresponds to a nested pair of ideals $I \subseteq J$ in the free ring $R$. The third isomorphism theorem guarantees that the doubly presented ring $(R/I)/(J/I)$ equals the singly presented $R/J$ — so a presentation can be developed incrementally, relation by relation, without changing the final ring. This is an out-of-distribution use because it is a *metatheorem about presentations* rather than a computation, yet it is a direct reading of the cancellation law.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings]]** — the parent. The third isomorphism theorem is the first theorem applied to the single coarsening homomorphism $\psi : R/I \to R/J$, $r + I \mapsto r + J$. Its two assertions — that $J/I$ is an ideal and that the double quotient is $R/J$ — are the kernel and the induced isomorphism of that one surjective map.

- **[[Thm - Second Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|Second Isomorphism Theorem for Rings]]** — the sibling. Both are corollaries of the first theorem via a clever choice of homomorphism: the second restricts a quotient map to a subring, the third *composes* the quotient maps $R \to R/I$ and $R \to R/J$ into the coarsening map. Together they are the standard toolkit for manipulating iterated quotients.

- **[[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|Ideal Correspondence]]** — the indispensable companion. The correspondence is what *names* the outer ideal of a double quotient: every ideal of $R/I$ is $J/I$ for a unique $I \subseteq J \trianglelefteq R$. The third isomorphism theorem then identifies the corresponding quotient as $R/J$. Read together, they say the quotients of $R/I$ are exactly the quotients $R/J$ of $R$ with $J \supseteq I$.

- **[[Thm - Third Isomorphism Theorem|Third Isomorphism Theorem for Groups]]** — the prototype in another category. The group statement $(G/K)/(L/K) \cong G/L$ for normal subgroups $K \subseteq L$ becomes the ring statement by replacing "normal subgroup" with "ideal"; the proof — apply the first isomorphism theorem to the coarsening map — is identical.
