# Notation

Throughout, $F$ is a [[Def - Unit and Field|field]] — a commutative ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse — and $F[X]$ is the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] in one variable over $F$. For a nonzero polynomial $f = a_0 + a_1 X + \dots + a_n X^n$ with $a_n \neq 0$, the **degree** is $\deg f = n$ and the **leading coefficient** is $a_n$; by convention $\deg 0 = -\infty$. We call $f$ the **dividend**, $g$ the **divisor**, $q$ the **quotient**, and $r$ the **remainder**. The phrase "$\deg r < \deg g$" is read so that $r = 0$ (degree $-\infty$) always satisfies it. The symbol $\trianglelefteq$ means "is an ideal of". The full symbol registry is on the parent page [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2]].

# Statement

> **Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials (division algorithm).** Let $F$ be a field and let $f, g \in F[X]$ with $g \neq 0$. Then there exist polynomials $q, r \in F[X]$ such that
> $$f = g q + r, \qquad \text{with } \deg r < \deg g \ \text{ (or } r = 0\text{)}.$$
> (The quotient $q$ and remainder $r$ are in fact unique, though the existence statement above is what the proof below establishes.)

---

# Motivation

The integers have a hidden engine: long division. Given any two integers, you can divide one by the other and get a quotient and a *strictly smaller* remainder, $a = bq + r$ with $0 \le r < |b|$. Almost every structural fact about $\mathbb{Z}$ — that every ideal is $n\mathbb{Z}$, that greatest common divisors exist and are integer combinations, that $\mathbb{Z}$ has unique factorisation — is downstream of that one capability. The natural question is: which *other* rings have a long-division engine, and do they inherit the same good behaviour?

The polynomial ring $F[X]$ over a field is the most important ring, after $\mathbb{Z}$ itself, that does. This theorem is the statement of its long-division engine. The only change from the integer case is the measure of size: where $\mathbb{Z}$ uses absolute value, $F[X]$ uses *degree*. "The remainder is smaller than the divisor" becomes "$\deg r < \deg g$". With that single substitution, the entire machinery of $\mathbb{Z}$ transplants to $F[X]$: every ideal of $F[X]$ is principal, generated by one polynomial; greatest common divisors exist and are polynomial combinations; $F[X]$ has unique factorisation into irreducibles. **This theorem is the reason $F[X]$ has all of its good properties** — it is the foundational fact from which the structure theory of polynomial rings is derived. When the source proves that every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ is principal, the proof "performs the Euclidean algorithm"; the very same proof, with this theorem in hand, shows every ideal of $F[X]$ is principal.

The motivation also makes plain *why the field hypothesis is not optional*. Look at how long division actually proceeds: to kill the top-degree term of the dividend $f$, you subtract a suitable multiple of the divisor $g$, and the multiplier you need is $a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m}$ — it contains the inverse of the leading coefficient of $g$. **You must be able to divide by the leading coefficient of the divisor.** In a field, every nonzero element is a unit, so $b_m^{-1}$ always exists and the algorithm always runs. Over a ring that is not a field this fails: in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, you cannot divide $X$ by $2X$ — there is no polynomial $q$ and constant remainder with $X = 2X \cdot q + r$, because that would need $\tfrac12 \in \mathbb{Z}$. The field hypothesis is exactly the hypothesis that makes the one essential subtraction step legal. (One can recover a weaker "pseudo-division" over a general ring by pre-multiplying $f$ by a power of $b_m$, but the clean theorem as stated needs a field.)

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The literal precondition is "a field $F$ and polynomials $f, g \in F[X]$ with $g \neq 0$". The source question is: when does a problem present a situation in which invoking polynomial division is the right move, even though no division is asked for?

The first disguised source is **a problem about the ideals of $F[X]$, or about an ideal of $F[X]$ presented by generators**. Whenever you must understand an ideal $\mathcal{I} \trianglelefteq F[X]$ — show it is principal, find a generator, decide membership — the move is: take a *nonzero element of least degree* in $\mathcal{I}$ and divide every other element by it. The remainder, having smaller degree, is forced to be zero, so $\mathcal{I}$ is generated by that least-degree element. The non-obvious step is recognising that "ideal of $F[X]$" is a cue to deploy the division algorithm — the theorem is the bridge from "$\mathcal{I}$ is an ideal" to "$\mathcal{I}$ is principal". *Example problem:* show $(X^2 + 1, X^3 - X) = (X^2 + 1)$ in $\mathbb{R}[X]$ by dividing the second generator by the first.

The second disguised source is **a quotient ring $F[X]/(g)$ whose elements you must pin down**. To work in $F[X]/(g)$ you need canonical representatives for the cosets. The division algorithm supplies them: every $f$ equals $gq + r$ with $\deg r < \deg g$, so every coset $f + (g)$ equals $r + (g)$ for a *unique* remainder $r$ of degree less than $\deg g$. The non-obvious recognition is that "I need to understand $F[X]/(g)$" should trigger "divide by $g$ to get normal-form representatives". *Example problem:* show every element of $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1)$ is uniquely $a + bX$ — exactly the source's computation, which is just the division algorithm applied with $g = X^2 + 1$.

The third disguised source is **a question about whether a polynomial has a given root, or shares a root with another polynomial**. Root questions are remainder questions: $\alpha$ is a root of $f$ if and only if $X - \alpha$ divides $f$ if and only if the remainder of $f$ on division by $X - \alpha$ is zero — and that remainder is the constant $f(\alpha)$. More generally, the greatest common divisor of $f$ and $g$, computed by iterating the division algorithm, encodes their common roots. The non-obvious step is translating "root" or "common root" into "remainder" or "gcd". *Example problem:* prove a polynomial of degree $n$ over a field has at most $n$ roots, by repeatedly dividing out linear factors $X - \alpha$.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusion is a single equation $f = gq + r$ with $\deg r < \deg g$. Combined with other facts it does a great deal.

Combine the conclusion with **the least-degree element of an ideal**. If $\mathcal{I} \trianglelefteq F[X]$ is nonzero and $g$ is a nonzero element of $\mathcal{I}$ of minimal degree, then for any $f \in \mathcal{I}$ the division algorithm gives $f = gq + r$; since $f \in \mathcal{I}$ and $gq \in \mathcal{I}$, also $r = f - gq \in \mathcal{I}$, and $\deg r < \deg g$ contradicts minimality unless $r = 0$. The further result $E$ is that $\mathcal{I} = (g)$: **every ideal of $F[X]$ is principal**, i.e. $F[X]$ is a principal ideal domain. This is non-obvious because the division equation, an arithmetic statement about two polynomials, is being leveraged into a structural statement about *all* ideals at once.

Combine the conclusion with **iteration (the gcd loop)**. Repeatedly dividing — $f$ by $g$, then $g$ by the remainder, then that divisor by the next remainder — produces a strictly decreasing sequence of degrees, which must terminate. The last nonzero remainder is a greatest common divisor of $f$ and $g$. The further result is Bézout's identity for polynomials: $\gcd(f,g) = af + bg$ for some $a, b \in F[X]$, recovered by back-substitution. This is non-obvious because a single division step seems to relate only two polynomials, yet iterating it manufactures the gcd *and* expresses it as a combination.

Combine the conclusion with **the divisor being linear**, $g = X - \alpha$. Then the remainder $r$ has degree $< 1$, so it is a constant; evaluating $f = (X - \alpha)q + r$ at $X = \alpha$ gives $r = f(\alpha)$. The further result $E$ is the **factor theorem**: $\alpha$ is a root of $f$ exactly when $(X - \alpha) \mid f$. Iterating, a degree-$n$ polynomial over a field has at most $n$ roots. This is non-obvious because the abstract division statement specialises, with $g$ linear, into the single most-used elementary fact about polynomial roots.

Combine the conclusion with **uniqueness**. The quotient and remainder are unique: if $gq_1 + r_1 = gq_2 + r_2$ with both remainders of degree $< \deg g$, then $g(q_1 - q_2) = r_2 - r_1$ has degree $< \deg g$, forcing $q_1 = q_2$ and $r_1 = r_2$ (a nonzero multiple of $g$ has degree $\ge \deg g$). The further result is that "remainder mod $g$" is a *well-defined function*, which is what lets $F[X]/(g)$ be computed by reduction to normal form.

# Why Is It True

Picture long division as you learned it for numbers, then transcribe it to polynomials. You have a dividend $f$ and a divisor $g$, and you want to whittle $f$ down by subtracting multiples of $g$ until what is left is too small — too low in degree — to subtract from any more.

Look at the top of $f$: its leading term is $a_n X^n$. Look at the top of $g$: its leading term is $b_m X^m$. If $f$ is at least as tall as $g$, that is $n \ge m$, you can knock the top term off $f$ in one stroke. Subtract from $f$ the polynomial
$$a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} \cdot g.$$
This multiple of $g$ has leading term $a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} \cdot b_m X^m = a_n X^n$ — exactly the leading term of $f$. So in the difference $f_1 = f - a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} g$, the $X^n$ terms cancel, and $f_1$ has *strictly smaller degree than $f$*. You have made $f$ shorter, at the cost of recording one term of the quotient.

Two things make this step go, and both are worth naming. First, you needed $b_m^{-1}$ — and this is the *entire* role of the field hypothesis. In a field every nonzero element is invertible, so $b_m^{-1}$ exists and the cancelling multiplier $a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m}$ is a legitimate polynomial. Strip away the field hypothesis and this step can simply fail to be performable: over $\mathbb{Z}$ you cannot cancel the leading $X$ of $f = X$ against the leading $2X$ of $g = 2X$, because $\tfrac12 \notin \mathbb{Z}$. Second, the step strictly *decreases the degree* of the thing you are working on. Degree is a non-negative integer (when the polynomial is nonzero), and a non-negative integer cannot decrease forever. So if you keep performing the step, you must eventually reach a polynomial whose degree has dropped below $\deg g$ — and at that point you can subtract no more. That stuck polynomial is the remainder $r$, and the sum of all the multipliers you subtracted is the quotient $q$.

That is the whole theorem. It is *induction on the degree of $f$* dressed as long division: either $\deg f < \deg g$ already, and you are done immediately with $q = 0$, $r = f$; or you do one cancellation step to get a strictly smaller $f_1$, apply the result to $f_1$ inductively, and fold the one recorded term back into the quotient. There is nothing to be clever about — the only ideas are "cancel the top term" and "degree cannot fall forever", and the field is there precisely so that "cancel the top term" is always legal.

# What Makes This Hard

The proof is a routine induction on $\deg f$, so the difficulty is not the argument but two bookkeeping traps. Most people stumble on the base case and the induction frame: you induct on the dividend's degree with the divisor $g$ fixed, and the case $\deg f < \deg g$ (handled by $q = 0$, $r = f$) is the base case, not a side remark — forgetting it leaves the induction with no floor. The non-obvious-in-hindsight load-bearing step is that the field hypothesis enters at exactly one point, the multiplier $a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m}$, and the most common error is to wave it through without noticing that $b_m^{-1}$ is precisely what would not exist over $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — so a "proof" that never mentions invertibility of the leading coefficient has not actually used the hypothesis and would wrongly prove the false statement over general rings.

---

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Fix the divisor $g$ and induct on $\deg f$. The base case is $\deg f < \deg g$. The inductive step kills the leading term of $f$ by subtracting the multiple $a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} g$ of $g$ — this is the only place the field is used — producing a strictly-lower-degree polynomial to which the inductive hypothesis applies.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Set up the induction.** Fix $g \neq 0$ of degree $m$. Induct on $n = \deg f$.
   - *Hint:* The statement to be proved, for each $n$, is "every $f$ of degree $\le n$ admits $q, r$ with $f = gq + r$ and $\deg r < m$".
   - *Why needed:* The divisor must be held fixed; only the dividend's degree decreases, so only it is a sound induction variable.

2. **Base case.** Handle $\deg f < \deg g$.
   - *Hint:* Take $q = 0$ and $r = f$; then $f = g \cdot 0 + f$ and $\deg r = \deg f < \deg g$ by assumption.
   - *Why needed:* Every chain of degree-reductions ends in this case; without it the induction has no floor.

3. **The cancellation step.** For $n = \deg f \ge m = \deg g$, form $f_1 = f - a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} g$ and show $\deg f_1 < n$.
   - *Hint:* The subtracted multiple has leading term $a_n X^n$, identical to that of $f$, so the $X^n$ terms cancel. The factor $b_m^{-1}$ exists because $F$ is a field.
   - *Why needed:* This is the engine — it strictly reduces the working degree and is the sole point where the field hypothesis is used.

4. **Apply the inductive hypothesis to $f_1$.** Obtain $q_1, r_1$ with $f_1 = g q_1 + r_1$, $\deg r_1 < m$.
   - *Hint:* $\deg f_1 < n$, so $f_1$ is covered by the inductive hypothesis (degree strictly smaller).
   - *Why needed:* It divides the smaller polynomial $f_1$, which is all that remains.

5. **Reassemble.** Substitute back to get $q$ and $r$ for $f$.
   - *Hint:* $f = a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} g + f_1 = a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} g + g q_1 + r_1 = g\big(a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} + q_1\big) + r_1$, so take $q = a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} + q_1$ and $r = r_1$.
   - *Why needed:* Produces the quotient and remainder for the original $f$, completing the inductive step.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Degree of a product, and degree under subtraction
> **Statement:** For nonzero $p, q \in F[X]$ with $F$ a field, $\deg(pq) = \deg p + \deg q$. Consequently a nonzero multiple of $g$ has degree $\ge \deg g$, and if $p$ and $q$ have equal degree and equal leading coefficient then $\deg(p - q) < \deg p$.
>
> **Hint:** The leading coefficient of $pq$ is the product of the leading coefficients; in a field that product is nonzero.
>
> **Why needed:** It is what guarantees the cancellation step strictly lowers the degree, and (in the uniqueness argument) that a nonzero multiple of $g$ cannot have small degree.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Write $p = a_k X^k + \dots$ with $a_k \neq 0$ and $q = b_l X^l + \dots$ with $b_l \neq 0$. The term of highest possible degree in $pq$ is $a_k b_l X^{k+l}$, and every other term has degree strictly less than $k + l$. Since $F$ is a field, it has no zero divisors, so $a_k b_l \neq 0$. Hence $X^{k+l}$ genuinely appears with nonzero coefficient, and $\deg(pq) = k + l = \deg p + \deg q$.
> >
> > In particular, for nonzero $q$, $\deg(gq) = \deg g + \deg q \ge \deg g$: a nonzero multiple of $g$ has degree at least $\deg g$.
> >
> > Finally, if $p$ and $q$ are nonzero with $\deg p = \deg q = d$ and the same leading coefficient $c$, then in $p - q$ the $X^d$ terms are $cX^d - cX^d = 0$, while all other terms had degree $< d$; so every surviving term has degree $< d$, giving $\deg(p - q) < d$ (with the convention $\deg 0 = -\infty$ covering $p - q = 0$).

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The leading term of $f$ can be cancelled by a multiple of $g$
> **Statement:** Let $f, g \in F[X]$ be nonzero with $\deg f = n \ge m = \deg g$, leading coefficients $a_n$ and $b_m$. Set $h = a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m}$. Then $f - hg$ has degree strictly less than $n$.
>
> **Hint:** Compute the leading term of $hg$ and check it equals the leading term of $f$.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the single inductive step of the whole theorem, and the only place the field hypothesis is invoked.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Because $F$ is a field and $b_m \neq 0$ (it is the leading coefficient of the nonzero polynomial $g$), the inverse $b_m^{-1} \in F$ exists. Hence $h = a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m}$ is a genuine element of $F[X]$ — and note $n - m \ge 0$, so $X^{n-m}$ is a legitimate monomial.
> >
> > The polynomial $hg$ has degree $(n - m) + m = n$ by Lemma 1, and its leading term is
> > $$\big(a_n b_m^{-1}\big) \cdot b_m \cdot X^{(n-m)+m} = a_n b_m^{-1} b_m X^n = a_n X^n,$$
> > which is exactly the leading term of $f$. Thus $f$ and $hg$ are both of degree $n$ with the same leading coefficient $a_n$. By the last clause of Lemma 1, $\deg(f - hg) < n$.

---

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $F$ be a field, $g \in F[X]$ with $g \neq 0$, and write $m = \deg g$ with leading coefficient $b_m \neq 0$. We prove, by strong induction on $n$, the statement: *for every $f \in F[X]$ with $\deg f \le n$ there exist $q, r \in F[X]$ with $f = gq + r$ and $\deg r < m$.* The divisor $g$ is held fixed throughout.
>
> **Base case / non-inductive case — $\deg f < m$.** If $f$ has degree less than $m$ (this includes $f = 0$), put $q = 0$ and $r = f$. Then $f = g \cdot 0 + f$ and $\deg r = \deg f < m$, as required. In particular this settles every $f$ with $\deg f \le n$ whenever $n < m$, giving the base of the induction.
>
> **Inductive step — $\deg f = n \ge m$.** Assume the statement holds for all polynomials of degree strictly less than $n$. Let $f$ be nonzero of degree exactly $n \ge m$, with leading coefficient $a_n \neq 0$. (If $\deg f < m$ we are in the base case above.)
>
> Define
> $$h = a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} \in F[X],$$
> which exists because $F$ is a field and $b_m \neq 0$, so $b_m^{-1}$ exists; and $n - m \ge 0$. Set
> $$f_1 = f - hg.$$
> By Lemma 2, $hg$ has degree $n$ and the same leading coefficient $a_n$ as $f$, so the $X^n$ terms cancel and
> $$\deg f_1 < n.$$
>
> Since $\deg f_1 < n$, the inductive hypothesis applies to $f_1$: there exist $q_1, r_1 \in F[X]$ with
> $$f_1 = g q_1 + r_1, \qquad \deg r_1 < m.$$
>
> Substituting $f_1 = f - hg$ and rearranging,
> $$f = hg + f_1 = hg + g q_1 + r_1 = g\,(h + q_1) + r_1.$$
> Therefore, setting
> $$q = h + q_1 = a_n b_m^{-1} X^{n-m} + q_1, \qquad r = r_1,$$
> we have $f = gq + r$ with $\deg r = \deg r_1 < m$. This completes the inductive step, and with it the proof of existence.
>
> **Uniqueness.** Suppose $f = g q_1 + r_1 = g q_2 + r_2$ with $\deg r_1, \deg r_2 < m$. Then
> $$g\,(q_1 - q_2) = r_2 - r_1.$$
> The right-hand side has degree $< m$ (a difference of two polynomials each of degree $< m$). If $q_1 - q_2 \neq 0$, then by Lemma 1 the left-hand side $g(q_1 - q_2)$ has degree $\ge \deg g = m$, a contradiction. Hence $q_1 - q_2 = 0$, so $q_1 = q_2$, and then $r_2 - r_1 = g \cdot 0 = 0$, so $r_1 = r_2$. The quotient and remainder are unique. $\qquad \blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Every ideal of $F[X]$ is principal.** This is the headline downstream payoff. Let $\mathcal{I} \trianglelefteq F[X]$ be a nonzero ideal and let $g$ be a nonzero element of $\mathcal{I}$ of least degree. For any $f \in \mathcal{I}$, divide: $f = gq + r$ with $\deg r < \deg g$. Since $f \in \mathcal{I}$ and $gq \in \mathcal{I}$ (ideals absorb multiplication), $r = f - gq \in \mathcal{I}$; minimality of $\deg g$ forces $r = 0$, so $f = gq \in (g)$. Hence $\mathcal{I} = (g)$. The application is non-obvious because the theorem speaks of *two* polynomials but is here applied to *every* element of an ideal against one fixed least-degree generator, converting an arithmetic fact into the statement that $F[X]$ is a principal ideal domain — the property the source promises but defers.

**The minimal polynomial of an algebraic element.** Let $\alpha$ be an element of some field extension of $F$ that satisfies *some* nonzero polynomial over $F$. The set of all polynomials in $F[X]$ killing $\alpha$ is an ideal; by the previous paragraph it is principal, generated by a polynomial $\mu$ of least degree — the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$. The division algorithm shows $\mu \mid p$ for every $p$ that kills $\alpha$. This is non-obvious as an application because "minimal polynomial" sounds like a definition handed down from on high, whereas it is *forced into existence* by polynomial division producing a principal generator.

**A polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n$ roots.** Specialise the divisor to a linear polynomial $X - \alpha$. Division gives $f = (X - \alpha)q + r$ with $r$ constant; evaluating at $\alpha$ shows $r = f(\alpha)$, so $\alpha$ is a root precisely when $(X-\alpha) \mid f$. Each distinct root strips off a linear factor and drops the degree by one, so a degree-$n$ polynomial can have at most $n$ roots. The application is non-obvious because this elementary, ubiquitous fact is rarely presented as a *corollary of a division algorithm* — yet it is exactly the linear-divisor case of this theorem, and it fails over rings with zero divisors (e.g. $X^2 - 1$ has four roots in $\mathbb{Z}/8\mathbb{Z}$, a non-field).

**Lagrange interpolation and the Chinese Remainder Theorem for polynomials.** Given distinct points $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_k \in F$, the linear polynomials $X - \alpha_i$ are pairwise coprime — their gcd, computable by the iterated division algorithm, is a unit. The polynomial Chinese Remainder Theorem then gives $F[X]/\prod(X - \alpha_i) \cong \prod F[X]/(X - \alpha_i) \cong F^k$, which is the structural content of Lagrange interpolation: a polynomial of degree $< k$ is determined by its values at $k$ points. The application is out-of-distribution because interpolation is usually taught as a formula, but its existence and uniqueness rest on coprimality, which rests on the Euclidean algorithm.

# Bridges

- **Euclidean algorithm in $\mathbb{Z}$** — the exact prototype. The integer division statement "$a = bq + r$ with $0 \le r < |b|$" becomes the polynomial statement by replacing the size measure $|\cdot|$ with $\deg$. Both equip their ring with a *Euclidean function*, and a ring carrying such a function is a Euclidean domain; $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F[X]$ are the two basic examples, and this theorem is the proof that $F[X]$ qualifies.

- **Euclidean domain $\Rightarrow$ principal ideal domain $\Rightarrow$ unique factorisation domain** — the chain this theorem feeds. The division algorithm makes $F[X]$ a Euclidean domain; the least-degree-generator argument promotes that to "principal ideal domain"; and every principal ideal domain has unique factorisation. So this single theorem is the root of the entire structure theory of $F[X]$.

- **[[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings]]** — the partner in identifying quotient rings. The division algorithm supplies unique normal-form representatives ($\deg r < \deg g$) for the cosets of $F[X]/(g)$, and the first isomorphism theorem then identifies that quotient ring with a known ring; together they are how one proves $\mathbb{R}[X]/(X^2+1) \cong \mathbb{C}$.

- **[[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|Ideal Correspondence]]** — a structural companion. Once the division algorithm shows every ideal of $F[X]$ is principal, the ideal correspondence describes the ideals of a quotient $F[X]/(g)$ as the principal ideals $(d)/(g)$ for divisors $d$ of $g$ — so the ideal lattice of $F[X]/(g)$ is the divisor lattice of $g$.

- **The factor theorem and root-counting** — the linear-divisor specialisation. Taking $g = X - \alpha$ collapses this theorem into "$\alpha$ is a root of $f$ if and only if $(X - \alpha) \mid f$", from which "degree $n$ $\Rightarrow$ at most $n$ roots" follows by iteration.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] $F[X]$ is a Principal Ideal Domain *(from Commutative Algebra)*
> The least-degree-element argument, powered by this theorem, shows every ideal of $F[X]$ is principal. This is the gateway to the structure theory of polynomial rings: principal ideal domains have greatest common divisors, Bézout identities, and unique factorisation into irreducibles.

> [!tip] Field Extensions and Minimal Polynomials *(from Field Theory)*
> Because polynomials killing an algebraic element $\alpha$ form a principal ideal, $\alpha$ has a minimal polynomial $\mu$, and $F[\alpha] \cong F[X]/(\mu)$. Polynomial division is the computational engine of the whole theory of algebraic field extensions.
