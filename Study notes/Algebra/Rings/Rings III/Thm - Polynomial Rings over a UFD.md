# Notation

Throughout, $R$ is a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]] (UFD) and $F = \operatorname{Frac}(R)$ is its [[Def - Field of Fractions|field of fractions]]. We work in the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] $R[X]$, with $R[X] \subseteq F[X]$. For $f \in R[X]$, the **content** $c(f)$ is a [[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial|gcd of the coefficients]], defined up to a [[Def - Unit and Field|unit]] of $R$; $f$ is **[[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial|primitive]]** when $c(f)$ is a unit, and every non-zero $f$ has a **content–primitive decomposition** $f = c(f) f_1$ with $f_1$ primitive. The symbol $\sim$ means "associate". An element is **[[Def - Irreducible and Prime Elements|irreducible]]** in a ring if it is a non-zero non-unit not expressible as a product of two non-units. Recall the units of $R[X]$ are exactly the units of $R$ (since $R$ is a domain), while the units of $F[X]$ are all of $F^\times$. The full symbol registry is on [[Rings III — §2.5–2.6]].

# Statement

> **Polynomial [[Def - Ring|rings]] over a UFD are UFDs.** If $R$ is a [[Def - Unique Factorization Domain|unique factorization domain]], then the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] $R[X]$ is also a unique factorization domain.
>
> By induction, if $R$ is a UFD then so is the multivariable polynomial ring $R[X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n]$ for every $n \geq 1$.

In particular, $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a UFD, and $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is a UFD for any field $F$. Since $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is **not** a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] — the ideal $(2, X)$ is not principal — this theorem produces the first natural examples of UFDs that are not PIDs.

---

# Motivation

By the time one meets this theorem, the supply of known UFDs is meagre and uniform. We know $\mathbb{Z}$ is a UFD (the fundamental theorem of arithmetic), and we know $F[X]$ is a UFD for $F$ a field — but in both cases for the *same structural reason*: each is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]], hence a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], hence a UFD by [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|the PID–UFD theorem]]. So every UFD we have is a PID. This raises a worry: **is "UFD" just a roundabout name for "PID"?** If the only way to be a UFD is to be a PID, the concept of UFD is not earning its keep as a separate definition.

This theorem is the decisive evidence that UFD is a genuinely broader notion. It exhibits UFDs that are *provably not PIDs*. The ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is the cleanest witness. It is a UFD by this theorem — its base ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is a UFD — yet it is not a PID, because the ideal $(2, X)$ of polynomials with even constant term cannot be generated by a single element. So unique factorization is a *strictly weaker and more widespread* property than having all [[Def - Ideal|ideals]] principal. The hierarchy
$$\text{Euclidean domain} \;\subsetneq\; \text{PID} \;\subsetneq\; \text{UFD}$$
has all inclusions strict, and this theorem is what proves the second inclusion strict.

There is also a constructive motive. We would like a *closure property*: a way to manufacture new UFDs from old ones. Number theory and algebraic geometry are full of polynomial rings — coordinate rings of affine space, rings like $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ that arise in studying algebraic integers — and we want to know these rings have good factorization theory. This theorem delivers exactly that: UFD-ness *propagates upward through polynomial extensions*. Start with any UFD and you may freely adjoin indeterminates, as many as you like, and unique factorization survives. Combined with the base cases $\mathbb{Z}$ and $F$, this single theorem certifies unique factorization for an entire universe of rings — every $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$, every $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ — that no amount of PID theory could reach, because almost none of these rings are PIDs.

Why should one *expect* the theorem to be true? The intuition is the content–primitive split. Any polynomial $f \in R[X]$ breaks into a *scalar part* $c(f) \in R$ and a *primitive part* $f_1$. The scalar part lives in $R$, which is a UFD by hypothesis, so it factors uniquely there. The primitive part is a content-free polynomial, and — this is the gift of [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] — its factorization is governed by the *field* polynomial ring $F[X]$, which is a UFD because $F$ is a field. So $f$ inherits unique factorization from two separate UFDs, one handling its scalar half and one its polynomial half. The theorem is the statement that these two halves can always be combined consistently.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The hypothesis is "$R$ is a UFD". The recognition skill is spotting that the base ring is a UFD, sometimes through several layers.

The first disguised source is **a base ring that is a PID, or Euclidean**. The property $B$ is "$R$ is a PID" (or Euclidean). By [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|the PID–UFD theorem]], $R$ is then a UFD, and this theorem makes $R[X]$ a UFD. The non-obvious part is the *two-step* upgrade: an algorithmic or ideal-theoretic hypothesis on $R$ certifies a factorization property of the much larger ring $R[X]$. *Example problem:* $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a UFD because $\mathbb{Z}$ is Euclidean, hence a PID, hence a UFD.

The second disguised source is **a polynomial ring used as the base ring**. The property $B$ is "$R = S[X_1, \dots, X_{n-1}]$ for some UFD $S$". The bridge is the theorem applied once already: $R$ is a UFD by a previous instance of this very theorem, so a further indeterminate may be adjoined. This is the recursion that yields the multivariable corollary. *Example problem:* $F[X, Y, Z]$ is a UFD — peel off one variable at a time, each peel justified by this theorem.

The third disguised source is **a field, viewed as a (degenerate) UFD**. The property $B$ is "$R$ is a field". A field is trivially a UFD — it has no non-zero non-units to factor. So this theorem applies and $F[X]$ is a UFD. The non-obviousness is that this recovers, as a special case, the familiar fact that $F[X]$ is a UFD, normally proved via the Euclidean algorithm — here it falls out of the general theorem with the base case being vacuous. *Example problem:* re-derive that $\mathbb{R}[X]$ is a UFD by applying this theorem to the field $\mathbb{R}$.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$R[X]$ (hence $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$) is a UFD".

Combine the conclusion with **the search for examples separating UFD from PID**. A polynomial ring in $\geq 1$ variable over a ring that is not a field, or in $\geq 2$ variables over any ring, is essentially never a PID, yet this theorem makes it a UFD. The further result $E$ is a rich stock of UFDs-that-are-not-PIDs — $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, $F[X, Y]$, $\mathbb{Z}[X, Y]$ — proving the inclusion PID $\subsetneq$ UFD strict. Non-obvious because the theorem is invoked precisely to *separate* two notions that the available examples had conflated.

Combine the conclusion with **the demand for greatest common divisors of polynomials**. Since $R[X]$ is now a UFD, gcds and lcms of polynomials over $R$ exist and are unique up to associates. The further result $E$ is the full divisibility calculus in $R[X]$ — coprimality, gcd, lcm — *without* needing $R[X]$ to be Euclidean or a PID. Non-obvious because gcd existence is usually associated with the Euclidean algorithm, which $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ lacks; the UFD route supplies gcds anyway.

Combine the conclusion with **a question about irreducibility of a multivariable polynomial**. In the UFD $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$, an element is irreducible if and only if it is prime, so an irreducible polynomial detects which factor of any product it divides. The further result $E$: Euclid-style divisibility arguments, and the well-definedness of "the irreducible factorization", become available for multivariable polynomials. Non-obvious because for $n \geq 2$ the ring is far from a PID, yet the prime = irreducible equivalence (a UFD phenomenon) still holds.

# Why Is It True

The proof is an exercise in *delegation*: every factorization question about $R[X]$ is handed off to one of two rings that already have unique factorization, and the theorem is the bookkeeping that confirms the two answers fit together.

Start with the **content–primitive decomposition**. Any non-zero $f \in R[X]$ splits as $f = c(f)\, f_1$, a scalar $c(f) \in R$ times a primitive polynomial $f_1$. This is a clean separation of $f$ into a part with "no polynomial structure" (the constant $c(f)$) and a part with "no scalar structure" (the content-free $f_1$). The two parts are factored by two different rings.

The scalar part $c(f)$ is just an element of $R$. By hypothesis $R$ is a UFD, so $c(f)$ factors into irreducibles of $R$, uniquely up to order and associates. And an irreducible of $R$ remains irreducible when regarded as a constant polynomial in $R[X]$ — it certainly cannot split into two non-constant polynomials (degrees would not add to zero), nor into two constants without one being a unit. So the scalar half of $f$ is taken care of: it factors uniquely, and we have not even had to think about polynomials.

The primitive part $f_1$ is where the genuine work is, and the key realization is: **do not try to factor it in $R[X]$ directly — factor it in $F[X]$ instead.** The ring $F[X]$, with $F$ a field, is Euclidean, hence a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|PID]], hence a [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|UFD]]. So $f_1$ has a unique factorization into irreducibles *over $F[X]$*. Now [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] is the bridge that drags this $F[X]$-factorization back down to $R[X]$. For *existence*: split $f_1$ over $F$, clear denominators and contents on each $F[X]$-irreducible factor to obtain genuine primitive polynomials of $R[X]$, and Gauss's lemma guarantees these stay irreducible in $R[X]$ — because a primitive polynomial irreducible in $F[X]$ is irreducible in $R[X]$. For *uniqueness*: given two factorizations of the primitive $f_1$ into irreducible primitives of $R[X]$, view them both in $F[X]$; Gauss's lemma says each $R[X]$-irreducible primitive is still irreducible in $F[X]$; and $F[X]$, being a UFD, forces the two factorizations to match up to associates *in $F[X]$*. The only gap is that an $F[X]$-associate (differing by a constant of $F^\times$) need not immediately look like an $R[X]$-associate (differing by a unit of $R$) — but a short content argument closes it: if two primitive polynomials of $R[X]$ are $F^\times$-multiples of each other, the multiplying constant must in fact be a unit of $R$, because both polynomials have unit content.

So the architecture is: **degree-zero part $\to$ delegated to $R$; positive-degree primitive part $\to$ delegated to $F[X]$; Gauss's lemma stitches the $F[X]$ answer back into $R[X]$.** Existence holds because both delegates produce factorizations and Gauss's lemma certifies the descent. Uniqueness holds because both delegates have *unique* factorization and Gauss's lemma certifies that distinct $R[X]$-irreducibles remain distinct over $F[X]$.

One more thing to believe: that the *splitting itself* is consistent — that the content does not interfere with the primitive part. This is precisely content-multiplicativity, $c(fg) \sim c(f) c(g)$, the corollary of Gauss's lemma. It guarantees that when you multiply polynomials, contents multiply among themselves and primitive parts multiply among themselves, with no cross-talk. So the decomposition $f = c(f) f_1$ is not just a one-time trick but is *respected by multiplication*, which is exactly what lets the two delegated factorization theories run in parallel without colliding.

Finally, why does $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ being a UFD-but-not-PID not contradict anything? Because the proof never claims $R[X]$ is a PID. It only claims unique *factorization of elements*. The ideal $(2, X)$ is genuinely non-principal, but $2$ and $X$ still each factor uniquely into irreducibles — irreducibility of elements and principality of ideals are different properties, and this theorem secures the first without the second. That is the whole point: it shows the two properties come apart.

# What Makes This Hard

The conceptual leap most people miss is that the primitive part should be factored in $F[X]$, *not* in $R[X]$ — attacking $R[X]$ directly stalls, and the trick is to delegate to the field polynomial ring and pull back via [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]]. In the uniqueness argument the subtle step is the final descent: two primitive polynomials that are associates *in $F[X]$* (differing by a constant of $F^\times$) must be shown to be associates *in $R[X]$* (differing by a unit of $R$), which needs a content argument — clear denominators, take contents, conclude the constant was a unit of $R$ all along. The most common error is to forget to separate the content first and to try to apply Gauss's lemma to a non-primitive polynomial, where its hypothesis fails.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Split each $f \in R[X]$ as content times primitive part, $f = c(f) f_1$. Factor $c(f)$ in the UFD $R$; factor $f_1$ by first factoring it in the UFD $F[X]$ and using [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] to descend each factor to a primitive irreducible of $R[X]$. For uniqueness, handle the content factors via uniqueness in $R$, and the primitive factors via uniqueness in $F[X]$ plus a content argument to convert $F[X]$-associates into $R[X]$-associates.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Reduce to the primitive case.** Show it suffices to factor primitive polynomials uniquely, after factoring the content.
   - *Hint:* Write $f = c(f) f_1$; factor $c(f) = p_1 \cdots p_n$ in the UFD $R$; each $p_i$ is irreducible in $R[X]$. Uniqueness of this content factorization is uniqueness in $R$.
   - *Why needed:* It peels off the scalar part and isolates the genuine polynomial content.

2. **Existence for primitive polynomials.** Show a primitive $f_1$ is a finite product of irreducibles of $R[X]$.
   - *Hint:* If $f_1$ is not irreducible, $f_1 = f_2 f_3$ with $f_2, f_3$ non-units; as $f_1$ is primitive so are $f_2, f_3$, hence both have *positive degree strictly less than* $\deg f_1$; induct on degree.
   - *Why needed:* It is the existence half of the UFD definition for the primitive part.

3. **Uniqueness for primitive polynomials.** Show two irreducible factorizations of a primitive $f_1$ in $R[X]$ agree up to order and associates.
   - *Hint:* The irreducible factors are primitive (factors of a primitive polynomial); view them in $F[X]$; by Gauss's lemma they stay irreducible there; $F[X]$ is a UFD, so the factorizations match up to $F^\times$-associates; a content argument converts these to $R^\times$-associates.
   - *Why needed:* It is the uniqueness half for the primitive part.

4. **Assemble and induct for many variables.** Combine the content and primitive factorizations; then iterate.
   - *Hint:* $R[X_1, \dots, X_n] = (R[X_1, \dots, X_{n-1}])[X_n]$; the base ring is a UFD by induction; apply the single-variable theorem.
   - *Why needed:* Gives the multivariable corollary.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: An irreducible of $R$ is irreducible as a constant in $R[X]$
> **Statement:** Let $R$ be an integral domain and $p \in R$ irreducible. Then $p$, viewed as a constant polynomial, is irreducible in $R[X]$.
>
> **Hint:** A factorization of the constant $p$ in $R[X]$ has factors whose degrees sum to $0$, so both factors are constants; use irreducibility of $p$ in $R$ and that the units of $R[X]$ are the units of $R$.
>
> **Why needed:** It lets the content factorization $c(f) = p_1 \cdots p_n$, obtained in $R$, count as a genuine factorization into irreducibles *of $R[X]$*.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Suppose $p = gh$ in $R[X]$. Since $R$ is an integral domain, $\deg(gh) = \deg g + \deg h$, and $\deg p = 0$, so $\deg g = \deg h = 0$: both $g, h$ are constants, $g, h \in R$. Thus $p = gh$ is a factorization in $R$, and since $p$ is irreducible in $R$, one of $g, h$ is a unit of $R$. The units of $R[X]$ are exactly the units of $R$ (as $R$ is a domain), so one of $g, h$ is a unit of $R[X]$. Hence $p$ admits no factorization into two non-units of $R[X]$; and $p$ is a non-zero non-unit of $R[X]$ since it is a non-zero non-unit of $R$. So $p$ is irreducible in $R[X]$. $\square$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Existence — every primitive polynomial factors into irreducibles of $R[X]$
> **Statement:** Let $R$ be a UFD and $f_1 \in R[X]$ primitive. Then $f_1$ is a finite product of irreducible elements of $R[X]$.
>
> **Hint:** Induct on $\deg f_1$. If $f_1$ is not irreducible, $f_1 = f_2 f_3$ with $f_2, f_3$ non-units; primitivity forces them non-constant, so $0 < \deg f_2, \deg f_3 < \deg f_1$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the existence half of the UFD property for the primitive part of any polynomial.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Induct on $d = \deg f_1 \geq 0$. If $d = 0$, then $f_1$ is a primitive constant, i.e. a unit of $R$, hence a unit of $R[X]$ — there is nothing to factor (the empty product), and $f_1$ is not a non-zero non-unit, so the claim is vacuous; the genuine content lives in $d \geq 1$.
> >
> > For $d \geq 1$: if $f_1$ is irreducible, it is its own factorization. Otherwise $f_1 = f_2 f_3$ with $f_2, f_3$ both non-units of $R[X]$. Since $f_1$ is primitive, by content-multiplicativity $c(f_1) \sim c(f_2) c(f_3)$ is a unit, so $c(f_2)$ and $c(f_3)$ are units — $f_2$ and $f_3$ are primitive. A primitive *constant* is a unit; since $f_2, f_3$ are non-units they are non-constant, so $\deg f_2, \deg f_3 \geq 1$. As $\deg f_2 + \deg f_3 = \deg f_1 = d$ (degrees add in the domain $R[X]$), we get $\deg f_2, \deg f_3 < d$. By the induction hypothesis each of $f_2, f_3$ is a finite product of irreducibles of $R[X]$; concatenating, so is $f_1$. $\square$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Uniqueness — two primitive factorizations agree up to order and $R[X]$-associates
> **Statement:** Let $R$ be a UFD, $F = \operatorname{Frac}(R)$, and let $f_1 \in R[X]$ be primitive with two factorizations $f_1 = q_1 \cdots q_m = r_1 \cdots r_\ell$ into irreducibles of $R[X]$. Then $m = \ell$ and, after reordering, $q_i \sim r_i$ in $R[X]$ for each $i$.
>
> **Hint:** Each $q_i, r_j$ is primitive (a factor of a primitive polynomial). View them in $F[X]$; Gauss's lemma keeps them irreducible there; $F[X]$ is a UFD so the factorizations match up to constants of $F^\times$; clear denominators and take contents to upgrade $F^\times$-associate to $R^\times$-associate.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the uniqueness half of the UFD property for the primitive part, and it is where Gauss's lemma and the UFD-ness of $F[X]$ are both used.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Each $q_i$ divides the primitive polynomial $f_1$, so $q_i$ is itself primitive: if $c(q_i)$ were a non-unit it would divide $c(f_1)$, contradicting primitivity of $f_1$. Likewise each $r_j$ is primitive. Also each $q_i, r_j$ has positive degree: an irreducible of $R[X]$ that were a constant would be an irreducible of $R$, but then $q_i \mid f_1$ would force $c(f_1)$ to be a non-unit — impossible.
> >
> > Now regard the equation $q_1 \cdots q_m = r_1 \cdots r_\ell$ in $F[X]$. Since $F$ is a field, $F[X]$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Domain|Euclidean domain]], hence a PID, hence a [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|UFD]]. By [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]], each $q_i$ — primitive and irreducible in $R[X]$ — is irreducible in $F[X]$; likewise each $r_j$. So both sides are factorizations of $f_1$ into irreducibles of the UFD $F[X]$. Uniqueness in $F[X]$ gives $m = \ell$ and, after reordering, $r_i = u_i q_i$ for some unit $u_i \in F[X]$, i.e. $u_i \in F^\times$ is a non-zero constant.
> >
> > It remains to show $u_i$ is in fact a unit of $R$. Write $u_i = a_i / b_i$ with $a_i, b_i \in R$, $b_i \neq 0$. From $b_i r_i = a_i q_i$ — an equation in $R[X]$ — take contents: by content-multiplicativity, $c(b_i r_i) \sim b_i\, c(r_i) \sim b_i$ (as $r_i$ is primitive) and $c(a_i q_i) \sim a_i\, c(q_i) \sim a_i$. Hence $a_i \sim b_i$ in $R$, so $u_i = a_i/b_i$ is a unit of $R$. Therefore $r_i \sim q_i$ in $R[X]$, as required. $\square$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $R$ be a UFD with field of fractions $F$. We show $R[X]$ is a UFD. ($R[X]$ is an integral domain since $R$ is.) Lemmas 1–3 above are taken as established; they rest on [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] and its content-multiplicativity corollary.
>
> ---
> **Existence.** Let $f \in R[X]$ be a non-zero non-unit. Using the [[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial|content–primitive decomposition]], write
> $$f = c(f)\, f_1, \qquad f_1 \text{ primitive}.$$
> The content $c(f)$ is an element of the UFD $R$. If $c(f)$ is a non-unit of $R$, factor it there:
> $$c(f) = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n, \qquad p_i \text{ irreducible in } R.$$
> By Lemma 1, each $p_i$ is irreducible in $R[X]$. (If $c(f)$ is a unit, take $n = 0$.) By Lemma 2, the primitive part factors as
> $$f_1 = q_1 q_2 \cdots q_m, \qquad q_j \text{ irreducible in } R[X].$$
> (If $f_1$ is a unit — i.e. $f$ was a non-unit constant — then $m = 0$ and $n \geq 1$.) Concatenating,
> $$f = p_1 \cdots p_n\, q_1 \cdots q_m$$
> is a finite product of irreducibles of $R[X]$. Since $f$ is a non-unit, $n + m \geq 1$, so the product is non-empty. Existence holds.
>
> ---
> **Uniqueness.** Suppose
> $$p_1 \cdots p_n\, q_1 \cdots q_m \;=\; p_1' \cdots p_{n'}'\, q_1' \cdots q_{m'}'$$
> are two factorizations of the same $f$ into irreducibles of $R[X]$, where the $p_i, p_i'$ are the *constant* irreducible factors (irreducibles of $R$) and the $q_j, q_j'$ are the *positive-degree* irreducible factors. We first argue this constant/positive-degree separation is intrinsic: an irreducible of $R[X]$ is either a constant — necessarily an irreducible of $R$ — or has positive degree, since a positive-degree factor cannot be a unit. So every irreducible factor on either side is classified, and the two classes do not mix.
>
> Take contents of both sides. Each positive-degree irreducible $q_j$ is primitive (an irreducible of $R[X]$ of positive degree that were imprimitive would split off a non-unit constant content, contradicting irreducibility). By content-multiplicativity, the content of the right side is $\sim p_1' \cdots p_{n'}'$ and of the left side is $\sim p_1 \cdots p_n$ (the primitive factors contributing unit content). Hence
> $$p_1 \cdots p_n \;\sim\; p_1' \cdots p_{n'}' \quad \text{in } R.$$
> Both are factorizations of (an associate of) $c(f)$ into irreducibles of the UFD $R$, so by uniqueness in $R$, $n = n'$ and, after reordering, $p_i \sim p_i'$ in $R$ — hence in $R[X]$.
>
> Cancelling the matched content factors (legal: $R[X]$ is a domain) leaves
> $$q_1 \cdots q_m \;\sim\; q_1' \cdots q_{m'}',$$
> an equality, up to a unit of $R$, of two factorizations of a primitive polynomial into positive-degree irreducibles of $R[X]$. By Lemma 3, $m = m'$ and, after reordering, $q_j \sim q_j'$ in $R[X]$.
>
> Combining: the two original factorizations have the same number of factors ($n + m = n' + m'$) and, after reordering, corresponding factors are associates in $R[X]$. Uniqueness holds.
>
> ---
> **Conclusion.** $R[X]$ has existence and uniqueness of factorization into irreducibles, so $R[X]$ is a UFD.
>
> ---
> **Multivariable corollary.** For $n \geq 1$, the ring $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ equals $\big(R[X_1, \dots, X_{n-1}]\big)[X_n]$, the single-variable polynomial ring over $R[X_1, \dots, X_{n-1}]$. Induct on $n$: the base $n = 1$ is the theorem just proved; for the step, $R[X_1, \dots, X_{n-1}]$ is a UFD by the induction hypothesis, so adjoining the one further indeterminate $X_n$ and applying the theorem makes $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ a UFD. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**$\mathbb{Z}[X]$ as the standard UFD-but-not-PID example.** Applying the theorem to $R = \mathbb{Z}$ shows $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a UFD. Yet $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not a PID: the ideal $(2, X)$ of polynomials with even constant term is not generated by any single element. The application is non-obvious because it deliberately *drives a wedge* between two properties — unique factorization of elements and principality of ideals — that every earlier example had bundled together; the theorem is what makes the wedge.

**Coordinate rings in algebraic geometry.** The polynomial ring $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ over a field $F$ is the coordinate ring of affine $n$-space, and by this theorem it is a UFD. Unique factorization there is the algebraic backbone of the fact that an irreducible hypersurface is cut out by a single irreducible polynomial. The application is non-obvious because a *geometric* statement about hypersurfaces is underwritten by an *algebraic* factorization theorem, and the relevant ring is a UFD only by the multivariable corollary, never by being a PID (for $n \geq 2$ it is not).

**Symmetric functions and the ring $\mathbb{Z}[e_1, \dots, e_n]$.** The ring of symmetric polynomials in $n$ variables over $\mathbb{Z}$ is itself a polynomial ring $\mathbb{Z}[e_1, \dots, e_n]$ in the elementary symmetric polynomials, hence a UFD by the theorem. The application is out-of-distribution because the ring does not *look* like a polynomial ring at first — it is presented as a [[Def - Subring|subring]] of $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ — and recognizing it as a free polynomial ring (the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials) is the non-obvious step that unlocks the UFD conclusion.

**Power series fail — a contrast that sharpens the theorem.** It is instructive to note where the analogue *breaks*: the theorem is specifically about *polynomial* rings, and the parallel claim for formal power series needs separate treatment. Asking a reader to test whether the content–primitive argument survives for $R[[X]]$ — it does not, because there is no "leading coefficient" and the degree induction in Lemma 2 has no base — battle-tests the understanding that the theorem's proof leans essentially on degrees being finite and additive.

---

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's Lemma]]** — the indispensable input. Gauss's lemma (with its content-multiplicativity corollary) is what transports factorizations of primitive polynomials between $R[X]$ and the UFD $F[X]$; without it, the primitive part could not be delegated to the field polynomial ring, and the proof has no engine.

- **[[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|PIDs are UFDs]]** — supplies both a base case and a sub-tool. It gives the base ring $\mathbb{Z}$ its UFD status (via $\mathbb{Z}$ Euclidean $\Rightarrow$ PID $\Rightarrow$ UFD), and it gives $F[X]$ its UFD status, which Lemma 3 consumes. This theorem then extends the supply of UFDs *beyond* the PIDs.

- **[[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial|Content and Primitive Polynomial]]** — the structural device. The content–primitive decomposition $f = c(f) f_1$ is the split that lets the scalar part be delegated to $R$ and the primitive part to $F[X]$; the proof is, in outline, just this decomposition made multiplicative.

- **[[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|Principal Ideal Domain]]** — the notion this theorem separates UFD from. The headline consequence — $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a UFD but not a PID — establishes that the inclusion PID $\subsetneq$ UFD is strict, so "UFD" is a genuinely weaker hypothesis than "PID".

- **The Hilbert Basis Theorem** — a parallel propagation result. Where this theorem says "$R$ a UFD $\Rightarrow R[X]$ a UFD", the Hilbert Basis Theorem says "$R$ Noetherian $\Rightarrow R[X]$ Noetherian". Both are closure properties of polynomial extensions; together they show that adjoining indeterminates preserves the good finiteness and factorization properties of a base ring.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Eisenstein's Criterion *(from this topic)*
> Now that $R[X]$ is known to be a UFD, the practical question becomes *deciding* which polynomials are irreducible. [[Thm - Eisenstein's Criterion|Eisenstein's criterion]] is the workhorse test, giving a one-glance sufficient condition for irreducibility in $R[X]$ (and, via Gauss's lemma, in $F[X]$).

> [!tip] Affine Varieties and the Nullstellensatz *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> The UFD structure of $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ underpins the dictionary between geometry and algebra: irreducible polynomials correspond to irreducible hypersurfaces, and unique factorization is the algebra behind decomposing a variety into irreducible components.

> [!tip] Modules over $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ and beyond *(from Module Theory)*
> Because $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is a UFD but not a PID, it is a standard testing ground for module theory — showing where the structure theorem for modules over a PID fails, and motivating the finer theory of modules over more general rings.
