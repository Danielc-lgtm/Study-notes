# Notation Registry

- $R, S$ — commutative rings with a multiplicative identity, as throughout the course
- $\mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ — the usual number systems, regarded as rings
- $\alpha, \beta$ — complex numbers, typically algebraic integers; $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ is the element whose arithmetic is under study
- "algebraic integer" — an $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ that is a root of some **monic** polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$
- $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — the [[Def - Polynomial Ring|polynomial ring]] in one indeterminate over $\mathbb{Z}$; the central ambient ring of §2.7, and *not* a principal ideal domain
- $f, g, h$ — polynomials, in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ or $\mathbb{Q}[X]$; **monic** means the leading coefficient is $1$
- $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C}$ — the **evaluation homomorphism** $g \mapsto g(\alpha)$, sending a polynomial to its value at the fixed algebraic integer $\alpha$
- $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ — the smallest [[Def - Subring|subring]] of $\mathbb{C}$ containing $\alpha$, equal to $\operatorname{im}\varphi$
- $I = \ker\varphi$ — the **kernel ideal**, the set of integer polynomials vanishing at $\alpha$; an [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$
- $f_\alpha$ — the **minimal polynomial** of $\alpha$, the irreducible monic generator of $I$, so $I = (f_\alpha)$
- $c(f)$ — the **content** of $f \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$, the greatest common divisor of its coefficients; $f$ is **primitive** when $c(f)$ is a unit (see [[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial|content]])
- $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ — an **ascending chain** of ideals; the chain **stabilises** if $I_N = I_{N+1} = \cdots$ from some index $N$ on
- ACC — the **ascending chain condition**: every ascending chain of ideals stabilises
- "Noetherian" — a ring satisfying the ACC, equivalently a ring in which every ideal is finitely generated
- $(r_1, \dots, r_n)$ — the ideal **generated by** $r_1, \dots, r_n$; an ideal is **finitely generated** when it equals such a $(r_1, \dots, r_n)$
- $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ — the polynomial ring in $n$ indeterminates over $R$
- $\deg f$ — the degree of $f$; the leading (top-power) coefficient is the recurring object of attention in §2.8

# Motivation

The two threads of this topic look unrelated, and the surprise is that they are not: both are about the word **finite**. Section 2.7 asks which complex numbers deserve to be called "integers", and the answer is a finiteness condition in disguise. Section 2.8 isolates the finiteness hypothesis — the ascending chain condition — that makes the whole machinery of commutative algebra and algebraic geometry run. By the end of the topic the connecting tissue is visible: §2.7 manufactures a ring of integers inside every number field, and §2.8 certifies that those [[Def - Ring|rings]], and the polynomial rings that geometry is built from, are never too big to handle.

Start with §2.7 and the question it answers. The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ sit inside $\mathbb{C}$, but so do many numbers that *behave* like integers without lying in $\mathbb{Z}$: the Gaussian integer $i$, the golden ratio, the cube roots of unity. What is the right notion of "integer" inside the complex numbers? The naive answer — "a complex number with integer real and imaginary parts" — is parochial; it privileges the basis $\{1, i\}$ for no structural reason. The course's answer is clean and basis-free. An **algebraic integer** is a complex number $\alpha$ that satisfies a *monic* polynomial equation with integer coefficients: $\alpha^n + a_{n-1}\alpha^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0 = 0$ with every $a_i \in \mathbb{Z}$. The single word *monic* is doing all the work. Drop it and you get the larger class of **algebraic numbers** — roots of *any* integer polynomial — which includes $\tfrac12$ (a root of $2X - 1$); algebraic numbers are the analogue of the rationals, algebraic integers the analogue of the integers, and monicity is exactly the line between them. There are only countably many monic integer polynomials, hence only countably many algebraic integers among uncountably many complex numbers, so the definition is genuinely restrictive — a first sanity check that it carves out something real.

Each algebraic integer $\alpha$ then carries a polynomial of its own, its **minimal polynomial**, and the way it arises is the heart of §2.7 and a beautiful application of everything in Rings I–III. Evaluation at $\alpha$ is a ring homomorphism $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C}$, $g \mapsto g(\alpha)$; its kernel $I$ is the set of integer polynomials that vanish at $\alpha$, and by the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] the subring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ is the quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/I$. Now comes the striking fact. The ambient ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is **not** a principal ideal domain — the ideal $(2, X)$ already needs two generators — so there is no general reason for $I$ to be generated by a single polynomial. And yet it is: $I = (f_\alpha)$ for one irreducible monic polynomial $f_\alpha$. A non-principal ring can still have principal *kernels of evaluation maps*, and proving so requires a genuine argument — the Euclidean algorithm in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, denominator-clearing, and a content calculation via [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]]. The reward is a second sanity check, the cleanest possible: a **rational** algebraic integer must be an ordinary integer. The numbers $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac73$ are algebraic, but they are not algebraic integers — $\mathbb{Z}$ is exactly the rational part of the algebraic integers, so the new notion genuinely *extends* the old one rather than competing with it.

Section 2.8 changes the subject to a structural property a ring may or may not have, and it is the most important finiteness hypothesis in algebra. Recall from the proof that [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|principal ideal domains are unique factorization domains]] that one step needed there was: *no infinite strictly ascending chain of [[Def - Ideal|ideals]]*. That property has a name — the **ascending chain condition**, and a ring satisfying it is called **Noetherian**. Equivalently, and this equivalence is the first theorem of §2.8, a ring is Noetherian exactly when *every ideal is finitely generated*. The two faces matter for different reasons: the chain condition is what you verify and what powers induction-style arguments, while finite generation is what you use — it means every ideal, however complicated, is pinned down by a finite list of elements. Fields and principal ideal domains are Noetherian for trivial reasons, but the substantial result is [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]]: if $R$ is Noetherian, so is the polynomial ring $R[X]$. Iterating, $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ and $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ are Noetherian. This is the finiteness backbone of algebraic geometry. A system of polynomial equations defines a geometric shape, and a priori one might need infinitely many equations to cut it out; Hilbert's theorem says no — every such shape is defined by *finitely many* polynomial equations, because the ideal of all equations vanishing on it is finitely generated. The abstract chain condition of §2.8 is the reason geometry is a finite subject.

# Concept Map

## §2.7 Algebraic Integers

- **[[Def - Algebraic Integer and Minimal Polynomial]]**
	- A complex number $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ is an **algebraic integer** if it is a root of a *monic* polynomial in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ — there exists monic $f \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ with $f(\alpha) = 0$ — the word *monic* being precisely what distinguishes algebraic integers (analogue of $\mathbb{Z}$) from the wider class of algebraic numbers (analogue of $\mathbb{Q}$, roots of *any* integer polynomial, e.g. $\tfrac12$ a root of $2X - 1$). Only countably many monic integer polynomials exist, so only countably many algebraic integers lie among the uncountably many complex numbers, confirming the notion is genuinely selective. The **minimal polynomial** $f_\alpha$ of $\alpha$ is the unique irreducible monic polynomial generating the kernel ideal $I = \ker(\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C},\ g \mapsto g(\alpha))$; equivalently it is the [[Def - Content and Primitive Polynomial|primitive]] member of $I$ of least degree. Standard examples: $i$ has $f_\alpha = X^2 + 1$, $\sqrt 2$ has $X^2 - 2$, and $\tfrac12(1 + \sqrt{-3})$ has $X^2 - X + 1$.

- **[[Thm - The Minimal Polynomial Generates the Kernel Ideal]]**
	- For an algebraic integer $\alpha$, the kernel $I = \ker(\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C})$ of evaluation at $\alpha$ is a **principal** ideal, $I = (f_\alpha)$, generated by the irreducible monic minimal polynomial $f_\alpha$. This is non-trivial precisely because $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is **not** a principal ideal domain — the ideal $(2, X)$ is not principal — so principality of $I$ is a special feature of *this* ideal, not a property of the ring. The proof picks $f_\alpha \in I$ of minimal degree (taken primitive), divides any $h \in I$ by $f_\alpha$ in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ via the [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]], clears denominators to land back in $\mathbb{Z}[X]$, evaluates at $\alpha$ to kill the remainder by minimality, then uses a [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|content]] argument to cancel the cleared denominator and obtain $h = f_\alpha q$ with $q \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. Irreducibility of $f_\alpha$ follows since $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha) \cong \mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \le \mathbb{C}$ is an integral domain, so $(f_\alpha)$ is a prime ideal.

- **[[Thm - Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers]]**
	- If $\alpha \in \mathbb{Q}$ is an algebraic integer, then $\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}$ — the rational algebraic integers are exactly the ordinary integers, so the notion of algebraic integer genuinely *extends* "integer" rather than diverging from it. The proof is short: the minimal polynomial $f_\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ is irreducible, and since $\alpha \in \mathbb{Q}$ the linear factor $X - \alpha$ divides $f_\alpha$ in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$; by [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] irreducibility over $\mathbb{Z}$ matches irreducibility over $\mathbb{Q}$, forcing $f_\alpha = X - \alpha$, which has integer coefficients, so $\alpha \in \mathbb{Z}$. Conceptually this says $\mathbb{Z}$ is **integrally closed** in $\mathbb{Q}$: no rational non-integer can satisfy a monic integer equation. It also rules out the tempting belief that $\tfrac12$ or $\tfrac73$ might sneak in as algebraic integers — they are algebraic numbers but not algebraic integers.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Rings of Integers and Dedekind Domains *(from Algebraic Number Theory)*
> The set of *all* algebraic integers in a number field $K$ (a finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}$) forms a subring $\mathcal{O}_K$, its **ring of integers** — a fact requiring that sums and products of algebraic integers are again algebraic integers, which this course states but defers. The ring $\mathcal{O}_K$ is the right generalization of $\mathbb{Z}$: it is a **Dedekind domain**, where unique factorization is restored not for elements but for *ideals*. This is the foundation of algebraic number theory. See [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2]] for the [[Def - Module|module]] theory that the structure of $\mathcal{O}_K$ rests on.

## §2.8 Hilbert's Basis Theorem

- **[[Def - Noetherian Ring]]**
	- A ring $R$ is **Noetherian** if it satisfies the **ascending chain condition (ACC)**: for every ascending chain of ideals $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ there is an index $N$ with $I_N = I_{N+1} = I_{N+2} = \cdots$, so the chain eventually stabilises and *no infinite strictly ascending chain exists*. The true content is a finiteness restriction: a Noetherian ring cannot have ideals that grow forever, which is what makes induction-by-ideal-size arguments terminate. Fields are Noetherian trivially (only two ideals), and every [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]] is Noetherian — this is the property used inside the proof that [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|PIDs are UFDs]]. The non-example to keep in mind is the polynomial ring in *infinitely many* variables $F[X_1, X_2, X_3, \dots]$, where $(X_1) \subsetneq (X_1, X_2) \subsetneq (X_1, X_2, X_3) \subsetneq \cdots$ never stabilises.

- **[[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals]]**
	- A ring $R$ is Noetherian **if and only if** every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated — that is, every ideal can be written $(r_1, \dots, r_n)$ for finitely many $r_i \in R$. The two directions are both natural: if every ideal is finitely generated, then given a chain $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq \cdots$ the union $I = \bigcup I_k$ is an ideal, hence equals $(r_1, \dots, r_n)$, and each generator already lies in some $I_{k_i}$, so taking $K = \max k_i$ gives $I_K = I$ and the chain stabilises. Conversely, an ideal $I$ that is *not* finitely generated lets you greedily pick $r_1 \in I$, then $r_2 \in I \setminus (r_1)$, then $r_3 \in I \setminus (r_1, r_2)$, and so on, manufacturing an infinite strictly ascending chain $(r_1) \subsetneq (r_1, r_2) \subsetneq \cdots$ and contradicting the ACC. This equivalence is what makes "Noetherian" usable: the chain condition is the face you verify, finite generation the face you exploit.

- **[[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem]]**
	- If $R$ is a Noetherian ring, then the polynomial ring $R[X]$ is also Noetherian — finiteness of the coefficient ring is inherited by the polynomial ring. The proof takes an ideal $J \trianglelefteq R[X]$ and, supposing it is not finitely generated, greedily picks $f_1, f_2, f_3, \dots \in J$ each of minimal degree outside the ideal generated by its predecessors; the **leading coefficients** $a_1, a_2, \dots$ then generate an ascending chain of ideals in $R$, which stabilises because $R$ is Noetherian, say at $(a_1, \dots, a_m)$. Writing $a_{m+1}$ as an $R$-combination of $a_1, \dots, a_m$ produces a polynomial $g \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$ matching the degree and leading term of $f_{m+1}$, so $f_{m+1} - g$ has strictly smaller degree yet still lies outside $(f_1, \dots, f_m)$ — contradicting the minimal-degree choice of $f_{m+1}$. Iterating, $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ and $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ are Noetherian, and via the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] every quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian, so **every finitely generated ring is Noetherian**.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Affine Varieties and the Nullstellensatz *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> An **affine variety** is the zero set in $\mathbb{C}^n$ of a collection of polynomials in $\mathbb{C}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$. Hilbert's basis theorem guarantees the ideal of *all* polynomials vanishing on a variety is finitely generated, so **every variety is cut out by finitely many equations** — the geometric finiteness on which the whole subject rests. Hilbert's *Nullstellensatz* then makes the dictionary precise: points of a variety correspond to [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|maximal ideals]] of its coordinate ring $\mathbb{C}[X_1, \dots, X_n]/I$, which is itself a finitely generated, hence Noetherian, ring.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of this topic chase two clusters of goals, one per section. The §2.7 cluster is about **certifying and computing with algebraic integers**: showing that a specific complex number *is* an algebraic integer by exhibiting a monic integer polynomial it satisfies; showing that some number is *not* an algebraic integer (the rational non-integers $\tfrac12$, $\tfrac73$ being the standard targets, ruled out by the integrally-closed lemma); and **computing a minimal polynomial**, which means producing the least-degree primitive integer polynomial vanishing at $\alpha$ and verifying its irreducibility. A subtler target is structural — proving that the kernel of an evaluation map is principal, or identifying the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ as a quotient $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$. The §2.8 cluster is about **establishing or exploiting the Noetherian property**: proving a given ring *is* Noetherian (almost always by exhibiting it as a quotient of a polynomial ring over $\mathbb{Z}$ or a field, then invoking Hilbert plus the quotient corollary); proving a ring is *not* Noetherian (by displaying an explicit non-stabilising chain or a non-finitely-generated ideal); and converting between the two faces of the definition — given a chain, extract a finitely generated ideal, or given a non-finitely-generated ideal, build a chain. These are the natural targets because between them they answer the two questions the topic poses: *which numbers are integers* and *which rings are finite enough to do algebra in*.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The assumptions are equally stereotyped. In §2.7 the recurring source is **a complex number presented by a relation it satisfies** — "$\alpha$ is a root of $X^2 - X + 1$", or "$\alpha = \tfrac12(1 + \sqrt{-3})$" — and the first move is to read off, or construct, a monic integer polynomial killing it; the **field of fractions $\mathbb{Q}$** is then available, switching on the [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]] in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, and the **primitivity** of polynomials lets [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|Gauss's lemma]] descend factorizations from $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ back to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$. In §2.8 the dominant source is **a ring presented as a polynomial ring, a quotient of one, or a finitely generated ring** — and any such presentation routes immediately through [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]] and the quotient corollary to "Noetherian". A second §2.8 source is **a chain of ideals that is given to you**, where the assumption to leverage is that the union of the chain is itself an ideal; a third is **an ideal known to be finitely generated**, or known *not* to be. The discipline, as always, is to route a source to a target: a relation satisfied by $\alpha$ routes through evaluation and the first isomorphism theorem to a minimal-polynomial identification; a polynomial-ring presentation routes through Hilbert's theorem to the Noetherian conclusion; a non-finitely-generated ideal routes through the greedy construction to a non-stabilising chain. The [[Rings IV — §2.7–2.8#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section lays these routes out explicitly.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything here is self-contained — a reader with no background should be able to follow each operation from its description.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Exhibit a monic integer polynomial to certify an algebraic integer.** To show $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ is an algebraic integer, the *only* obligation is to produce one monic $f \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ with $f(\alpha) = 0$. The standard manufacturing trick: if $\alpha$ is built from a surd, isolate the surd and square (or cube) to clear it — for $\alpha = \tfrac12(1 + \sqrt{-3})$, the relation $2\alpha - 1 = \sqrt{-3}$ squares to $4\alpha^2 - 4\alpha + 1 = -3$, i.e. $\alpha^2 - \alpha + 1 = 0$, and the result is monic. The trigger is any "show $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer" request; the whole task is engineering a *monic* relation, since a non-monic one only proves $\alpha$ is an algebraic *number*.

2. **Build the evaluation homomorphism $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C}$.** Fixing an algebraic integer $\alpha$ and sending $g \mapsto g(\alpha)$ is a [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]], and the instant you have it you also have an ideal $\ker\varphi$ (the polynomials vanishing at $\alpha$) and a subring $\operatorname{im}\varphi = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$. The trigger is any question about $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$, about the minimal polynomial, or about which polynomials vanish at $\alpha$ — evaluation is the bridge from the abstract number $\alpha$ to the concrete ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$.

3. **Apply the first isomorphism theorem to identify $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$.** Since evaluation $\varphi$ is surjective onto $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ with kernel $I$, the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] gives $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \cong \mathbb{Z}[X]/I$, and once $I = (f_\alpha)$ is known this reads $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \cong \mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$. The trigger is any request to identify the subring generated by an algebraic integer; this is also how irreducibility of $f_\alpha$ is *deduced* — the quotient sits inside $\mathbb{C}$, hence is an integral domain, hence $(f_\alpha)$ is prime.

4. **Divide with remainder in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, then clear denominators.** When you need to compare an arbitrary $h \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ against a fixed $f_\alpha$, the [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|Euclidean algorithm]] over the *field* $\mathbb{Q}$ writes $h = f_\alpha q + r$ with $\deg r < \deg f_\alpha$ — but $q, r$ may have rational coefficients. Multiply through by a common denominator $a \in \mathbb{Z}$ to get $ah = f_\alpha (aq) + (ar)$ with $aq, ar \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. The trigger is wanting to run a Euclidean-style argument inside the non-field ring $\mathbb{Z}[X]$: do the division upstairs in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, then descend by clearing denominators.

5. **Take contents to cancel a spurious integer factor.** After clearing denominators you typically hold $ah = f_\alpha \cdot (aq)$ and want to divide both sides by $a$. Apply the [[Thm - Gauss's Lemma|content]] function: $c(ah) = c(f_\alpha (aq)) = c(f_\alpha)\,c(aq)$, and if $f_\alpha$ is primitive this is just $c(aq)$, forcing $a \mid c(aq)$, which means $aq$ is $a$ times an honest integer polynomial — so $a$ cancels and $q \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$. The trigger is any "I have a factorization over $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ but with an unwanted scalar attached" situation; contents are the tool that strips the scalar.

6. **Translate between the two faces of Noetherian.** A ring is Noetherian (ascending chain condition) **if and only if** every ideal is [[Def - Noetherian Ring|finitely generated]]. To verify Noetherianness, prove finite generation; to *use* it, switch to whichever face the problem wants — given a chain, pass to its union (an ideal), finitely generate it, and note the generators sit in some finite stage; given a non-finitely-generated ideal, greedily extract a strictly ascending chain. The trigger is any problem mentioning chains, finite generation, or the Noetherian property — the equivalence is the universal first move.

7. **Invoke Hilbert's basis theorem to climb to polynomial rings.** If $R$ is Noetherian, then $R[X]$ is Noetherian, and by induction $R[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian. Since $\mathbb{Z}$ and every field $F$ are Noetherian, [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's theorem]] hands you that $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ and $F[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ are all Noetherian for free. The trigger is any request to prove a polynomial ring — or anything built from one — is Noetherian; you almost never check the chain condition directly.

8. **Push Noetherianness through a quotient.** If $R$ is Noetherian and $I \trianglelefteq R$, then $R/I$ is Noetherian: by the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] an ideal $J \trianglelefteq R/I$ comes from an ideal $J' \supseteq I$ of $R$, which is finitely generated by $r_1, \dots, r_n$, and then $J$ is generated by the [[Def - Coset|cosets]] $r_1 + I, \dots, r_n + I$. Combined with operation 7, this gives the headline corollary: a finitely generated ring is a quotient of some $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$, hence is Noetherian. The trigger is any ring presented as a quotient, or by finitely many generators and relations.

9. **Prove a ring is not Noetherian by exhibiting a chain that never stabilises.** To certify the *failure* of the Noetherian property, display one explicit ascending chain of ideals $I_1 \subsetneq I_2 \subsetneq I_3 \subsetneq \cdots$ with all inclusions strict — equivalently, one ideal that needs infinitely many generators. The canonical witness is $F[X_1, X_2, \dots]$ in infinitely many variables, where $(X_1) \subsetneq (X_1, X_2) \subsetneq \cdots$. The trigger is a "show this ring is not Noetherian" request; a single non-stabilising chain is a complete proof.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Concluding the kernel ideal is principal *because* every ideal "obviously" is
> Having seen [[Thm - Euclidean Algorithm for Polynomials|every ideal of F[X] is principal]] and [[Thm - Euclidean Domains are Principal Ideal Domains|of ℤ is principal]], it is tempting to wave through $I = \ker\varphi \trianglelefteq \mathbb{Z}[X]$ as principal for the same reason. But $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is **not** a principal ideal domain — the ideal $(2, X)$ of polynomials with even constant term genuinely needs two generators, as proved in [[Rings I — §2.1–2.2|Rings I]]. So principality of $I$ is *not* a free consequence of the ring; it is a special property of the particular ideal $I$, and [[Thm - The Minimal Polynomial Generates the Kernel Ideal|its proof]] requires real work — the Euclidean algorithm in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, denominator-clearing, and a content argument. The lesson: "kernel of an evaluation map" is a much more special ideal than "arbitrary ideal".

> [!warning] 2. Forgetting *monic* and conflating algebraic integers with algebraic numbers
> Every algebraic integer is a root of an integer polynomial, so it is tempting to define it that way and forget the word *monic*. But "root of *any* integer polynomial" defines the strictly larger class of **algebraic numbers**: $\tfrac12$ is a root of $2X - 1$, so $\tfrac12$ is an algebraic number, yet it is *not* an algebraic integer, because [[Thm - Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers|a rational algebraic integer must lie in ℤ]]. Monicity is the entire distinction — it is the analogue of the line between $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Z}$. Drop it and the theory collapses: the integrally-closed lemma fails, and "$\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$" stops being a well-behaved ring of integers.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming a subring or a quotient of a Noetherian ring is automatically Noetherian
> Quotients of Noetherian rings *are* Noetherian (operation 8) — but **[[Def - Subring|subrings]] need not be**. The temptation is to treat "Noetherian" as inherited by every substructure, by analogy with finiteness. It is not: the polynomial ring $F[X_1, X_2, \dots]$ in infinitely many variables is *not* Noetherian, yet it is a subring of its own field of fractions $F(X_1, X_2, \dots)$, which — being a field — *is* Noetherian. So a non-Noetherian ring sits inside a Noetherian one, and "subring of Noetherian" carries no information. Only the *quotient* direction is safe, and even then it relies on the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]], not on any general inheritance principle.

> [!warning] 4. Believing the leading coefficient of $f_{m+1} - g$ behaves predictably in the Hilbert proof
> In the proof of [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]] one builds $g \in (f_1, \dots, f_m)$ with the *same degree and same leading coefficient* as $f_{m+1}$, then concludes $f_{m+1} - g$ has strictly smaller degree. It is tempting to think the construction also controls the lower-order terms, or that $f_{m+1} - g$ could vanish. Neither is needed and neither is true in general: the only guarantee is the cancellation of the *top* term, hence the strict drop in degree. The contradiction comes purely from $f_{m+1} - g$ lying outside $(f_1, \dots, f_m)$ — because $g$ is inside and $f_{m+1}$ is not — while having degree below the minimal degree that defined $f_{m+1}$. Trying to say more about $f_{m+1} - g$ is wasted effort and a common source of a stalled proof.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic split cleanly along the §2.7 / §2.8 line, and within each section there is a small, recognizable set of problem classes. Begin by classifying which you face.

If the problem **asks you to certify that a complex number is an algebraic integer**, the entire task is to produce a *monic* integer polynomial it satisfies, and the route is algebraic manipulation, not theory. Take the number, isolate any surd, and clear it by raising to a power: the relation defining $\alpha$ becomes a polynomial equation, and you must check the polynomial came out monic — if it did not, you have only proved $\alpha$ is an algebraic *number*, and you must rescale or rework the relation. If instead the problem asks you to show some number is *not* an algebraic integer, and that number is rational, the route is immediate: [[Thm - Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers|a rational algebraic integer is an ordinary integer]], so any rational non-integer such as $\tfrac12$ is disqualified in one line. This theorem is the workhorse for all "not an algebraic integer" problems with a rational target, and recognizing that the number is rational is the whole decision.

If the problem **asks you to find a minimal polynomial or to identify the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$**, the route runs through the evaluation homomorphism $\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C}$, $g \mapsto g(\alpha)$. First find *some* monic integer polynomial $f$ killing $\alpha$; this $f$ lies in $I = \ker\varphi$. The minimal polynomial $f_\alpha$ is the least-degree primitive member of $I$, so the practical task is to confirm your $f$ has minimal degree — usually by checking it is irreducible, since an irreducible $f$ vanishing at $\alpha$ must be (an associate of) $f_\alpha$. Then [[Thm - The Minimal Polynomial Generates the Kernel Ideal|the kernel ideal theorem]] gives $I = (f_\alpha)$ and the [[Thm - First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings|first isomorphism theorem]] gives $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \cong \mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$. The non-obvious point — the one that makes these problems more than bookkeeping — is that principality of $I$ is *not* automatic, because $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not a principal ideal domain; the proof of principality is itself a reusable template (divide in $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, clear denominators, take contents), and a problem may ask you to run that template rather than cite its conclusion.

If the problem **asks you to prove a ring is Noetherian**, the productive move is almost never to verify the ascending chain condition directly. Instead, exhibit the ring as a polynomial ring over $\mathbb{Z}$ or a field, or as a quotient of one, or as finitely generated. [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]] then certifies the polynomial ring is Noetherian, and the quotient corollary — proved through the [[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|ideal correspondence]] — carries Noetherianness down to any quotient. Since every finitely generated ring is a quotient of some $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$, the chain "finitely generated $\Rightarrow$ quotient of a polynomial ring $\Rightarrow$ Noetherian" handles the overwhelming majority of these problems. The recognition skill is to spot the finite presentation: a ring described by finitely many generators and relations is finitely generated, and that is the only hypothesis you need.

If the problem **asks you to prove a ring is *not* Noetherian**, the route is to exhibit an explicit infinite strictly ascending chain of ideals, or equivalently an ideal that is not finitely generated; [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals|the equivalence theorem]] says these two tasks are interchangeable, so do whichever the ring makes easy. The canonical example to keep in mind is the polynomial ring in infinitely many variables, $F[X_1, X_2, \dots]$, with the chain $(X_1) \subsetneq (X_1, X_2) \subsetneq \cdots$; many non-Noetherian rings are recognized by finding a copy of this pattern inside them — an infinite family of elements no finite subset of which generates the rest.

Finally, a meta-strategy threads through §2.8: **the two faces of Noetherian are tools for opposite directions of argument.** The ascending chain condition is the face you use to make a process *terminate* — the Hilbert proof, and the proof that PIDs are UFDs, both run by building a chain and invoking the ACC to stop it. Finite generation is the face you use to *pin an ideal down* — to know that an ideal, however large, is controlled by a finite list. When a problem feels stuck, ask which direction you need: if you are running an infinite process and want it to halt, reach for the chain condition; if you have an unwieldy ideal and want a handle on it, reach for finite generation. The equivalence theorem is precisely the licence to switch between them at will.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - The Minimal Polynomial Generates the Kernel Ideal|Principality of the kernel ideal]]**: for an algebraic integer $\alpha$, the kernel $I = \ker(\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C})$ is $(f_\alpha)$ for the irreducible monic $f_\alpha$. Its reusability is twofold. As a *statement*, it lets you treat the minimal polynomial as a genuine generator — every integer polynomial vanishing at $\alpha$ is a $\mathbb{Z}[X]$-multiple of $f_\alpha$ — which is the fact that makes $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \cong \mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$. As a *technique*, its proof is the canonical recipe for descending an argument from $\mathbb{Q}[X]$ to $\mathbb{Z}[X]$: divide with remainder over the field, clear denominators, take contents to cancel the scalar. That recipe recurs throughout the interaction of $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ with $\mathbb{Q}[X]$, so internalizing the proof pays off well beyond this one theorem.

- **[[Thm - Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers|ℤ is integrally closed in ℚ]]**: a rational number satisfying a monic integer polynomial is already an integer. This is constantly reused as a *rigidity* statement and a *disqualifier*. The recognizable setup is any claim that some explicitly rational quantity is an algebraic integer — the theorem refutes it instantly unless the quantity is a plain integer. More structurally, it is the prototype of "integral closure": the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of a number field is defined as the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $K$, and this lemma is the base case $K = \mathbb{Q}$ certifying that the construction returns $\mathbb{Z}$ itself. Whenever a problem mixes rationality with a monic integral relation, this is the first theorem to reach for.

- **[[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals|Noetherian Leftrightarrow every ideal finitely generated]]**: the equivalence of the ascending chain condition with finite generation of all ideals. Its reusability *is* the equivalence: it lets you verify Noetherianness through whichever face is convenient and then exploit it through the other. The common pattern is to establish the property via finite generation (often inherited from a polynomial ring) and then *use* it via the chain condition to terminate an infinite process. It is also the proof engine behind both Hilbert's basis theorem and the fact that PIDs are UFDs — each builds an ascending chain and stops it — so the effort spent internalizing the equivalence pays compound interest.

- **[[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]]**: $R$ Noetherian $\Rightarrow R[X]$ Noetherian. This is the workhorse for *establishing* Noetherianness. The recognizable setup is "show this ring is Noetherian" where the ring is, or can be exhibited as, a polynomial ring over $\mathbb{Z}$ or a field, or a quotient of one. Combined with the quotient corollary it yields the master fact that *every finitely generated ring is Noetherian*, which covers essentially every ring that arises in algebraic geometry. Recognize its applicability whenever a ring is presented by finitely many generators and relations: the finite presentation is exactly the hypothesis Hilbert's theorem needs, and the conclusion — finite generation of *every* ideal — is what makes the geometry of the ring tractable.

- **[[Thm - Ideal Correspondence|The ideal correspondence]]**, applied to Noetherianness: ideals of $R/I$ correspond to ideals of $R$ containing $I$. Its reuse here is the bridge that carries the Noetherian property through quotients — an ideal of $R/I$ pulls back to a finitely generated ideal of $R$, and the generators push forward to generate the original. This is the single step that upgrades "polynomial rings are Noetherian" to "finitely generated rings are Noetherian". More generally, the correspondence is the standard device for transporting any ideal-theoretic property (Noetherian, prime-ideal structure, principality of specific ideals) between a ring and its quotients, and it is worth reaching for whenever a problem about a quotient ring is more naturally phrased upstairs.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic number theory — the ring of integers of a number field.** Section 2.7 builds the local picture for a single algebraic integer $\alpha$: the subring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \cong \mathbb{Z}[X]/(f_\alpha)$. Algebraic number theory globalizes it. A **number field** $K$ is a finite field extension of $\mathbb{Q}$, and its **ring of integers** $\mathcal{O}_K$ is the set of *all* algebraic integers lying in $K$ — the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ inside $K$. That $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a ring at all rests on the fact this course states but defers: sums and products of algebraic integers are algebraic integers. The [[Thm - Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers|rational-algebraic-integers theorem]] is the base case, certifying $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}} = \mathbb{Z}$. The crucial discovery downstream is that $\mathcal{O}_K$ usually *fails* to be a unique factorization domain — elements can factor non-uniquely — but it is always a **Dedekind domain**, in which unique factorization is restored at the level of *ideals*. The whole subject is the study of how badly $\mathcal{O}_K$ departs from $\mathbb{Z}$, measured by its class group, and §2.7 is its first chapter.

2. **Algebraic geometry — varieties are finitely defined.** An **affine variety** is the common zero set in $\mathbb{C}^n$ of a family of polynomials in $\mathbb{C}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$. A priori the family could be infinite, and a geometric shape might need infinitely many equations. [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]] forbids this: the polynomial ring $\mathbb{C}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$ is Noetherian, so the ideal of *all* polynomials vanishing on a variety is finitely generated, and therefore **every affine variety is cut out by finitely many equations**. This is the finiteness that makes algebraic geometry a workable subject — schemes are routinely required to be **Noetherian** for precisely this reason. The dictionary is completed by Hilbert's *Nullstellensatz*: points of a variety correspond to [[Def - Prime and Maximal Ideal|maximal ideals]] of the coordinate ring $\mathbb{C}[X_1, \dots, X_n]/I$, and that coordinate ring, being a quotient of a polynomial ring, is itself finitely generated and Noetherian. Geometry inherits its finiteness from the abstract chain condition of §2.8.

3. **Commutative algebra — the Noetherian hypothesis as the standing assumption.** Commutative algebra past the introductory stage is, to a first approximation, the structure theory of **Noetherian rings and modules**. The ascending chain condition is the hypothesis under which the deep theorems hold: primary decomposition of ideals, the Krull intersection theorem, the dimension theory of local rings, the finiteness of the integral closure. The reason is uniform — each of these is proved by an argument that builds a chain or an inductive process and needs it to terminate, and [[Def - Noetherian Ring|Noetherian]] is exactly the finiteness that guarantees termination. The equivalence with finite generation of ideals, [[Thm - Noetherian Rings and Finitely Generated Ideals|proved in §2.8]], is what makes the hypothesis verifiable in practice. Emmy Noether's insight, which this section names, was that this single chain condition is the right abstraction — the minimal finiteness assumption under which commutative algebra works — and Hilbert's basis theorem is what guarantees the assumption is satisfied by every ring geometry and number theory actually produce.

4. **Module theory — the Noetherian condition relativized.** A ring $R$ is Noetherian exactly when, viewed as a [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|module]] over itself, it satisfies the ascending chain condition on submodules — because the submodules of $R$ as an $R$-module are precisely its ideals. This reframing generalizes: an $R$-module $M$ is called **Noetherian** if it satisfies the ascending chain condition on submodules, equivalently if every submodule is finitely generated. Over a Noetherian ring, every finitely generated module is a Noetherian module, and this is the property that makes the structure theory of finitely generated modules — the classification over a principal ideal domain, and its descendants — possible. The §2.8 material is thus the ring-level shadow of a module-level finiteness condition, and the bridge to [[Modules I — §3.1–3.2|module theory]] is the observation that "ideal of $R$" and "submodule of $R$" are the same thing.

# Insights

**An algebraic integer is the right generalization of "integer", and the slogan that pins it down is "integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{C}$".** The textbook definition — root of a monic integer polynomial — is the right thing to *check* but not the right thing to *picture*. The picture is this. The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ sit inside the rationals $\mathbb{Q}$, and they are characterized among the rationals by a closure property: $\mathbb{Z}$ is exactly the rationals that satisfy a monic integer equation, which is what [[Thm - Rational Algebraic Integers are Integers|the rational-algebraic-integers theorem]] says. Now run the same construction with $\mathbb{C}$ in place of $\mathbb{Q}$: the algebraic integers are exactly the complex numbers satisfying a monic integer equation. So "algebraic integer" is not an arbitrary new definition — it is the *same* construction that produces $\mathbb{Z}$ from itself, applied in a larger ambient ring. The technical name for this construction is **integral closure**: the algebraic integers are the integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathbb{Z}$ being its own integral closure in $\mathbb{Q}$ is the statement that $\mathbb{Z}$ is *integrally closed*. The single word *monic* is what makes the construction work: it is the analogue of denominator-free, the precise feature distinguishing the integers from the rationals, and dropping it gives algebraic *numbers* — the integral closure's larger cousin, the analogue of $\mathbb{Q}$. Once you see the definition this way, the otherwise mysterious facts become expected: of course rational algebraic integers are ordinary integers (integral closure is idempotent), and of course the algebraic integers form a ring (an integral closure is always a ring), even though the latter is hard to prove directly.

**The true name of "Noetherian" is "no infinite strictly ascending chain", and it is the finiteness hypothesis that makes commutative algebra and algebraic geometry possible.** It is worth being precise about *why* this particular condition, out of all possible finiteness conditions, is the one mathematics settled on. A ring could be finite, but that is far too strong — $\mathbb{Z}$ and every polynomial ring are infinite. It could have finitely many ideals, still too strong. It could have all ideals principal — that is a [[Def - Principal Ideal Domain|principal ideal domain]], and $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ already fails it. The Noetherian condition is weaker than all of these and yet strong enough: it asks only that no ideal grows *forever*, that every ascending chain of ideals eventually stops. This is exactly the hypothesis that makes inductive and recursive arguments **terminate**. Look at the two major proofs of this part of the course. The proof that [[Thm - Principal Ideal Domains are Unique Factorization Domains|PIDs are UFDs]] builds a chain of ideals to factor an element and needs the chain to halt. The proof of [[Thm - Hilbert's Basis Theorem|Hilbert's basis theorem]] greedily picks polynomials of minimal degree and needs the process to halt. In both, "Noetherian" is the precise minimum that supplies the halting. Emmy Noether's contribution was to recognize that this chain condition — not finiteness, not principality — is the *correct* abstraction, the load-bearing hypothesis. And the deep reason the abstraction is not vacuous is Hilbert's basis theorem itself: it guarantees the condition is *satisfied* by every ring that number theory and geometry actually build, since those rings are all finitely generated, hence quotients of $\mathbb{Z}[X_1, \dots, X_n]$, hence Noetherian. The definition would be a curiosity if Hilbert's theorem did not certify that the world is full of examples.

**The two sections are one idea: finiteness controlled through ideals.** It is tempting to file §2.7 and §2.8 as separate topics — number theory and abstract ring structure — but they are the same theme seen twice. Section 2.7 takes a transcendental-looking question, "which complex numbers are integers", and answers it with a finiteness condition: $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer exactly when the kernel ideal $\ker(\varphi : \mathbb{Z}[X] \to \mathbb{C})$ is non-zero and (as it turns out) finitely generated by a single polynomial. Section 2.8 isolates finite generation of ideals as a property in its own right and proves it propagates to polynomial rings. The connecting object is the ideal: in §2.7 the *kernel* ideal of an evaluation map encodes everything about $\alpha$, and the surprise is that it is principal even though $\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is not a principal ideal domain; in §2.8 the *finite generation* of every ideal is the definition of Noetherian, and the surprise is that this finiteness survives the passage to polynomial rings. Both surprises say the same thing in different keys — that an ideal which has no a priori reason to be finitely controlled turns out to be. And both feed the same downstream subject: the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_K$ of §2.7's bridge is a Noetherian ring by §2.8's corollary, and its good behaviour as a Dedekind domain is exactly the statement that its ideals, though its elements may factor badly, remain finitely controlled. The unifying lesson of the topic is that the right way to impose finiteness on an infinite ring is not on its elements but on its **ideals**.
