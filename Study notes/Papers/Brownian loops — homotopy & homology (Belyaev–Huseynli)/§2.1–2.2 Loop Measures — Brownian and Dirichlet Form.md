# Notation

**Standing conventions.** $\Delta_X = -\operatorname{div}_g\operatorname{grad}_g$ is the *positive* Laplace–Beltrami operator, so $\operatorname{spec}(\Delta_X)\subseteq[0,\infty)$. Brownian motion is run at **speed 2**: it is the diffusion generated by $-\Delta_X$, not $-\tfrac12\Delta_X$. Where $\partial X \neq \emptyset$ the boundary conditions are Dirichlet, so the process is killed on first hitting $\partial X$.

- $(X,g)$ — a complete orientable Riemannian surface, with or without boundary
- $\mathrm{vol}_g$ — the Riemannian volume measure on $X$ ([[Def - Riemannian Volume Form]])
- $p_X(t,x,y)$ — the heat kernel, $p : (0,\infty)\times X\times X \to (0,\infty)$; symmetric in $x,y$; the integral kernel of $e^{-t\Delta_X}$ as a density against $\mathrm{vol}_g$, so $(e^{-t\Delta_X}f)(x)=\int_X p_X(t,x,y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g(y)$. The subscript $X$ is dropped when unambiguous.
- $W^t_x$ — the law of Brownian motion started at $x$, a measure on the path space $C([0,t],X)$
- $W^t_{x\to y}$ — the **unnormalised** bridge measure from $x$ to $y$ in time $t$, defined by disintegrating $W^t_x$ by the endpoint: $W^t_x = \int_X W^t_{x\to y}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g(y)$. Its total mass is $|W^t_{x\to y}| = p(t,x,y)$; the conditional law given $\omega(t)=y$ is $W^t_{x\to y}/p(t,x,y)$
- $\mathcal{C}^*_X$ — the space of parametrised, oriented, rooted loops $\{(t,\omega) : t\geq 0,\ \omega\in C([0,t];X),\ \omega(0)=\omega(t)\}$; from §2.2 onwards, with $C$ replaced by the càdlàg space $D$
- $\mathcal{C}_X = \mathcal{C}^*_X/\!\sim$ — the space of unrooted, unparametrised, oriented loops
- $\mu^*_X$, $\mu_X$ — the rooted and unrooted Brownian loop measures
- $(E,m)$ — a locally compact separable metric space with a Radon measure of full support
- $(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})$ — a regular symmetric Dirichlet form on $L^2(E,m)$; $A$ its generator, a non-negative self-adjoint operator with $\mathcal{F}=\operatorname{Dom}(A^{1/2})$
- $p^{\mathcal{E}}(t,x,y)$ — the transition density of the associated process against $m$
- $\mu^{*,\mathcal{E}}_X$, $\mu^{\mathcal{E}}_X$ — the rooted and unrooted Dirichlet-form loop measures

# What this section is for

Everything in this paper is a statement about the mass of a set of loops. So the first thing to build is a measure on loops — and the striking fact, which is the reason the whole subject exists, is that the natural such measure is *not* a probability measure and could not usefully be one.

Here is the difficulty it solves. Suppose you want to say "the loops in this free homotopy class have such-and-such weight". A loop is a closed path, and the obvious way to produce closed paths from Brownian motion is to condition a Brownian bridge to return to where it started. But then you have made two arbitrary choices: the duration $t$ of the loop, and the basepoint $x$ where it starts. Both choices are artefacts. A loop as a *geometric* object has no duration and no basepoint; those belong to a parametrisation you happened to pick. So you must integrate them out. Integrating out the basepoint is easy — integrate against $\mathrm{vol}_g$. Integrating out the duration is where the interesting decision lives: the weight must be $\mathrm{d}t/t$, the multiplicative Haar measure on $(0,\infty)$, because that is the unique (up to scale) measure invariant under the rescalings $t\mapsto \lambda t$ that a duration ought to be indifferent to.

The price is that the resulting measure has infinite total mass. Both ends of the $t$-integral diverge: near $t=0$, the short-time asymptotic $p(t,x,x)\sim 1/(4\pi t)$ makes the integrand blow up like $1/t^2$, so the tiny loops swamp everything. This is not a defect to be repaired; it is the correct statement that a surface contains overwhelmingly many very small loops. The measure remains $\sigma$-finite, which is all that is ever needed: $\sigma$-finiteness is what lets you use Tonelli, push measures forward, and — the payoff of §3.3 — use $\mu_X$ as the intensity of a Poisson point process. What is *lost* is the ability to say "pick a loop at random". The paper spends §4–§6 earning that back, by finding a sub-family (the non-trivial non-peripheral classes) whose total mass is finite and can be normalised.

The second thing this section does is generalise. The Brownian loop measure is built from three ingredients — a heat kernel, its bridge measures, and the two weights $\mathrm{d}t/t$ and $\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g$ — and nothing about that recipe cares that the process is Brownian motion. Any process with a symmetric transition density will do. The clean way to say "any such process" is a [[Def - Dirichlet Form and the Hunt Process Correspondence|regular symmetric Dirichlet form]], because Fukushima's theorem makes the correspondence between forms and processes exact. Replacing continuous paths by càdlàg paths lets jumps in. This costs something real, and the paper is explicit about it: **conformal invariance is a two-dimensional Brownian phenomenon and does not survive.** For a subordinate process the operator depends on the metric $g$ itself and not merely on its conformal class, which is why from §2.3 onwards $X$ is a Riemannian surface rather than a Riemann surface.

# Results

## Definition 2.1 — the Brownian loop measure

> [!abstract] Type card — Definition 2.1 (Brownian loop measure)
> **Given.** A complete orientable Riemannian surface $(X,g)$, with Dirichlet conditions on $\partial X$ if non-empty; its [[Constr - The Brownian Loop Measure|heat kernel]] $p_X$ and its unnormalised bridge measures $W^t_{x\to x}$.
>
> **Produces.** A $\sigma$-finite measure $\mu_X$ on [[Def - The Space of Unrooted Unparametrised Loops|the space of unrooted unparametrised oriented loops]] $\mathcal{C}_X$, of infinite total mass, obtained as the pushforward under the quotient map of $\mu^*_X = \int_0^\infty \frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\int_X W^t_{x\to x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g(x)$.
>
> **Lets you.** Assign a mass to a family of loops — in particular to a free homotopy class — without any probability normalisation; and inherit two structural properties, **restriction** and **conformal invariance**, that no probability measure on paths would give you.

![[Constr - The Brownian Loop Measure#The construction]]

The two structural properties are what make the object worth having, and they are used in genuinely different places. **Restriction** says that for $X'\subseteq X$ open, $\mathrm{d}\mu_{X'}(\eta) = \mathbf{1}_{\eta\subseteq X'}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_X(\eta)$: the loop measure of a subdomain is literally the restriction of the ambient one to loops that stay inside. **Conformal invariance** says that replacing $g$ by $e^{2\sigma}g$ leaves $\mu_X$ unchanged, so $\mu_X$ depends only on the conformal class $[g]$ and $X$ may be taken to be a Riemann surface. Restriction is used in §3.4 to see that puncturing a surface at a polar set does not change any class's mass; conformal invariance is used there too, and again in §5.1.1 for the Polyakov formula. Both are set out with their consumers on [[Constr - The Brownian Loop Measure]].

## Definition 2.2 — the Dirichlet-form loop measure

> [!abstract] Type card — Definition 2.2 (Dirichlet-form loop measure)
> **Given.** A regular symmetric [[Def - Dirichlet Form and the Hunt Process Correspondence|Dirichlet form]] $(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})$ on $L^2(X,\mathrm{vol}_g)$ whose semigroup $e^{-tA}$ admits a jointly measurable symmetric transition density $p^{\mathcal{E}}(t,x,y)$ against $\mathrm{vol}_g$.
>
> **Produces.** A $\sigma$-finite measure $\mu^{\mathcal{E}}_X$ on the space of unrooted unparametrised càdlàg loops, of total rooted mass $\int_0^\infty \frac1t\int_X p^{\mathcal{E}}(t,x,x)\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g(x)\,\mathrm{d}t$.
>
> **Lets you.** Run the entire construction for jump processes as well as diffusions — which is the whole point of §2.3 — at the cost of losing conformal invariance. Restriction survives, because the part form $\mathcal{E}_{X'}$ is exactly the form of the process killed on leaving $X'$.

![[Constr - The Dirichlet-Form Loop Measure#The construction]]

**Strategy.** There is no proof here — Definition 2.2 is Definition 2.1 with $C([0,t];X)$ replaced by $D([0,t];X)$ and $p_X$ replaced by $p^{\mathcal{E}}$. What has to be checked is that the equivalence relation of §2.1 still makes sense, and it does: circular time-shifts and increasing continuous reparametrisations both preserve the càdlàg property. Invariance of $\mu^{*,\mathcal{E}}_X$ under the circular shift is Le Jan's, quoted from [LJ11, Chapter 2], and is what makes the pushforward to $\mathcal{C}_X$ well defined.

The one thing worth pausing on is why a Dirichlet form is the right level of generality rather than, say, "any symmetric Markov process". The answer is Fukushima's correspondence: a regular symmetric Dirichlet form determines a Hunt process, unique up to quasi-everywhere equivalence, and conversely. So "regular symmetric Dirichlet form" *is* the analytic name for the class of processes for which this construction runs, and phrasing the hypothesis that way turns "which processes work?" into a checkable condition on a quadratic form. The paper flags in passing that the non-symmetric theory exists (Ma–Röckner) but has fewer connections to Markov processes; symmetry is what makes $p^{\mathcal{E}}(t,x,y)=p^{\mathcal{E}}(t,y,x)$, and that symmetry is used implicitly every time a bridge measure from $x$ to $y$ is treated interchangeably with one from $y$ to $x$.

# What to carry forward

Three things, and nothing else from this section is used again.

**The recipe.** A loop measure is $\int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\int_X W^{t}_{x\to x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g(x)$, pushed forward to unrooted unparametrised loops. Every loop measure in the paper — Brownian, Dirichlet-form, subordinate, killing, $\alpha$-stable, and the three-dimensional versions of all of them — is this expression with a different transition density substituted. When a later section says "the loop measure", this is what it means.

**That the mass of a set of loops is $\int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\int(\cdots)$ with the diagonal kernel inside.** Every computation in §3 and §7 begins by writing the class mass in this form and then doing something to the inner integral. The $\mathrm{d}t/t$ is never touched until [[Thm - Collapsing the Time Integral into the Weighted Potential Measure|Lemma 2.11]] eats it.

**Restriction and conformal invariance, and which one survives what.** Restriction holds for every Dirichlet-form loop measure, because the part form on an open subset is the form of the killed process. Conformal invariance holds only for two-dimensional Brownian motion. §3.4 is entirely an exercise in seeing what is left of a conformal-invariance argument once the process is subordinated, and the answer is: very little.
