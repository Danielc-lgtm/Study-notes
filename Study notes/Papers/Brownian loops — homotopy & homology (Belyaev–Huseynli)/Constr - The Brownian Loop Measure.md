# Notation

- $(X,g)$ — a complete orientable Riemannian surface, with or without boundary; Dirichlet conditions on $\partial X$ when non-empty, so the process is killed on first hitting the boundary
- $\Delta_X = -\operatorname{div}_g\operatorname{grad}_g$ — the **positive** Laplace–Beltrami operator; $\operatorname{spec}(\Delta_X)\subseteq[0,\infty)$
- $p_X(t,x,y)$ — the heat kernel, $p:(0,\infty)\times X\times X\to(0,\infty)$, symmetric, the integral kernel of $e^{-t\Delta_X}$ as a density against $\mathrm{vol}_g$
- $W^t_x$ — the law of Brownian motion (run at speed $2$, that is generated by $-\Delta_X$) started at $x$, a measure on $C([0,t],X)$
- $W^t_{x\to y}$ — the **unnormalised** bridge measure, defined by $W^t_x=\int_X W^t_{x\to y}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g(y)$; total mass $p(t,x,y)$
- $\mathcal{C}^*_X$, $\mathcal{C}_X$ — rooted parametrised and unrooted unparametrised oriented loop spaces
- $\mu^*_X$, $\mu_X$ — the rooted and unrooted Brownian loop measures
- $\sigma$ — a smooth real function, in the conformal rescaling $g\mapsto e^{2\sigma}g$

# In plain language

The Brownian loop measure is what you get when you take the family of Brownian bridges that return to their starting point, and then integrate away the two things a *geometric* loop does not have: a starting point and a duration.

Integrating away the starting point is unremarkable — integrate against the Riemannian volume. Integrating away the duration forces a choice of weight, and the choice is $\mathrm{d}t/t$, the multiplicative Haar measure on $(0,\infty)$, because that is the unique measure (up to scale) invariant under the rescalings $t\mapsto\lambda t$ that a duration ought to be indifferent to. The result is a measure that is not finite and never can be made finite: near $t=0$ the on-diagonal heat kernel behaves like $p(t,x,x)\sim1/(4\pi t)$, so the integrand blows up like $1/t^2$, and there are overwhelmingly many very small loops. That is a correct description of a surface, not a defect.

What survives is $\sigma$-finiteness, and $\sigma$-finiteness is enough for everything the paper does: Tonelli applies, pushforwards make sense, and — the payoff of §3.3 — the measure can serve as the intensity of a Poisson point process. What is given up is the phrase "pick a loop at random". §4 through §6 earn that phrase back by restricting to a sub-family whose mass is finite.

# The construction

> **Definition 2.1 (Brownian loop measure).** The **rooted** Brownian loop measure on $X$ is the infinite but $\sigma$-finite measure on $\mathcal{C}^*_X$
> $$\mu^*_X := \int_0^\infty\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t}\int_X W^t_{x\to x}\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g(x).$$
> The **Brownian loop measure** $\mu_X$ is the pushforward of $\mu^*_X$ under the quotient map $\mathcal{C}^*_X\to\mathcal{C}_X=\mathcal{C}^*_X/\!\sim$ to the space of unparametrised, oriented, unrooted loops.

The equivalence relation $\sim$, and the fact that the pushforward is well defined, are on [[Def - The Space of Unrooted Unparametrised Loops]]. The one point to keep in mind is that $\sim$ forgets the root and the parametrisation while **remembering the orientation** — the identifications are generated by circular time-shifts together with increasing continuous reparametrisations, and an orientation-reversal is neither.

# Type card

> [!abstract] Type card — the Brownian loop measure
> **Given.** A complete orientable Riemannian surface $(X,g)$, with Dirichlet conditions on $\partial X$ if non-empty; its heat kernel $p_X$ and its unnormalised bridge measures $W^t_{x\to x}$; the weights $\mathrm{d}t/t$ on $(0,\infty)$ and $\mathrm{d}\mathrm{vol}_g$ on $X$.
>
> **Produces.** A $\sigma$-finite measure $\mu_X$ on the space $\mathcal{C}_X$ of unrooted unparametrised oriented loops. **Infinite total mass**, divergent at the small-$t$ end like $\int_0 t^{-2}\,\mathrm{d}t$. Not a probability measure and not normalisable.
>
> **Lets you.** Assign a mass to any measurable family of loops — in particular to a free homotopy class — with no probability normalisation; use $\mu_X$ as a Poisson intensity; and inherit **restriction** and **conformal invariance**, two structural properties that no probability measure on paths would give you.

# Properties relied on later

Two, both from Lawler–Werner. Only these two are used; the paper quotes no others.

**Restriction.** If $X'\subseteq X$ is open, then $\mu_{X'}$ is the restriction of $\mu_X$ to loops contained in $X'$:
$$\mathrm{d}\mu_{X'}(\eta) = \mathbf{1}_{\eta\subseteq X'}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_X(\eta).$$
The reason this is believable is that Dirichlet boundary conditions mean the process on $X'$ is the ambient process killed on leaving $X'$, so the bridge measures on $X'$ are the ambient bridge measures restricted to paths that stay inside. That argument uses nothing specific to Brownian motion, which is why restriction survives for every [[Constr - The Dirichlet-Form Loop Measure|Dirichlet-form loop measure]]: the part form $\mathcal{E}_{X'}$ on an open subset *is* the form of the killed process.

**Conformal invariance.** Replacing $g$ by a conformally equivalent metric $e^{2\sigma}g$ leaves $\mu_X$ unchanged. Consequently $\mu_X$ depends only on the conformal class $[g]$, and one may take $X$ to be a Riemann surface rather than a Riemannian surface.

**Conformal invariance is a two-dimensional Brownian phenomenon and nothing else in this paper has it.** For a subordinate process the associated operator depends on the metric $g$ itself and not only on its conformal class, so from §2.3 onwards $X$ must be treated as a Riemannian surface. The precise obstruction, spelled out in §3.4: a conformal change rescales the Laplacian as $\Delta_{X,g'}=e^{-2\sigma}\Delta_{X,g}$, and $\phi(e^{-2\sigma}\Delta_{X,g})\neq e^{-2\sigma}\phi(\Delta_{X,g})$ unless $\phi(\lambda)=c\lambda$. Conformal covariance is a linear phenomenon; nonlinear subordination destroys it.
