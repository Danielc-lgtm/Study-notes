# Notation

$G$ a topological group; $H \leq G$ a subgroup (not necessarily closed). $\overline{H}$ the topological closure of $H$ in $G$. $HH = \{hk : h, k \in H\}$, $H^{-1} = \{h^{-1} : h \in H\}$. The full registry is on the topic page.

---

# Motivation

A subgroup $H$ of a topological group $G$ need not be closed: think of $\mathbb{Q} \leq \mathbb{R}$. The closure $\overline{H}$ contains $H$ plus its limit points. The natural question: is $\overline{H}$ itself a subgroup? In other words, does the topological closure operation preserve the *algebraic* subgroup property?

The answer is yes, and the proof reveals how continuity of the group operations interacts with topology: the continuous image of a closure is contained in the closure of the image. So:
- $\overline{H} \cdot \overline{H} \subseteq \overline{H \cdot H} \subseteq \overline{H}$ (using $HH \subseteq H$ for the second inclusion).
- $\overline{H}^{-1} = \overline{H^{-1}} = \overline{H}$ (using $H^{-1} \subseteq H$).

These two are the closure of $H$ under multiplication and inversion, exactly the subgroup axioms.

This theorem is the most basic example of an algebraic property being **topologically robust** in a topological group: a property of $H$ extends to $\overline{H}$ via continuity. The same theorem applies to normal subgroups (Bredon's Proposition 15.10): if $H \trianglelefteq G$, then $\overline{H} \trianglelefteq G$ as well. This is what justifies the standard practice in topological group theory of *passing to closures freely* — if $H$ is "almost" a subgroup, $\overline{H}$ truly is.

---

# Statement

Let $G$ be a topological group and $H \leq G$ a subgroup.

1. **Closure is a subgroup:** $\overline{H} \leq G$ is a subgroup.

2. **Closure preserves normality:** If $H \trianglelefteq G$ (i.e., $H$ is a normal subgroup), then $\overline{H} \trianglelefteq G$.

---

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition "$H \leq G$ a subgroup" is general. The theorem applies in many disguised settings:

**$H$ generated by a set.** Property $B$: $H$ is the subgroup generated by a subset $S \subseteq G$ — i.e., $H = \langle S \rangle$ is the intersection of all subgroups containing $S$. The bridge: such an $H$ is a subgroup, so $\overline{H}$ is a subgroup. *Example:* the closure of the subgroup generated by a single element $\overline{\langle g \rangle}$ is the closure of the cyclic subgroup. For an element of finite order, $\langle g \rangle$ is already closed (finite); for infinite-order $g$ in a Lie group, the closure can be much larger (e.g., the closure of an irrational line on the torus is the whole torus).

**$H$ a dense subgroup.** Property $B$: $H$ is dense in $G$ (i.e., $\overline{H} = G$). The bridge: $\overline{H} = G$ is the whole group, trivially a subgroup. *Example:* every topological group has the closure of $\{e\}$ as a subgroup — if $G$ is Hausdorff, $\{e\}$ is closed and the closure is just $\{e\}$; if not, the closure is more interesting.

**The orbit of a continuous action.** Property $B$: $H$ is the stabilizer of a point under a continuous action. The bridge: stabilizers are subgroups, and continuity of the action makes them closed.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion "$\overline{H}$ is a subgroup" amplifies:

Combine with **the closure equals the subgroup for closed subgroups.** Property $D$: $H$ is closed. The amplified result $E$: $H = \overline{H}$, so the theorem is vacuous in this case but motivates the *only* notion of subgroup we want: the closed one. *Example:* in Lie theory, the only subgroups one studies are closed ones (Cartan's closed subgroup theorem: a closed subgroup of a Lie group is a Lie group).

Combine with **the connected component of the identity.** Property $D$: $G^0$, the connected component of $e$. The amplified result $E$: $G^0$ is a closed normal subgroup of $G$. Closed because connected components are closed; subgroup because translation by an element of $G^0$ maps $G^0$ to itself (continuity + connectedness). Normal because conjugation by any $g$ is a homeomorphism, hence sends $G^0$ to a connected subset containing $e$, which is $G^0$.

Combine with **quotient construction.** Property $D$: a closed subgroup $H$. The amplified result $E$: $G/H$ is a Hausdorff homogeneous $G$-space; if $H$ is also normal, $G/H$ is a topological group. The "closed" part is essential — quotients by non-closed subgroups can fail to be Hausdorff.

# Why Is It True

The argument is pure unwinding of the continuity of multiplication and inversion.

**Closure under multiplication.** We want to show $\overline{H} \cdot \overline{H} \subseteq \overline{H}$. Multiplication $\mu : G \times G \to G$ is continuous, and $\mu(H \times H) = HH \subseteq H$ (since $H$ is a subgroup). By the general fact "continuous image of closure is in closure of image":
$$\mu(\overline{H \times H}) \subseteq \overline{\mu(H \times H)} \subseteq \overline{H}.$$
Now $\overline{H \times H} = \overline{H} \times \overline{H}$ in the product topology (the closure of a product is the product of closures). So
$$\overline{H} \cdot \overline{H} = \mu(\overline{H} \times \overline{H}) \subseteq \overline{H}.$$

**Closure under inversion.** Inversion $\iota : G \to G$ is continuous, and $\iota(H) = H^{-1} \subseteq H$ (since $H$ is a subgroup). So
$$\iota(\overline{H}) \subseteq \overline{\iota(H)} = \overline{H^{-1}} \subseteq \overline{H}.$$
Hence $\overline{H}^{-1} \subseteq \overline{H}$. Applying inversion again gives $\overline{H} \subseteq \overline{H}^{-1}$, so $\overline{H}^{-1} = \overline{H}$.

**Contains identity.** $e \in H \subseteq \overline{H}$. Trivially.

These three together say $\overline{H}$ is a subgroup.

For normality: if $H$ is normal, $gHg^{-1} = H$ for all $g \in G$. Conjugation $C_g$ is a homeomorphism (by [[Thm - Translations are Homeomorphisms]]), so $C_g(\overline{H}) = \overline{C_g(H)} = \overline{H}$. Hence $g \overline{H} g^{-1} = \overline{H}$ for all $g$, and $\overline{H}$ is normal.

The deep principle is **continuity transports closure**: any algebraic property that is "continuous" (preserved under continuous maps) can be transferred from $H$ to $\overline{H}$ by taking closure. Multiplication and inversion satisfy this. So do conjugation (giving normality), the identity (trivial), and other group-theoretic operations.

---

# What Makes This Hard

The proof requires recognizing two facts: (i) the closure of a product is the product of closures in the product topology, and (ii) the continuous image of the closure is contained in the closure of the image. The second is sometimes confused with equality (continuous image *equal to* closure of image), which is *false* — the continuous image of a closed set need not be closed. The correct subset relation is one-way: $\mu(\overline{H} \times \overline{H}) \subseteq \overline{\mu(H \times H)}$. Failing to use the correct direction is the most common error.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**High-level strategy:**
Continuity of multiplication and inversion transports the closure: $\mu(\overline{H \times H}) \subseteq \overline{\mu(H \times H)} = \overline{HH} \subseteq \overline{H}$, and similarly for inversion. Combined with $e \in \overline{H}$, $\overline{H}$ is a subgroup.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Closure under multiplication.** $\overline{H} \cdot \overline{H} \subseteq \overline{H}$ via continuity of $\mu$ and the product-of-closures identity.
   - *Hint:* $\mu$ is continuous, $\overline{H \times H} = \overline{H} \times \overline{H}$, and continuous image of closure is in closure of image.

2. **Closure under inversion.** $\overline{H}^{-1} = \overline{H}$ via continuity of $\iota$.
   - *Hint:* Apply $\iota$ to both $\overline{H}$ and $H$.

3. **Closure contains identity.** $e \in H \subseteq \overline{H}$.

4. **Normality preservation.** Conjugation $C_g$ is a homeomorphism, so $C_g(\overline{H}) = \overline{C_g(H)} = \overline{H}$.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Continuous image of closure is in closure of image
> **Statement:** For a continuous $f : X \to Y$ and $A \subseteq X$, $f(\overline{A}) \subseteq \overline{f(A)}$.
>
> **Hint:** $f^{-1}(\overline{f(A)})$ is closed and contains $A$, so contains $\overline{A}$.
>
> **Why needed:** Transports closure under multiplication and inversion.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > $\overline{f(A)}$ is closed in $Y$. By continuity, $f^{-1}(\overline{f(A)})$ is closed in $X$. It contains $A$ (since $f(A) \subseteq \overline{f(A)}$). Since $\overline{A}$ is the smallest closed set containing $A$, $\overline{A} \subseteq f^{-1}(\overline{f(A)})$. Apply $f$: $f(\overline{A}) \subseteq \overline{f(A)}$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Closure of product equals product of closures
> **Statement:** For $A \subseteq X, B \subseteq Y$, $\overline{A \times B} = \overline{A} \times \overline{B}$ in the product topology.
>
> **Hint:** $\overline{A} \times \overline{B}$ is closed (product of closed sets), and contains $A \times B$. Conversely, every point of $\overline{A} \times \overline{B}$ is a limit of a net in $A \times B$.
>
> **Why needed:** Lets us write $\overline{H} \times \overline{H} = \overline{H \times H}$ in the multiplication step.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > $(\supseteq)$ $\overline{A} \times \overline{B}$ is closed in $X \times Y$ (product of closed sets is closed in product topology). It contains $A \times B$. So $\overline{A \times B} \subseteq \overline{A} \times \overline{B}$.
> >
> > $(\subseteq)$ Let $(a, b) \in \overline{A} \times \overline{B}$. There are nets $a_i \to a$ in $A$ and $b_j \to b$ in $B$. The product net $(a_i, b_j) \to (a, b)$ in $X \times Y$, and all members are in $A \times B$. So $(a, b) \in \overline{A \times B}$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> **$\overline{H}$ is a subgroup.**
>
> *Contains identity.* $e \in H \subseteq \overline{H}$.
>
> *Closed under multiplication.* Multiplication $\mu : G \times G \to G$ is continuous. By Lemma 1, $\mu(\overline{H \times H}) \subseteq \overline{\mu(H \times H)}$. By Lemma 2, $\overline{H \times H} = \overline{H} \times \overline{H}$. So
> $$\overline{H} \cdot \overline{H} = \mu(\overline{H} \times \overline{H}) = \mu(\overline{H \times H}) \subseteq \overline{\mu(H \times H)} = \overline{HH} \subseteq \overline{H}$$
> where the last inclusion uses $HH \subseteq H$ (since $H$ is a subgroup), so $\overline{HH} \subseteq \overline{H}$.
>
> *Closed under inversion.* Inversion $\iota : G \to G$ is continuous. By Lemma 1, $\iota(\overline{H}) \subseteq \overline{\iota(H)} = \overline{H^{-1}}$. Since $H^{-1} = H$ (as $H$ is a subgroup), $\overline{H^{-1}} = \overline{H}$. So $\overline{H}^{-1} = \iota(\overline{H}) \subseteq \overline{H}$. Applying inversion to this gives $\overline{H} \subseteq \overline{H}^{-1}$, so $\overline{H}^{-1} = \overline{H}$.
>
> Hence $\overline{H}$ is a subgroup.
>
> **Closure preserves normality.** Suppose $H \trianglelefteq G$, so $gHg^{-1} = H$ for all $g \in G$. Conjugation $C_g : G \to G$, $h \mapsto ghg^{-1}$, is a homeomorphism by [[Thm - Translations are Homeomorphisms]]. A homeomorphism takes closures to closures: $C_g(\overline{H}) = \overline{C_g(H)} = \overline{H}$. So $g \overline{H} g^{-1} = \overline{H}$ for all $g \in G$, hence $\overline{H} \trianglelefteq G$. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Closure of the kernel of a non-continuous homomorphism.** Suppose $\varphi : G \to G'$ is an algebraic homomorphism but not continuous. Its kernel $\ker \varphi$ is an algebraic subgroup; is $\overline{\ker \varphi}$ a subgroup? Yes — the theorem applies because we are using the closure in $G$, not continuity of $\varphi$.

**Closures of orbits in Lie group actions.** A Lie group $G$ acts on a manifold $M$. For $x \in M$, the orbit $G \cdot x$ is the image of $G$ under the orbit map; its closure $\overline{G \cdot x}$ is a closed $G$-invariant set. The general theorem says it's a finite union of orbits (each lower-dimensional) — used in computing minimal models in moduli problems.

**Closures of recurrent orbits in dynamics.** For a dynamical system $\Phi_t : X \to X$ with $\mathbb{R}$-action, the closure of an orbit $\Phi_\mathbb{R}(x)$ is a closed subgroup of the *enveloping semigroup* (a topological group when the system is uniquely ergodic). The structure theorem decomposes the closure into pieces.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Closed Subgroup Theorem *(from Lie Theory)*
> **Cartan's closed subgroup theorem** says: every closed subgroup of a Lie group is itself a Lie group (smooth manifold + smooth operations). So the topological theorem here, combined with the smooth structure, gives a much stronger statement: closures of subgroups in Lie groups inherit not just a subgroup structure but a Lie group structure.

> [!tip] Identity Component is Closed Normal *(from Topological Groups)*
> The connected component $G^0$ of the identity is closed (as a connected component) and normal (as a continuous image of itself under conjugation, which preserves connectedness). It is the largest connected subgroup of $G$, and $G/G^0$ is a (totally disconnected) topological group.

> [!tip] Centralizer and Normalizer Closures *(from Algebra)*
> For $S \subseteq G$, the **centralizer** $Z_G(S) = \{g : gs = sg \, \forall s \in S\}$ and **normalizer** $N_G(S) = \{g : gSg^{-1} = S\}$ are subgroups. In a topological group, they are closed (intersections of preimages of $\{e\}$ under continuous maps). This is a corollary of the more general principle here.
