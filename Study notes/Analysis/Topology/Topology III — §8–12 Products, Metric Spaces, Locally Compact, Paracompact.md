# Notation Registry

- $X, Y, Z, X_\alpha$ — topological spaces
- $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ — Cartesian product, with the product (Tychonoff) topology unless stated otherwise
- $\pi_\alpha : \prod_\beta X_\beta \to X_\alpha$ — coordinate projection
- $X \sqcup Y$, $\bigsqcup_\alpha X_\alpha$ — disjoint union (topological sum)
- $(X, d)$ — a metric space with metric $d$
- $\operatorname{diam}(A) = \sup\{d(p, q) : p, q \in A\}$ — diameter of a subset
- A **Cauchy sequence** is $\{x_n\}$ with $d(x_n, x_m) \to 0$ as $n, m \to \infty$
- $X$ **complete** means every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges in $X$
- $X$ **totally bounded** means every $\varepsilon > 0$ admits a finite cover by $\varepsilon$-balls
- $X^+ = X \cup \{\infty\}$ — the one-point compactification
- $\beta X$ — the Stone–Čech compactification of $X$
- $\operatorname{supp}(f) = \overline{\{x : f(x) \neq 0\}}$ — the support of $f$
- $\mathcal{U} \succeq \mathcal{V}$ — $\mathcal{U}$ is a **refinement** of $\mathcal{V}$ (every member of $\mathcal{U}$ lies in some member of $\mathcal{V}$)
- A collection is **locally finite** if every point has a neighborhood meeting only finitely many of its members
- $C_c(X), C_0(X), C_b(X)$ — continuous compactly supported / vanishing at $\infty$ / bounded continuous real functions on $X$
- $\sigma$-compact: $X = \bigcup_n K_n$ with each $K_n$ compact

# Motivation

Sections 8 through 12 build the *operational toolkit* that topology supplies to the rest of analysis. The opening notion of §1–7 — compactness, connectedness, Hausdorffness — was about *recognizing* good behavior in a given space. The notions of §8–12 are about *constructing* good behavior: building new spaces from old, controlling sequences in metric spaces well enough to guarantee limits exist, producing continuous functions where they were not given, and ensuring partitions of unity for gluing local data to global. By the end of this topic you have the machinery analysts actually use.

§8 introduces the **product topology**. Given a family of topological spaces $\{X_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$, there is essentially one reasonable topology on $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ — the one that makes all projections continuous and is the coarsest such. This is generated by the subbasis of "cylinders" $\pi_\alpha^{-1}(U_\alpha)$, and on a *finite* product reduces to the usual box topology (open rectangles). On infinite products, the cylinder topology and the box topology *differ*, and the right one to choose is the cylinder one: it is the one that makes the universal property work, the one that preserves compactness under Tychonoff's theorem, and the one that arises naturally in function spaces $X^A = \{f : A \to X\}$ under pointwise convergence. **Tychonoff's theorem** — the product of any family of compact spaces is compact — is the highlight of the section, and it is one of the most powerful theorems in topology. For finite products it follows from the tube lemma; for infinite products it is equivalent to the Axiom of Choice and is the source of Banach–Alaoglu, the existence of ultrafilters, and the compactness theorem in logic.

§9 returns to **metric spaces** to develop completeness, total boundedness, and metrizability. Completeness says every Cauchy sequence converges — the topological space remembers enough about the metric to close up under limit-of-limits. Total boundedness says the space can be $\varepsilon$-covered by finitely many balls for every $\varepsilon$. The remarkable theorem of the section is that for a metric space, the three properties — **compact**, **sequentially compact**, **complete + totally bounded** — are equivalent. Each of the three views is the right one in a different problem class: compactness for general topology, sequential compactness for analysis, complete+totally bounded for explicit constructions. The section closes with the **Urysohn metrization theorem**: a second countable Hausdorff regular space is metrizable, with an explicit construction embedding it into $[0, 1]^\mathbb{N}$ via a countable family of separating functions. This is the bridge from "topological space with nice enough separation" to "metric space", and it explains why analysts can often *assume* a metric without loss.

§10 produces the **continuous functions** that the Urysohn metrization theorem assumed exist. **Urysohn's lemma** says: in a normal space, given disjoint closed sets $F, G$, there is a continuous $f : X \to [0, 1]$ that is $0$ on $F$ and $1$ on $G$. This is the topological analog of building a bump function, and the proof is a beautiful inductive construction using only normality and the dyadic rationals — explicit, no choice axiom needed. **Tietze's extension theorem** then says: in a normal space, any continuous bounded real-valued function on a closed subspace extends to the whole space. Urysohn's lemma is the special case where the original function takes values $0$ and $1$ on two disjoint closed sets. Together, Urysohn + Tietze make normal spaces *function-rich* enough to do all the standard constructions of analysis.

§11 introduces **local compactness**. A space is locally compact if every point has a compact neighborhood. $\mathbb{R}^n$ is locally compact but not compact; infinite-dimensional Banach spaces and most function spaces are not locally compact. Locally compact Hausdorff (LCH) spaces are the natural setting for much of analysis — they have enough compactness for Heine–Borel-style arguments locally, and they have enough Hausdorffness for limits. The **one-point compactification** $X^+ = X \cup \{\infty\}$ adjoins a single point at infinity to any LCH space, producing a compact Hausdorff space. The **Stone–Čech compactification** $\beta X$ is the larger, "maximal" compactification: every bounded continuous function on $X$ extends to $\beta X$, and every continuous map from $X$ to a compact Hausdorff space factors through $\beta X$. The two compactifications are at opposite ends of a spectrum, and each is the right one for different problems.

§12 introduces **paracompactness**, the most flexible covering condition. A space is paracompact if every open cover has a *locally finite* open refinement. Compactness is the special case where one can extract a *finite* subcover; paracompactness is the right generalization for non-compact spaces, and the cleanest known sufficient condition for the existence of *partitions of unity*. A **partition of unity** subordinate to a cover $\{U_\alpha\}$ is a family of continuous functions $\{\rho_\alpha\}$ with $\operatorname{supp}(\rho_\alpha) \subseteq U_\alpha$, $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha \equiv 1$, and the supports forming a locally finite cover. Partitions of unity are *the* tool for gluing local data into global on a manifold: every theorem about manifolds whose proof says "by partition of unity" relies on this section. **Theorem 12.11** (every locally compact, σ-compact Hausdorff space is paracompact) is what guarantees partitions of unity exist on every smooth manifold.

The unifying frame for §8–12: we are taking the bare topological axioms of §1–7 and adding precisely as much structure as is needed for analysis. Products produce new spaces; completeness ensures limits; metrizability ensures sequences suffice; normality ensures separating functions; local compactness ensures local control; paracompactness ensures global gluing. Each axiom is a specific commodity: this topic is the inventory of commodities and the theorems that say when each one is available.

# Concept Map

## §8 Products

- **[[Def - Product Topology]]**
	- The **product topology** on $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ is the topology generated by the subbasis of cylinders $\pi_\alpha^{-1}(U_\alpha)$ where $U_\alpha \subseteq X_\alpha$ is open. Equivalently, it is the coarsest topology making every projection $\pi_\alpha$ continuous. A basis consists of finite intersections of cylinders — sets $\prod_\alpha V_\alpha$ where $V_\alpha = X_\alpha$ for all but finitely many $\alpha$. The product is universal: a map $f : Z \to \prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ is continuous if and only if every coordinate $\pi_\alpha \circ f$ is continuous. The product topology agrees with the box topology *only* on finite products; on infinite products they differ, and the product topology is the analytically useful one.

- **[[Thm - Tychonoff Theorem for Finite Products]]**
	- The product of finitely many compact spaces is compact. The proof uses the tube lemma: induct on the number of factors, and for two factors $X \times Y$ with both compact, given an open cover, the tube lemma extracts a compact tube around each $\{x\} \times Y$, which can then be combined into a finite subcover of $X \times Y$. This is the case that doesn't need the axiom of choice and that gives compactness of cubes $[0, 1]^n$, hence Heine–Borel.

- **[[Thm - Tychonoff Theorem]]**
	- The product of any family of compact spaces is compact in the product topology. The cleanest proof routes through universal nets: a universal net in $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ has each coordinate as a universal net in $X_\alpha$, which converges by compactness, hence the original net converges. This is equivalent to the Axiom of Choice and is the source of Banach–Alaoglu, the existence of ultrafilters, the compactness theorem of first-order logic, and the existence of the Stone–Čech compactification.

- **[[Thm - Net Convergence in Product is Coordinatewise]]**
	- A net $\{x_\alpha^{(\beta)}\}_{\beta \in D}$ in $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ converges to $(x_\alpha)$ if and only if every coordinate net $\{x_\alpha^{(\beta)}\}_{\beta \in D}$ converges to $x_\alpha$ in $X_\alpha$. This is the *defining* property of the product topology — pointwise convergence — and is what makes the Tychonoff proof slick. For functions $X^A$, it specializes to "$f_\beta \to f$ in product topology iff $f_\beta(a) \to f(a)$ for every $a \in A$".

- **[[Def - Disjoint Union (Topological Sum)]]**
	- The **disjoint union** $\bigsqcup_\alpha X_\alpha$ has underlying set $\bigsqcup_\alpha X_\alpha = \bigcup_\alpha X_\alpha \times \{\alpha\}$ with each $X_\alpha \times \{\alpha\}$ clopen and homeomorphic to $X_\alpha$. Equivalently, the finest topology making each inclusion $X_\alpha \hookrightarrow \bigsqcup_\beta X_\beta$ continuous. Universal property: a map $f : \bigsqcup_\alpha X_\alpha \to Y$ is continuous if and only if each restriction $f|_{X_\alpha}$ is continuous. This is the "coproduct" of topological spaces, dual to the product.

- **[[Ex - Product of Hausdorff is Hausdorff]]** (⭐)
	- Show that an arbitrary product of Hausdorff spaces is Hausdorff. Given distinct $x = (x_\alpha), y = (y_\alpha)$, pick $\alpha_0$ with $x_{\alpha_0} \neq y_{\alpha_0}$, separate in $X_{\alpha_0}$, and pull back the disjoint opens via $\pi_{\alpha_0}$.

- **[[Ex - The diagonal is closed iff Hausdorff]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that $X$ is Hausdorff if and only if the diagonal $\Delta = \{(x, x) : x \in X\}$ is closed in $X \times X$. This is the cleanest reformulation of Hausdorffness, and it converts a logical statement (separation by opens) into a topological one (closedness of a specific set).

- **[[Ex - Box vs product topology on a countable product]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- On $\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$ (countable product of copies of $\mathbb{R}$), show that the box topology (basis: arbitrary open boxes $\prod U_n$) is strictly finer than the product topology (basis: cylinders $\prod U_n$ with $U_n = \mathbb{R}$ for cofinitely many $n$). Exhibit a sequence converging in the product but not the box topology — e.g. $x^{(k)}$ with $x^{(k)}_n = 1/k$ for all $n$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Function spaces and pointwise convergence *(from Analysis)*
> The space $\mathbb{R}^X$ of all functions $X \to \mathbb{R}$, with the product topology, has the property that $f_\alpha \to f$ if and only if $f_\alpha(x) \to f(x)$ for every $x$. This is the topology of **pointwise convergence**, and it is one of the standard topologies on function spaces. Uniform convergence corresponds to a finer topology (the sup-norm topology on $C_b(X)$), and the relationship between them is part of every functional analysis course.

## §9 Metric Spaces Again

- **[[Def - Cauchy Sequence and Complete Metric Space]]**
	- A **Cauchy sequence** in $(X, d)$ is $\{x_n\}$ such that for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $N$ with $d(x_n, x_m) < \varepsilon$ for all $n, m \geq N$. A metric space $X$ is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence converges. Completeness is a property of the *metric*, not the topology — two homeomorphic metric spaces can have different completeness (e.g. $\mathbb{R}$ is complete, $(0, 1)$ is not, but they are homeomorphic). Completeness is what one wants for fixed-point theorems (Banach), for existence theorems (Picard–Lindelöf), and for the construction of Banach spaces.

- **[[Def - Totally Bounded Metric Space]]**
	- $X$ is **totally bounded** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a finite cover by $\varepsilon$-balls. Total boundedness is stronger than boundedness — a bounded subset of an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space need not be totally bounded (the unit ball is bounded but contains an infinite orthonormal set whose pairwise distances are $\sqrt{2}$). Total boundedness is the topological essence of "finite" in metric spaces.

- **[[Thm - Compactness in Metric Spaces (Three Equivalents)]]**
	- For a metric space $X$, the following are equivalent: (1) $X$ is compact; (2) $X$ is sequentially compact; (3) $X$ is complete and totally bounded. This trilateral equivalence is the central theorem of metric space theory and the reason analysts can freely interconvert between the three perspectives. The hardest implication (3) ⇒ (2) uses a diagonal extraction argument; the equivalence is also called the **Heine–Borel–Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem** in this generality.

- **[[Thm - Lebesgue Number Lemma]]**
	- For a compact metric space $X$ and an open cover $\{U_\alpha\}$, there exists $\delta > 0$ (the **Lebesgue number**) such that every subset of $X$ of diameter $< \delta$ is contained in some $U_\alpha$. The proof: for each $x$, find a ball $B_{2\varepsilon(x)}(x) \subseteq U_{\alpha(x)}$; extract a finite subcover of the smaller $B_{\varepsilon(x)}$ balls; let $\delta = \min \varepsilon(x_i)$ over the finite subcover. This is the bridge from open covers to uniform fineness, used to prove uniform continuity on compacts and the homotopy lifting property.

- **[[Def - Completely Regular Space]]**
	- A Hausdorff space $X$ is **completely regular** (or $T_{3\frac{1}{2}}$, Tychonoff) if for every $x \in X$ and closed $C \subseteq X$ with $x \notin C$ there is a continuous $f : X \to [0, 1]$ with $f(x) = 0$ and $f \equiv 1$ on $C$. Completely regular is strictly between regular and normal: it is the minimal hypothesis for the existence of "enough" continuous functions to separate points from closed sets. Completely regular spaces are exactly the subspaces of products of intervals.

- **[[Thm - Urysohn Metrization Theorem]]**
	- A second countable Hausdorff regular space is metrizable. The proof constructs a countable family of separating functions (from complete regularity, which is automatic given second countable + regular), embeds the space into $[0, 1]^\mathbb{N}$ via these functions, and uses the explicit metric $d(x, y) = \sum_n d_n(x_n, y_n)/2^n$ on the product to pull back a metric. This is the cleanest sufficient condition for metrizability — every space one meets in practice that is "topologically nice" satisfies it.

- **[[Ex - The completion of a metric space]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Construct the completion $\widehat{X}$ of a metric space $X$ as the set of equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences (two Cauchy sequences equivalent if $d(x_n, y_n) \to 0$), with metric $d([\{x_n\}], [\{y_n\}]) = \lim d(x_n, y_n)$. Verify this is a metric, $X \hookrightarrow \widehat{X}$ is an isometric embedding with dense image, and $\widehat{X}$ is complete.

- **[[Ex - A bounded metric topologically equivalent to any metric]]** (⭐)
	- Show that $d'(x, y) = \min(d(x, y), 1)$ and $d''(x, y) = d(x, y)/(1 + d(x, y))$ are bounded metrics topologically equivalent to $d$. This lets one always *assume* a bounded metric, useful for the countable product construction in the Urysohn metrization theorem.

- **[[Ex - Compactness via complete and totally bounded]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove (3) ⇒ (1) of the metric compactness equivalence directly: $X$ complete + totally bounded implies compact, by a diagonal argument extracting Cauchy subsequences from arbitrary sequences.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Banach spaces and the contraction principle *(from Functional Analysis)*
> A **Banach space** is a complete normed vector space — the metric induced by the norm is complete. The **Banach fixed point theorem** says: a strict contraction on a complete metric space has a unique fixed point, reachable by iteration. This is the engine of the Picard–Lindelöf existence theorem for ODEs, the inverse function theorem (which uses it in [[Topology III — §8–12 Products, Metric Spaces, Locally Compact, Paracompact|Topology III]]), and a dozen other existence proofs. See [[Thm - The Contraction Mapping Principle]].

## §10 Existence of Real-Valued Functions

- **[[Thm - Urysohn's Lemma]]**
	- If $X$ is normal and $F \subseteq U$ with $F$ closed and $U$ open, there is a continuous $f : X \to [0, 1]$ with $f \equiv 0$ on $F$ and $f \equiv 1$ on $X \setminus U$. The proof uses the normality axiom recursively at dyadic levels: produce open sets $U_{m/2^n}$ for all dyadic rationals in $[0, 1]$ with $\overline{U_r} \subseteq U_s$ when $r < s$, then define $f(x) = \inf\{r : x \in U_r\}$. The key trick is the dyadic interpolation — recursively applying normality between any two already-constructed levels. This is the "bump function" of general topology.

- **[[Thm - Tietze Extension Theorem]]**
	- If $X$ is normal and $F \subseteq X$ is closed, every continuous $f : F \to \mathbb{R}$ extends to a continuous $g : X \to \mathbb{R}$, with $\sup_X g = \sup_F f$ and $\inf_X g = \inf_F f$. The proof iterates Urysohn's lemma to construct a uniformly convergent series of approximations. This generalizes Urysohn (which is the case of a function on $F \cup G$ taking values $0$ on $F$ and $1$ on $G$) and is the standard tool for "extending a continuous function to a larger space".

- **[[Ex - Urysohn's lemma in a metric space]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For disjoint closed $F, G$ in a metric space, exhibit $f(x) = d(x, F)/(d(x,F) + d(x,G))$ as the Urysohn function. This is the explicit construction; the abstract proof in general normal spaces is a generalization.

- **[[Ex - Tietze extension for vector-valued functions]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that the Tietze extension theorem extends to maps $F \to \mathbb{R}^n$ by extending each coordinate separately. The closed F can be arbitrary, the target finite-dimensional.

- **[[Ex - Failure of Tietze without normality]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Construct a non-normal Hausdorff space $X$ with a closed $F$ and continuous $f : F \to \mathbb{R}$ that has no continuous extension. (The Tychonoff plank — a non-normal space — is the standard example.) This shows normality is essential.

## §11 Locally Compact Spaces

- **[[Def - Locally Compact Space]]**
	- $X$ is **locally compact** if every point has a compact neighborhood. In a Hausdorff space this is equivalent to: every point has a neighborhood basis of compact neighborhoods. $\mathbb{R}^n$ is locally compact; infinite-dimensional Banach spaces and most function spaces are not. Locally compact Hausdorff (LCH) spaces are the natural setting for much of measure theory, harmonic analysis, and the theory of locally compact groups.

- **[[Thm - LCH Implies Completely Regular]]**
	- A locally compact Hausdorff space is completely regular. The proof: given $x$ and closed $C$ not containing $x$, find a compact neighborhood $K$ of $x$ disjoint from $C$; on the compact Hausdorff $K^+$ (one-point compactification), normality gives a Urysohn function; restrict back. This places LCH spaces in the function-rich part of the separation hierarchy and explains why measure theory and harmonic analysis can do without normality assumptions when the underlying space is locally compact Hausdorff.

- **[[Thm - One-Point Compactification]]**
	- Let $X$ be locally compact Hausdorff. Define $X^+ = X \cup \{\infty\}$ with opens $\{U \subseteq X^+ : U \text{ open in } X\} \cup \{X^+ \setminus K : K \subseteq X \text{ compact}\}$. Then $X^+$ is compact Hausdorff with $X$ embedded as an open dense subset. This is *the unique* compact Hausdorff space in which $X$ embeds as an open subset whose complement is a single point. The one-point compactification is the "minimal" compactification; the Stone–Čech is the "maximal".

- **[[Thm - Stone–Čech Compactification]]**
	- Let $X$ be a completely regular space. There is a compact Hausdorff space $\beta X$ with $X \hookrightarrow \beta X$ as a dense subspace, such that every bounded continuous $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ extends uniquely to a continuous $\bar f : \beta X \to \mathbb{R}$. Equivalently, $\beta X$ has the universal property: every continuous map from $X$ to a compact Hausdorff space factors uniquely through $\beta X$. The construction embeds $X$ in $[0, 1]^{C_b(X)}$ and takes the closure. This is the universal compactification.

- **[[Ex - The one-point compactification of Rn is the sphere]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show $(\mathbb{R}^n)^+ \cong S^n$. Stereographic projection from the north pole gives the homeomorphism: the north pole maps to $\infty$, and the rest of the sphere maps to $\mathbb{R}^n$. The map is a continuous bijection from the compact $S^n$ to the Hausdorff $\mathbb{R}^n \cup \{\infty\}$, hence a homeomorphism.

- **[[Ex - LC + Hausdorff is needed for closure-of-open-bounded properties]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Provide an example where local compactness fails (an infinite-dimensional Banach space) and exhibit a closed bounded set that is not compact, to drive home that LCH is the structural assumption needed for many "compactness arguments".

- **[[Ex - Stone–Čech compactification of N]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Discuss $\beta\mathbb{N}$, the Stone–Čech compactification of the natural numbers (with the discrete topology). Show it has cardinality $2^{2^{\aleph_0}}$, contains $\mathbb{N}$ as a dense subset, has $\beta\mathbb{N} \setminus \mathbb{N}$ as a closed nowhere-dense subset, and elements of $\beta\mathbb{N}$ correspond to ultrafilters on $\mathbb{N}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Riesz Representation Theorem *(from Measure Theory)*
> On a locally compact Hausdorff space $X$, every positive linear functional $\Lambda$ on $C_c(X)$ is given by integration against a unique Radon measure $\mu$: $\Lambda f = \int f\,d\mu$. The local compactness gives the compact supports the right scaling properties; the Hausdorff gives uniqueness. This is the bridge from topology to measure theory — see [[Measure Theory I — §1 Measure Spaces]] — and it is why measure theory is most naturally developed on LCH spaces.

## §12 Paracompact Spaces

- **[[Def - Locally Finite Family and Refinement]]**
	- A collection $\mathcal{U}$ of subsets of $X$ is **locally finite** if every point has a neighborhood meeting only finitely many $U \in \mathcal{U}$. A collection $\mathcal{V}$ is a **refinement** of $\mathcal{U}$ if every $V \in \mathcal{V}$ is contained in some $U \in \mathcal{U}$. Locally finite is much weaker than finite: an open cover of $\mathbb{R}$ by intervals $(n - 1, n + 1)$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ is locally finite (each point lies in at most two), but it is infinite.

- **[[Def - Paracompact Space]]**
	- A Hausdorff space $X$ is **paracompact** if every open cover has a locally finite open refinement. Compactness implies paracompactness (every finite subcover is locally finite). Metric spaces are paracompact (a deep theorem of Stone). The non-trivial examples of paracompactness are non-compact: $\mathbb{R}^n$, smooth manifolds, locally compact Hausdorff $\sigma$-compact spaces.

- **[[Thm - Paracompact Implies Normal]]**
	- Every paracompact Hausdorff space is normal. The proof uses local finiteness to construct disjoint opens around any two disjoint closed sets: separate locally and combine using local finiteness to ensure the union of separations stays open and disjoint. This places paracompactness above normality in the hierarchy, so paracompact spaces have Urysohn and Tietze.

- **[[Def - Partition of Unity]]**
	- A **partition of unity** subordinate to an open cover $\{U_\alpha\}$ is a family of continuous functions $\{\rho_\alpha : X \to [0, 1]\}$ such that $\operatorname{supp}(\rho_\alpha) \subseteq U_\alpha$, the supports form a locally finite cover, and $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha \equiv 1$ pointwise. Partitions of unity are *the* tool for converting local constructions on a manifold to global ones — every theorem in differential geometry that uses local coordinates and patches together typically invokes a partition of unity.

- **[[Thm - Paracompact Has Partitions of Unity]]**
	- In a paracompact Hausdorff space, every open cover admits a subordinate partition of unity. The proof: pass to a locally finite open refinement, shrink each set in the refinement to a closed set still covering, apply Urysohn (normality from paracompact) to get bump functions, normalize. This is the existence theorem that makes paracompactness useful.

- **[[Thm - Locally Compact σ-Compact Hausdorff is Paracompact]]**
	- A locally compact, $\sigma$-compact, Hausdorff space is paracompact. The proof constructs an exhaustion by compact sets $K_n$ with $K_n \subseteq \operatorname{int}(K_{n+1})$, and refines a given open cover within each "shell" $K_{n+1} \setminus \operatorname{int}(K_n)$. Every smooth manifold (paracompact by definition or by being locally Euclidean second countable) satisfies this. This is the theorem that guarantees partitions of unity exist on every reasonable space encountered in geometry and analysis.

- **[[Ex - Partitions of unity on Rn]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Construct an explicit partition of unity on $\mathbb{R}^n$ subordinate to the cover $\{B(k, 2) : k \in \mathbb{Z}^n\}$ (balls of radius $2$ centered at integer points). Use bump functions and shifts.

- **[[Ex - A non-paracompact space]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- The long line (uncountable analog of $[0, \infty)$ glued from $\omega_1$ copies of $[0, 1)$) is locally Euclidean but not paracompact. Show that any open cover by intervals of length $< 1$ has no locally finite refinement, since locally finite would force countability of the cover.

- **[[Ex - Partition of unity for a smooth manifold]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Outline why every smooth manifold (Hausdorff second countable) has $C^\infty$ partitions of unity: it is locally compact Hausdorff σ-compact, so paracompact; bump functions in $\mathbb{R}^n$ pull back to smooth functions on coordinate charts.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Riemannian Metrics on Smooth Manifolds *(from Differential Geometry)*
> A **Riemannian metric** on a smooth manifold is a smoothly varying inner product on each tangent space. Such metrics exist on every smooth manifold, and the existence is proved by a partition of unity argument: take local inner products in charts (the Euclidean one in $\mathbb{R}^n$), and glue them globally using a partition of unity. The whole apparatus of Riemannian geometry — geodesics, curvature, length, distance — flows from this existence theorem, which depends entirely on paracompactness.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — What do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring targets in §8–12 cluster into four families. The first is **constructing a topology** on a set, then verifying it has desired properties — products and disjoint unions, completions of metric spaces, compactifications. The standard route is via universal properties: the topology one wants is characterized by which maps in or out should be continuous, and that characterization is enough to construct it.

The second is **proving compactness** of a constructed space — products of compacts, closures of bounded sets, one-point compactifications. Tychonoff (in any form) is the dominant tool; for metric spaces, the equivalence with completeness + total boundedness gives an alternate route.

The third is **constructing continuous functions** — Urysohn's lemma for separating sets, Tietze extension for extending from closed subspaces, partitions of unity for gluing. These take an abstract separation hypothesis (normal, paracompact) and produce concrete continuous bump functions, which are the building blocks of every gluing or approximation argument in analysis.

The fourth is **metrizability** — showing that a given topological space admits a metric inducing its topology. The Urysohn metrization theorem (second countable Hausdorff regular ⇒ metrizable) is the standard tool. Once metrizability is established, the full toolkit of metric space analysis becomes available.

**Sources — What assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The most leveraged hypotheses are: a *compactness* hypothesis (on the space, on its closure, on a covering family), a *completeness* hypothesis (the metric space has all Cauchy limits), a *separation* hypothesis (Hausdorff, regular, normal, completely regular), a *countability* hypothesis (second countable, separable, $\sigma$-compact), or a *local* hypothesis (locally compact, locally connected). Each of these gates a specific theorem:

- Compact + Hausdorff ⇒ normal (proved in §7) ⇒ Urysohn ⇒ Tietze
- Normal + second countable + regular ⇒ Urysohn metrization
- Locally compact Hausdorff ⇒ completely regular, has one-point compactification
- Locally compact Hausdorff $\sigma$-compact ⇒ paracompact ⇒ partitions of unity
- Complete + totally bounded ⇒ compact (in metric spaces)
- Compact ⇒ Tychonoff (product preservation)

Most problems route through one of these chains and are easier once one recognizes which hypotheses are present.

# Legal Operations

1. **Construct an embedding into a product.** When you want to show $X$ embeds in some standard space, find a family of separating continuous functions $\{f_\alpha : X \to Y_\alpha\}$ and use them to define $X \to \prod_\alpha Y_\alpha$ by $x \mapsto (f_\alpha(x))$. The universal property of the product makes this map continuous. To show it is an embedding, you need (a) injectivity (the $f_\alpha$ separate points), (b) the induced topology agrees with the original (use closedness or openness checks). *Trigger:* you have many continuous maps out of $X$ and want to "package" them.

2. **Pass to the one-point or Stone–Čech compactification.** When dealing with a non-compact space and you need compactness, embed it in $X^+$ or $\beta X$. For LCH spaces, $X^+$ is small and analyzable. For arbitrary completely regular spaces, $\beta X$ exists but is harder to compute. *Trigger:* a compactness argument needed for a non-compact space. *Pattern:* extend continuous bounded functions to the compactification, use compactness there, restrict back.

3. **Apply Urysohn's lemma to separate closed sets by a continuous function.** In a normal space, any two disjoint closed sets can be separated by a continuous function. This is the standard tool to produce bump functions, characteristic-function-like objects, and weighted averages. *Trigger:* you need a continuous $f$ that is $0$ on $F$ and $1$ on $G$, with $F, G$ disjoint closed.

4. **Apply Tietze to extend a continuous function from a closed subspace.** In a normal space, any continuous real-valued function on a closed subspace extends. Most common variant: extend a continuous map on $F$ to a continuous map on $X$. *Trigger:* you have a function on a closed set and need it on the whole space.

5. **Use partitions of unity to glue local data into global.** In a paracompact Hausdorff space, given a local construction on each $U_\alpha$ in a cover, combine using $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha \cdot (\text{local thing}_\alpha)$ to get a global construction. *Trigger:* a local-to-global construction. *Pattern:* this is *the* construction used for Riemannian metrics, connections, smooth functions on manifolds, integration over manifolds, sheaf cohomology, partitions of unity in PDE.

6. **Reduce to a finite subcover, then partition.** Compactness lets you reduce an arbitrary cover to a finite one; finiteness lets you induct or assemble explicitly. The combination — compact + Urysohn — gives explicit constructions of separating functions on each piece. *Trigger:* compact space + a global construction needed.

7. **Use the three metric compactness equivalents.** To prove a metric space is compact, you can show: (a) it is bounded and closed in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (Heine–Borel — but only in $\mathbb{R}^n$); (b) every sequence has a convergent subsequence; (c) it is complete and totally bounded. Each is the cleanest route in different settings. *Trigger:* metric space + compactness needed. *Pattern:* check (c) when explicit, (b) when sequences are easy, (a) only in Euclidean.

8. **Use Tychonoff to assemble compactness from coordinate compactness.** If each $X_\alpha$ is compact, $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ is compact in product topology. This is the engine of Banach–Alaoglu, of the existence of ultrafilters, and of many "limit of a function space" arguments. *Trigger:* you have a family of compact factors. *Pattern:* embed the desired object into a product, identify it as a closed subset, conclude.

9. **Use the Lebesgue number lemma to choose a "uniform" scale.** Given a compact metric space and an open cover, the lemma gives a $\delta > 0$ such that every $\delta$-small set is contained in some cover element. *Trigger:* you need a uniform scale at which every piece of the space is "in one chart" — essential for uniform continuity, homotopy lifting, the existence of geodesics.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming bounded ⇒ compact outside $\mathbb{R}^n$
> Heine–Borel — "closed and bounded ⇒ compact" — holds *only* in finite-dimensional normed spaces. In infinite-dimensional Banach spaces (sequence spaces, function spaces, Hilbert spaces), the closed unit ball is *not* compact, by F. Riesz's theorem. In general metric spaces, the right replacement is "complete + totally bounded ⇒ compact", and total boundedness is much stronger than bounded.

> [!warning] 2. Using the box topology on an infinite product
> The "natural" topology to put on $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ where each $X_\alpha$ has a topology might *seem* to be the box topology (open sets generated by arbitrary boxes $\prod U_\alpha$). For infinite products, this is *wrong* — it makes the projections continuous but the universal property fails (a map into the product is continuous in box topology iff each coordinate is, plus a much stronger uniform condition). The product topology (using cylinders with $U_\alpha = X_\alpha$ cofinitely often) is the only choice that gives Tychonoff and pointwise convergence.

> [!warning] 3. Claiming Urysohn/Tietze for non-normal spaces
> Urysohn's lemma and Tietze's extension theorem fail without normality. The Tychonoff plank is a Hausdorff space where neither holds. *Always check normality* before invoking these; in compact Hausdorff or paracompact Hausdorff spaces, normality is automatic, but in other settings it must be verified.

> [!warning] 4. Treating completeness as a topological invariant
> Completeness is a property of the *metric*, not the topological space. $\mathbb{R}$ is complete with the usual metric but $(0, 1)$ is not — yet they are homeomorphic via $x \mapsto \tan(\pi x - \pi/2)$. The standard remedies are *completely metrizable* (admitting some metric in which the space is complete) or *Polish space* (complete metrizable separable), which *are* topological properties. Make sure your hypotheses about completeness refer to the right metric.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in §8–12 cluster into four families: constructing a topology and verifying its universal property, proving compactness or metrizability of a given space, constructing a continuous function with prescribed values on certain sets, and gluing local data to global.

For **constructing a topology**, the universal property is the master tool. Want a topology on $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ making all projections continuous? Take the coarsest one — the cylinder-generated topology. Want a topology on $X \sqcup Y$ making both inclusions continuous? Take the finest — clopen each piece. Want a topology on $X/{\sim}$ making the quotient map continuous? Take the finest — opens are those whose preimage is open. Each of these is forced by the universal property; you do not have to be clever. Then verify the universal property as a separate step (a map is continuous if and only if the relevant maps in or out are), which is usually a few lines.

For **proving compactness of a constructed space**, your tool depends on the construction. Products of compacts: Tychonoff. Closures of bounded subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$: Heine–Borel. Metric space: the three-equivalent theorem gives complete+totally bounded as the explicit route. One-point compactification: by construction. Stone–Čech: by construction as the closure of a product embedding. Often, the path to compactness is "embed in a known compact, identify as closed subset". This is the route for Banach–Alaoglu: embed the unit ball of $V^*$ in $\prod_x [-\lVert x\rVert, \lVert x\rVert]$, compact by Tychonoff, identify the unit ball as closed.

For **constructing a continuous function**, Urysohn's lemma is the workhorse. The cleanest setup: a normal space, two disjoint closed sets, a function $0$ on one and $1$ on the other. If you need finer control — a function with a specific shape — use Urysohn to produce the bumps, then linearly combine. For extensions, Tietze. For piecewise constructions on a cover, partitions of unity (paracompactness assumed). The hierarchy here is *exactly* the separation hierarchy: each additional axiom buys one more class of constructions.

For **gluing local to global**, a partition of unity. The standard recipe: cover by $\{U_\alpha\}$, get $\rho_\alpha$ with $\operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha \subseteq U_\alpha$, $\sum\rho_\alpha = 1$. Define the local object on each $U_\alpha$, multiply by $\rho_\alpha$, and sum. The sum is well-defined because at each point only finitely many $\rho_\alpha$ are nonzero (local finiteness). The construction inherits the regularity ($C^k$, $C^\infty$, analytic, ...) of the local pieces, modified by the regularity of the $\rho_\alpha$. This is *the* construction in differential geometry, sheaf theory, and PDE.

A non-obvious general principle: **separation axioms are commodities; spend them carefully**. Many theorems can be proved with less than the maximum separation. For instance, the existence of a Riesz representation theorem only needs locally compact Hausdorff, not normality. The Banach–Alaoglu theorem only needs $V$ to be a normed space (not a Banach space), because the relevant compactness lives in the dual. Always identify which separation property is *really* needed for the conclusion you want — using more is wasteful and may exclude examples.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Tychonoff Theorem|Tychonoff's theorem]]**: The product of any family of compact spaces is compact in the product topology. This is the single most-cited result in topology for analysis — it underwrites Banach–Alaoglu, the existence of ultrafilters, the compactness theorem in logic, the existence of Stone–Čech compactifications, and much else. Recognize it any time you have a family of compact spaces or need to construct a compact "limit" of some kind: embed in a product, identify as a closed subset.

- **[[Thm - Urysohn's Lemma|Urysohn's lemma]]**: In a normal space, any two disjoint closed sets are separated by a continuous function. This converts abstract separation hypotheses into concrete continuous bump functions, the building blocks of every "patch together pieces" argument. The pattern of use: find disjoint closed sets, invoke Urysohn, get a function, combine. The setup is normal + disjoint closeds; the output is a continuous $[0,1]$-valued function.

- **[[Thm - Compactness in Metric Spaces (Three Equivalents)|Compactness = sequential compactness = complete + totally bounded]] in metric spaces**: This trilateral equivalence is what makes metric spaces tractable. When proving compactness of a metric space, choose whichever side is easiest: sequential compactness for sequences, complete + totally bounded for explicit estimates. The translation between forms is automatic.

- **[[Thm - Paracompact Has Partitions of Unity|Partitions of unity]] on paracompact Hausdorff spaces**: This existence result is the engine of every "local-to-global on a manifold" construction — Riemannian metrics, connections, smooth functions, integration. Recognize when you need a partition of unity: any time the construction is "easy locally and you need to combine without canceling".

- **[[Thm - One-Point Compactification|One-point compactification]] of an LCH space**: Adds a single point at infinity to make a non-compact space compact. Standard tool for compactness arguments on $\mathbb{R}^n$ or any LCH space — extend by $0$ at infinity, work in $X^+$, restrict back. Recognize it when you need compactness but the natural space is open / unbounded.

# Bridges

1. **Functional Analysis — Banach–Alaoglu.** The closed unit ball $B^* \subseteq V^*$ of the dual of a normed space is **weak-$*$ compact**. The proof: embed $V^*$ in $\prod_{x \in V} [-\lVert x\rVert, \lVert x\rVert]$ via $\varphi \mapsto (\varphi(x))_x$. The product is compact by Tychonoff, the image lies in a closed subset (closedness comes from the equation $\varphi(\alpha x + y) = \alpha\varphi(x) + \varphi(y)$ which is preserved under pointwise limits), hence the image is compact. The image with subspace topology agrees with the weak-$*$ topology on $B^*$. This is the foundational compactness in functional analysis, and it is just Tychonoff in disguise — the weak topology of [[Topology I — §1–3 Metric and Topological Spaces|Topology I]] applied to the compact product of [[Topology III — §8–12 Products, Metric Spaces, Locally Compact, Paracompact|this topic]].

2. **Probability — Prokhorov's Theorem.** A family of probability measures $\{\mu_n\}$ on a complete separable metric space is **tight** iff for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a compact $K$ with $\mu_n(K) > 1 - \varepsilon$ for all $n$. Tightness is equivalent to relative compactness in the weak topology — see [[Thm - Prokhorov's Theorem]]. The mechanism: tightness keeps mass from escaping to infinity, which is exactly the failure of compactness for measures on a non-compact space. The proof uses the locally compact Hausdorff structure to convert tightness into a compact subset of an LCH space, then extracts a weak limit via Banach–Alaoglu. The notion of "complete separable" (Polish space) is built from [[Topology III — §8–12 Products, Metric Spaces, Locally Compact, Paracompact|this topic]]'s metric machinery.

3. **Measure Theory — Riesz Representation Theorem.** On a locally compact Hausdorff space $X$, every positive linear functional $\Lambda : C_c(X) \to \mathbb{R}$ is integration against a unique Radon measure: $\Lambda f = \int f\, d\mu$. The local compactness ensures compactly supported continuous functions exist (via Urysohn) and have the right scaling; the Hausdorff ensures uniqueness. This is the standard bridge from topology to measure — see [[Measure Theory I — §1 Measure Spaces]] — and it explains why measure theory is most naturally developed on LCH spaces. The supply of continuous functions guaranteed by §10–11 is exactly what makes the theorem possible.

4. **Differential Geometry — Riemannian metrics and partitions of unity.** A **Riemannian metric** on a smooth manifold $M$ is a smoothly varying inner product on each tangent space. Existence is proved by a partition of unity: take local inner products in charts (the Euclidean one), and patch them together with $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha g_\alpha$ where $\{\rho_\alpha\}$ is a smooth partition of unity subordinate to a chart cover. Every Riemannian geometry construction — geodesics, curvature, length, distance — follows from this existence theorem, which depends entirely on the paracompactness of manifolds proved in §12. See [[Multivariate Analysis II — Inverse and Implicit Function Theorems]] for submanifolds, the geometric input.

5. **Group Theory — Locally compact groups.** A **topological group** is **locally compact** if its underlying topological space is. Locally compact groups admit **Haar measure** — a translation-invariant Radon measure unique up to scaling. The existence of Haar measure is one of the foundational results of harmonic analysis, and it depends on Tychonoff (to extract a weak limit of approximate measures) and on local compactness (to have compact neighborhoods of the identity). The bridge to algebra is [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]]; the topological side waits in Topology IV.

# Insights

The **unifying frame** of §8–12 is that *topology supplies what analysis assumes*. The intuition you build doing $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ analysis in $\mathbb{R}^n$ rests on Heine–Borel, normality, metrizability, separating functions, partitions of unity. Each of these is a *theorem* requiring specific hypotheses, and §8–12 is the inventory of which hypotheses imply which theorems. The lesson is structural: do not assume "things are fine"; identify the specific axiom that makes the construction you want available. This discipline transfers to every subsequent topic — functional analysis routinely uses non-locally-compact spaces, algebraic geometry uses non-Hausdorff spaces (the Zariski topology), and the right tools differ in each setting.

The **true name** of compactness in metric spaces is "complete and totally bounded". The open-cover definition is the general one, but it is the metric form that one *computes with*. Completeness says limits exist when they should; total boundedness says the space is "almost finite" at every scale. Together they give compactness. This dual perspective is the cleanest way to internalize compactness: a space is compact when it is both globally well-behaved (every Cauchy converges) and locally constrained (finite $\varepsilon$-coverage at every scale).

A **density-as-strategy** observation: many existence theorems in §8–12 proceed by *embedding a space densely into a larger nicer space*. The completion embeds $X$ in $\widehat X$. The Stone–Čech embeds completely regular $X$ in $\beta X$. The Urysohn metrization embeds second-countable Hausdorff regular $X$ in $[0, 1]^\mathbb{N}$. The pattern is: there is a "nicer" space in which $X$ lives densely; theorems about $X$ can be proved by proving them in the nicer space and pulling back. This is one of the most-used moves in analysis, and §8–12 supplies the specific embeddings.

A **trigger-reaction pattern**: when you need a continuous function with specific values on closed sets, the trigger is "are there disjoint closed sets?", and the reaction is Urysohn (if normal) or pre-Urysohn (find a Urysohn function manually if normality is not in scope). When you need partition of unity for gluing, the trigger is "is the cover locally finite or can be refined to be?", and the reaction is paracompactness. When you need compactness of a constructed space, the trigger is "what does it embed into?", and the reaction is Tychonoff + closed subset.

A final pragmatic observation: the constructions of this chapter — products, completions, compactifications, extensions — are *universal constructions* in the category-theoretic sense. The product is universal among spaces with projections to each factor; the completion is universal among complete metric spaces with isometric embeddings of $X$; the Stone–Čech is universal among compactifications. This universality is not just a tidy organizing principle — it is the *uniqueness* in each case, and it is why each construction is "the right" one rather than an arbitrary choice. Recognizing this universality makes the proofs flow: define the desired property as a universal property, build the object that satisfies it.
