# Notation

Let $\{X_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ be a family of topological spaces indexed by an arbitrary set $A$. The Cartesian product $\prod_{\alpha \in A} X_\alpha$ has elements $x = (x_\alpha)_{\alpha \in A}$, where $x_\alpha \in X_\alpha$ for each $\alpha$. The coordinate projection is $\pi_\alpha : \prod_\beta X_\beta \to X_\alpha$, $x \mapsto x_\alpha$. We write $X^A$ for the product $\prod_{\alpha \in A} X$ — the set of all functions $f : A \to X$, viewed as tuples. The full registry is on [[Topology III — §8–12 Products, Metric Spaces, Locally Compact, Paracompact]].

---

# Axiom Motivation

We are given a family of topological spaces $\{X_\alpha\}$ and want to put a topology on their Cartesian product. There are many topologies one could put on a set, and we need a principle to pick the right one. The standard principle is the universal property of products: the product topology should be the one that makes mappings into the product easy to describe — namely, a map $f : Z \to \prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ should be continuous if and only if each composition $\pi_\alpha \circ f : Z \to X_\alpha$ is continuous. This is what "the product is the right product" means in category-theoretic terms.

That requirement *forces* the topology. For every $\pi_\alpha \circ f$ to be continuous it is necessary that each $\pi_\alpha$ itself be continuous, i.e. that $\pi_\alpha^{-1}(U)$ be open for every open $U \subseteq X_\alpha$. These sets — call them **cylinders** — must therefore be in the topology. Conversely, if we declare the cylinders open and take the smallest topology containing them, then a map into the product is continuous if and only if the preimage of every subbasis element is open, which is exactly the condition that every $\pi_\alpha \circ f$ is continuous. So the topology generated by cylinders is *forced*: it is the coarsest topology making all projections continuous, and it is also the unique topology making the universal property work.

Now we ask: why not put a *finer* topology on the product — say, the topology generated by *boxes* $\prod_\alpha U_\alpha$ where each $U_\alpha \subseteq X_\alpha$ is open with no restriction? This is the **box topology**, and on a finite product it agrees with the cylinder topology (any basis box on a finite product is a finite intersection of cylinders). On an infinite product the box topology is *strictly finer*: there are box-open sets that are not cylinder-open. The cost of the extra openness is severe. First, the universal property fails: a map $f : Z \to \prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ being continuous in box topology requires a much stronger condition than coordinate-wise continuity — for every $z$ and every box neighborhood of $f(z)$, *all coordinates* simultaneously stay in their respective opens, which is a uniform-type condition. Second, **Tychonoff's theorem** — that a product of compact spaces is compact — *fails* in the box topology. The classic counterexample: $\{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}$ with the box topology is not compact, because every singleton $\{x\}$ is box-open (its $n$-th coordinate stays in the box $\{x_n\}$ for each $n$), making the space discrete and infinite. Third, the topology of pointwise convergence on a function space $\mathbb{R}^X$ — the natural one for many constructions — is the *cylinder*, not the box, topology.

The lesson: when forming an infinite product, the natural infinite-dimensional cylinder topology requires *cofinitely many* factors to be all of $X_\alpha$ (i.e., the cylinder restricts only finitely many coordinates). This is the unique topology for which the universal property works, Tychonoff holds, and pointwise convergence is the meaning of net convergence. The box topology looks tempting because it generalizes the finite case directly, but on infinite products it has none of the desired properties.

# The Definition

Let $\{X_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ be a family of topological spaces. The **product topology** on $\prod_{\alpha \in A} X_\alpha$ is the topology generated by the subbasis

$$\mathcal{S} = \{\pi_\alpha^{-1}(U_\alpha) : \alpha \in A, \ U_\alpha \subseteq X_\alpha \text{ open}\}.$$

Equivalently, a basis for the product topology consists of finite intersections of subbasis elements: sets of the form

$$\prod_{\alpha \in A} V_\alpha \quad \text{where} \quad V_\alpha \subseteq X_\alpha \text{ open and } V_\alpha = X_\alpha \text{ for all but finitely many } \alpha.$$

Equivalently again, the product topology is the **coarsest topology** on $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ making every projection $\pi_\alpha$ continuous.

The **universal property** characterizes it: for any topological space $Z$ and any family of continuous maps $\{f_\alpha : Z \to X_\alpha\}$, there is a unique continuous map $f : Z \to \prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ with $\pi_\alpha \circ f = f_\alpha$, namely $f(z) = (f_\alpha(z))_\alpha$.

For *finite* products $X_1 \times \cdots \times X_n$, the basis simplifies to *all* open rectangles $U_1 \times \cdots \times U_n$, since the cofiniteness condition is vacuous. The product topology coincides with the box topology on finite products.

The **box topology** on $\prod_{\alpha \in A} X_\alpha$ is the topology generated by the basis $\{\prod_\alpha U_\alpha : U_\alpha \subseteq X_\alpha \text{ open}\}$, with no cofiniteness restriction. On an infinite product the box topology is strictly finer than the product topology.

# Categorical Definition

In the category of topological spaces, the product topology realizes the **categorical product** of the family $\{X_\alpha\}$. A categorical product of a family of objects is an object $P$ together with morphisms $\pi_\alpha : P \to X_\alpha$ such that for any object $Z$ and any family of morphisms $\{f_\alpha : Z \to X_\alpha\}$, there is a *unique* morphism $f : Z \to P$ with $\pi_\alpha \circ f = f_\alpha$. The set $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ with the product topology and the obvious projections satisfies this universal property in the category of topological spaces and continuous maps. Up to canonical homeomorphism, this characterizes the product topology completely.

The categorical viewpoint immediately yields functorial behavior: a family of continuous maps $\{g_\alpha : Y_\alpha \to X_\alpha\}$ induces a continuous map $\prod_\alpha g_\alpha : \prod_\alpha Y_\alpha \to \prod_\alpha X_\alpha$. It also explains why the **box topology** is *not* a categorical product on infinite families: the universal property fails because a map into the box product requires more than coordinate-wise continuity.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The product topology is literally the same construction as the **initial topology** induced by a family of maps. Given a set $Y$ and a family of maps $\{f_\alpha : Y \to X_\alpha\}$ into topological spaces, the initial topology on $Y$ is the coarsest topology making each $f_\alpha$ continuous — generated by the subbasis of preimages $f_\alpha^{-1}(U)$. The product topology is the special case $Y = \prod_\alpha X_\alpha$, $f_\alpha = \pi_\alpha$.

In **functional analysis**, the **weak topology** on a normed space $V$ is the initial topology induced by the family of bounded linear functionals $V^* = \{\varphi : V \to \mathbb{R}\}$. It is the coarsest topology on $V$ making every $\varphi \in V^*$ continuous, just as the product topology is the coarsest making every $\pi_\alpha$ continuous. The **weak-$*$ topology** on $V^*$ is the initial topology induced by evaluation functionals $\varphi \mapsto \varphi(x)$ for $x \in V$ — equivalently, the subspace topology when $V^*$ is embedded in $\mathbb{R}^V$ with the product topology. This is why **Banach–Alaoglu** is a consequence of Tychonoff: the weak-$*$ compactness of the unit ball is the compactness of a closed subset of a Tychonoff-compact product.

In **measure theory**, the topology of **pointwise convergence** on a function space $\mathbb{R}^X$ is the product topology — a sequence (or net) of functions $f_n \to f$ pointwise if and only if $f_n \to f$ in the product topology on $\mathbb{R}^X$.

In **algebraic geometry**, the **Zariski topology** on $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$ is *not* the product topology when $R$ is a product of rings (one has to be more careful with $\operatorname{Spec}$ and coproducts), but the topology of a fiber product of schemes is constructed in a similar universal way.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — finite product of intervals.** On $\mathbb{R}^n$, the product topology is the standard Euclidean topology: the basis of finite open boxes $U_1 \times \cdots \times U_n$ is the same as the basis of open balls modulo refinement. So $\mathbb{R}^n$ as a product of $n$ copies of $\mathbb{R}$ is exactly Euclidean $\mathbb{R}^n$ — no surprise, but worth checking that the construction reproduces what you already know.

**Is an instance — the Hilbert cube $[0,1]^\mathbb{N}$.** The product of countably many copies of $[0,1]$ with the product topology is the **Hilbert cube**. It is compact (by Tychonoff for countable products, which does not require AC), Hausdorff, and metrizable: an explicit metric is $d(x, y) = \sum_n |x_n - y_n|/2^n$. Every separable metric space embeds in the Hilbert cube.

**Is NOT an instance — the box topology on $\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$.** The box topology has basis elements $\prod_n U_n$ with no cofiniteness restriction, e.g., $\prod_n (-1/n, 1/n)$ is box-open but not product-open. The sequence $x^{(k)}$ defined by $x^{(k)}_n = 1/k$ for all $n$ converges to $0$ in the product topology (each coordinate goes to $0$) but does *not* converge in the box topology (the box neighborhood $\prod_n (-1/n, 1/n)$ of $0$ contains no $x^{(k)}$, since the $n$-th coordinate $1/k$ exits $(-1/n, 1/n)$ for $n \geq k$). This is the cleanest diagnostic: the box topology does not encode pointwise convergence.

**Corollary — convergence is coordinatewise.** A net $\{x^{(\beta)}\}$ in $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ converges to $x$ in the product topology if and only if $\pi_\alpha(x^{(\beta)}) \to x_\alpha$ in $X_\alpha$ for every $\alpha$. This is what makes the product topology the right one for function spaces. See [[Thm - Net Convergence in Product is Coordinatewise]].

**Corollary — the diagonal embedding is continuous.** The map $\Delta : X \to X \times X$ by $\Delta(x) = (x, x)$ is continuous, being the product of two continuous maps (both identity). More generally, for any continuous $f : X \to Y_\alpha$, the assembled map $x \mapsto (f_\alpha(x))_\alpha$ into $\prod_\alpha Y_\alpha$ is continuous by the universal property.

**Corollary — products preserve Hausdorffness.** If each $X_\alpha$ is Hausdorff, then $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ is Hausdorff. Given distinct points $x = (x_\alpha), y = (y_\alpha)$, pick any $\alpha_0$ with $x_{\alpha_0} \neq y_{\alpha_0}$, separate them in $X_{\alpha_0}$ by disjoint opens $U, V$, and pull back via $\pi_{\alpha_0}$: $\pi_{\alpha_0}^{-1}(U)$ and $\pi_{\alpha_0}^{-1}(V)$ are disjoint opens in the product separating $x$ and $y$.

**Calibration check.** Verify: (i) the product of the discrete topology on $\{0, 1\}$ with itself $\mathbb{N}$ times is the **Cantor set** $\{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}$ — compact, Hausdorff, totally disconnected, metrizable; (ii) the product topology on $\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{R}$ is *not* metrizable (no countable basis at any point in an uncountable product); (iii) the box topology on $\{0, 1\}^\mathbb{N}$ has every singleton open (since $\{x\} = \prod_n \{x_n\}$ is box-open in the discrete factors), hence is discrete and non-compact, *contradicting* Tychonoff — this is why box topology fails.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Weak and Weak-$*$ Topologies *(from Functional Analysis)*
> The **weak topology** on a normed space and the **weak-$*$ topology** on its dual are both initial topologies induced by families of evaluation functionals. The product topology is the prototype: the weak-$*$ unit ball $B^* \subseteq V^*$ embeds in $\prod_{x \in V}[-\lVert x\rVert, \lVert x\rVert]$ with the product topology, and **Banach–Alaoglu** says it is compact — a direct application of Tychonoff.

> [!tip] Function Spaces and Pointwise Convergence *(from Analysis)*
> On $\mathbb{R}^X$ (functions $X \to \mathbb{R}$), the product topology *is* the topology of pointwise convergence. The finer topologies of uniform convergence on compacts, uniform convergence, $L^p$ convergence, are all studied in functional analysis as comparisons to this baseline.

> [!tip] Stone–Čech Compactification *(from this topic)*
> The Stone–Čech compactification of a completely regular space $X$ is constructed as the closure of the image of the diagonal map $\Phi : X \to [0, 1]^{C_b(X)}$ given by $\Phi(x)(f) = f(x)$ — an embedding into a product. See [[Thm - Stone–Čech Compactification]].
