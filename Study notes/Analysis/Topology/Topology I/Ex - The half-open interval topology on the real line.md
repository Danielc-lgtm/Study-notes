# Problem Statement

Let $\mathcal{B} = \{[a, b) : a, b \in \mathbb{R},\ a < b\}$. The topological space $(\mathbb{R}, \tau_S)$ generated by $\mathcal{B}$ is called the **Sorgenfrey line** (or *lower limit topology*), denoted $\mathbb{R}_S$.

1. Verify that $\mathcal{B}$ is a [[Def - Basis and Subbasis for a Topology|basis]] for a topology on $\mathbb{R}$.
2. Show that every basic open set $[a, b)$ is *both* open *and* closed (**clopen**) in $\mathbb{R}_S$.
3. Show that $\tau_S$ is **strictly finer** than the standard topology $\tau$ on $\mathbb{R}$: every standard-open set is Sorgenfrey-open, but the converse fails.
4. Deduce that the identity $\operatorname{id} : \mathbb{R}_S \to \mathbb{R}$ is [[Def - Continuous Map|continuous]] but the identity $\operatorname{id} : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_S$ is *not* continuous — so it is not a [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphism]].

**Recall:**

A [[Def - Basis and Subbasis for a Topology|basis]] $\mathcal{B}$ for a topology on $X$ is a collection of subsets such that (i) every point of $X$ lies in some $B \in \mathcal{B}$, and (ii) if $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$ for $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$, then there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$. The [[Def - Topological Space|topology]] *generated by* $\mathcal{B}$ is then defined as the collection of arbitrary unions of basis elements (together with the empty set).

A set is [[Def - Open and Closed Sets in a Metric Space|closed]] iff its complement is open. A topology $\tau_1$ is **finer** than $\tau_2$ (equivalently $\tau_2$ is **coarser**) if $\tau_2 \subseteq \tau_1$, equivalently the identity $(X, \tau_1) \to (X, \tau_2)$ is [[Def - Continuous Map|continuous]]. A [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphism]] is a continuous bijection with continuous inverse.

The standard topology $\tau$ on $\mathbb{R}$ has the open-interval basis $\{(a, b) : a < b\}$; every standard-open set is a union of open intervals.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** Construct an explicit non-standard topology on a familiar set and analyze how it differs from the standard one. The Sorgenfrey line is a foundational counterexample — it is *the* example one reaches for to disprove a conjecture in general topology.

**Assumption pattern.** All the structure is encoded in the half-open intervals $[a, b)$. Every question reduces to "what does this $[a, b)$ basis element look like?", and the answers fall out from elementary inequalities.

**Theorem routing.** Step 1 is a direct basis-axiom check: an intersection of two half-open intervals is itself a half-open interval. Step 2 is the construction of the complement of $[a, b)$ as a Sorgenfrey-open set — a union of half-open intervals. Step 3 follows from $(a, b) = \bigcup_n [a + 1/n, b)$ on the easy side, and the failure on the hard side from the observation that $[a, b)$ contains the boundary point $a$ but no standard open interval about $a$ lies inside $[a, b)$. Step 4 is then a one-line application of the fact that the identity map between two topologies on a set is continuous iff the source is finer.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious step is showing $[a, b)$ is *closed*. The complement $(-\infty, a) \cup [b, \infty)$ is a union of two pieces, each of which must be exhibited as Sorgenfrey-open by writing it as a union of half-open intervals. $(-\infty, a) = \bigcup_n [a - n, a)$ and $[b, \infty) = \bigcup_n [b, b + n)$ — these expressions use the unbounded growth of $n$.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following operations from [[Topology I — §1–3 Metric and Topological Spaces#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Verify the basis axioms by computing intersections.** The intersection of two half-open intervals $[a, b) \cap [c, d)$ is either empty or another half-open interval $[\max(a, c), \min(b, d))$.

2. **Write a set as a union of basis elements to show it is open.** $(a, b) = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} [a + 1/n, b)$ uses the fact that every $x \in (a, b)$ has $a + 1/n \leq x$ for $n$ large; the union of half-open intervals is therefore Sorgenfrey-open.

3. **Write the complement as a union of basis elements to show closedness.** $(-\infty, a) = \bigcup_n [a - n, a)$ and $[b, \infty) = \bigcup_n [b, b + n)$.

4. **Read off identity-map continuity from topology refinement.** If $\tau_1$ is finer than $\tau_2$ (more open sets), the identity $(X, \tau_1) \to (X, \tau_2)$ is continuous; the reverse is not.

5. **Find a topology-distinguishing set.** Exhibit one set that is open in $\tau_S$ but not in $\tau$. $[0, 1)$ itself suffices: it contains the boundary point $0$, but every standard-open neighbourhood of $0$ contains some $(-\delta, \delta)$, which is not contained in $[0, 1)$.

> [!note]- Hint 3

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For "the converse fails": take the basic Sorgenfrey-open set $[0, 1)$. Find a point $x \in [0, 1)$ such that no standard-open interval around $x$ lies inside $[0, 1)$. Which point of $[0, 1)$ is the "problem point"?

# Solution

The Sorgenfrey line is a topology on $\mathbb{R}$ in which each point can see "to the right" through arbitrarily short half-open windows but cannot see "to the left" — a manifest asymmetry not present in the standard topology. All the structural properties of the example follow from explicit unions of half-open intervals.

**Step 1: $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis.**

The basis conditions are (i) covering and (ii) closure under intersection up to nesting. Both follow from the explicit formula $[a, b) \cap [c, d) = [\max(a, c), \min(b, d))$ (interpreted as empty if $\max \geq \min$).

> [!note]- Derivation
> *(i) Covering.* For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $x \in [x, x + 1) \in \mathcal{B}$. So $\mathbb{R} = \bigcup_{a \in \mathbb{R}} [a, a + 1)$ — every point is covered.
>
> *(ii) Intersection property.* Suppose $x \in [a, b) \cap [c, d)$. Then $a \leq x < b$ and $c \leq x < d$, so $\max(a, c) \leq x < \min(b, d)$. The interval $[\max(a, c), \min(b, d))$ is a basis element containing $x$ and contained in $[a, b) \cap [c, d)$. So we can take $B_3 = [\max(a, c), \min(b, d))$.
>
> Hence $\mathcal{B}$ is a [[Def - Basis and Subbasis for a Topology|basis]] for a topology on $\mathbb{R}$. The generated topology $\tau_S$ has as opens all unions of basis elements $[a, b)$.

**Step 2: Every $[a, b)$ is clopen.**

$[a, b)$ is a basis element, hence open. To show it is closed, write the complement as a union of half-open intervals:
$$\mathbb{R} \setminus [a, b) = (-\infty, a) \cup [b, \infty) = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} [a - n, a) \cup \bigcup_{n \geq 1} [b, b + n).$$
Each piece is a union of basis elements, hence open in $\tau_S$. So the complement is open, hence $[a, b)$ is closed.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *$(-\infty, a) = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} [a - n, a)$.* For "$\subseteq$": given $x < a$, choose $n$ with $a - n \leq x$ (any $n \geq a - x$ works); then $x \in [a - n, a)$. For "$\supseteq$": every $[a - n, a) \subseteq (-\infty, a)$ since $x \in [a - n, a)$ means $a - n \leq x < a$.
>
> *$[b, \infty) = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} [b, b + n)$.* For "$\subseteq$": given $x \geq b$, choose $n$ with $x < b + n$ (any $n \geq x - b + 1$); then $x \in [b, b + n)$. For "$\supseteq$": $[b, b + n) \subseteq [b, \infty)$ is immediate.
>
> So $\mathbb{R} \setminus [a, b)$ is a union of basis elements and is open in $\tau_S$. Hence $[a, b)$ is closed in $\tau_S$, and being a basis element, also open — *clopen*.

**Step 3: $\tau_S$ is strictly finer than the standard topology.**

*Every standard-open set is $\tau_S$-open.* It suffices to show every open interval $(a, b)$ is $\tau_S$-open, since arbitrary opens are unions of such. Write $(a, b) = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} [a + 1/n, b)$.

*Some $\tau_S$-open set is not standard-open.* The basis element $[0, 1)$ is $\tau_S$-open but not standard-open: every standard-open neighbourhood of $0$ contains an interval $(-\delta, \delta)$, which has points $-\delta/2 < 0$ that are not in $[0, 1)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Standard-open implies $\tau_S$-open.* Every standard-open $U$ is a union of open intervals $(a, b)$. For each interval, $(a, b) = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} [a + 1/n, b)$:
> - "$\subseteq$": given $x \in (a, b)$, choose $n$ with $a + 1/n \leq x$ — possible since $x > a$ — then $x \in [a + 1/n, b)$.
> - "$\supseteq$": every $[a + 1/n, b) \subseteq (a, b)$ since the left endpoint satisfies $a + 1/n > a$.
>
> So $(a, b) \in \tau_S$, and unions of standard opens are $\tau_S$-open. Hence $\tau \subseteq \tau_S$.
>
> *$\tau_S$-open does not imply standard-open.* The set $[0, 1) \in \tau_S$. Suppose for contradiction $[0, 1) \in \tau$. Then $0 \in [0, 1)$ would have to be an interior point in the standard topology, so some standard-open interval $(-\delta, \delta)$ with $\delta > 0$ would have to be a subset of $[0, 1)$. But $-\delta/2 \in (-\delta, \delta)$ and $-\delta/2 \notin [0, 1)$ — contradiction.
>
> So $\tau \subsetneq \tau_S$: the Sorgenfrey topology is *strictly* finer than the standard topology.

**Step 4: The identity map is continuous one way only.**

The identity $\operatorname{id} : \mathbb{R}_S \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous because its source is *finer*: every standard-open $U$ has preimage $\operatorname{id}^{-1}(U) = U \in \tau \subseteq \tau_S$, hence open in the source.

The identity $\operatorname{id} : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_S$ is *not* continuous: the preimage of $[0, 1) \in \tau_S$ is $[0, 1) \notin \tau$.

Hence $\operatorname{id}$ is a continuous bijection that is *not* a homeomorphism.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *$\operatorname{id} : \mathbb{R}_S \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous.* By [[Def - Continuous Map|definition]], $\operatorname{id}$ is continuous iff $\operatorname{id}^{-1}(U)$ is open in the source for every $U$ open in the target. Here $\operatorname{id}^{-1}(U) = U$, and $U$ standard-open implies $U \in \tau_S$ by Step 3. So $\operatorname{id}$ is continuous.
>
> *$\operatorname{id} : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_S$ is not continuous.* Take the open set $V = [0, 1) \in \tau_S$. Then $\operatorname{id}^{-1}(V) = [0, 1)$, which is not in $\tau$ by Step 3. So the preimage of an open set is not open.
>
> *Not a homeomorphism.* A [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphism]] $f$ is a continuous bijection with continuous inverse. Here $f = \operatorname{id}$ is a bijection, and continuous as $\mathbb{R}_S \to \mathbb{R}$, but its inverse — *also* the identity, but with source and target swapped — is not continuous. So $\operatorname{id}$ is not a homeomorphism.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **(1)** $\mathcal{B} = \{[a, b)\}$ covers $\mathbb{R}$ (via $[x, x+1)$) and is closed under intersection: $[a, b) \cap [c, d) = [\max(a,c), \min(b, d))$. So it is a basis. **(2)** $[a, b)$ is a basis element, hence open. Its complement $(-\infty, a) \cup [b, \infty) = \bigcup_n [a - n, a) \cup \bigcup_n [b, b+n)$ is a union of basis elements, hence open, so $[a, b)$ is closed. **(3)** $(a, b) = \bigcup_n [a + 1/n, b)$ shows $\tau \subseteq \tau_S$. $[0, 1) \in \tau_S$ but $0$ is not an interior point in $\tau$, so $[0, 1) \notin \tau$; hence $\tau \subsetneq \tau_S$. **(4)** Continuity of $\mathbb{R}_S \to \mathbb{R}$ follows from $\tau \subseteq \tau_S$ (every preimage is the set itself, which is finer-open). Discontinuity of $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_S$ is witnessed by $V = [0, 1)$, which is target-open but source-not-open. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**A finer topology has more open sets, more continuous *functionals on* the space, and fewer continuous *maps into* the space — and the Sorgenfrey line is the cleanest example of a topology refining the standard one on a familiar set.** This is the same monotonicity principle that drives the comparison of any two topologies on a common set. Finer means: (a) the identity from the finer side is automatically continuous, (b) every continuous map *out of* the finer-topology space remains continuous (more preimages allowed), and (c) continuous maps *into* the finer-topology space become more restrictive (more open sets to pull back). The reflex to develop: when you read "the topology $\tau_1$ is finer than $\tau_2$", convert this to "the identity $(X, \tau_1) \to (X, \tau_2)$ is continuous; the identity in reverse may not be" — this is the operational form.

**Half-open intervals $[a, b)$ being *clopen* is what makes the Sorgenfrey line a foundational counterexample in topology.** A space with many clopen sets is highly *disconnected*: the [[Topology II — §4–7 Connectivity, Separation, Nets, Compactness|connected components]] are the equivalence classes under "no separation by clopen sets", and in $\mathbb{R}_S$ the connected components are singletons — $\mathbb{R}_S$ is *totally disconnected*. This is one of the structural surprises: the underlying set is $\mathbb{R}$ (which has the strongest possible connectedness in $\tau$), but the topology has destroyed all of it. The Sorgenfrey line also shows up in other guises: it is paracompact but not metrizable, not second countable but still Lindelöf, and the *square* $\mathbb{R}_S \times \mathbb{R}_S$ — the Sorgenfrey plane — fails to be normal despite being a product of nice spaces. Every topology textbook returns to this example to refute a plausible-looking conjecture.

**The construction $(a, b) = \bigcup_n [a + 1/n, b)$ is the *unwinding* technique: peel off a thin slice on the left, fit a half-open interval starting after the slice, take the union as the slice shrinks.** This is the basic trick for showing one topology is finer than another via explicit basis-element-as-union arguments. The same construction shows $(a, \infty) = \bigcup_n [a + 1/n, n)$, that any standard-open subset of $\mathbb{R}_S$ is Sorgenfrey-open, and (in higher dimensions) that the $\ell^\infty$ ball $\{|x_i| < r\}$ is a union of $\ell^1$ balls — see [[Ex - Three equivalent metrics on Rn]]. Whenever proving "every $\tau_2$-open set is $\tau_1$-open" between two topologies on the same set, the strategy is: pick a basis for $\tau_2$ and exhibit each basis element as a union of $\tau_1$-basis elements.

**A continuous bijection is *not* automatically a homeomorphism — the identity on $\mathbb{R}$ viewed as $\mathbb{R}_S \to \mathbb{R}$ is the canonical example, as is $[0, 2\pi) \to S^1$ via $t \mapsto e^{it}$.** The two examples have the same logical shape: a continuous bijection whose inverse is discontinuous at one or more specific points. In the Sorgenfrey case, the inverse $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_S$ is discontinuous *everywhere*, because every point $x$ has Sorgenfrey-neighbourhoods $[x, x + \varepsilon)$ that do not lie inside any standard-open neighbourhood of $x$. The general principle to internalize: when a continuous bijection is *not* a homeomorphism, look for the *gap between the topologies* — the open set in the target that does not pull back to an open set in the source. The hypothesis that *closes* the gap in many situations is the "compact-to-Hausdorff" theorem proved in [[Topology II — §4–7 Connectivity, Separation, Nets, Compactness]]: a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is automatically a homeomorphism. Neither $\mathbb{R}_S$ nor $[0, 2\pi)$ is compact, which is exactly why the conclusion fails.
