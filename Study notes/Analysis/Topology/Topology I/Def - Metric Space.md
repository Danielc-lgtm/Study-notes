# Notation

Throughout, $X$ is a set (typically nonempty), and $d$ denotes the candidate metric on $X$. The notation $(X, d)$ is the **metric space**: the set together with the metric. We write $d(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ for the distance from $x$ to $y$. The Euclidean distance in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $|x - y|$; this is the prototype against which all later metrics are compared. We will also use $\rho$ when a second metric is in play on the same set, e.g. for comparing $d$ with $\rho$. For the full symbol registry see the parent page [[Topology I — §1–3 Metric and Topological Spaces]].

# Axiom Motivation

The whole point of a metric is to support the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition of continuity. We learned in real analysis that $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous at $a$ when for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ with $|x - a| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - f(a)| < \varepsilon$. The definition consists almost entirely of the *distance* $|x - a|$. So if we want to talk about continuity beyond $\mathbb{R}^n$ — about continuity of functions of continuous functions, or continuity on a graph, or on a manifold whose ambient space is not Euclidean — the first thing we need is a substitute for the absolute value. What does the absolute value *do* in that definition? It tells us when two things are close. So a metric is, at minimum, a function $d : X \times X \to \mathbb{R}$ that returns a number measuring how close two points are. Now ask the harder question: which properties of the absolute value are the proof techniques in real analysis actually leaning on? The three axioms below are the answer, and the way to *invent* them is to walk through a standard $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ proof and notice which inequalities you used.

The first inequality you reach for is **positivity** with **identification of points**: $d(x, y) \geq 0$, with equality if and only if $x = y$. The non-negativity is mostly cosmetic — we want "distance" to be a magnitude. The substantive half is the "if and only if": distinct points must have positive distance. Without this, $d$ would be allowed to lie. You could have $d(x, y) = 0$ even though $x$ and $y$ are different points, and then "$\delta$-close" no longer means "close" — it means "close, or possibly the same up to identification". This is exactly the failure mode of *pseudometrics*, which we will get to in a moment. The positivity axiom is what ensures that points are *resolved* by the distance function: a sequence converging to $x$ in the metric really converges to $x$ and only to $x$, not to some equivalence class.

The second axiom is **symmetry**: $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$. This says distance is direction-free. The reason to insist on it is that the entire $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition is asymmetric in $x$ and $y$ in *appearance* — you fix $x$ and let $y$ vary — but the statements you want to derive (like "the composition of continuous functions is continuous") use both directions of comparison. If you allow asymmetric distances, you get the theory of *quasi-metric spaces* or *Lawvere metrics*, which are perfectly legitimate but require careful tracking of which argument is which. Most theorems in classical analysis tacitly use symmetry; making it an axiom keeps the theory clean.

The third axiom is the **triangle inequality**: $d(x, z) \leq d(x, y) + d(y, z)$. This is the load-bearing axiom. Without it, you cannot prove that the composition of continuous functions is continuous, because the standard proof goes: $f$ is continuous, so $d_Y(f(x), f(x_0)) < \varepsilon$ when $d_X(x, x_0) < \delta$; $g$ is continuous, so $d_Z(g(y), g(y_0)) < \varepsilon'$ when $d_Y(y, y_0) < \delta'$; chain these and use the triangle inequality to bound the total error. Without the triangle inequality, errors do not compose. The triangle inequality is also what makes the open balls $B_\varepsilon(x)$ *open* — a point $y$ inside $B_\varepsilon(x)$ has its own little ball $B_{\varepsilon - d(x,y)}(y)$ that still lies inside $B_\varepsilon(x)$, by triangle. Drop the triangle inequality and the very topology generated by the balls falls apart. The triangle inequality is the axiomatic content of "there are no shortcuts": you cannot get from $x$ to $z$ faster than the sum of $x$ to $y$ and $y$ to $z$, no matter which intermediate point $y$ you pick.

To convince yourself these are the *right* three axioms, consider what happens when each is weakened. Drop positivity (allow $d(x, y) = 0$ for $x \neq y$) and you have a **pseudometric**. Pseudometrics arise naturally in analysis: on the space of measurable functions, $d(f, g) = \int |f - g|$ is a pseudometric because two functions that differ only on a measure-zero set have distance zero but are not equal. The usual fix is to quotient by the equivalence relation "$d(f, g) = 0$", which is how the $L^p$ spaces are constructed. The pseudometric is genuinely useful — it captures the right notion of closeness on measurable functions — but to recover a metric, you must collapse the indistinguishable points.

Drop symmetry and you have a **quasi-metric**. Quasi-metrics are useful in computer science (where $d$ might be the cost of going from one state to another, and the costs differ by direction) and in some corners of analysis (the *Wasserstein* metric has a symmetric form, but optimal transport theory in general is asymmetric). They are perfectly respectable, but the apparatus they require is heavier — every theorem that uses symmetry must be re-examined.

If instead we *strengthen* the triangle inequality to $d(x, z) \leq \max(d(x, y), d(y, z))$ — the **ultrametric** inequality, also called the strong triangle inequality — we get a metric of a very different flavour. The $p$-adic numbers $\mathbb{Q}_p$ carry such a metric: $|x|_p = p^{-v_p(x)}$ where $v_p$ is the $p$-adic valuation. In an ultrametric every triangle is isosceles (the two larger sides equal), every ball is both open and closed, and a series converges if and only if its terms tend to zero. These facts make $p$-adic analysis look like calculus from a parallel universe. The ultrametric is the right axiom for number theory, and the failure of the strong triangle inequality on $\mathbb{R}$ is exactly the reason real analysis is harder than $p$-adic analysis in many respects. We keep the weaker triangle inequality precisely because we want the geometry of $\mathbb{R}^n$, not the geometry of $\mathbb{Q}_p$.

So the three axioms — positivity-with-identification, symmetry, triangle inequality — are the minimal data needed to support the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition of continuity in a clean way. Each can be weakened to interesting effect, each can be strengthened to a different theory, but the three together give us classical analysis.

# The Definition

Let $X$ be a set. A **metric** on $X$ is a function
$$d : X \times X \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$$
satisfying the following three axioms for all $x, y, z \in X$:

1. **(Positivity / identification of points)** $d(x, y) \geq 0$, with $d(x, y) = 0$ if and only if $x = y$.

2. **(Symmetry)** $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$.

3. **(Triangle inequality)** $d(x, z) \leq d(x, y) + d(y, z)$.

The pair $(X, d)$ is a **metric space**. When the metric is understood from context we refer to $X$ alone as a metric space.

If only axioms (2) and (3) hold and (1) is weakened to $d(x, x) = 0$ (allowing $d(x, y) = 0$ for some $x \neq y$), the pair $(X, d)$ is a **pseudometric space**. If axiom (3) is strengthened to $d(x, z) \leq \max(d(x, y), d(y, z))$, the pair is an **ultrametric space**.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A metric is a particular kind of *enriched-category-theoretic* gadget — specifically, a category enriched over the monoidal poset $([0, \infty], \geq, +)$, in the formulation due to Lawvere. The objects are the points of $X$; the "hom" from $x$ to $y$ is the number $d(x, y) \in [0, \infty]$; composition is given by the triangle inequality $d(x, z) \leq d(x, y) + d(y, z)$; the identity is $d(x, x) = 0$. The Lawvere generalization drops symmetry (which the categorical formulation does not need) and allows $d(x, y) = \infty$ (also not needed by the categorical formulation); ordinary metric spaces are the *symmetric, finite-distance* enriched categories over this base. This compresses the three axioms into "an enriched category", and explains why so many constructions on metric spaces (products, limits, completions) have universal-property formulations.

In **probability**, a metric space is what you need to define convergence in distribution and weak convergence of measures: a sequence of probability measures $\mu_n$ on a metric space $X$ converges weakly to $\mu$ if $\int f\, d\mu_n \to \int f\, d\mu$ for every bounded continuous $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$. Without a metric (or at least a topology), "bounded continuous function" has no meaning. The metric is the bridge from probability to topology.

In **PDE and functional analysis**, every function space — $L^p$, $C^k$, Sobolev $W^{k,p}$, Hölder $C^{k,\alpha}$ — carries a metric (almost always coming from a norm), and the entire enterprise of solving differential equations is the study of these metrics: which sequences converge, which solutions are limits of approximate ones, which operators are continuous. The metric on $L^p$ is the engine of the Lebesgue integration theory, and the metric on Sobolev spaces is the engine of distributional solutions to PDE.

In **Riemannian geometry**, a Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$ has an *induced* metric: the distance $d(x, y)$ is the infimum of lengths of smooth paths from $x$ to $y$. The three axioms become geometric statements — positivity says distinct points are connected by paths of positive length, symmetry says path length does not depend on orientation, and the triangle inequality is the statement that the path-length distance is genuinely a distance (proved using path concatenation). The metric is the bridge from the local (the Riemannian tensor $g$) to the global (the geometry of the manifold).

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^n$ with $d(x, y) = |x - y| = \sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - y_i)^2}$.** This is the prototype. Positivity and symmetry are clear; the triangle inequality follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Every other metric one meets is, in some sense, designed to behave like this one — or designed *not* to, for contrast.

**Is an instance — $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the taxicab metric $d_1(x, y) = \sum_i |x_i - y_i|$.** The triangle inequality is immediate from the triangle inequality on $\mathbb{R}$ summed coordinate-by-coordinate. The balls are diamonds (rotated squares), not discs, but this metric defines exactly the same notion of "open set" and "continuous function" as the Euclidean metric — it is *topologically equivalent*, as we will see in [[Def - Equivalent Metrics]].

**Is an instance — $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the sup metric $d_\infty(x, y) = \max_i |x_i - y_i|$.** Same comment. The balls are axis-aligned squares.

**Is an instance — $C[0, 1]$ with the uniform metric $d_\infty(f, g) = \sup_{t \in [0,1]} |f(t) - g(t)|$.** The supremum is finite because continuous functions on a compact set are bounded; positivity, symmetry, and the triangle inequality all transfer pointwise from $|\cdot|$ on $\mathbb{R}$. This metric is the natural one for uniform convergence: $f_n \to f$ in this metric is precisely $f_n \to f$ uniformly.

**Is an instance — $C[0, 1]$ with the $L^1$ metric $d_1(f, g) = \int_0^1 |f(t) - g(t)|\, dt$.** Positivity uses that a non-negative continuous function with integral zero is identically zero (the same fact, packaged differently, that makes $L^1$ on non-continuous functions a *pseudo*metric). Symmetry is obvious; triangle follows from $|f - h| \leq |f - g| + |g - h|$ pointwise and integration. This metric is genuinely different from the uniform one: a thin tall spike has small $L^1$ distance from zero but large $L^\infty$ distance. The two are *not* topologically equivalent on $C[0, 1]$.

**Is an instance — the discrete metric $d(x, y) = 1$ for $x \neq y$, $d(x, x) = 0$.** The triangle inequality follows because the right-hand side is at least 1 unless both summands are 0 (i.e. $x = y = z$), in which case the left-hand side is also 0. The discrete metric makes *every* subset open and every subset closed; every function from a discrete metric space is continuous. It is the metric you reach for when you want a counterexample.

**Is an instance — any subset $A \subseteq X$ of a metric space, with the restricted metric $d|_{A \times A}$.** The three axioms restrict trivially. So every subset of a metric space is itself a metric space — this is the **subspace metric**.

**Is NOT an instance — pseudometric on measurable functions.** On the space of integrable functions on $[0, 1]$ (not just continuous), $d(f, g) = \int |f - g|$ is a pseudometric: $d(f, g) = 0$ when $f = g$ almost everywhere, but $f$ and $g$ may still differ on a measure-zero set, so they are not equal as functions. To recover a metric, one identifies functions that agree almost everywhere — this is how the Lebesgue space $L^1[0, 1]$ is built from the pseudo-normed space of integrable functions.

**Is NOT an instance — the "metric" $d(x, y) = (x - y)^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$.** This satisfies positivity and symmetry but *fails* the triangle inequality: with $x = 0, y = 1, z = 2$, we get $d(0, 2) = 4$ but $d(0, 1) + d(1, 2) = 1 + 1 = 2$, so $4 \leq 2$ fails. This is a useful warning: any function $d : X \times X \to [0, \infty)$ that vanishes on the diagonal and is symmetric is *not* automatically a metric — the triangle inequality is a genuine constraint.

**Corollary — reverse triangle inequality.** $|d(x, z) - d(y, z)| \leq d(x, y)$. *Proof:* the triangle inequality gives $d(x, z) \leq d(x, y) + d(y, z)$, so $d(x, z) - d(y, z) \leq d(x, y)$; swapping $x$ and $y$ gives the other direction. This is the form of the triangle inequality used to prove that $x \mapsto d(x, x_0)$ is a continuous (in fact 1-Lipschitz) function on $X$.

**Corollary — the function $x \mapsto d(x, x_0)$ is continuous.** By the reverse triangle inequality, $|d(x, x_0) - d(y, x_0)| \leq d(x, y)$, so taking $\delta = \varepsilon$ in the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition gives continuity at every point.

**Calibration check.** Verify that the function $d(x, y) = \min(|x - y|, 1)$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is a metric (the truncated Euclidean distance), and that it generates the same open sets as the usual Euclidean metric — every set that is "small" in $d$ is "small" in $|\cdot|$ and vice versa, once you are inside a ball of radius less than 1. Then verify that $d'(x, y) = |x - y|^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is *not* a metric (triangle inequality fails). If you can also explain why $L^1$ on continuous functions gives a metric but on all measurable functions only a pseudometric, you have understood every clause of the definition.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] **Open Sets in a Metric Space** *(from this topic)*
> Once you have $d$, you can define the **open ball** $B_\varepsilon(x) = \{y : d(x, y) < \varepsilon\}$ and call a set $U \subseteq X$ open if every $x \in U$ is the centre of some open ball contained in $U$. See [[Def - Open and Closed Sets in a Metric Space]]. This is the bridge from "I have a distance function" to "I have a topology".

> [!tip] **Continuity in a Metric Space** *(from this topic)*
> The $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition extends verbatim: $f : (X, d_X) \to (Y, d_Y)$ is continuous at $x$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ with $d_X(x', x) < \delta \Rightarrow d_Y(f(x'), f(x)) < \varepsilon$. The reformulation in terms of open sets — preimages of opens are open — is the bridge from metric to topology.

> [!tip] **Completeness and Banach Spaces** *(from Functional Analysis)*
> A metric space is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence converges. Complete metric spaces are the natural setting for fixed-point theorems (Banach contraction principle), for solving differential equations by Picard iteration, and for defining function spaces like $L^p$ and Sobolev spaces. The completeness axiom is the second-order datum that turns a metric space into a serious analytic object.

> [!tip] **Hausdorff Distance and the Space of Compact Sets** *(from Geometric Measure Theory)*
> Given a metric space $(X, d)$, the collection of nonempty compact subsets of $X$ becomes itself a metric space under the **Hausdorff distance** $d_H(A, B) = \max(\sup_{a \in A} d(a, B),\; \sup_{b \in B} d(b, A))$. This is the natural metric for studying convergence of geometric objects — a sequence of compact sets converges in Hausdorff distance to a limit set — and is one of the building blocks of geometric measure theory.

> [!tip] **Riemannian Distance on a Manifold** *(from Differential Geometry)*
> A Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$ carries an induced metric $d(x, y) = \inf\{\text{length}(\gamma) : \gamma \text{ a smooth path } x \to y\}$. The three axioms become geometric statements — positivity from path-positivity, symmetry from path-reversal, triangle from path-concatenation — and the metric is the engine connecting the local Riemannian structure to the global geometry of $M$.
