# Notation

Throughout, $(X, \tau)$ is a topological space and $\mathcal{B}, \mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ are collections of subsets of $X$, viewed as candidates for a basis or subbasis. We write $\tau(\mathcal{B})$ for the topology generated by $\mathcal{B}$ (the smallest topology containing $\mathcal{B}$), and similarly $\tau(\mathcal{S})$ for the topology generated by $\mathcal{S}$. The standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$ has the open intervals $(a, b)$ as a basis; the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$ has the open balls $B_\varepsilon(x)$ as a basis. For the full registry of symbols see [[Topology I — §1–3 Metric and Topological Spaces]].

# Axiom Motivation

A topology on a set $X$ is a collection $\tau$ of "open" subsets satisfying three closure axioms. In principle, to specify a topology one must list every open set. But for $\mathbb{R}$ there are uncountably many open sets, and one cannot list them all. There must be a more efficient way to *specify* a topology — by giving a small family of subsets and "closing it under the topology axioms".

The intuition comes from metric spaces. In a metric space $(X, d)$, the topology consists of every set $U$ such that for every $x \in U$, some open ball $B_\varepsilon(x)$ lies in $U$. Equivalently, every open set is a union of open balls. So the topology is *generated* by the collection of open balls, in the sense that every open set is a union of them. The collection of open balls is much smaller than the topology — it is a *family of generators*. This is the prototype of a **basis** for a topology.

So the right notion of "basis" is: a collection $\mathcal{B}$ of subsets such that every open set is a union of elements of $\mathcal{B}$. What conditions does $\mathcal{B}$ need to satisfy for this to actually define a topology? Two conditions, both forced.

The first: $\mathcal{B}$ must **cover $X$**. Reason: $X$ itself is open, so $X$ must be expressible as a union of basis elements. If $\mathcal{B}$ does not cover, the resulting topology cannot have $X$ as one of its sets, and the topology axiom $X \in \tau$ fails.

The second: the **intersection condition**. If $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ and $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$, then there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$. Reason: $B_1 \cap B_2$ is a finite intersection of opens (in the topology we are building), hence must be open, hence must be a union of basis elements. So every point of $B_1 \cap B_2$ must lie in some basis element contained in $B_1 \cap B_2$. The intersection of two basis elements need not itself be a basis element, but every point of the intersection must have a basis element around it inside the intersection. This is the local-version of "the intersection is open".

These two conditions are *exactly* what we need: if $\mathcal{B}$ satisfies the cover and intersection conditions, then $\tau(\mathcal{B}) := \{\text{unions of elements of }\mathcal{B}\}$ is a topology, and $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for it. Verification: $\emptyset$ is the empty union; $X$ is the union of $\mathcal{B}$ (by the cover condition); arbitrary unions of unions are unions; finite intersections of unions are unions, by the intersection condition. The four topology axioms are satisfied.

A basis is the *cleanest* way to specify a topology, but sometimes even the basis is hard to write down. Then we use a **subbasis**: any collection $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$. There are no conditions on $\mathcal{S}$ — any collection works. The topology generated by $\mathcal{S}$ is built in two steps: first form all *finite intersections* of elements of $\mathcal{S}$ (this gives a basis), then form all unions of finite intersections (this gives the topology). The basis condition is automatic: the finite intersections satisfy the intersection condition because the intersection of two finite intersections is a finite intersection. The cover condition is also automatic if we include the empty intersection by convention — $\bigcap \emptyset = X$, so $X$ is a finite intersection. (Equivalently, one explicitly adds $X$ to $\mathcal{S}$.)

The subbasis is the most general way to specify a topology — it gives the *smallest* topology containing $\mathcal{S}$. This is the construction one uses when defining the *weak topology* in functional analysis, the *quotient topology* in topology, the *Zariski topology* in algebraic geometry, and the *box / product topology* on a product space. The pattern is universal: define a small generating family, take the topology it generates.

A second motivation comes from **continuity**: to check that $f : X \to Y$ is continuous, one must verify $f^{-1}(U)$ is open for every open $U \subseteq Y$. This is impractical if $Y$ has too many open sets. But if $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\tau_Y$, then it suffices to check $f^{-1}(B)$ is open for every $B \in \mathcal{B}$ — and the same applies to a subbasis. Reason: continuity is preserved by intersection and union, both of which preimage respects. So once $f^{-1}$ takes basis (or subbasis) elements to opens, it takes every open to an open. This is the engine of [[Thm - Continuity via Bases and Neighbourhood Bases]] and is the *practical* reason to introduce bases at all.

The metric-ball example is the prototype: to check $f : (X, d_X) \to (Y, d_Y)$ is continuous, one checks $f^{-1}(B_\varepsilon(y))$ is open for every metric ball, equivalently, one performs an $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ argument. The basis-criterion for continuity *is* the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition.

A final piece of motivation: bases let one *compare* topologies efficiently. To show $\tau_1$ refines $\tau_2$, it suffices to show that every $\tau_2$-basis element is a union of $\tau_1$-basis elements. To show $\tau_1 = \tau_2$, show both refinements. This is much easier than comparing all the open sets directly, and it is the standard technique for proving things like "the product topology equals the metric topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$" or "the three norms on $\mathbb{R}^n$ generate the same topology".

# The Definition

Let $X$ be a set.

**Basis.** A collection $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ is a **basis for a topology on $X$** if it satisfies the following two conditions:

1. **(Cover.)** For every $x \in X$, there exists $B \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B$.

2. **(Intersection condition.)** For every $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ and every $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$, there exists $B_3 \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$.

The **topology generated by $\mathcal{B}$** is the collection
$$\tau(\mathcal{B}) = \left\{ \bigcup_{i \in I} B_i : I \text{ an arbitrary index set, } B_i \in \mathcal{B} \right\}$$
(including the empty union, which is $\emptyset$). Equivalently, $U \in \tau(\mathcal{B})$ if and only if for every $x \in U$ there is $B \in \mathcal{B}$ with $x \in B \subseteq U$.

**Basis of a given topology.** If $(X, \tau)$ is a topological space and $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau$, we say $\mathcal{B}$ is a **basis for $\tau$** if $\tau(\mathcal{B}) = \tau$ — equivalently, every open set $U \in \tau$ is a union of elements of $\mathcal{B}$.

**Subbasis.** A collection $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ is a **subbasis for a topology on $X$** with no further conditions. The **topology generated by $\mathcal{S}$** is the smallest topology containing $\mathcal{S}$, constructed as:
$$\tau(\mathcal{S}) = \tau(\mathcal{B}_\mathcal{S}), \quad \text{where} \quad \mathcal{B}_\mathcal{S} = \left\{S_1 \cap \dots \cap S_n : n \in \mathbb{N}, S_i \in \mathcal{S}\right\} \cup \{X\}$$
is the collection of finite intersections of elements of $\mathcal{S}$ (including the empty intersection $X$). The collection $\mathcal{B}_\mathcal{S}$ is automatically a basis (the cover and intersection conditions are immediate from the closure of finite intersections under intersection).

**Neighbourhood basis at a point.** A collection $\mathcal{B}_x$ of neighbourhoods of $x \in X$ is a **neighbourhood basis at $x$** if every neighbourhood of $x$ contains some element of $\mathcal{B}_x$. (A *neighbourhood* of $x$ is a set $N$ containing an open set $U$ with $x \in U \subseteq N$.) In a metric space, the balls $\{B_{1/n}(x) : n \geq 1\}$ form a countable neighbourhood basis at $x$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A basis is a *presentation* of a topology by generators — the same way a vector space is presented by a spanning set, a group by generators and relations, a module by a generating system. The topology is the *closure* of the basis under the topology axioms (arbitrary unions and finite intersections), and different bases can give the same topology, just as different spanning sets can give the same vector space.

In **algebraic geometry**, the **Zariski topology** on $\mathbb{A}^n = \mathbb{C}^n$ is generated by the subbasis of *principal open sets* $D(f) = \{x : f(x) \neq 0\}$ for $f$ a polynomial. The topology generated by this subbasis is exactly the Zariski topology, where the closed sets are zero sets of ideals. This is the natural way to define the Zariski topology — listing all the closed sets directly would be unwieldy.

In **functional analysis**, the **weak topology** on a normed space $V$ is generated by the subbasis $\{\varphi^{-1}(U) : \varphi \in V^*, U \subseteq \mathbb{R}\ \text{open}\}$ — preimages of open sets under continuous linear functionals. This is, by construction, the *coarsest* topology making every $\varphi \in V^*$ continuous. The weak topology has no nicer presentation: it is *defined* by its subbasis. Similarly the **weak-$*$ topology** on $V^*$ is generated by the subbasis $\{\text{ev}_v^{-1}(U) : v \in V, U \subseteq \mathbb{R}\ \text{open}\}$ of point-evaluation preimages. Banach–Alaoglu's theorem — the unit ball of $V^*$ is weak-$*$ compact — has no statement at all without these subbasis-generated topologies.

In **product topology**, the **box basis** on $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ is the collection $\{\prod_\alpha U_\alpha : U_\alpha \subseteq X_\alpha \text{ open}\}$. The **product topology** is generated by the subbasis $\{\pi_\alpha^{-1}(U_\alpha) : \alpha \in I, U_\alpha \subseteq X_\alpha\ \text{open}\}$ — preimages of opens under projections — and is *coarser* than the box topology on infinite products. The subbasis presentation is what makes the universal property of the product (a map into the product is continuous if each component is) tractable.

In **measure theory**, the analogue of "basis" is a **generating collection** for a σ-algebra: a small family $\mathcal{C}$ such that the smallest σ-algebra containing $\mathcal{C}$ is the target σ-algebra. The Borel σ-algebra on $\mathbb{R}$ is generated by the intervals; the corresponding measurable functions are exactly the continuous ones, plus their limits.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the open intervals as a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$.** $\mathcal{B} = \{(a, b) : a < b\}$ covers $\mathbb{R}$ (every real is in some interval) and satisfies the intersection condition trivially (the intersection of two open intervals is either empty or an open interval). The topology generated is the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$: every standard open set is a union of open intervals.

**Is an instance — the open balls as a basis for the metric topology.** In any metric space $(X, d)$, the collection $\mathcal{B} = \{B_\varepsilon(x) : x \in X, \varepsilon > 0\}$ is a basis for $\tau_d$. The cover condition: every $x$ lies in $B_1(x)$. The intersection condition: if $y \in B_{\varepsilon_1}(x_1) \cap B_{\varepsilon_2}(x_2)$, take $\delta = \min(\varepsilon_1 - d(x_1, y), \varepsilon_2 - d(x_2, y)) > 0$; then $B_\delta(y) \subseteq B_{\varepsilon_1}(x_1) \cap B_{\varepsilon_2}(x_2)$ by the triangle inequality. So the open balls are a basis.

**Is an instance — open balls of rational radius centred at rational points as a basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$.** The collection $\{B_q(x) : x \in \mathbb{Q}^n, q \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}\}$ is a *countable* basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$. Verification: every open set contains, around each of its points, some standard open ball, which contains a rational-centred rational-radius ball. So $\mathbb{R}^n$ is **second countable** — has a countable basis. This is one of the key countability axioms.

**Is an instance — the half-open intervals as a basis for the Sorgenfrey topology.** $\mathcal{B} = \{[a, b) : a < b\}$ is a basis for the **Sorgenfrey line** topology on $\mathbb{R}$. The cover condition: every $x$ lies in $[x, x+1)$. The intersection condition: $[a_1, b_1) \cap [a_2, b_2) = [\max(a_1, a_2), \min(b_1, b_2))$ (if non-empty), which is a basis element. The Sorgenfrey topology is *finer* than the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$ (every $(a, b) = \bigcup_n [a + 1/n, b)$) but is not second countable (any basis must have one element starting at each $x$, giving uncountably many).

**Is an instance — the principal open sets as a subbasis for the Zariski topology.** On $\mathbb{R}^n$ or $\mathbb{C}^n$, take $\mathcal{S} = \{D(f) : f \text{ polynomial}\}$ where $D(f) = \{x : f(x) \neq 0\}$. Finite intersections give $D(f_1) \cap \dots \cap D(f_k) = D(f_1 \cdots f_k)$, so $\mathcal{B} = \mathcal{S}$ is already closed under finite intersections. The topology generated is the Zariski topology: open sets are complements of zero sets of polynomial ideals.

**Is an instance — the preimages of opens under linear functionals as a subbasis for the weak topology.** On a normed space $V$, take $\mathcal{S} = \{\varphi^{-1}(U) : \varphi \in V^*, U \subseteq \mathbb{R}\ \text{open}\}$. Finite intersections give "tubes" $\{v : a_1 < \varphi_1(v) < b_1, \dots, a_n < \varphi_n(v) < b_n\}$, which are the basis elements of the weak topology. This is the standard construction.

**Is an instance — neighbourhood basis at a point in a metric space.** $\mathcal{B}_x = \{B_{1/n}(x) : n \geq 1\}$ is a countable neighbourhood basis at $x$. Every neighbourhood of $x$ contains some $B_\varepsilon(x)$ (by definition), which contains $B_{1/n}(x)$ for $1/n < \varepsilon$. So every metric space is **first countable**: every point has a countable neighbourhood basis.

**Is NOT an instance — $\mathcal{B} = \{(a, b) : a < b\} \cup \{[0, 1]\}$ is not a basis.** The intersection $[0, 1] \cap (0, 2)$ contains the point $0$ (well, no — $0 \in [0,1]$ and $0 \notin (0,2)$, let me adjust). Take $\mathcal{B} = \{[0,1], (1/2, 2)\}$ and the intersection $[0, 1] \cap (1/2, 2) = (1/2, 1]$. For this to be expressible as a union of basis elements, we would need to cover the point $1 \in (1/2, 1]$ by a basis element contained in $(1/2, 1]$. But the only basis elements are $[0, 1]$ (not contained in $(1/2, 1]$) and $(1/2, 2)$ (not contained in $(1/2, 1]$). So the intersection condition fails. Such a $\mathcal{B}$ is not a basis for any topology in which both members are open.

**Is NOT an instance of equality — the box topology vs. the product topology on $\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$.** On $\prod_n \mathbb{R}$, the box topology (basis: products $\prod_n U_n$ for $U_n$ open) is *strictly finer* than the product topology (subbasis: preimages of single-factor opens, $\pi_n^{-1}(U_n)$; basis: products $\prod_n U_n$ with $U_n = \mathbb{R}$ for all but finitely many $n$). The box topology contains the set $\prod_n (-1/n, 1/n)$, which is not open in the product topology. The product topology is the "right" topology for many purposes (it is the categorical product), while the box topology is generally too fine. On *finite* products, the two coincide.

**Corollary — a topology is determined by any of its bases.** If $\mathcal{B}_1$ and $\mathcal{B}_2$ are both bases for the same topology, they generate the same topology, even if $\mathcal{B}_1 \neq \mathcal{B}_2$.

**Corollary — basis criterion for refinement.** A topology $\tau_1$ on $X$ refines a topology $\tau_2$ (i.e., $\tau_1 \supseteq \tau_2$) if and only if every $\tau_2$-basis element is open in $\tau_1$. Equivalently, the identity map $(X, \tau_1) \to (X, \tau_2)$ is continuous (preimages of $\tau_2$-opens are $\tau_1$-open).

**Corollary — basis criterion for continuity.** $f : X \to Y$ is continuous if and only if $f^{-1}(B)$ is open in $X$ for every $B$ in some basis (or subbasis) of $\tau_Y$. This is the standard practical tool: rather than checking preimages of *all* open sets, check preimages of a generating family.

**Calibration check.** Verify that the open intervals form a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$ (cover and intersection both immediate). Construct a countable basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$ (rational-radius balls about rational points). Show that the collection $\{[a, b)\}$ is a basis for a topology on $\mathbb{R}$ (the Sorgenfrey) that is finer than the standard topology but not second countable. If you can also explain why a subbasis is *any* collection of subsets, with no conditions — and why finite-intersections-then-arbitrary-unions then satisfies the topology axioms — you have understood every clause of the definition.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] **Continuity via Bases** *(from this topic)*
> To check $f : X \to Y$ continuous, check $f^{-1}(B)$ open for $B$ in a basis (or subbasis) of $\tau_Y$. This is the standard practical tool — checking every open set is rarely tractable. See [[Thm - Continuity via Bases and Neighbourhood Bases]].

> [!tip] **First and Second Countable Spaces** *(from this topic)*
> A space is **first countable** if every point has a countable neighbourhood basis (true of every metric space). It is **second countable** if the whole topology has a countable basis (true of $\mathbb{R}^n$, false of the Sorgenfrey line). These countability axioms control how well topology mimics metric-space intuition.

> [!tip] **Product Topology** *(from this topic / Functional Analysis)*
> Given topological spaces $\{X_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}$, the **product topology** on $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ is generated by the subbasis $\{\pi_\alpha^{-1}(U_\alpha)\}$. This is the *coarsest* topology making every projection continuous, and it satisfies the universal property: a continuous map into the product is the same data as a continuous map into each factor.

> [!tip] **Weak Topology** *(from Functional Analysis)*
> On a normed space $V$, the **weak topology** is generated by the subbasis $\{\varphi^{-1}(U) : \varphi \in V^*\}$ — preimages of opens under continuous linear functionals. Banach–Alaoglu and weak convergence are stated in this language.

> [!tip] **Zariski Topology** *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> Generated by the principal open sets $D(f) = \{x : f(x) \neq 0\}$ for $f$ a polynomial. The standard topology on schemes; the foundation of sheaf theory in algebraic geometry.

> [!tip] **Quotient Topology** *(from this topic / Algebraic Topology)*
> Given a topological space $X$ and an equivalence relation $\sim$, the quotient $X/{\sim}$ carries the **quotient topology**: a set $U \subseteq X/{\sim}$ is open if and only if its preimage in $X$ is open. This is the topology whose universal property is: a map out of the quotient is continuous iff its lift to $X$ is.

> [!tip] **Strong Operator Topology and Other Operator Topologies** *(from Functional Analysis)*
> On the space $B(H)$ of bounded operators on a Hilbert space, several topologies are defined by subbases: the strong operator topology (by $T \mapsto Tv$ for $v \in H$), the weak operator topology (by $T \mapsto \langle Tv, w \rangle$), the ultraweak topology, the norm topology. Each is a topology in the sense of these axioms, and the choice of topology is a strategic decision.
