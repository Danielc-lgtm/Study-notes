# Notation

$X$ is a topological space and $\tau$ its topology. A **basis** $\mathcal{B}$ for $\tau$ is a family of open sets generating $\tau$ via arbitrary unions. A **neighbourhood basis** at $x$ is written $\mathcal{B}_x$ — a family of neighbourhoods such that every neighbourhood of $x$ contains some element. "Countable" means finite or countably infinite. The full notation registry sits on [[Topology I — §1–3 Metric and Topological Spaces]].

---

# Axiom Motivation

The general definition of a topological space is extremely permissive — so permissive that many of the comfortable analytic moves one makes in $\mathbb{R}^n$ stop being legal in a general space. The most conspicuous casualty is the *sequence-based* style of reasoning: in $\mathbb{R}$, a point lies in the closure of a set $A$ if and only if some sequence in $A$ converges to it; continuity of a function is equivalent to its preservation of convergent sequences. Both of these break in general topological spaces. The countability axioms are how we precisely characterize the spaces in which they do *not* break.

The first axiom one wants is local. To say "every neighbourhood of $x$ contains some basis element" is to say that we can probe the local structure at $x$ with a generating family of neighbourhoods. If that family is *countable*, we can list it as a decreasing sequence $B_1 \supseteq B_2 \supseteq \dots$ (intersect successive elements) and produce a *sequence* of test points $x_n \in B_n$ that converges to $x$ — and conversely, any sequence converging to $x$ eventually lies in any chosen $B_n$. This is the precise meaning in which a countable neighbourhood basis lets sequences see all of the local structure. It is the *minimal* condition under which the metric-style analysis of pointwise behaviour works, and it is called **first countability**.

The natural next question is whether one can demand a *global* analogue: a single countable family of open sets generating the topology of the whole space, rather than countable families at each point separately. This is a strictly stronger condition — it implies first countability, since the global basis can be intersected with each point — and it is called **second countability**. It captures the idea that the topology of $X$ is *generated by countably much data*, which is the right "cardinality of complexity" condition for many constructive theorems.

The difference between first and second countability turns out to control three downstream phenomena. First, *separability* — the existence of a countable dense subset — follows from second countability (pick one point from each basis element) but not from first countability. Second, *Lindelöf-ness* — every open cover has a countable subcover — follows from second countability but not from first. Third, and most importantly, **metrizability** — the existence of a metric inducing the topology — requires second countability for the standard sufficient conditions (regular plus second countable, the Urysohn metrization theorem). So second countability is what bridges general topology back to metric topology.

A reasonable question is why we do not just demand metrizability. The reason is that many natural and important spaces are *not* metrizable: the [[Topology I — §1–3 Metric and Topological Spaces|product topology on an uncountable product]], the weak topology on an infinite-dimensional Banach space, the Zariski topology on an algebraic variety. We want a vocabulary that distinguishes these from $\mathbb{R}^n$ without demanding a metric, and the countability axioms supply exactly such a vocabulary. They tell us how *sequence-friendly* a topology is, without committing to a distance.

The choice of "countable" rather than "finite" or "of cardinality $\kappa$" is forced by the same considerations: finite would be much too restrictive (only finite or trivial spaces have finite bases), while higher cardinalities are studied (the cardinality of the smallest basis is the **weight** of the space) but countable is the cardinality at which "analysis-like" behaviour begins, because countable sequences are the convergence-test tool of analysis.

# The Definition

Let $X$ be a topological space.

**First countable.** $X$ is **first countable** if every point $x \in X$ has a *countable* neighbourhood basis: there is a countable collection $\{B_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ of neighbourhoods of $x$ such that every neighbourhood of $x$ contains some $B_n$. Without loss of generality the basis can be taken to be a decreasing sequence (replace $B_n$ by $B_1 \cap \dots \cap B_n$) and to consist of open sets.

**Second countable.** $X$ is **second countable** if the topology $\tau$ has a *countable* basis: there is a countable collection $\mathcal{B} = \{U_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ of open sets such that every open set in $X$ is a union of elements of $\mathcal{B}$.

**Separable.** $X$ is **separable** if it contains a countable dense subset: a countable $D \subseteq X$ with $\overline{D} = X$.

**Lindelöf.** $X$ is **Lindelöf** if every open cover of $X$ has a countable subcover.

The implications among these properties: **second countable $\implies$ first countable** (the global basis localizes by intersecting with $\{U \in \mathcal{B} : x \in U\}$), **second countable $\implies$ separable** (one point per basis element), and **second countable $\implies$ Lindelöf**. In metric spaces all three of {second countable, separable, Lindelöf} are equivalent, but in general topological spaces they diverge.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The countability axioms are best understood as **complexity-of-topology** axioms: they measure how much information is needed to specify the topology. A second countable space is one whose topology fits in countably much data; a first countable one needs only countably much data *locally*. In information-theoretic terms, second countability bounds the *global* descriptive complexity of the topology, first countability bounds its *local* descriptive complexity.

This is parallel to the situation in **measure theory**, where the analogous condition is **$\sigma$-finiteness** — a measure space is $\sigma$-finite if it is a countable union of finite-measure pieces. Both $\sigma$-finiteness and second countability are "the topology / measure fits in countably much" conditions, and both unlock theorems that need countability for their proofs (Fubini's theorem in measure theory; sequential characterizations of closure in topology).

In **algebraic geometry**, the Zariski topology on $\mathbb{A}^n$ over a field $k$ is second countable when $k$ is countable, but not in general — the failure of second countability is what makes Zariski spaces topologically pathological from the analyst's perspective even when they are perfectly natural from the algebraist's.

In **functional analysis**, the norm topology on an infinite-dimensional Banach space is first countable (metric), but the weak topology is *not* first countable. This is the reason for the technical apparatus of nets and weak-$*$ convergence in the dual: sequence-based reasoning fails, and one must reach for nets or filters.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance of both — $\mathbb{R}^n$ in the standard topology.** Take the collection of open balls $B_r(q)$ with $r \in \mathbb{Q}_{>0}$ and $q \in \mathbb{Q}^n$: there are countably many such, and they form a basis for the topology. So $\mathbb{R}^n$ is second countable, hence first countable, separable (the rational points are dense), and Lindelöf. Every separable metric space is in fact second countable, by the same construction with $D$ replacing $\mathbb{Q}^n$.

**Is an instance — every metric space is first countable.** In a metric space $(X, d)$, the balls $\{B_{1/n}(x)\}_{n \geq 1}$ form a countable, decreasing neighbourhood basis at $x$. This is the structural reason metric spaces behave like analysis: every local question reduces to a sequence question. The first-countable spaces are exactly those for which sequences suffice for closure (see [[Thm - Characterizations of the Closure]]).

**Is NOT an instance of second countable — the discrete topology on an uncountable set.** Let $X$ be uncountable with the discrete topology (every subset is open). Every point $x$ has the singleton $\{x\}$ as a one-element neighbourhood basis, so $X$ is first countable. But the singletons $\{x\}$ are all open, and any basis must include each one (since $\{x\}$ has no smaller open set inside it) — so any basis has at least $|X|$ elements, uncountably many. The space is first countable but *not* second countable, separable, or Lindelöf.

**Is NOT an instance of first countable — the cofinite topology on an uncountable set.** Let $X$ be uncountable and equip it with the cofinite topology: open sets are $\emptyset$ and the complements of finite sets. Fix $x \in X$ and suppose $\{N_n\}$ were a countable neighbourhood basis at $x$. Each $N_n$ contains some open $U_n \ni x$, and each $U_n$ has finite complement $F_n$. The union $\bigcup F_n$ is countable, so there is a point $y \in X \setminus (\{x\} \cup \bigcup F_n)$, and then $X \setminus \{y\}$ is an open neighbourhood of $x$ that contains no $U_n$ (each $U_n$ contains $y$). Contradiction. So this is a standard example of a topological space which is genuinely *not* first countable, and on which sequence-based reasoning fails.

**Is an instance — the Sorgenfrey line is first countable but not second countable.** The half-open interval topology on $\mathbb{R}$, generated by intervals $[a, b)$, is first countable: at $x$, the basis $\{[x, x + 1/n)\}_{n \geq 1}$ works. But it is not second countable: every basis must include, for each $x \in \mathbb{R}$, some interval of the form $[x, y)$ (since these are the half-open intervals containing $x$ that are not in the standard topology), and there are uncountably many $x$. The Sorgenfrey line is the canonical example of a first-but-not-second-countable space that is also separable and Lindelöf — showing all these implications can fail to reverse.

**Corollary — first-countable spaces are sequentially adequate.** In a first-countable space $X$, a point $x$ lies in $\overline{A}$ if and only if some sequence in $A$ converges to $x$, and a function $f$ is continuous at $x$ if and only if it preserves convergent sequences at $x$. This is precisely the content of [[Thm - Characterizations of the Closure]] under first countability. Without first countability, both equivalences fail, and one must use nets.

**Corollary — separability is countable density.** A space is separable if it has a countable subset whose closure is everything. In second-countable spaces, picking one point from each basis element produces a countable dense set. In metric spaces, conversely, a countable dense set $\{q_n\}$ gives a countable basis $\{B_{1/m}(q_n)\}_{n, m \geq 1}$. So for metric spaces the three notions "second countable", "separable", and "Lindelöf" coincide. This collapse is what makes metric topology much friendlier than general topology.

**Calibration check.** Verify that the discrete topology on a *countable* set is second countable (every singleton is an open basis element, countably many of them); that $\mathbb{R}$ with the cocountable topology — open sets are $\emptyset$ and complements of countable sets — is not first countable at any point; and that the order topology on the first uncountable ordinal $\omega_1$ (the space of ordinals less than $\omega_1$) is first countable but not second countable, not separable, not Lindelöf.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Sequential Closure Equals Closure *(in this topic)*
> First countability is the precise condition that makes "closure" and "sequential closure" coincide — see clause (e) of [[Thm - Characterizations of the Closure]]. Without first countability one needs nets or filters; with it, all of analysis-style sequence reasoning is available.

> [!tip] Urysohn Metrization Theorem *(from Topology III)*
> A regular second-countable Hausdorff space is metrizable — there exists a metric inducing its topology. This is the **Urysohn metrization theorem**, the deepest theorem connecting metric and topological spaces, and it is the reason second countability is the right cardinality condition for "analysis-like" topology. The proof embeds the space into a countable product of intervals.

> [!tip] Lindelöf and Paracompactness *(from Topology II onward)*
> A Lindelöf space supports countable arguments in covering problems — every open cover refines to a countable one, which can then be handled by partition-of-unity-style constructions. Second countability is the most common route to Lindelöf-ness, and through it to **paracompactness**, the technical assumption that underlies the existence of smooth partitions of unity on manifolds.
