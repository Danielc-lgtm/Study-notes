# Problem Statement

Let $X$ be a set and $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ an arbitrary collection of subsets. Define
$$\mathcal{B} = \left\{S_1 \cap S_2 \cap \dots \cap S_n : n \geq 0,\ S_i \in \mathcal{S}\right\}$$
(the collection of finite intersections of elements of $\mathcal{S}$, including the empty intersection $X$ when $n = 0$); and
$$\tau = \left\{\bigcup_{\alpha \in I} B_\alpha : I \text{ arbitrary},\ B_\alpha \in \mathcal{B}\right\} \cup \{\emptyset\}.$$

1. Show that $\mathcal{B}$ is a [[Def - Basis and Subbasis for a Topology|basis]] for a topology on $X$.
2. Show that $\tau$ is a [[Def - Topological Space|topology]] on $X$, $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \tau$, and $\tau$ is the *smallest* topology on $X$ containing $\mathcal{S}$ (universal property).
3. Prove the subbasis criterion for continuity: a function $f : Y \to (X, \tau)$ from a topological space $Y$ is [[Def - Continuous Map|continuous]] if and only if $f^{-1}(S)$ is open in $Y$ for every $S \in \mathcal{S}$.
4. Apply this: on the product $\prod_{\alpha} Y_\alpha$ of a family of topological spaces, the **product topology** is generated by the subbasis $\{\pi_\beta^{-1}(U) : \beta \in I,\ U \subseteq Y_\beta\ \text{open}\}$, where $\pi_\beta$ denotes the $\beta$-th projection. Read off the corresponding continuity criterion: a map into the product is continuous if and only if every component map is continuous.

**Recall:**

![[Def - Basis and Subbasis for a Topology#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Topological Space|topology]] $\tau$ on $X$ is a collection of subsets of $X$ containing $\emptyset$ and $X$, closed under finite intersections and arbitrary unions. A **basis** $\mathcal{B}$ for a topology generates that topology by taking arbitrary unions; the basis axioms (covering, intersection property) are exactly what is needed for the collection of unions to be a topology. A **subbasis** is any collection $\mathcal{S}$, with no requirements — the basis it generates is the collection of finite intersections of subbasis elements.

A function $f : Y \to X$ between topological spaces is [[Def - Continuous Map|continuous]] iff $f^{-1}(U)$ is open in $Y$ for every open $U \subseteq X$. The [[Thm - Continuity via Bases and Neighbourhood Bases|basis-criterion for continuity]] reduces this to checking $f^{-1}(B)$ is open for every $B$ in a basis of $X$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** Construct a topology from arbitrary data and characterize it by a universal property. This is the prototype "generated by" construction in topology, mirroring the same kind of construction in group theory (subgroup generated by a set), measure theory ($\sigma$-algebra generated by a set), and linear algebra (subspace spanned by a set).

**Assumption pattern.** The construction has a *two-step* structure: take finite intersections to get a basis $\mathcal{B}$, then arbitrary unions to get the topology $\tau$. This is necessary because a topology must be closed under both operations, but the input data $\mathcal{S}$ is closed under neither; the smallest enlargement closed under both is the iterated construction.

**Theorem routing.** Step 1 is checking the basis axioms — covering ($X$ is the empty intersection, which is in $\mathcal{B}$) and the intersection property (finite intersections of finite intersections are still finite intersections). Step 2 is verifying that the unions form a topology, that the topology contains the original subbasis (each $S$ is a 1-element intersection), and the universal property (any topology containing $\mathcal{S}$ must contain $\mathcal{B}$ and then $\tau$, since topologies are closed under finite intersections and arbitrary unions). Step 3 is the standard reduction: continuity of $f$ from preimages of opens reduces first to preimages of basis elements (preimage commutes with union), then to preimages of subbasis elements (preimage commutes with intersection). Step 4 applies Step 3 to the projection-defined subbasis.

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious move in Step 3 is the *direction* of the reductions: continuity is naturally a universal statement ("every open"), but it can be tested on the much smaller subbasis because the preimage operation commutes with both unions and intersections. This is the reason subbasis criteria are useful — they reduce an existential check over the entire topology to a check over the small generating set.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following operations from [[Topology I — §1–3 Metric and Topological Spaces#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Verify the basis axioms via the intersection-closure of finite intersections.** $\mathcal{B}$ is closed under finite intersections by construction, which is exactly the basis intersection axiom.

2. **Read off the universal property from the construction.** Any topology containing $\mathcal{S}$ must contain finite intersections (closed under finite intersections), hence $\mathcal{B}$; and arbitrary unions of $\mathcal{B}$ (closed under arbitrary unions), hence $\tau$.

3. **Use preimage-intersection commutation to reduce continuity to a basis check.** $f^{-1}(B_1 \cap \dots \cap B_n) = f^{-1}(B_1) \cap \dots \cap f^{-1}(B_n)$ and $f^{-1}(\bigcup U_\alpha) = \bigcup f^{-1}(U_\alpha)$ are the standard preimage identities.

4. **Apply the universal property of the product topology.** The product topology is *defined* as the coarsest topology making every projection continuous — equivalently, the subbasis-generated topology with $\pi_\beta^{-1}(U)$ as subbasis elements.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> For Step 1, the intersection property of $\mathcal{B}$ is *trivial*: if $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$ are each a finite intersection of $S_i$'s, then $B_1 \cap B_2$ is the finite intersection of all the $S_i$'s together — still in $\mathcal{B}$. And take $B_3 = B_1 \cap B_2$ in the basis axiom.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> For Step 2's universal property: suppose $\tau'$ is any topology with $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \tau'$. Then $\tau'$ contains all finite intersections of $\mathcal{S}$-elements (it is closed under finite intersections), hence $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau'$. And $\tau'$ contains all arbitrary unions of these (closed under unions), hence $\tau \subseteq \tau'$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For Step 3, the key identity: $f^{-1}(A \cap B) = f^{-1}(A) \cap f^{-1}(B)$ and $f^{-1}(\bigcup_\alpha A_\alpha) = \bigcup_\alpha f^{-1}(A_\alpha)$. These let you reduce continuity from preimages of opens to preimages of subbasis elements.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For Step 4, a function $f : Y \to \prod_\alpha Y_\alpha$ has components $f_\alpha = \pi_\alpha \circ f$. By Step 3, $f$ is continuous iff $f^{-1}(\pi_\beta^{-1}(U))$ is open for every $\beta$ and every open $U \subseteq Y_\beta$. But $f^{-1}(\pi_\beta^{-1}(U)) = (\pi_\beta \circ f)^{-1}(U) = f_\beta^{-1}(U)$.

# Solution

The construction takes an arbitrary collection of subsets and "closes it up" under the two operations a topology must allow: finite intersections (one round) then arbitrary unions (one more round). The universal property says this is the most efficient closure; the subbasis continuity criterion is the operational consequence.

**Step 1: $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis.**

$\mathcal{B}$ contains $X$ (the empty intersection) and is closed under finite intersections by construction. Hence the basis axioms are immediate.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Covering.* The empty intersection is the empty conjunction, which is the entire ambient set $X$ (since a vacuous condition is satisfied by every $x \in X$). So $X \in \mathcal{B}$, and every $x \in X$ lies in $X \in \mathcal{B}$.
>
> *Intersection property.* Let $B_1, B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$. Then $B_1 = S_1 \cap \dots \cap S_m$ and $B_2 = T_1 \cap \dots \cap T_k$ for some $S_i, T_j \in \mathcal{S}$. So $B_1 \cap B_2 = S_1 \cap \dots \cap S_m \cap T_1 \cap \dots \cap T_k$ is itself a finite intersection of $\mathcal{S}$-elements, hence in $\mathcal{B}$. So for any $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$, take $B_3 = B_1 \cap B_2 \in \mathcal{B}$: then $x \in B_3 \subseteq B_1 \cap B_2$.
>
> Both axioms hold, so $\mathcal{B}$ is a [[Def - Basis and Subbasis for a Topology|basis]].

**Step 2: $\tau$ is the smallest topology containing $\mathcal{S}$.**

By definition $\tau$ is the collection of arbitrary unions of $\mathcal{B}$-elements (with $\emptyset$ thrown in). We verify the topology axioms and the universal property.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *$\tau$ is a topology.*
> - *$\emptyset \in \tau$* by construction. *$X \in \tau$* as $X \in \mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau$ (each $B$ is a 1-element union of itself).
> - *Arbitrary unions.* A union of $\tau$-elements is a union of unions of $\mathcal{B}$-elements, which is itself a union of $\mathcal{B}$-elements, hence in $\tau$.
> - *Finite intersections.* Let $U_1, U_2 \in \tau$; write $U_1 = \bigcup_\alpha B_\alpha$, $U_2 = \bigcup_\beta B'_\beta$ with $B_\alpha, B'_\beta \in \mathcal{B}$. Then $U_1 \cap U_2 = \bigcup_{\alpha, \beta} (B_\alpha \cap B'_\beta)$. Each $B_\alpha \cap B'_\beta \in \mathcal{B}$ (Step 1: $\mathcal{B}$ is closed under finite intersections). So $U_1 \cap U_2$ is a union of $\mathcal{B}$-elements, hence in $\tau$. Induction gives the same for any finite intersection.
>
> *$\mathcal{S} \subseteq \tau$.* Each $S \in \mathcal{S}$ is a 1-fold intersection, so $S \in \mathcal{B}$; and $S$ is a 1-element union of itself, so $S \in \tau$.
>
> *Universal property: $\tau$ is the smallest topology containing $\mathcal{S}$.* Let $\tau'$ be any topology on $X$ with $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \tau'$. Since $\tau'$ is closed under finite intersections, every finite intersection of $\mathcal{S}$-elements lies in $\tau'$ — that is, $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau'$. Since $\tau'$ is closed under arbitrary unions, every union of $\mathcal{B}$-elements lies in $\tau'$ — that is, $\tau \subseteq \tau'$. So $\tau$ is contained in every topology containing $\mathcal{S}$, hence is the smallest.

**Step 3: The subbasis continuity criterion.**

A function $f : Y \to (X, \tau)$ is continuous iff $f^{-1}(S)$ is open in $Y$ for every $S \in \mathcal{S}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Forward.* If $f$ is continuous, then $f^{-1}(U)$ is open for every $U \in \tau$. Since $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \tau$, $f^{-1}(S)$ is open for every $S \in \mathcal{S}$.
>
> *Reverse.* Suppose $f^{-1}(S)$ is open in $Y$ for every $S \in \mathcal{S}$. To show $f$ is continuous, we show $f^{-1}(U)$ is open for every $U \in \tau$.
>
> *Step A: every $B = S_1 \cap \dots \cap S_n \in \mathcal{B}$ has open preimage.* Using preimage-intersection commutation,
> $$f^{-1}(B) = f^{-1}(S_1 \cap \dots \cap S_n) = f^{-1}(S_1) \cap \dots \cap f^{-1}(S_n),$$
> a finite intersection of opens in $Y$ (by hypothesis), hence open in $Y$ (topology closed under finite intersection).
>
> *Step B: every $U = \bigcup_\alpha B_\alpha \in \tau$ has open preimage.* Using preimage-union commutation,
> $$f^{-1}(U) = f^{-1}\Big(\bigcup_\alpha B_\alpha\Big) = \bigcup_\alpha f^{-1}(B_\alpha),$$
> a union of opens (Step A), hence open in $Y$ (topology closed under arbitrary unions).
>
> So $f$ is continuous. This is a strengthening of the basis criterion [[Thm - Continuity via Bases and Neighbourhood Bases]] — testing on the smaller subbasis suffices.

**Step 4: Application — product topology.**

For $\prod_\alpha Y_\alpha$, the **product topology** is the topology generated by the subbasis $\mathcal{S} = \{\pi_\beta^{-1}(U) : \beta \in I,\ U \subseteq Y_\beta\ \text{open}\}$. By Step 3, a function $f : Z \to \prod_\alpha Y_\alpha$ is continuous iff $f^{-1}(\pi_\beta^{-1}(U))$ is open in $Z$ for every $\beta \in I$ and every open $U \subseteq Y_\beta$. But $f^{-1}(\pi_\beta^{-1}(U)) = (\pi_\beta \circ f)^{-1}(U) = f_\beta^{-1}(U)$, where $f_\beta = \pi_\beta \circ f$ is the $\beta$-th component of $f$. So:

$f : Z \to \prod_\alpha Y_\alpha$ is continuous **if and only if every component $f_\beta : Z \to Y_\beta$ is continuous**.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The product topology is *defined* as the coarsest topology on $\prod_\alpha Y_\alpha$ making every projection $\pi_\beta$ continuous. By the universal property in Step 2, this is the topology generated by the subbasis $\mathcal{S} = \{\pi_\beta^{-1}(U) : \beta, U\}$, where we ensure $\pi_\beta$ is continuous by including $\pi_\beta^{-1}(U)$ as an open set for every $U \subseteq Y_\beta$ open.
>
> Basis elements: finite intersections $\pi_{\beta_1}^{-1}(U_1) \cap \dots \cap \pi_{\beta_n}^{-1}(U_n)$ — these are *cylinder sets*: tuples whose $\beta_i$-th coordinate lies in $U_i$, with arbitrary values in other coordinates.
>
> *Continuity criterion.* By Step 3, $f : Z \to \prod_\alpha Y_\alpha$ is continuous iff $f^{-1}(\pi_\beta^{-1}(U))$ is open in $Z$ for every $\beta$ and every open $U \subseteq Y_\beta$. Now use $f^{-1} \circ \pi_\beta^{-1} = (\pi_\beta \circ f)^{-1}$:
> $$f^{-1}(\pi_\beta^{-1}(U)) = (\pi_\beta \circ f)^{-1}(U) = f_\beta^{-1}(U),$$
> where $f_\beta(z) = \pi_\beta(f(z))$ is the $\beta$-th component of $f$. So $f$ is continuous iff $f_\beta^{-1}(U)$ is open for every $\beta, U$, iff every $f_\beta$ is continuous.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **(1)** $\mathcal{B}$ contains the empty intersection $X$ and is closed under finite intersections (concatenating intersection lists), so the basis axioms hold. **(2)** $\tau$ contains $\emptyset, X$, is closed under arbitrary unions (a union of unions is a union) and finite intersections (the intersection of unions distributes back to unions of intersections of basis elements). $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{B} \subseteq \tau$. Universal property: any topology containing $\mathcal{S}$ contains $\mathcal{B}$ (closed under intersections) and then $\tau$ (closed under unions). **(3)** Forward: $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \tau$ so $f^{-1}(S)$ is open. Reverse: $f^{-1}$ commutes with intersections and unions, so preimages of basis elements (finite intersections) are open, and preimages of unions of basis elements are open. **(4)** The product topology has subbasis $\{\pi_\beta^{-1}(U)\}$; applying Step 3 with $f^{-1}(\pi_\beta^{-1}(U)) = f_\beta^{-1}(U)$ gives the component-wise continuity criterion. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**The "generated by" pattern is one of the most reusable constructions across mathematics, and the topology version follows the universal-algebra template exactly.** Every "generate-the-smallest-X-containing-$\mathcal{S}$" construction has the same shape: take the intersection of all $X$'s containing $\mathcal{S}$ (when this intersection is well-defined and inherits the $X$-structure), or build up from $\mathcal{S}$ by repeatedly applying the closure operations of $X$. For topologies, the closure operations are finite intersection and arbitrary union, applied in *one round each*: first finite intersection to get a basis, then arbitrary union to get a topology. The two-step structure is forced — applying both at the same time, or doing arbitrary unions before finite intersections, fails to terminate or produces the wrong answer. The same iterate-closure-operations construction appears in: subgroups (closure under multiplication and inversion), $\sigma$-algebras (closure under complement and countable union, in $\omega_1$ rounds), vector subspaces (closure under linear combinations, in one round because composition of two affine combinations is again an affine combination), and convex hulls (closure under convex combinations, also one round).

**The subbasis continuity criterion is the *workhorse* for verifying continuity into spaces with non-obvious topologies.** This is the operational power of the construction: continuity into $(X, \tau)$ is checked on $\mathcal{S}$, not on $\tau$ or even $\mathcal{B}$. Examples where this is essential: continuity into a *product* space ($\mathcal{S}$ = preimages of opens under projections, see Step 4); continuity into a *quotient* space (universal property says $f : Z \to X/\sim$ is continuous iff $f \circ q : Z \to X$ is, where $q$ is the quotient map); continuity into a space with the *weak* topology induced by a family of functionals (functional analysis). In each case the subbasis is the natural "defining data" of the topology, and a continuity check on the subbasis is what one actually performs.

**The product-topology continuity criterion — *continuous into a product = continuous in each component* — is one of the small set of universal properties that solves perhaps half of all continuity problems.** When asked to prove a map $f : Z \to \prod_\alpha Y_\alpha$ is continuous, do not check open sets directly. Verify that each *component* $f_\beta = \pi_\beta \circ f$ is continuous, individually, and the product map is automatically continuous. This is why the curve $t \mapsto (\cos t, \sin t) : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is continuous (each component is); why a linear map $T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is continuous iff each row is (each component is a linear functional, automatically continuous on finite-dimensional spaces); why the diagonal map $\Delta : X \to X \times X$, $\Delta(x) = (x, x)$, is continuous (each component is the identity). The pattern is so pervasive that one often does not even invoke it by name.

**The construction is *coarsest* — the smallest topology making $\mathcal{S}$ open — and this is what makes it universal in the categorical sense.** Dually, one could ask for the *finest* topology in which $\mathcal{S}$ becomes a system of *closed* sets, or the finest topology in which certain maps are continuous. In topology, the two universal constructions are: **initial topology** on $X$ given a family of functions $f_\alpha : X \to Y_\alpha$, which is the coarsest topology on $X$ making every $f_\alpha$ continuous; **final topology** on $Y$ given a family of functions $g_\alpha : X_\alpha \to Y$, which is the finest topology on $Y$ making every $g_\alpha$ continuous. The product topology is the initial topology for projections; the quotient topology is the final topology for the projection $X \to X/\sim$. Each is given by the relevant generated-by construction, with the appropriate direction of coarseness. Recognizing a topology as an initial or final topology is often the cleanest way to characterize it — the universal property does the work, and concrete open-set descriptions are avoided.
