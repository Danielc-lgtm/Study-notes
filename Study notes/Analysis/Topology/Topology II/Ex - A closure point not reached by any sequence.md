# Problem Statement

The space $\omega_1 + 1$ is the set of all ordinals up to and including the first uncountable ordinal $\omega_1$, equipped with the *order topology* — the topology generated by the subbasis of open rays $(-\infty, \alpha) = \{\beta : \beta < \alpha\}$ and $(\alpha, +\infty) = \{\beta : \beta > \alpha\}$. Concretely, a basis for the order topology is given by the open intervals $(\alpha, \beta) = \{\gamma : \alpha < \gamma < \beta\}$ together with the rays $[0, \beta) = \{\gamma : \gamma < \beta\}$ (since $0$ is the minimum element). Take $A = [0, \omega_1) = \{\alpha : \alpha < \omega_1\}$, the subset of all countable ordinals.

**(a)** Show that $\omega_1$ is a limit point of $A$ in $\omega_1 + 1$: every open neighbourhood of $\omega_1$ in $\omega_1 + 1$ meets $A$. Hence $\omega_1 \in \overline{A}$.

**(b)** Show that no *sequence* in $A$ converges to $\omega_1$. (Hence $\omega_1 \in \overline{A}$ but $\omega_1$ is not in the sequential closure of $A$.)

**(c)** Conclude that $\omega_1 + 1$ is *not first-countable* at $\omega_1$: there is no countable neighbourhood basis at $\omega_1$.

The **first uncountable ordinal** $\omega_1$ is the set of all countable ordinals (it is uncountable but every $\alpha < \omega_1$ is a countable ordinal). The key property: the supremum of any countable subset of $\omega_1$ is again in $\omega_1$ (a countable union of countable sets is countable, so $\sup_n \alpha_n$ for countable $\alpha_n$ is again countable, hence $< \omega_1$).

A topological space $X$ is **first-countable** at a point $x$ if there is a countable neighbourhood basis at $x$ — a countable collection $\{U_n\}$ of open neighbourhoods of $x$ such that every open $U \ni x$ contains some $U_n$.

In a first-countable space, the closure $\overline{A}$ equals the *sequential closure*: $x \in \overline{A}$ iff some sequence in $A$ converges to $x$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** Construct the *canonical example* of a closure point not reached by a sequence — the standard non-first-countable example. This shows that sequences are insufficient to characterize closure in general topology, motivating nets.

**Assumption pattern.** The space $\omega_1 + 1$ has the order topology, and the key set-theoretic input is the *countable cofinality* property of $\omega_1$: a countable supremum of countable ordinals is countable, hence $< \omega_1$. This is what blocks sequences (which have countable index set) from approaching $\omega_1$ from below.

**Theorem routing.** Part (a) is direct from the order topology basis. Part (b) is the central calculation: any sequence in $A$ is countable, has countable supremum $\sigma < \omega_1$, and the open interval $(\sigma, \omega_1]$ is a neighbourhood of $\omega_1$ containing no sequence terms. Part (c) is a corollary: first-countability would force the sequential and topological closures to agree, contradicting (a)+(b).

**Key decision point.** The non-obvious step is *why $\sigma < \omega_1$*. A sequence in $A$ takes values $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \ldots$, each a countable ordinal. Their supremum $\sigma = \sup_n \alpha_n$ is the supremum of a countable set of countable ordinals. By the basic ordinal-arithmetic fact "a countable union of countable sets is countable" (which requires only countable choice), $\sigma$ is a countable ordinal, hence $\sigma < \omega_1$. This is *the* key insight: $\omega_1$ has *uncountable cofinality*, and countable sequences cannot reach it.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from the [[Topology II — §4–7 Connectivity, Separation, Nets, Compactness#Legal Operations|topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Promote sequential statements to net statements in non-first-countable spaces.** This is the canonical example where sequences fail; the cure is to switch to nets. Indeed [[Thm - Closure via Nets|the closure-via-nets theorem]] applies even though the closure-via-sequences statement does not.

2. **Use cardinality (countable vs. uncountable) as a discriminator in ordinal arguments.** The cofinality of $\omega_1$ — the size of the smallest cofinal subset — is $\omega_1$ itself, i.e., uncountable. So no countable subset of $A = [0, \omega_1)$ can be cofinal in $A$.

3. **Exploit order-topology bases.** The order topology has a small, explicit basis (open intervals), which allows neighbourhoods of any ordinal to be checked concretely.

(These are the only relevant basic opens at the maximum element $\omega_1$.) Each such interval contains the ordinal $\alpha + 1$, which is in $A = [0, \omega_1)$ (it is countable). So every open neighbourhood of $\omega_1$ meets $A$.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> For (b): suppose $\{\alpha_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ is a sequence in $A$. Each $\alpha_n$ is a countable ordinal. Take $\sigma = \sup_n \alpha_n$. Show that $\sigma$ is a countable ordinal (countable union of countable sets is countable). Hence $\sigma < \omega_1$. Now the open neighbourhood $(\sigma, \omega_1]$ of $\omega_1$ contains no $\alpha_n$ — so the sequence is not eventually in this neighbourhood, hence does not converge to $\omega_1$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For (c): suppose for contradiction $\omega_1$ had a countable neighbourhood basis $\{U_n\}$. Each $U_n$ contains some interval $(\beta_n, \omega_1]$, and one can choose $\beta_n < \omega_1$. Define the sequence $\alpha_n = \beta_n + 1$ — but wait, this would converge to $\omega_1$ if the $U_n$ are a basis. Actually, this is the standard argument: pick $\alpha_n \in U_n \cap A$ at each $n$. Then $\sigma = \sup_n \alpha_n < \omega_1$, and $(\sigma, \omega_1]$ is a neighbourhood of $\omega_1$, hence by the basis property contains some $U_N$, so $\alpha_N \in U_N \subseteq (\sigma, \omega_1]$, giving $\alpha_N > \sigma \geq \alpha_N$ — contradiction. So no countable basis exists.

# Solution

The example $\omega_1 + 1$ is the standard non-first-countable space, and the non-first-countability is detected at the maximum point $\omega_1$ via the "uncountable cofinality" structure of the ordinal $\omega_1$ itself.

**Step 1: Part (a) — $\omega_1$ is a limit point of $A$.**

Every open neighbourhood $U$ of $\omega_1$ in $\omega_1 + 1$ contains an interval of the form $(\alpha, \omega_1]$ for some $\alpha < \omega_1$, by the basis of the order topology at the maximum. This interval contains $\alpha + 1 \in A$, so $U \cap A \neq \emptyset$. Hence $\omega_1 \in \overline{A}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The order topology has subbasis $\{(-\infty, \alpha) : \alpha\} \cup \{(\alpha, +\infty) : \alpha\}$. In $\omega_1 + 1$, the maximum is $\omega_1$, so the relevant subbasis elements containing $\omega_1$ are the rays $(\alpha, +\infty) = (\alpha, \omega_1]$ for $\alpha < \omega_1$. A finite intersection of such rays is again a ray of the form $(\max \alpha_i, \omega_1]$.
>
> Now $\alpha < \omega_1$ means $\alpha$ is a countable ordinal. Its successor $\alpha + 1$ is also countable, hence $\alpha + 1 < \omega_1$, hence $\alpha + 1 \in A$. And $\alpha < \alpha + 1 \leq \omega_1$, so $\alpha + 1 \in (\alpha, \omega_1]$, hence $\alpha + 1 \in U \cap A$.
>
> So $U \cap A \neq \emptyset$ for every open neighbourhood $U$ of $\omega_1$, giving $\omega_1 \in \overline{A}$.

**Step 2: Part (b) — no sequence in $A$ converges to $\omega_1$.**

Let $\{\alpha_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ be any sequence in $A$ — that is, each $\alpha_n$ is a countable ordinal. Set $\sigma = \sup_n \alpha_n$. We claim $\sigma$ is a countable ordinal, hence $\sigma < \omega_1$. Then $(\sigma, \omega_1]$ is an open neighbourhood of $\omega_1$ that contains no $\alpha_n$, so the sequence is not eventually in this neighbourhood, hence does not converge to $\omega_1$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Each $\alpha_n$ is a countable ordinal, so as a set, $\alpha_n = \{\beta : \beta < \alpha_n\}$ has cardinality $\leq \aleph_0$. The supremum $\sigma$ of a set of ordinals is their union: $\sigma = \bigcup_n \alpha_n$. (Viewed as ordinals: $\sigma$ is the least ordinal $\geq$ all the $\alpha_n$, which is exactly the union.)
>
> $\sigma = \bigcup_n \alpha_n$ is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable (using countable choice — a mild form of the axiom of choice, often implicit). So $\sigma$ is a countable ordinal, hence $\sigma < \omega_1$.
>
> Now $(\sigma, \omega_1] = \{\beta : \sigma < \beta \leq \omega_1\}$ is an open neighbourhood of $\omega_1$ in the order topology (since $\sigma < \omega_1$ means $\sigma$ is in the space, and the interval is one of the basic open neighbourhoods at $\omega_1$). For any $n$, $\alpha_n \leq \sigma$ by definition of $\sigma = \sup$, so $\alpha_n \notin (\sigma, \omega_1]$. Hence the sequence is *never* in $(\sigma, \omega_1]$, in particular not eventually in it. So $\alpha_n \not\to \omega_1$.

**Step 3: Part (c) — $\omega_1 + 1$ is not first-countable at $\omega_1$.**

Suppose for contradiction $\omega_1$ has a countable neighbourhood basis $\{U_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$. Then we can build a sequence in $A$ converging to $\omega_1$, contradicting (b).

> [!note]- Derivation
> Suppose $\{U_n\}$ is a countable neighbourhood basis at $\omega_1$. We can assume each $U_n$ is of the form $(\beta_n, \omega_1]$ for some $\beta_n < \omega_1$ (each $U_n$ is open and contains $\omega_1$, hence contains some basic open of this form, and replacing $U_n$ by this basic open we still have a basis).
>
> Choose $\alpha_n = \beta_n + 1 \in A$ for each $n$. Then $\alpha_n \in (\beta_n, \omega_1] = U_n$, so the sequence $\{\alpha_n\}$ has $\alpha_n \in U_n$ for each $n$.
>
> *Claim:* this sequence converges to $\omega_1$. For any open $U \ni \omega_1$, $\{U_n\}$ is a basis, so $U_N \subseteq U$ for some $N$. The same argument applies for the cofinality of the basis: actually let us refine. We have $\{U_n\}$ a basis means *every* open $U \ni \omega_1$ contains some $U_N$. But that does not directly give $\alpha_n \in U$ for $n \geq N$ — only that $\alpha_N \in U_N \subseteq U$. To get the full convergence we need monotonicity.
>
> *Refinement.* Replace $\{U_n\}$ by a *nested* basis: set $V_n = U_1 \cap U_2 \cap \cdots \cap U_n$. Each $V_n$ is open (finite intersection of opens), contains $\omega_1$, and is nested decreasing: $V_1 \supseteq V_2 \supseteq \cdots$. The collection $\{V_n\}$ is still a neighbourhood basis: for any open $U \ni \omega_1$, some $U_N \subseteq U$, hence $V_N \subseteq U_N \subseteq U$.
>
> Now define $\alpha_n \in V_n \cap A$ for each $n$ (such $\alpha_n$ exists by Step (a) — $V_n$ is an open neighbourhood of $\omega_1$, hence meets $A$). The sequence $\{\alpha_n\}$ has the property: for any open $U \ni \omega_1$, take $N$ with $V_N \subseteq U$; then for $n \geq N$, $\alpha_n \in V_n \subseteq V_N \subseteq U$. So $\alpha_n \to \omega_1$.
>
> But by (b), no sequence in $A$ converges to $\omega_1$ — contradiction. Hence no countable neighbourhood basis at $\omega_1$ exists.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **(a)** A basic open neighbourhood of $\omega_1$ in the order topology of $\omega_1 + 1$ has the form $(\alpha, \omega_1]$ for some $\alpha < \omega_1$. The ordinal $\alpha + 1$ is countable, hence in $A$, and lies in $(\alpha, \omega_1]$. So every open neighbourhood of $\omega_1$ meets $A$, giving $\omega_1 \in \overline{A}$.
>
> **(b)** Let $\{\alpha_n\}$ be a sequence in $A$. Each $\alpha_n < \omega_1$ is a countable ordinal, hence has at most $\aleph_0$ elements as a set. The supremum $\sigma = \sup_n \alpha_n = \bigcup_n \alpha_n$ is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable (by countable choice), hence $\sigma < \omega_1$. The neighbourhood $(\sigma, \omega_1]$ of $\omega_1$ contains no $\alpha_n$ (each $\alpha_n \leq \sigma$). So $\alpha_n \not\to \omega_1$.
>
> **(c)** If $\omega_1$ had a countable neighbourhood basis $\{U_n\}$, take a nested refinement $V_n = U_1 \cap \cdots \cap U_n$; pick $\alpha_n \in V_n \cap A$ (exists by (a)). Then $\alpha_n \to \omega_1$ (every open $U \ni \omega_1$ contains some $V_N$, and for $n \geq N$, $\alpha_n \in V_n \subseteq V_N \subseteq U$). This contradicts (b). Hence $\omega_1 + 1$ is not first-countable at $\omega_1$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**$\omega_1$ has uncountable cofinality, which is the structural reason sequences cannot reach it from below; this is the canonical mechanism for failure of first-countability.** The cofinality $\operatorname{cf}(\omega_1) = \omega_1$ (uncountable) means: no countable subset of $\omega_1$ is unbounded in $\omega_1$. Equivalently, every countable subset of $\omega_1$ has its supremum strictly less than $\omega_1$. Since sequences are exactly countable subsets, they cannot approach $\omega_1$ from $A = [0, \omega_1)$. The trigger for the analogous phenomenon: any time you have a space with a point of uncountable cofinality (no countable basis), sequences are insufficient and nets are needed to detect convergence to that point.

**The closure-via-nets theorem ([[Thm - Closure via Nets]]) is exactly what is needed for spaces like $\omega_1 + 1$ — sequences fail, nets succeed.** Build the canonical net: directed set $D = $ open neighbourhoods of $\omega_1$ ordered by reverse inclusion, $\Phi(U) = $ any point of $U \cap A$. This net converges to $\omega_1$ even though no sequence does. The fact that this construction works precisely because we can index by an *uncountable* directed set is the reason nets exist. The take-home: any time you find yourself in a space where the closure-versus-sequential-closure distinction matters, the routing through nets is the route through which classical theorems generalize.

**First-countable spaces have sequences sufficient for closure and continuity; non-first-countable spaces require nets.** This is the cleanest statement of when nets are needed. Metric spaces are first-countable (basis: $\{B_{1/n}(x)\}$), so sequences suffice in metric spaces — this is why "metric topology = sequential topology". Spaces that are *not* first-countable include: $\omega_1 + 1$ (this exercise), Stone-Čech compactifications, product spaces over uncountable index sets, weak topologies on infinite-dimensional Banach spaces. In each of these, sequences see only the "countable shadow" of the topology, missing the genuine structure.

**Order topologies on ordinals are an under-appreciated source of pathologies: they are compact, Hausdorff, totally ordered, but not first-countable at limit ordinals of uncountable cofinality.** The space $\omega_1 + 1$ is in fact compact and Hausdorff (compact: it is a closed bounded subset of an order topology on a well-ordered set; Hausdorff: order topologies are always Hausdorff). It is normal (compact Hausdorff implies normal). It is *not* first-countable, *not* sequentially compact in the naive sense (sequences in $[0, \omega_1)$ don't have convergent subsequences to $\omega_1$ — though they do converge to ordinals $< \omega_1$). Knowing these pathologies is essential for testing intuition built in metric spaces. The trigger: whenever a topological claim seems "obviously true" because it is true in metric spaces, test it on $\omega_1 + 1$ before believing it.

**The technique "sup of a countable set lives in $\omega_1$" recurs throughout descriptive set theory and large cardinal theory.** This is a special case of cofinality arguments: the cofinality $\operatorname{cf}(\kappa)$ of a cardinal $\kappa$ is the least size of a cofinal subset, and for *regular* cardinals like $\omega_1$, $\operatorname{cf}(\omega_1) = \omega_1$, so smaller (countable) subsets cannot approach $\omega_1$. The same pattern shows up in: closed unbounded sets in $\omega_1$ (CUB filter), Fodor's lemma, stationary sets, and the structure of countable ordinals. Mastery of this single technique is the entry point to set-theoretic topology.
