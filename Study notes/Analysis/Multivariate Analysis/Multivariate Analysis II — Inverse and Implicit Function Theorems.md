# Notation Registry

- $U, V, W \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ — open sets; $\Omega$ — an open neighbourhood of a base point
- $x, p, p_0, x_0$ — points of $\mathbb{R}^n$; $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ in coordinates
- $B_r(x)$ — the open ball of radius $r$ centred at $x$ in the Euclidean norm
- $|x|$ — the Euclidean norm of $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$ (the topic uses $|\cdot|$ for $\lVert\cdot\rVert$)
- $f, F, g, G$ — maps between Euclidean spaces; $f : U \to \mathbb{R}^m$
- $Df_x$ or $DF(x)$ — the total derivative of $f$ at $x$, a linear map $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$
- $Jf(x)$ — the Jacobian matrix of $f$ at $x$, the matrix of $Df_x$ in standard coordinates
- $\partial_i f$ — the $i$-th partial derivative $\partial f / \partial x_i$
- $\nabla f(x)$ — the gradient of a scalar $f$, the column vector $(\partial_1 f, \dots, \partial_n f)^T = Jf(x)^T$
- $Hf(x)$ or $D^2 f(x)$ — the Hessian matrix, $(Hf)_{ij} = \partial_i \partial_j f$
- $\Delta f$ — the Laplacian, $\Delta f = \operatorname{tr} Hf = \sum_i \partial_{ii} f$
- $C^k(U, \mathbb{R}^m)$ — maps $U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with continuous partial derivatives up to order $k$
- $f|_M$ — the restriction of $f$ to a subset $M$
- $M$ — a constraint set, a level set, or a submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^n$
- $T_p M$ — the tangent space to a submanifold $M$ at a point $p$
- $\lambda, \lambda_0, \dots, \lambda_k$ — Lagrange multipliers
- $L(x, \lambda)$ — the Lagrangian function
- $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ — the group of invertible $n \times n$ real matrices
- $O(n)$ — the orthogonal group, $\{A : A^T A = I\}$
- $\operatorname{Id}$ or $I$ — the identity map or the identity matrix
- $\delta_{ij}$ — the Kronecker delta
- $\langle x, y \rangle = x^T y$ — the standard inner product on $\mathbb{R}^n$
- $o(\cdot)$ — Landau little-oh: $r(h) = o(|h|)$ means $r(h)/|h| \to 0$ as $h \to 0$
- "regular value", "regular point" — a value (point) at which the derivative has maximal rank

# Motivation

Single-variable calculus has two great theorems past the basic differentiation rules. One says that if $f'(x_0) \neq 0$ then $f$ is locally invertible near $x_0$, with $(f^{-1})'(y_0) = 1/f'(x_0)$. The other says that the curve $f(x,y) = c$ can be solved for $y$ as a function of $x$ wherever $\partial_y f \neq 0$. In one variable these facts feel almost too obvious to dignify with a name — the first is the monotonicity of a function with nonvanishing derivative, the second is just "the curve is not vertical". In several variables they become deep, and they become the entire structural backbone of the subject. This topic is built around them.

The starting question is **optimization**. You have a function $f : U \to \mathbb{R}$ and you want its largest and smallest values. Weierstrass guarantees a continuous function on a compact set attains its extrema, but says nothing about *where*. In one variable you learned to check where $f' = 0$. The multivariable version, in §2.1, replaces $f' = 0$ with $\nabla f = 0$ — the [[Thm - First-Order Optimality Condition|first-order condition]] — and replaces the second-derivative test with the [[Thm - Second-Order Optimality Conditions|sign of the Hessian]]. But the genuinely new phenomenon is **constraints**. Most real optimization is not over an open set; it is over a curve, a surface, a sphere — a set $M$ cut out by equations $g_1 = \dots = g_k = 0$. On such a set $\nabla f = 0$ is the wrong condition entirely, because $f$ restricted to $M$ can be changing fast even where $\nabla f$ is large, as long as $\nabla f$ points *off* the constraint set. The right condition is the [[Thm - The Method of Lagrange Multipliers|method of Lagrange multipliers]]: at a constrained extremum, $\nabla f$ must be a linear combination of the constraint gradients $\nabla g_j$. This is one of the most-used theorems in all of applied mathematics, and §2.1 derives it, applies it, and — strikingly — uses it to prove the spectral theorem.

The second half of the topic answers the question that Lagrange multipliers quietly raise: *what kind of object is the constraint set $M$, and why does it have "tangent directions" along which $\nabla f$ must vanish?* The answer is the **inverse and implicit function theorems** of §2.2, which are really one theorem stated twice. The [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]] says: if $Df_{x_0}$ is an invertible linear map, then $f$ itself is invertible near $x_0$ — the linear approximation's invertibility propagates to the nonlinear map, *locally*. The word "locally" is not a technicality to be apologized for; it is the heart of the matter, and a recurring theme of this topic is being precise about exactly when local conclusions can and cannot be made global. The [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] is the same statement rearranged: if part of the derivative is invertible, then the equation $f(x,y) = 0$ can be solved, *locally*, for $y$ as a smooth function of $x$. Both rest on a single elementary engine, the [[Thm - The Contraction Mapping Principle|contraction mapping principle]] — solving an equation by iterating a map that shrinks distances.

Section §2.3 then collects the payoff. The implicit function theorem says a level set $\{g = 0\}$, near any point where $Dg$ has maximal rank, is the graph of a smooth function — and a graph is the simplest possible curved object. Assembling these local graph pictures over the whole level set gives the notion of a **submanifold** of $\mathbb{R}^n$: a subset that looks, near each of its points, like a flat $\mathbb{R}^d$ slightly bent. The [[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem|regular value theorem]] is the clean criterion — preimages of regular values are submanifolds — and it is the device that turns "the sphere", "the orthogonal group", "the set of matrices of fixed determinant" from sets defined by equations into genuine geometric spaces with dimensions and tangent spaces. This is the doorway to **differential geometry**: a manifold is defined exactly by abstracting the local-graph property away from any ambient $\mathbb{R}^n$. The constraint set on which Lagrange multipliers operate, the level sets of conserved quantities in **dynamical systems**, the configuration spaces of mechanics, the spacetime manifold of **special relativity** — all of them are submanifolds, and §2.3 is where that word acquires meaning.

# Concept Map

## §2.1 Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers

- **[[Def - Critical Point, Hessian, and Definiteness]]**
	- A **critical point** of $f \in C^1(U)$ is a point $x_0$ where $\nabla f(x_0) = 0$, equivalently $Df_{x_0} = 0$. The **Hessian** $Hf(x_0)$ of a $C^2$ function is the symmetric matrix of second partials $\partial_i\partial_j f$; symmetry is Schwarz's theorem. A symmetric matrix is **positive definite** if all eigenvalues are positive, **negative definite** if all negative, **indefinite** if it has both signs, and **degenerate** if $0$ is an eigenvalue. These four sign classes of the Hessian classify the behaviour of $f$ near a critical point, exactly as the sign of $f''$ does in one variable.

- **[[Thm - First-Order Optimality Condition]]**
	- If $f \in C^1(U)$ has a local extremum at an interior point $x_0$ of its domain, then $\nabla f(x_0) = 0$. The proof restricts $f$ to each coordinate line through $x_0$, reducing to the one-variable fact that a differentiable function has vanishing derivative at an interior extremum. The condition is **necessary but not sufficient** — saddle points satisfy it too — and it requires $x_0$ to be *interior*: on a domain with boundary the extremum may sit on the boundary with $\nabla f \neq 0$.

- **[[Thm - Second-Order Optimality Conditions]]**
	- At a critical point $x_0$ of $f \in C^3(U)$, the sign type of the Hessian decides the local behaviour: positive definite gives a strict local minimum, negative definite a strict local maximum, indefinite-and-nondegenerate a saddle point. The proof is the second-order Taylor expansion $f(x_0+h) - f(x_0) = \tfrac12 h^T Hf(x_0) h + o(|h|^2)$ together with the spectral bound $h^T H h \geq \lambda_{\min}|h|^2$. When the Hessian is **degenerate** the test is inconclusive — $f(x,y) = ax^4 + by^4$ shows all outcomes occur with the same (zero) Hessian.

- **[[Thm - The Method of Lagrange Multipliers]]**
	- If $f, g_1, \dots, g_k \in C^1$ and $f$ restricted to the constraint set $M = \{g_1 = \dots = g_k = 0\}$ has a local extremum at $x_0$, then $\nabla f(x_0), \nabla g_1(x_0), \dots, \nabla g_k(x_0)$ are linearly dependent: there exist $\lambda_0, \dots, \lambda_k$, not all zero, with $\lambda_0 \nabla f + \sum \lambda_j \nabla g_j = 0$. When the constraint gradients are themselves independent (the **regular** case) one may take $\lambda_0 = 1$ and write $\nabla f = \sum \lambda_j \nabla g_j$. Geometrically, $\nabla f$ must be normal to the constraint set; the multipliers measure the constraints' "shadow prices".

- **[[Ex - Classifying critical points with the Hessian]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For $f(x,y) = x^3 - y^3 + 3\alpha xy$, find every critical point as a function of the parameter $\alpha$ and classify each via the Hessian, handling the degenerate case by hand.

- **[[Ex - Constrained optimization via Lagrange multipliers]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Find the extrema of a linear or simple function on a compact constraint set, combining interior critical points, boundary-of-constraint points, and the Lagrange candidates into one complete list.

- **[[Ex - The spectral theorem via constrained optimization]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Prove that every real symmetric matrix has an orthonormal basis of real eigenvectors by repeatedly extremising the quadratic form $\langle x, Ax\rangle$ on the unit sphere intersected with the orthogonal complement of previously found eigenvectors.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Morse Theory *(from Differential Topology)*
> A critical point whose Hessian is nondegenerate is called a **nondegenerate** or **Morse** critical point, and the number of negative Hessian eigenvalues is its **index**. **Morse theory** shows that the topology of a manifold — how many holes of each dimension it has — is encoded in the indices of the critical points of any sufficiently generic function on it. The Hessian sign classification of §2.1 is the local input to that global theory.

## §2.2 The Inverse and Implicit Function Theorems

- **[[Thm - The Contraction Mapping Principle]]**
	- If $X$ is a complete metric space and $T : X \to X$ satisfies $d(Tx, Ty) \leq r\, d(x,y)$ for a fixed $r < 1$, then $T$ has a unique fixed point, and the iterates $T^k y_0$ converge to it from any starting point $y_0$. The proof shows the iterates form a Cauchy sequence by summing a geometric series. This is the universal device for **solving equations**: rewrite the equation as a fixed-point problem $Tx = x$, check the contraction estimate, and the solution exists, is unique, and is computable by iteration.

- **[[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem]]**
	- Let $f \in C^k(U, \mathbb{R}^n)$, $k \geq 1$, and suppose $Df_{x_0}$ is an invertible linear map. Then there is an open neighbourhood $U_0$ of $x_0$ on which $f$ is injective, $f(U_0)$ is open, and the inverse $f^{-1} : f(U_0) \to U_0$ is itself $C^k$, with $D(f^{-1})_{f(x)} = (Df_x)^{-1}$. An invertible linear approximation forces local invertibility of the nonlinear map. The conclusion is **strictly local** — global injectivity is false in general ($f(x,y) = (e^x\cos y, e^x\sin y)$ has invertible derivative everywhere yet is not injective).

- **[[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem]]**
	- Let $f \in C^k$ near $(x_0, y_0) \in \mathbb{R}^d \times \mathbb{R}^{n-d}$ with $f(x_0,y_0) = 0$, and suppose the partial derivative $D_y f(x_0,y_0)$ in the $y$-variables is invertible. Then near $(x_0,y_0)$ the equation $f(x,y) = 0$ is equivalent to $y = g(x)$ for a unique $C^k$ function $g$, with $Dg(x) = -(D_y f)^{-1}(D_x f)$. Equations with an invertible "partial Jacobian" can be solved locally for the corresponding variables. It is the inverse function theorem applied to the auxiliary map $(x,y) \mapsto (x, f(x,y))$.

- **[[Ex - Local invertibility of a nonlinear map]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Verify that polar coordinates $(r,\theta) \mapsto (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ have invertible Jacobian away from $r = 0$, conclude local invertibility, and identify exactly where global invertibility fails and why.

- **[[Ex - Solving an implicit equation locally]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Given a system of equations in several unknowns, identify a base solution, compute the partial Jacobian, verify its invertibility, and conclude that some variables are smooth functions of the others — then compute the derivative of the implicit solution.

- **[[Ex - Failure of inversion at a singular Jacobian]]** (⭐)
	- Exhibit maps with non-invertible derivative at a point that are genuinely non-invertible there ($x \mapsto x^3$, the squaring map on $2\times 2$ matrices) and diagnose precisely which hypothesis of the inverse function theorem fails.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Rank Theorem and Immersions/Submersions *(from Differential Geometry)*
> The inverse function theorem is the constant-rank case $\operatorname{rank} = n$. Allowing the derivative to have constant rank $r < n$ gives the **rank theorem**: such a map looks, in suitable local coordinates, like the linear projection $(x_1,\dots,x_n) \mapsto (x_1,\dots,x_r,0,\dots,0)$. The special cases — derivative injective (**immersion**) and derivative surjective (**submersion**) — are the two ways one manifold sits inside or maps onto another.

## §2.3 Submanifolds of Euclidean Space

- **[[Def - Submanifold of Euclidean Space]]**
	- A nonempty $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a **$d$-dimensional $C^k$ submanifold** if near every point it can be straightened: there is a local $C^k$ diffeomorphism $\Psi$ carrying $M$ onto a piece of the coordinate subspace $\{y_{d+1} = \dots = y_n = 0\}$. Four equivalent local descriptions exist — the **submanifold chart** (straightening), the **implicit** description ($M$ is locally $\{f = 0\}$ with $Df$ of maximal rank), the **parametric** description ($M$ is locally the image of an immersion), and the **graphical** description ($M$ is locally the graph of a function). Each is the natural form for a different task.

- **[[Def - The Tangent Space to a Submanifold]]**
	- The **tangent space** $T_p M$ to a $d$-dimensional submanifold at a point $p$ is the set of all velocity vectors $\gamma'(0)$ of $C^1$ curves $\gamma$ in $M$ through $p$ — equivalently, all limits of secant directions $(p_k - p)/r_k$ with $p_k \to p$ in $M$. It is a $d$-dimensional linear subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$: for a parametrization $G$ it is the image $\operatorname{im} DG$, and for an implicit description $\{f = 0\}$ it is the kernel $\ker Df_p$. The affine subspace $p + T_p M$ is the best flat approximation to $M$ at $p$.

- **[[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem]]**
	- If $f \in C^k(U, \mathbb{R}^{n-d})$ and $c$ is a **regular value** — meaning $Df_p$ is surjective at every $p$ with $f(p) = c$ — then the level set $f^{-1}(c)$ is a $d$-dimensional $C^k$ submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^n$, with tangent space $T_p f^{-1}(c) = \ker Df_p$. This converts the verification of "is this set a manifold?" into a rank computation. It is the standard manufacturing device for submanifolds: spheres, tori, the orthogonal group, special linear groups are all regular level sets.

- **[[Ex - The sphere as a regular level set]]** (⭐)
	- Show that the unit sphere $S^{n-1} = \{|x|^2 = 1\}$ is a smooth $(n-1)$-dimensional submanifold by checking that $1$ is a regular value of $x \mapsto |x|^2$, and identify its tangent spaces.

- **[[Ex - The orthogonal group as a submanifold]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Prove that $O(n) = \{A : A^T A = I\}$ is a submanifold of the space of matrices by showing $I$ is a regular value of $A \mapsto A^T A$ into the symmetric matrices, compute its dimension $n(n-1)/2$, and identify its tangent space at the identity as the skew-symmetric matrices.

- **[[Ex - Computing a tangent space]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For a given submanifold described implicitly, parametrically, or as a graph, compute the tangent space at a specified point by all three routes — kernel of the derivative, image of the parametrization derivative, and graph of the linearization — and verify they agree.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Abstract Smooth Manifolds *(from Differential Geometry)*
> An **abstract smooth manifold** is defined by abstracting the submanifold-chart property away from any ambient $\mathbb{R}^n$: a topological space with an atlas of charts whose transition maps are smooth. Whitney's embedding theorem shows every abstract manifold is in fact diffeomorphic to a submanifold of some $\mathbb{R}^N$, so the two notions describe the same objects — but the abstract definition is what frees geometry from a chosen embedding.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Lie Groups and Lie Algebras *(from Lie Theory)*
> A **Lie group** is a group that is also a submanifold, with smooth multiplication and inversion — $O(n)$, $\operatorname{SL}_n(\mathbb{R})$, $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ are the basic examples. Its tangent space at the identity, with the commutator bracket, is its **Lie algebra**. The computation that $T_I O(n)$ is the skew-symmetric matrices is the first instance of the Lie group / Lie algebra correspondence. See [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]] for the group axioms and [[Def - Group Action]] for how such groups act on spaces.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of this topic chase a small, recognizable set of goals. The first is **locating and classifying extrema** — find every point where $f$ could be largest or smallest, and decide for each whether it is a maximum, a minimum, or a saddle. This splits into the unconstrained case (critical points, then the Hessian) and the constrained case (Lagrange candidates, then comparison by hand), and a complete solution always produces a *finite candidate list* and then evaluates $f$ on it. The second target is **local solvability**: showing that an equation, or a system, can be solved for some of its variables in terms of the others near a known solution, and computing the derivative of that implicit solution. The third is **local invertibility**: showing a map is a local diffeomorphism, and — just as importantly — diagnosing where and why it fails to be one globally. The fourth is **establishing manifold structure**: proving that a set defined by equations is genuinely a submanifold, computing its dimension, and computing its tangent space at a point. These four — extremise, solve, invert, recognize-as-manifold — recur because each is a way of replacing a nonlinear object by a linear one you control: the Hessian linearizes the second-order behaviour of $f$, the implicit function theorem linearizes a constraint into a graph, the inverse function theorem linearizes a map into its derivative, and the tangent space linearizes a manifold into a subspace.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped, and the entire skill of the topic is recognizing which one you have. **A function is $C^1$ (or $C^2$, or smooth) on an open set** — this is the admission ticket: every theorem here needs continuous derivatives, and the *order* of differentiability you are handed is exactly the order you get back (the inverse and implicit functions inherit the smoothness class of $f$). **A derivative is invertible at a point** — this single algebraic fact, the nonvanishing of a Jacobian determinant, is what unlocks the inverse function theorem; it routes immediately to local invertibility. **A partial Jacobian is invertible** — the same fact restricted to a block, routing to the implicit function theorem and a local solution $y = g(x)$. **A derivative has maximal rank** — surjectivity of $Df$, the hypothesis of the regular value theorem, routing to "this level set is a manifold". **Constraint gradients are linearly independent** — the regularity hypothesis that lets Lagrange's theorem be used in its clean form $\nabla f = \sum\lambda_j\nabla g_j$. **A set is compact** — Weierstrass then guarantees extrema *exist*, so the candidate list produced by the other tools is guaranteed to contain the answer. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: an invertible Jacobian routes through the inverse function theorem to local invertibility; an invertible partial Jacobian routes through the implicit function theorem to local solvability; a surjective derivative routes through the regular value theorem to manifold structure; and compactness routes through Weierstrass to guarantee the search terminates.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves almost every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything here is self-contained — a reader with no background should be able to follow each operation from its description.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Set $\nabla f = 0$ to find interior extrema.** On an open set, a $C^1$ function can only have a local extremum where every partial derivative vanishes (the [[Thm - First-Order Optimality Condition|first-order condition]]). The operation is: write down the system $\partial_1 f = \dots = \partial_n f = 0$ and solve it. This produces the *candidate list* for interior extrema. The trigger is any request to maximise or minimise a function over an open region, or over the interior of a region. Remember the candidates are only *candidates* — the condition is necessary, not sufficient.

2. **Read the Hessian's sign to classify a critical point.** Once $x_0$ is a critical point, compute the Hessian matrix $Hf(x_0)$ of second partials and determine its sign type via its eigenvalues (or, in $2\times 2$, via the determinant and the top-left entry). Positive definite means strict local minimum, negative definite means strict local maximum, indefinite means saddle (the [[Thm - Second-Order Optimality Conditions|second-order conditions]]). The trigger is "you have a critical point and must decide what kind it is". If the Hessian is degenerate, this operation fails and you must look at higher-order terms by hand.

3. **Set up the Lagrange equations on a constraint set.** To extremise $f$ subject to $g_1 = \dots = g_k = 0$, do not set $\nabla f = 0$; instead solve the augmented system $\nabla f = \sum_j \lambda_j \nabla g_j$ together with the constraints $g_j = 0$, treating the multipliers $\lambda_j$ as extra unknowns (the [[Thm - The Method of Lagrange Multipliers|method of Lagrange multipliers]]). The trigger is the literal presence of equality constraints. Equivalently, form the [[Thm - The Method of Lagrange Multipliers|Lagrangian]] $L(x,\lambda) = f - \sum\lambda_j g_j$ and set *all* its partials, in $x$ and in $\lambda$, to zero.

4. **Check a Jacobian determinant to claim local invertibility.** To show a map $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is locally invertible near $x_0$, compute $\det Jf(x_0)$; if it is nonzero, the [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]] gives a local $C^k$ inverse for free. The trigger is "is this map a change of coordinates near here?". The operation costs one determinant computation. Crucially it only ever yields a *local* statement.

5. **Split the variables and check a partial Jacobian to solve an equation.** Given $f(x,y) = 0$ with a known solution $(x_0,y_0)$, to solve for $y$ in terms of $x$, single out the $y$-variables, form the partial Jacobian $D_y f$, and check it is invertible at $(x_0,y_0)$; the [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] then produces $y = g(x)$. The trigger is any equation or system you want to "solve for some variables". The choice of *which* variables to solve for is yours — pick a block whose partial Jacobian is invertible.

6. **Differentiate the defining identity to compute the derivative of an implicit function.** Once $y = g(x)$ solves $f(x,g(x)) = 0$, you rarely need a formula for $g$ — you need $Dg$. Differentiate the identity $f(x,g(x)) = 0$ with the chain rule: $D_x f + D_y f \cdot Dg = 0$, hence $Dg = -(D_y f)^{-1} D_x f$. The trigger is "find the derivative / tangent / rate of change of the implicitly defined quantity". This works without ever knowing $g$ explicitly.

7. **Check that a value is regular, then declare the level set a manifold.** To prove a set $M = \{f = c\}$ is a submanifold, verify that $Df_p$ is surjective (has maximal rank) at every $p \in M$; the [[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem|regular value theorem]] then certifies $M$ is a $C^k$ submanifold of dimension $n - (n-d)$. The trigger is a set presented as a level set or solution set of equations. The operation reduces a geometric question to a rank computation done pointwise on $M$.

8. **Compute a tangent space as a kernel or an image.** For $M = \{f = c\}$ given implicitly, $T_p M = \ker Df_p$ — the directions in which the constraints do not change to first order. For $M$ given by a parametrization $G$, $T_p M = \operatorname{im} DG$ — the directions you can move by varying the parameters. The trigger is any request for tangent vectors, normal vectors, or the tangent plane. Both descriptions give the same $d$-dimensional subspace; use whichever the problem hands you.

9. **Rewrite an equation as a fixed-point problem and contract.** To solve $\Phi(x) = 0$ or $\Phi(x) = y$, algebraically rearrange it into the form $Tx = x$, then show $T$ shrinks distances by a factor $r < 1$ on a complete space; the [[Thm - The Contraction Mapping Principle|contraction mapping principle]] delivers existence, uniqueness, and an iteration scheme. The trigger is an equation with no closed-form solution, especially "solve $F(x) = y$ for $x$" — the inverse function theorem itself is this operation applied to $x \mapsto y - \varphi(x)$.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Promoting a local inverse to a global one
> The [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]] gives an inverse on *some* neighbourhood $U_0$ of $x_0$, and it is tempting to conclude $f$ is invertible on all of its domain when $Df$ is invertible *everywhere*. This is false. The map $f(x,y) = (e^x\cos y, e^x\sin y)$ has $\det Jf = e^{2x} \neq 0$ at every point, so it is a local diffeomorphism everywhere — yet $f(x, y+2\pi) = f(x,y)$, so it is wildly non-injective. Invertibility of every $Df_x$ guarantees only that $f$ is a *local* diffeomorphism; globalizing it needs a genuine extra hypothesis, such as $f$ being proper, or the symmetric part of $Df$ being positive definite on a convex domain (this last is exactly the criterion that *does* force global injectivity — see [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem]]).

> [!warning] 2. Using $\nabla f = 0$ to find extrema on a constraint set
> When $f$ is being extremised over a curve or surface $M = \{g = 0\}$, it is tempting to look where $\nabla f = 0$, as for an open domain. This finds the wrong points. On the circle $x^2+y^2 = 1$ the function $f(x,y) = x$ attains its extrema at $(\pm 1, 0)$, where $\nabla f = (1,0)$ is *not* zero — it is nonzero everywhere. The constrained extremum is governed not by $\nabla f = 0$ but by $\nabla f$ being *parallel to $\nabla g$* (the [[Thm - The Method of Lagrange Multipliers|Lagrange condition]]): only the component of $\nabla f$ *tangent to $M$* must vanish, and $\nabla f$ may be large as long as it points purely off the surface.

> [!warning] 3. Trusting the Hessian test when the Hessian is degenerate
> The [[Thm - Second-Order Optimality Conditions|second-order test]] is tempting to apply mechanically, but it says *nothing* when $0$ is an eigenvalue of $Hf(x_0)$. The functions $f(x,y) = x^4 + y^4$, $-x^4-y^4$, and $x^4 - y^4$ all have the *same* Hessian at the origin — the zero matrix — yet the origin is respectively a strict minimum, a strict maximum, and a saddle. A degenerate Hessian means the quadratic Taylor term vanishes in some direction and the answer is decided by third- or fourth-order terms, which must be examined directly.

> [!warning] 4. Calling every level set a submanifold
> It is tempting to think $\{f = c\}$ is always a smooth submanifold. It is not — the conclusion needs $c$ to be a *regular* value. For $f(x,y) = x^2 - y^2$ the level set $f^{-1}(0)$ is the union of the two lines $y = \pm x$, which crosses itself at the origin and is *not* a manifold there; the trouble is exactly that $\nabla f(0,0) = 0$, so $0$ is not a regular value. For $f(x,y,z) = x^2+y^2-z^2$ the set $f^{-1}(0)$ is a cone with a singular vertex. The [[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem|regular value theorem]] only applies where $Df$ has maximal rank; at critical points of $f$ the level set may pinch, cross, or degenerate.

> [!warning] 5. Applying Lagrange multipliers at a non-regular constraint point
> The clean form $\nabla f = \sum\lambda_j\nabla g_j$ assumes the constraint gradients $\nabla g_j$ are linearly independent at the point. Where they are not, the method can *miss the actual extremum entirely*. On the constraint $g(x,y) = y^3 - x^2 = 0$ (a cusped curve), the point $(0,0)$ has $\nabla g(0,0) = 0$; the constrained minimum of $f(x,y) = 4y - 3x$ over the compact piece of this curve actually occurs at $(0,0)$, yet no choice of $\lambda$ satisfies $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ there since $\nabla f(0,0) \neq 0$. The general statement of [[Thm - The Method of Lagrange Multipliers|Lagrange's theorem]] only promises a *nontrivial* relation $\lambda_0\nabla f + \sum\lambda_j\nabla g_j = 0$ allowing $\lambda_0 = 0$; non-regular constraint points must always be checked separately, by hand.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this topic are won by first classifying which of the four problem types you face — optimization, local solvability, local invertibility, manifold structure — and then routing the given hypothesis to the matching theorem.

If the problem is **optimization** — maximise or minimise $f$ — the first question is always *where is the search happening*. If it is over an open set, the route is direct: solve $\nabla f = 0$ to get the [[Thm - First-Order Optimality Condition|critical points]], then classify each with the [[Thm - Second-Order Optimality Conditions|Hessian]]. If the search is over a *compact* set, the structure is richer and the candidate list has three parts that must *all* be assembled before comparing values. First, the interior critical points, found by $\nabla f = 0$. Second, the points lying on the boundary or on a constraint surface, found by [[Thm - The Method of Lagrange Multipliers|Lagrange multipliers]] applied with the boundary equations as constraints. Third — and this is the most-forgotten part — the non-regular points of the constraint set, the corners and cusps and self-intersections where the constraint gradients fail to be independent, which Lagrange's clean form *cannot see* and which must be checked individually. Compactness, via the Weierstrass theorem, guarantees the global extremum exists and therefore *is* one of the finitely many candidates you have listed; you then simply evaluate $f$ on the list and read off the largest and smallest. The reason this works is that you have replaced an optimization over infinitely many points with a comparison over finitely many — but only if the list is genuinely complete, which is why the non-regular points cannot be skipped.

If the problem is **local solvability** — "show the equation $F(x,y) = c$ can be solved for $y$" or "show $y$ is implicitly a function of $x$" — the route runs through the [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]]. The recipe is mechanical and the difficulty is concentrated in two choices. First, identify a known solution point $(x_0, y_0)$ — the theorem is purely local and needs a base point. Second, decide *which variables to solve for*: partition the variables into those you will express ($y$) and those that remain free ($x$), form the partial Jacobian $D_y F$ in the dependent variables, and check it is invertible at $(x_0,y_0)$. If it is, the theorem hands you a smooth $g$ with $y = g(x)$ near $x_0$. You will almost never want a formula for $g$; what you want is $Dg$, and that you get by differentiating the identity $F(x,g(x)) = c$ with the chain rule and solving the resulting linear system $D_xF + D_yF\cdot Dg = 0$. When the dependent block is one-dimensional ($\ell = 1$), the condition $D_y F$ invertible degenerates to the single scalar inequality $\partial_y F \neq 0$, and a permutation of variables shows that *as long as $\nabla F \neq 0$* you can always solve for *some* variable — which is precisely why nonvanishing gradient is the gateway to manifold structure.

If the problem is **local invertibility** — "is $f$ a local diffeomorphism near $x_0$" or "is this a valid change of coordinates" — the route is the [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]], and it costs exactly one determinant: compute $\det Jf(x_0)$, and if it is nonzero you are done, with a $C^k$ inverse of the same smoothness as $f$. The discipline here is to be relentlessly precise about the word *local*. The theorem never gives a global inverse, and a recurring exam trap is a map with everywhere-invertible derivative that is nevertheless globally non-injective; the correct response is to state the local conclusion and then *separately* investigate global behaviour, which requires extra information — periodicity, properness, monotonicity along lines, or positive-definiteness of the symmetric part of $Df$ on a convex set. When $\det Jf(x_0) = 0$ the theorem simply does not apply, and you should expect — and verify — that invertibility genuinely fails: at such a point $f$ typically folds, like $x \mapsto x^3$ at $0$ or the matrix squaring map at a non-identity square root of the identity.

If the problem is **manifold structure** — "show this set $M$ is a submanifold, find its dimension and tangent space" — the route is the [[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem|regular value theorem]]. Present $M$ as a level set $\{f = c\}$, then verify that $c$ is a *regular* value by checking $Df_p$ is surjective at *every* point $p$ of $M$ — this is a pointwise rank computation, and it is the entire content of the proof. The dimension of $M$ is then $n$ minus the number of independent equations, and the tangent space at $p$ is $\ker Df_p$, the directions killed by the linearized constraints. The subtlety, and the thing to check before reaching for the theorem, is whether $c$ really is regular: if $Df$ drops rank somewhere on $M$, the set may have a singular point — a crossing, a cusp, a cone vertex — and is not a manifold there. When the set is instead handed to you parametrically, by a map $G$, the tangent space is the *image* $\operatorname{im} DG$ rather than a kernel, and you must check $DG$ is injective (maximal rank $d$) for $G$ to genuinely parametrize a $d$-dimensional manifold.

A meta-strategy threads through all four types: **linearize, then transfer.** Every theorem in this topic has the same shape — a hypothesis about the *derivative* (invertible, surjective, maximal rank) yields a conclusion about the *map or set itself* (locally invertible, locally solvable, locally a manifold). The derivative is a linear object you can compute with finite linear algebra; the conclusion is a nonlinear local statement. So whenever you are stuck, compute the derivative, ask what linear-algebraic property it has, and let the matching theorem transfer that property from the linear approximation to the nonlinear object. The transfer is always *local* — that caveat is not a weakness of the theorems but a precise statement of what linearization can and cannot promise.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|Inverse Function Theorem]]**: invertible $Df_{x_0}$ implies $f$ is a local $C^k$ diffeomorphism near $x_0$. This is the structural workhorse of the whole topic, because every other theorem here is a corollary of it: the implicit function theorem is the inverse function theorem applied to $(x,y)\mapsto(x,f(x,y))$, and the regular value theorem and the equivalence of the four submanifold descriptions are the implicit function theorem applied locally. The recognizable setup is "I have a map and I want a local change of coordinates" or "I want to invert a transformation". Reach for it whenever a Jacobian determinant is computable and you only need a *local* conclusion; combine it with a smoothness hypothesis and the inverse comes back with the *same* smoothness for free.

- **[[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|Implicit Function Theorem]]**: an invertible partial Jacobian $D_y f$ lets you solve $f(x,y) = 0$ locally as $y = g(x)$, with $Dg = -(D_y f)^{-1}D_x f$. This is the workhorse for *eliminating variables* and for *turning constraints into graphs*. The typical use is to convert a set defined by equations into a parametrized object you can differentiate along, and the derivative formula is what makes Lagrange multipliers, the tangent-space computations, and comparative-statics arguments in economics all go through. Recognize its applicability whenever you see an equation you wish were a function: split the variables, find a block with invertible partial Jacobian, and the equation becomes a function of the rest.

- **[[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem|Regular Value Theorem]]**: the preimage $f^{-1}(c)$ of a regular value is a submanifold of dimension $n - (n-d)$, with $T_p f^{-1}(c) = \ker Df_p$. Its typical use is to *manufacture manifolds without effort* — instead of constructing charts by hand, you write the set as a level set and check one rank condition. It is the reason spheres, tori, matrix groups, and conserved-quantity level sets are all known to be manifolds the moment they are written down. It also delivers the tangent space as a kernel, which is the most computable description there is. The companion caution is built into the word *regular*: the theorem is silent at critical values.

- **[[Thm - The Contraction Mapping Principle|Contraction Mapping Principle]]**: a distance-shrinking self-map of a complete space has a unique fixed point, reached by iteration. This is the most reusable property of all because it is the deepest layer — it is the actual *engine* underneath the inverse function theorem, and it reappears far outside this topic. The typical use is to prove an equation has a solution when no formula exists: rewrite as a fixed point, verify a Lipschitz constant below $1$, and you get existence, uniqueness, and a convergent algorithm at once. It is what proves the local existence of solutions to differential equations (**Picard iteration**) and underlies Newton's method.

- **[[Thm - The Method of Lagrange Multipliers|Lagrange Multipliers]]**: at a constrained extremum, $\nabla f$ lies in the span of the constraint gradients. Its typical use is every constrained optimization problem in applied mathematics, physics, and economics — but its deeper reusable content is the geometric statement that $\nabla f$ must be *normal to the constraint manifold*, equivalently that the tangential component of $\nabla f$ vanishes. Once you see that the constraint set is a [[Def - Submanifold of Euclidean Space|submanifold]] with a [[Def - The Tangent Space to a Submanifold|tangent space]], Lagrange's theorem is just "$\nabla f \perp T_p M$", and this reframing is what generalizes it to optimization on abstract manifolds.

# Bridges

1. **Differential geometry — submanifolds are the concrete model of abstract manifolds.** The [[Def - Submanifold of Euclidean Space|submanifold]] of §2.3 is defined by a *local straightening* property: near each point a diffeomorphism flattens $M$ onto a coordinate plane. An **abstract smooth manifold** keeps exactly this local picture but discards the ambient $\mathbb{R}^n$: it is a topological space equipped with an atlas of charts to $\mathbb{R}^d$ whose transition maps are smooth. The bridge runs both ways. Every submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an abstract manifold (the submanifold charts *are* an atlas), and Whitney's embedding theorem says every abstract manifold is diffeomorphic to a submanifold of some $\mathbb{R}^N$ — so the two notions have the same content. The [[Def - The Tangent Space to a Submanifold|tangent space]], defined here as a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$ via curves, becomes in the abstract setting the space of derivations or of equivalence classes of curves — the same object built without reference to an embedding. The whole of differential geometry is this topic's §2.3 with the ambient space deleted.

2. **Dynamical systems — conserved quantities cut out invariant submanifolds, and contraction proves existence of trajectories.** Two bridges run to dynamical systems. First, the [[Thm - The Contraction Mapping Principle|contraction mapping principle]] is the proof engine of the Picard–Lindelöf theorem: an initial value problem $y' = F(t,y)$, $y(t_0) = y_0$ is recast as the integral equation $y(t) = y_0 + \int_{t_0}^t F(s,y(s))\,ds$, the right-hand side is a contraction on a space of continuous functions when $F$ is Lipschitz, and the unique fixed point is the unique local solution. Second, when a dynamical system has a conserved quantity $E$ — an energy, a Hamiltonian, an integral of motion — every trajectory is confined to a level set $\{E = c\}$, and by the [[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem|regular value theorem]] this level set is a submanifold wherever $c$ is regular. Phase portraits, energy surfaces, and the confinement arguments that give global existence of solutions are all submanifold statements.

3. **Special relativity — Minkowski spacetime and the mass shell are submanifolds, and the Lorentz group is a matrix manifold.** In **special relativity** the set of four-velocities of a particle of mass $m$ is the **mass shell** $\{p : \langle p, p\rangle = -m^2\}$, a level set of the Minkowski quadratic form; it is a submanifold by the regular value theorem, and it is the relativistic analogue of the sphere $\{|x|^2 = 1\}$ studied in [[Ex - The sphere as a regular level set]]. The Lorentz group, the symmetries of Minkowski space preserving the form $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, is defined by $\Lambda^T \eta \Lambda = \eta$ — *exactly* the equation $A^T A = I$ defining $O(n)$ in [[Ex - The orthogonal group as a submanifold]] with the Euclidean inner product replaced by the Minkowski one. The same regular-value computation that makes $O(n)$ a manifold of dimension $n(n-1)/2$ makes the Lorentz group a $6$-dimensional manifold, and its tangent space at the identity — the *generators* of Lorentz transformations, the boosts and rotations — is computed by the identical skew-symmetry argument.

4. **Convex optimization — the second-order test is the foundation of optimality conditions, and Lagrange multipliers become KKT.** A function is [[Def - Critical Point, Hessian, and Definiteness|convex]] exactly when its Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere, and for a convex function every critical point is a global minimum — the local-to-global gap that haunts general optimization simply closes. **Convex optimization** builds on §2.1 in two ways: the [[Thm - Second-Order Optimality Conditions|Hessian sign test]] becomes the convexity criterion, and the [[Thm - The Method of Lagrange Multipliers|method of Lagrange multipliers]] generalizes, when inequality constraints $g_j \leq 0$ are allowed, to the **Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions** — the same stationarity equation $\nabla f = \sum\lambda_j\nabla g_j$ with the extra requirement that multipliers of inactive constraints vanish. The multipliers, here abstract scalars, become in optimization the *dual variables* and *shadow prices* of the problem.

5. **Lie theory and group theory — matrix groups are submanifolds, and the tangent space at the identity is the Lie algebra.** The orthogonal group $O(n)$ is simultaneously a group (see [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]] for the axioms, and [[Def - Group Action]] for how it acts on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by rotations) and, by [[Ex - The orthogonal group as a submanifold]], a submanifold of the space of matrices — a **Lie group**. The bridge is that the [[Def - The Tangent Space to a Submanifold|tangent space]] at the identity element carries the algebraic shadow of the group: for $O(n)$ it is the skew-symmetric matrices, which form the **Lie algebra** $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ under the commutator bracket. The exponential map sends the Lie algebra back into the group. This is the meeting point of the algebra of [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2]] and the geometry of this topic, and it is the foundation of how continuous symmetry is studied throughout physics.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: every theorem in this topic transfers a linear-algebraic fact about the derivative to a nonlinear local fact about the map.** This is the single idea that organizes §2.2 and §2.3, and it is worth stating starkly. You have a nonlinear object — a map, an equation, a set — that you cannot analyze directly. You compute its derivative, which is a *linear* object: a matrix, finite, fully understood by linear algebra. You determine a linear-algebraic property of that matrix: invertible, surjective, a particular rank, a particular partial block invertible. Then the relevant theorem *transfers that property* to the nonlinear object, in a neighbourhood of the base point. Invertible derivative transfers to locally invertible map (inverse function theorem). Invertible partial derivative transfers to locally solvable equation (implicit function theorem). Surjective derivative transfers to "the level set is locally a manifold" (regular value theorem). The recurring word "locally" is the precise price of the transfer: linearization is exact only infinitesimally, so its consequences propagate only to a neighbourhood. Once you see all four theorems as one transfer principle, you know what to do when stuck — compute the derivative and ask what linear-algebraic property it has.

**The true name of "submanifold" is "locally a graph".** The official definition of a submanifold — a set that can be straightened by a local diffeomorphism — is the right thing for proving general theorems but the wrong thing for *thinking*. The operational picture is the [[Def - Submanifold of Euclidean Space|graphical representation]]: a $d$-dimensional submanifold is, near each of its points, the graph of a smooth function of $d$ of the coordinates. A graph is the most concrete curved object imaginable — you can see it, parametrize it, integrate over it, compute its tangent plane as the graph of the linearization. The implicit function theorem is exactly the machine that converts the abstract description ("$M$ is a level set with nonvanishing gradient") into this concrete one ("$M$ is a graph"). So whenever you meet a submanifold, do not picture an abstract straightening diffeomorphism — picture a graph $z = g(x)$, and remember that the implicit function theorem guarantees you can always find such a graph locally, just possibly after permuting which coordinates are the "inputs".

**Local always; global never — without an extra hypothesis whose nature you should be able to name.** The reader's stated difficulty is knowing which operations are legal for far-out-of-distribution generalization, and this topic offers an unusually clean case study, because the legal/illegal boundary is *exactly* the local/global boundary. Every theorem here is local, and every theorem here is sharp — the local conclusion genuinely cannot be upgraded to a global one for free. The inverse function theorem with invertible derivative everywhere still does not give global injectivity ($e^x\cos y, e^x\sin y$). The implicit function theorem solves $f(x,y) = 0$ near a point but the global solution set may be a circle, on which no single function $y = g(x)$ works. The regular value theorem makes a level set a manifold *where* the value is regular, but the same equation may have a singular point elsewhere. The discipline is: whenever you want a global conclusion, you must supply, and name, the extra hypothesis that buys it — properness, compactness, monotonicity, convexity, positive-definiteness of $Df$'s symmetric part on a convex domain, or simply checking the non-regular points by hand. A global claim with no such hypothesis is the characteristic illegal move of the topic.

**Lagrange multipliers fail silently, and the failure mode is geometric.** The clean Lagrange condition $\nabla f = \sum\lambda_j\nabla g_j$ is so reliable in routine use that it is easy to forget it can simply not see the answer. The failure happens at *non-regular constraint points* — where the constraint gradients $\nabla g_j$ are linearly dependent — and the geometry of the failure is precise: at such a point the constraint set has no well-defined tangent space (it has a corner, a cusp, a self-crossing), and "$\nabla f$ normal to the constraint set" is therefore meaningless. The general statement of [[Thm - The Method of Lagrange Multipliers|Lagrange's theorem]] honestly reflects this: it only ever promises a *nontrivial* dependence $\lambda_0\nabla f + \sum\lambda_j\nabla g_j = 0$, and the degenerate case $\lambda_0 = 0$ is exactly the case where the relation says nothing about $f$ at all. The practical rule is permanent: a constrained optimization is not finished until the non-regular points of the constraint set have been added to the candidate list and checked individually. The cusp example $y^3 = x^2$, whose extremum sits precisely at the non-regular point, is the warning every solution should be tested against.

**Compactness is what guarantees the search terminates.** It is easy to treat the Weierstrass theorem as background scenery, but it plays a precise and indispensable role in every optimization problem over a compact set. The first-order condition, the Hessian test, and Lagrange multipliers all produce *candidate* points — necessary conditions, satisfied by the answer but also by impostors. None of these tools, on its own, tells you a maximum *exists*. Compactness does: a continuous function on a compact set attains its extrema, so the global maximum and minimum are guaranteed to be among the finitely many candidates your other tools produced. This is why the strategy "list all candidates, then compare values" is valid — it is valid precisely because compactness certifies that the list is non-empty and contains the true answer. On a non-compact set the list may be complete and yet contain no global extremum at all, because none exists.
