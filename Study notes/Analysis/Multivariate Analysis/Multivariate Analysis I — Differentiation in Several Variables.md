# Notation Registry

- $U, V, O \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ — open sets (the natural domain of a differentiable function; openness is what guarantees room to move in every direction)
- $f, g, F, G : U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ — functions of several variables; $f_1, \dots, f_m$ are the component functions $f_j : U \to \mathbb{R}$
- $x, y, h, v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ — points and vectors, written as column vectors; $x_\circ$ is a fixed base point
- $|x| = (x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2)^{1/2}$ — the Euclidean norm on $\mathbb{R}^n$ (the same symbol serves for $\mathbb{R}^m$; context fixes the dimension)
- $e_1, \dots, e_n$ — the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$
- $Df_{x_\circ}$ or $Df(x_\circ)$ — the **total derivative** (differential) of $f$ at $x_\circ$, a linear map $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$
- $J f(x_\circ)$ — the **Jacobian matrix**, the $m \times n$ matrix of $Df_{x_\circ}$ in the standard bases
- $\partial_j f$, $\partial f / \partial x_j$, $D_j f$ — the $j$-th partial derivative of $f$
- $\partial_v f(x_\circ)$ — the **directional derivative** of $f$ at $x_\circ$ in the direction $v$
- $\nabla f$ — the **gradient** of a scalar-valued $f$, the column vector $(\partial_1 f, \dots, \partial_n f)^{\mathsf T}$
- $R(x)$ or $R(x_\circ, h)$ — the remainder in an affine approximation; $R(h) = o(|h|)$ means $|R(h)|/|h| \to 0$
- $o(|h|)$, $O(|h|)$ — Landau little-o and big-O: $f = o(|g|)$ means $|f|/|g| \to 0$; $f = O(|g|)$ means $|f|/|g|$ stays bounded
- $C^k(U, \mathbb{R}^m)$ — functions whose partial derivatives of order $\le k$ all exist and are continuous on $U$; $C^k(U)$ when $m = 1$; $C^\infty = \bigcap_k C^k$
- $\partial_i \partial_j f$, $\partial^2 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j$ — second-order partial derivatives
- $D^2 f(x_\circ)$ — the **Hessian**, the $n \times n$ matrix $(\partial_i \partial_j f)$ for a scalar $f$
- $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n) \in \mathbb{N}^n$ — a **multi-index**; $|\alpha| = \alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_n$, $\alpha! = \alpha_1! \cdots \alpha_n!$, $h^\alpha = h_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots h_n^{\alpha_n}$, $\partial^\alpha = \partial_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots \partial_n^{\alpha_n}$
- $\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{R}^m)$ — the space of linear maps $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, identified with $m \times n$ matrices
- $\| M \|$ — operator norm of a linear map $M$; $|Mv| \le \|M\| \, |v|$ for all $v$

# Motivation

Single-variable calculus rests on one idea: near a point, a differentiable function looks like a straight line. The derivative $f'(x_\circ)$ is the slope of that line, and everything else — the chain rule, the mean value theorem, Taylor's theorem — is bookkeeping about how these lines fit together. The whole of this topic is the answer to a single question: **what is the right replacement for "looks like a line" when the input has $n$ coordinates and the output has $m$?**

The naive answer is "take the derivative in each variable separately" — the partial derivatives. This is necessary but, on its own, a trap. A function can have all $n$ partial derivatives at a point and still fail to be continuous there, let alone differentiable. The partial derivatives only probe the function along the $n$ coordinate axes; they say nothing about the infinitely many other directions of approach, and a function can be well-behaved along every axis while doing something pathological along a diagonal. So partial derivatives are not the derivative. They are coordinates of a candidate for the derivative, and the candidate has to be tested.

The correct definition takes the third of the single-variable viewpoints — the derivative as the coefficient of the best infinitesimal linear approximation — and promotes it. A function $f : U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is differentiable at $x_\circ$ if there is a **linear map** $L : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ such that $f(x_\circ + h) = f(x_\circ) + L(h) + o(|h|)$. The derivative is no longer a number, or even a vector; it is a linear transformation, the unique linear map that approximates $f$ to first order in *every* direction at once. This is the single conceptual leap of the topic, and once it is made, the rest is the systematic transfer of single-variable calculus to this setting. The chain rule becomes the statement that the derivative of a composite is the *composite of the derivatives* — which, written in coordinates, is matrix multiplication, and that is the deep reason matrices were worth inventing. The mean value theorem survives in scalar form but must be downgraded to an *inequality* for vector-valued functions, because there is no single intermediate point that works for all components simultaneously. Taylor's theorem survives intact, organised by the algebra of multi-indices.

A second theme runs underneath: the gap between *having partial derivatives*, *being differentiable*, and *being continuously differentiable* ($C^1$). These three conditions form a strict hierarchy, and the central structural theorems of §1.1 pin down exactly where the implications hold and where they fail. Continuous partial derivatives imply differentiability; differentiability implies continuity; neither converse holds. Knowing precisely which implication is valid — and being able to name the counterexample for each that is not — is what lets you generalise safely. That precision is the point of the Legal Operations section, and it is the reader's stated reason for studying the topic: when you push these ideas far out of their original setting, the only safe guide is knowing the exact validity condition for every move.

# Concept Map

## §1.1 The Differential and the Jacobian

- **[[Def - The Total Derivative and Differentiability]]**
	- A function $f : U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ on an open set $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is differentiable at $x_\circ$ if there is a linear map $L : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with $f(x_\circ + h) = f(x_\circ) + L(h) + o(|h|)$ as $h \to 0$; this $L$, written $Df_{x_\circ}$, is unique. The derivative is a *linear map*, not a number — it is the best infinitesimal linear approximation, the unique linear map matching $f$ to first order in every direction. Differentiability is equivalent to the differentiability of each component $f_j$ separately. This is the single foundational definition of the topic, and the existence of $L$ is a genuine condition, strictly stronger than the existence of partial derivatives.

- **[[Def - Partial Derivatives and the Jacobian Matrix]]**
	- The $j$-th partial derivative $\partial_j f(x_\circ)$ is the ordinary one-variable derivative of $f$ along the $j$-th coordinate axis, all other coordinates frozen. When $f$ is differentiable, the matrix of $Df_{x_\circ}$ in the standard bases is the **Jacobian** $Jf(x_\circ) = (\partial f_i / \partial x_j)$, an $m \times n$ matrix whose columns are the partial derivatives $\partial_j f(x_\circ)$. The Jacobian *represents* the derivative once bases are chosen; the distinction between the linear map $Df_{x_\circ}$ and its matrix $Jf(x_\circ)$ is pedantic here but becomes essential in differential geometry. Partial derivatives can exist without $f$ being differentiable.

- **[[Def - Directional Derivative and the Gradient]]**
	- The directional derivative $\partial_v f(x_\circ) = \frac{d}{ds}\big|_{s=0} f(x_\circ + sv)$ measures the rate of change of $f$ along the line through $x_\circ$ in direction $v$. When $f$ is differentiable, $\partial_v f(x_\circ) = Df_{x_\circ}(v)$, so the directional derivative is *linear in* $v$ — a strong constraint that fails for merely partially-differentiable functions. For a scalar $f$, the gradient $\nabla f$ is the vector representing $Df_{x_\circ}$ under the dot product: $\partial_v f = \nabla f \cdot v$, so $\nabla f$ points in the direction of steepest ascent and its length is the maximal rate of increase.

- **[[Thm - Differentiability Implies Continuity]]**
	- If $f$ is differentiable at $x_\circ$, then $f$ is continuous at $x_\circ$. The reason is that $f(x_\circ + h) - f(x_\circ) = Df_{x_\circ}(h) + o(|h|)$, and both terms on the right vanish as $h \to 0$ — the linear term because linear maps on $\mathbb{R}^n$ are continuous, the remainder by definition. This is the multivariate analogue of the single-variable fact and confirms the definition of differentiability is "strong enough". The converse fails, and so does the weaker hope that mere existence of partial derivatives implies continuity.

- **[[Thm - Continuous Partials Imply Differentiability]]**
	- If all partial derivatives $\partial_j f$ exist and are continuous on $U$ — that is, $f \in C^1(U)$ — then $f$ is differentiable at every point of $U$. This is the workhorse theorem: it converts the hard-to-check condition "differentiable" into the easy-to-check condition "compute the partials and confirm they are continuous". The proof telescopes $f(x_\circ + h) - f(x_\circ)$ into $n$ one-variable increments and applies the mean value theorem to each. Continuity of the partials is essential; mere existence is not enough.

## §1.2 The Chain Rule and the Mean Value Theorem

- **[[Thm - The Chain Rule]]**
	- If $f : U \to V$ is differentiable at $x_\circ$ and $g : V \to \mathbb{R}^k$ is differentiable at $f(x_\circ)$, then $g \circ f$ is differentiable at $x_\circ$ and $D(g \circ f)_{x_\circ} = Dg_{f(x_\circ)} \circ Df_{x_\circ}$ — the derivative of a composite is the composite of the derivatives. In matrix form this is $J(g \circ f) = Jg \cdot Jf$, ordinary matrix multiplication, which is the conceptual justification for the definition of matrix product. The componentwise version is the familiar "sum over intermediate variables" rule. It is the single most-used computational tool in the topic.

- **[[Thm - The Mean Value Inequality]]**
	- For scalar $f : U \to \mathbb{R}$ differentiable on $U$, with the segment from $x_\circ$ to $x_\circ + h$ inside $U$, there is $t \in (0,1)$ with $f(x_\circ + h) - f(x_\circ) = Df_{x_\circ + th}(h)$ — an exact mean value *equality*. For vector-valued $f : U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ no single $t$ works for all components at once, so the statement is downgraded to the inequality $|f(x) - f(y)| \le \big(\sup_\xi \|Df_\xi\|\big)\,|x - y|$ on a convex domain. Consequences: a $C^1$ map is locally Lipschitz, and $Df \equiv 0$ on a connected open set forces $f$ to be constant.

## §1.3 Higher Derivatives and Taylor's Theorem

- **[[Def - Higher-Order Derivatives and Ck Maps]]**
	- A function is $C^k$ if all its partial derivatives of order up to $k$ exist and are continuous; $C^\infty$ if it is $C^k$ for every $k$. Higher partials are defined recursively, $\partial_i \partial_j f = \partial_i(\partial_j f)$, and the **multi-index** notation $\partial^\alpha = \partial_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots \partial_n^{\alpha_n}$, $|\alpha| = \sum \alpha_i$, compresses the bookkeeping. Sums, products, and compositions of $C^k$ functions are $C^k$. The $C^k$ classes are a regularity ladder; each rung is a hypothesis under which more theorems become available, and most of analysis is the study of which rung a given construction lands on.

- **[[Thm - Schwarz's Theorem on Mixed Partials]]**
	- If $f \in C^2(U)$, then mixed second partials commute: $\partial_i \partial_j f = \partial_j \partial_i f$. The order in which you differentiate does not matter, *provided the second partials are continuous*. The proof reads the quantity $f(\text{corner sum})/h^2$ two ways, applying the one-variable mean value theorem along each pair of edges of a small square. Continuity of the second partials is exactly the hypothesis that makes the two readings agree in the limit; without it, mixed partials genuinely can differ. For $C^k$ functions, all partials of order $\le k$ are order-independent, which is what makes multi-index notation legitimate.

- **[[Thm - Taylor's Theorem in Several Variables]]**
	- For $f \in C^{k+1}(U)$, with the segment from $x_\circ$ to $x_\circ + h$ in $U$, $f(x_\circ + h) = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} \frac{\partial^\alpha f(x_\circ)}{\alpha!} h^\alpha + R_{k+1}(x_\circ, h)$, the multivariate Taylor expansion, with an explicit integral remainder of size $O(|h|^{k+1})$. The proof restricts $f$ to the segment, producing a one-variable function, and applies single-variable Taylor plus the chain rule; the multinomial theorem supplies the multi-index coefficients. The order-two term is $\frac{1}{2} h \cdot D^2 f(x_\circ) h$, the Hessian quadratic form — the basis of the second-derivative test for extrema.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Tangent Space and the Differential of a Map *(from Differential Geometry)*
> Once the derivative is understood as a *linear map* rather than a matrix, the tangent space of a manifold and the differential $df_p : T_p M \to T_{f(p)} N$ become the natural next step: a manifold is a space that looks like $\mathbb{R}^n$ near each point, and the differential is $Df$ done chart by chart, with the chain rule guaranteeing the answer is chart-independent. See **Multivariate Analysis II** for submanifolds and tangent spaces as the first concrete instance.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Metric Tensor and Covariant Differentiation *(from Tensor Calculus and Special Relativity)*
> The Jacobian is how vector and tensor components transform under a change of coordinates; demanding that physical laws be expressible independently of coordinates is exactly the requirement that they be tensor equations. The chain rule in Jacobian form is the transformation law. This is the computational engine of **tensor calculus** and of the coordinate-change machinery of **special relativity** and general relativity.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Euler–Lagrange Equation *(from Variational Calculus)*
> Taylor's theorem to second order says a critical point is a local extremum when the Hessian is definite. Replacing the finite-dimensional domain $\mathbb{R}^n$ by an infinite-dimensional space of functions turns "set the gradient to zero" into the **Euler–Lagrange equation**, and the Hessian test into the second variation. Multivariate differentiation is the finite-dimensional rehearsal for the calculus of variations.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of this topic chase a small, recognisable set of goals. The most frequent is **deciding whether a given function is differentiable at a point** — typically a function defined piecewise, smooth away from the origin and assigned a value there by hand. The job is to decide, and to decide for the right reason: either by exhibiting the linear map $L$ and verifying the $o(|h|)$ condition, or by invoking continuity of the partials, or by proving non-differentiability through a directional-derivative or continuity obstruction. A second target is **computing a derivative**: producing a Jacobian, a gradient, a directional derivative, or a Taylor polynomial, where the work is organisational rather than conceptual but the chain rule must be deployed correctly. A third is **establishing a bound** — showing a function does not change too fast, that a map is Lipschitz, that an increment is controlled — which is the natural home of the mean value inequality. A fourth is **proving a qualitative structural fact**: that a function is constant, that two functions agree, that a function depending on fewer effective variables really does, that mixed partials coincide. The unifying remark is that all four targets are statements about *first-order behaviour*: they ask either "what is the linear approximation" or "what does control of the linear approximation force on the function". You understand a multivariate function when you know its derivative, where that derivative exists, and what the derivative lets you conclude.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **The function is given by an explicit formula** — then the partials can be computed by Analysis I rules, and if they come out continuous, [[Thm - Continuous Partials Imply Differentiability|continuous partials imply differentiability]] settles differentiability at once; this is the single richest source. **The function is given as a composite** — then the [[Thm - The Chain Rule|chain rule]] applies, converting the problem into a product of known Jacobians. **A bound on the derivative is given or available**, perhaps because the domain is compact and $Df$ continuous — then the [[Thm - The Mean Value Inequality|mean value inequality]] converts the derivative bound into a bound on the function. **The function is assumed $C^k$** — then Schwarz's theorem licenses reordering partials and [[Thm - Taylor's Theorem in Several Variables|Taylor's theorem]] supplies a polynomial approximation. **A symmetry or invariance is given** — homogeneity, constancy along curves, dependence on a single combination of the variables — then differentiating the symmetry relation produces an identity (Euler's identity for homogeneous functions is the prototype). The recurring move is to route a source to a target: an explicit formula routes through continuous partials to differentiability; a composite routes through the chain rule to a Jacobian; a derivative bound routes through the mean value inequality to a function bound; a $C^k$ hypothesis routes through Taylor to an approximation. The [[Multivariate Analysis I — Differentiation in Several Variables#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves almost every problem in the topic is assembled from. The reader's stated difficulty is knowing which operations stay legal when the situation is pushed far from its original setting, so each operation below states its **validity condition** explicitly — the precise hypothesis under which it is sound. When stuck, scan the list and try each one; when generalising, check the validity condition before trusting the move.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Differentiate componentwise.** A map $f : U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is differentiable at $x_\circ$ if and only if every component $f_j : U \to \mathbb{R}$ is, and then $(Df_{x_\circ})_j = (Df_j)_{x_\circ}$ — the $j$-th row of the Jacobian is the derivative of the $j$-th component. *Validity condition:* this is unconditional; it holds for any $f$ into $\mathbb{R}^m$ because the norm on $\mathbb{R}^m$ is equivalent to the max of the coordinate norms. The trigger is any vector-valued target: split into scalar problems, solve each, reassemble. Note the asymmetry — you may always split the *codomain* this way, but you may *never* split the domain, because the variables of $\mathbb{R}^n$ interact (that is the entire content of "partials are not the derivative").

2. **Verify differentiability by checking continuity of the partials.** Compute all $n$ partial derivatives by Analysis I rules; if they exist and are continuous on $U$, then $f \in C^1(U)$ and $f$ is differentiable everywhere on $U$, by [[Thm - Continuous Partials Imply Differentiability]]. *Validity condition:* the partials must exist on a whole neighbourhood (not just at the point) and be continuous there. Mere existence at the point is not enough — this is the single most common illegal shortcut. The trigger is any differentiability question about an explicitly given formula; this is the cheapest route and almost always the right first move.

3. **Recover the derivative from the partials.** If $f$ is *known* to be differentiable, its derivative is forced: $Df_{x_\circ}(v) = \sum_i v_i \, \partial_i f(x_\circ)$, the matrix is the Jacobian $Jf(x_\circ)$, and for a scalar $f$, $Df_{x_\circ}(v) = \nabla f(x_\circ) \cdot v$. *Validity condition:* differentiability must already be established by some other means. Given differentiability, the partials *are* the derivative; without it, the Jacobian is merely a candidate matrix that may fail the $o(|h|)$ test. The trigger is "compute the derivative of this (known-differentiable) map".

4. **Compose with the chain rule.** For a composite $g \circ f$, the derivative is the matrix product $J(g \circ f)(x_\circ) = Jg(f(x_\circ)) \cdot Jf(x_\circ)$, equivalently the composite of linear maps $Dg_{f(x_\circ)} \circ Df_{x_\circ}$, by [[Thm - The Chain Rule]]. *Validity condition:* $f$ must be differentiable at $x_\circ$ and $g$ differentiable at $f(x_\circ)$ — and the inner image must land in the domain of $g$. The matrix form is valid only after bases are fixed; the order of multiplication is outer-times-inner and is not negotiable. The trigger is any function presented as a chain of simpler maps, or any coordinate change.

5. **Restrict to a line and reduce to one variable.** Replace $f$ by $\varphi(t) = f(x_\circ + tv)$, a function of the single variable $t$, and apply Analysis I. The derivative is $\varphi'(t) = Df_{x_\circ + tv}(v) = \partial_v f(x_\circ + tv)$ by the chain rule. *Validity condition:* the segment $x_\circ + tv$ must stay inside the open domain for the range of $t$ considered. This is how the mean value theorem and Taylor's theorem are *proved* — both are one-variable results transported along a segment. The trigger is any problem where a one-variable theorem would finish the job if only the domain were one-dimensional.

6. **Convert a derivative bound into a function bound.** If $\|Df_\xi\| \le M$ for all $\xi$ on the segment from $x$ to $y$, then $|f(x) - f(y)| \le M |x - y|$, by [[Thm - The Mean Value Inequality]]. *Validity condition:* the domain must be **convex** (so segments stay inside it), or at least the relevant segment must; on a non-convex domain the bound can fail spectacularly. The trigger is any request to bound an increment, to prove Lipschitz continuity, or to prove a function constant (take $M = 0$). This is the multivariate replacement for "$|f(b) - f(a)| \le \sup|f'| \cdot |b - a|$".

7. **Swap the order of partial derivatives.** Replace $\partial_i \partial_j f$ by $\partial_j \partial_i f$, and more generally reorder any string of partials, by [[Thm - Schwarz's Theorem on Mixed Partials]]. *Validity condition:* $f$ must be $C^2$ (for a single swap) or $C^k$ (to reorder partials of order $\le k$) — the partials being swapped must exist *and be continuous*. This is exactly what makes multi-index notation $\partial^\alpha$ well-defined. The trigger is any computation with second or higher partials; reorder freely once $C^2$ is confirmed, never before.

8. **Approximate by a Taylor polynomial.** Replace $f$ near $x_\circ$ by $\sum_{|\alpha| \le k} \frac{\partial^\alpha f(x_\circ)}{\alpha!} h^\alpha$, with a controlled remainder, by [[Thm - Taylor's Theorem in Several Variables]]. *Validity condition:* $f \in C^{k+1}$ for the integral-remainder form, $C^k$ for the Lagrange-type form; the segment from $x_\circ$ to $x_\circ + h$ must lie in the domain. The trigger is any local analysis — extrema, the behaviour of $f$ to leading order, identifying a function from its expansion. The order-two term is the Hessian quadratic form.

9. **Differentiate an identity.** If $f$ satisfies a relation that holds on an open set — homogeneity $f(rx) = r^\lambda f(x)$, invariance $f(x) = f(g(x))$, constancy along a family of curves — differentiate both sides using the chain rule to extract a new identity among the partials. *Validity condition:* the relation must hold on an open set (so differentiation is legal) and $f$ must be differentiable. The trigger is any stated symmetry; Euler's identity $\sum_j x_j \partial_j f = \lambda f$ for homogeneous functions is the canonical payoff.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Concluding differentiability from the existence of all partial derivatives
> The partials are computed by freezing all but one variable, so they only see $f$ along the $n$ coordinate axes. A function can have all $n$ partials at a point and still be discontinuous there — the standard witness is $f(x,y) = xy/(x^2+y^2)$ extended by $f(0,0)=0$, which has $\partial_x f(0,0) = \partial_y f(0,0) = 0$ yet takes the value $\tfrac12$ all along the diagonal $y = x$, so it is not even continuous at the origin, let alone differentiable (see [[Ex - Partial derivatives exist without differentiability]]). The missing condition is *continuity of the partials*: that upgrade, and only that upgrade, restores the implication via [[Thm - Continuous Partials Imply Differentiability]]. Existence of partials is necessary for differentiability, never sufficient.

> [!warning] 2. Splitting the domain — treating a function of $n$ variables as $n$ independent one-variable problems
> Because the chain rule and the mean value theorem are *proved* by restricting to a line, it is tempting to believe a multivariate function is "really" $n$ separate one-variable functions glued together. It is not. The variables of $\mathbb{R}^n$ are not independent in the relevant sense: the value $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ depends on all of them jointly, and the derivative is a linear map that must approximate $f$ along *every* direction, not only the axes. You may always split the *codomain* (operation 1), because $\mathbb{R}^m$ genuinely is a product; you may never split the domain. The function $xy/(x^2+y^2)$ is the cautionary tale — perfectly well-behaved along each axis, pathological on the diagonal.

> [!warning] 3. Using the mean value *equality* for a vector-valued function
> For scalar $f$ there is an exact $t \in (0,1)$ with $f(x+h) - f(x) = Df_{x+th}(h)$. For $f : U \to \mathbb{R}^m$ with $m \ge 2$ the tempting move is to apply this to each component — but each component supplies its *own* intermediate point $t_j$, and there is in general no single $t$ that works for all components at once. The curve $\gamma(t) = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$ has $\gamma(1) - \gamma(0) = 0$, yet $\gamma'(t)$ is never zero, so no mean value *equality* can hold. The legal substitute is the mean value *inequality* $|f(x) - f(y)| \le \sup\|Df\| \cdot |x-y|$, which survives because it only needs an upper bound, not an exact equality. Reach for the inequality, never the equality, the moment $m \ge 2$.

> [!warning] 4. Reordering partial derivatives without checking $C^2$
> Multi-index notation writes $\partial^\alpha$ as if the order of differentiation were irrelevant, and for $C^2$ functions it is. But the equality $\partial_i \partial_j f = \partial_j \partial_i f$ is a theorem with a hypothesis, not a definition. There is a classical function — $f(x,y) = xy(x^2-y^2)/(x^2+y^2)$ extended by $f(0,0)=0$ — whose mixed partials at the origin are $\partial_x\partial_y f(0,0) = -1$ and $\partial_y\partial_x f(0,0) = +1$ (see [[Ex - A function with unequal mixed partials]]). Both mixed partials exist; they simply are not equal, because they are not continuous at the origin. Reorder partials only after confirming the function is $C^2$ on the relevant set.

> [!warning] 5. Applying the mean value inequality on a non-convex domain
> The bound $|f(x) - f(y)| \le \sup\|Df\| \cdot |x-y|$ is proved by integrating $Df$ along the *straight segment* from $y$ to $x$. If that segment leaves the domain, the proof has no ground to stand on and the conclusion can be false: on an annulus or a slit plane, a function with a small bounded derivative can have wildly different values at two nearby points connected only by a long way around. The validity condition is that the segment lie in the domain — guaranteed if the domain is convex. On a non-convex but connected domain the salvage is to chain the inequality along a polygonal path, picking up the path length rather than $|x-y|$.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this topic are won at the moment you classify what is being asked, because each class has a characteristic source pattern and a characteristic route to the answer. Begin there.

If the problem **asks whether a function is differentiable at a point**, the first question is whether the function is given by a single smooth formula or is defined piecewise. For a single formula, the route is almost always the cheapest one: compute the partial derivatives by the ordinary rules of Analysis I, check that they are continuous, and invoke [[Thm - Continuous Partials Imply Differentiability]]. This settles differentiability on the entire open set in one stroke and you are done. The interesting case is the piecewise function — smooth away from a point, assigned a value there by hand. Now you must work at the suspect point directly. There are two outcomes and two methods. To prove differentiability, write down the only candidate linear map (its matrix is the Jacobian of partials, which you compute at the point from the limit definition) and verify the defining limit $|f(x_\circ + h) - f(x_\circ) - L(h)|/|h| \to 0$ honestly. To prove *non*-differentiability, you do not need the definition at all; you exhibit an obstruction. The obstruction hierarchy, cheapest first: is $f$ discontinuous at the point? Then it cannot be differentiable, by [[Thm - Differentiability Implies Continuity]]. If $f$ is continuous, is the directional derivative $\partial_v f$ *non-linear* in $v$, or does it fail to exist for some $v$? Differentiability forces $\partial_v f = Df(v)$ to be linear in $v$, so any non-linearity is a contradiction. Only if both of these pass and you still suspect non-differentiability do you go back to the limit definition and find a path along which it fails. The reason to climb the hierarchy from the bottom is that the cheap obstructions, when they work, are decisive in one line.

If the problem **asks you to compute a derivative** — a Jacobian, a gradient, a directional derivative, a Taylor polynomial — the work is organisational and the only real decision is whether to use the [[Thm - The Chain Rule|chain rule]]. Use it whenever the function is naturally a composite or a coordinate change; the payoff is that you multiply known Jacobians instead of differentiating a messy explicit formula. For directional derivatives of a known-differentiable scalar function, do not take a limit — use $\partial_v f = \nabla f \cdot v$. For Taylor polynomials, the factorials in the multi-index formula are a nuisance; the efficient route is often to substitute a known one-variable expansion and read off the coefficients, justified because the Taylor polynomial is *unique* (a polynomial agreeing with $f$ to order $k$ in the $o(|h|^k)$ sense has exactly the Taylor coefficients).

If the problem **asks for a bound** — that a function is Lipschitz, that an increment is small, that a sequence of values converges — the instrument is the [[Thm - The Mean Value Inequality|mean value inequality]]. The route is: bound the operator norm of $Df$ on the relevant region, then read off $|f(x) - f(y)| \le \sup\|Df\| \cdot |x-y|$. The one thing to check before trusting this is convexity of the domain, or at least that the segment between the two points stays inside it; on a non-convex domain you must chain the inequality along a path and the bound picks up the path length. A frequently disguised instance of this class is "prove $f$ is constant": that is the bound problem with $\sup\|Df\| = 0$, and it works on any connected domain because connectivity lets you chain the zero bound from point to point.

If the problem **asks you to prove a structural identity** — that mixed partials agree, that a function depending on a single combination of variables really has the corresponding derivative structure, that a homogeneous function satisfies Euler's identity — the route is to **differentiate a given relation**. Whenever the hypothesis is an equation valid on an open set, differentiating both sides (with the chain rule) produces a new equation among the derivatives, and the target identity usually drops out after one or two such differentiations. The model is Euler's identity: from $f(rx) = r^\lambda f(x)$, differentiate in $r$ and set $r = 1$ to get $\sum_j x_j \partial_j f(x) = \lambda f(x)$. The same pattern handles "a function constant along a family of curves has gradient orthogonal to those curves": differentiate the constancy relation along the curve.

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle, and it is the answer to the reader's stated worry about which operations survive far-out-of-distribution generalisation: **every theorem here has an exact validity condition, and the safe move is to name it before using it.** Continuous partials imply differentiability — but the partials must be continuous on a neighbourhood, not merely exist at a point. The chain rule's matrix form is matrix multiplication — but only after bases are fixed and only when the inner map is differentiable at the point and the outer at its image. The mean value statement is an equality for scalars and only an inequality for vector outputs. Mixed partials commute — for $C^2$ functions. The mean value inequality holds — on convex domains. When you carry these tools into differential geometry, tensor calculus, or the calculus of variations, the validity condition is what you must re-verify; the formula itself almost always survives, but only inside the condition. Knowing the condition is the difference between a correct generalisation and a plausible-looking error.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Continuous Partials Imply Differentiability|Continuous partials imply differentiability]]**: $f \in C^1(U) \Rightarrow f$ differentiable on $U$. This is the most-used single fact in the topic because it converts an abstract, limit-based condition into a mechanical check. The recognisable setup is "here is a function given by a formula; is it differentiable?" — and the answer is almost always "compute the partials, see that they are continuous, done". It is also the gateway to everything downstream: the chain rule, the mean value inequality, and Taylor's theorem all take "$C^1$" or "$C^k$" as their hypothesis, and this theorem is what lets you verify that hypothesis. Recognise its applicability whenever a function is explicit enough to differentiate by Analysis I rules.

- **[[Thm - The Chain Rule|The Chain Rule]]**: $D(g \circ f) = Dg \circ Df$, in matrices $J(g\circ f) = Jg \cdot Jf$. This is the workhorse for *computation* and the structural backbone of the topic. Its recognisable setup is any composite or any change of coordinates. It is typically combined with the restrict-to-a-line operation to *prove* the mean value theorem and Taylor's theorem, so internalising it pays compound interest. Its most powerful conceptual use is the observation that derivatives compose like linear maps — this is what makes the derivative a *functorial* construction and what licenses the coordinate-free language of differential geometry. Recognise it whenever a function is built from simpler pieces or a problem changes variables.

- **[[Thm - The Mean Value Inequality|The Mean Value Inequality]]**: $|f(x) - f(y)| \le \sup_\xi\|Df_\xi\| \cdot |x-y|$ on a convex domain. The reusable content is the *direction* of the implication: it turns information about the derivative into information about the function. Reach for it whenever you must bound how much a function changes, prove Lipschitz continuity, prove a contraction estimate (the input to the contraction mapping principle in **Multivariate Analysis II**), or prove a function constant. Its disguised uses are the most valuable: "$f$ is constant on a connected open set" is this theorem with bound zero, and "the sequence $f(x_k)$ is Cauchy" often follows by applying it to consecutive terms.

- **[[Thm - Schwarz's Theorem on Mixed Partials|Schwarz's Theorem]]**: $\partial_i \partial_j f = \partial_j \partial_i f$ for $C^2$ functions. Its typical use is silent — it is the permission slip that makes the multi-index notation $\partial^\alpha$, the symmetry of the Hessian, and the whole apparatus of Taylor's theorem legitimate. Whenever you reorder a string of partial derivatives, freely write a higher derivative as $\partial^\alpha f$, or use that the Hessian is a symmetric matrix (hence has an orthonormal eigenbasis, hence a clean definiteness test), you are using Schwarz. Recognise that it is a *hypothesis-bearing* fact: the moment continuity of the second partials is in doubt, the reordering is not free.

- **[[Def - Directional Derivative and the Gradient|The gradient identity]]** $\partial_v f = \nabla f \cdot v$: for a differentiable scalar function, the directional derivative in *every* direction is read off from a single vector. The reusable consequences are immediate and constant: the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, it is orthogonal to level sets, and the directional derivative is linear in the direction. Its typical use is to replace a limit computation by a dot product, and to convert geometric statements ("$f$ increases fastest this way", "this curve stays on a level set") into algebraic ones about $\nabla f$. It is the bridge from the analytic derivative to the geometry of the function.

# Bridges

1. **Linear algebra — the derivative is a linear map, and the Jacobian is its matrix.** The single conceptual move of the topic is to recognise the derivative as an element of $\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{R}^m)$, the space of linear maps. Everything linear-algebraic then transfers verbatim. The chain rule is composition of linear maps, which is why its matrix form is matrix multiplication — matrix multiplication was *defined* to make composition of linear maps correspond to a product of arrays, and the chain rule is the place that definition pays off. The rank of the Jacobian measures the rank of the derivative as a linear map, and this is the hinge of the inverse and implicit function theorems in **Multivariate Analysis II**: a map is locally invertible exactly where its derivative is an invertible linear map. The Hessian is a symmetric bilinear form, so the spectral theorem applies and definiteness is an eigenvalue condition. Multivariate calculus is, structurally, linear algebra applied infinitesimally at every point.

2. **Differential geometry — charts, tangent spaces, and the coordinate-free derivative.** A smooth manifold is a space that looks like an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ near every point, glued together by transition maps. The derivative of a map between manifolds, $df_p : T_p M \to T_{f(p)} N$, is defined by computing $Df$ in charts; the [[Thm - The Chain Rule|chain rule]] is precisely the theorem that guarantees this is independent of the chart chosen, because two charts differ by a transition map and the chain rule says the derivative transforms consistently. The careful distinction between the linear map $Df_{x_\circ}$ and its matrix $Jf(x_\circ)$, pedantic in $\mathbb{R}^n$, becomes unavoidable on a manifold, where there is no canonical basis. The tangent space is the abstract home of directional derivatives, and the gradient — which secretly used the dot product — splits into a genuinely covariant object (the differential, a covector) and a contravariant one (the gradient vector), a distinction invisible until a metric is in play.

3. **The calculus of variations — extremising over an infinite-dimensional space.** [[Thm - Taylor's Theorem in Several Variables|Taylor's theorem]] to second order gives the finite-dimensional theory of extrema: a critical point is where the gradient vanishes, and the Hessian quadratic form decides whether it is a minimum, maximum, or saddle. The calculus of variations runs this exact argument with the domain $\mathbb{R}^n$ replaced by an infinite-dimensional space of functions. "Set the gradient to zero" becomes the **Euler–Lagrange equation**, the first variation; the Hessian test becomes the second variation. The first-order condition $Df(x_\circ) = 0$ and the convexity-of-Hessian criterion are the finite-dimensional rehearsal — the conceptual content is identical, and only the analysis of the infinite-dimensional space is genuinely new.

4. **Numerical analysis and optimisation — the derivative as the engine of iterative methods.** Newton's method for solving $f(x) = 0$ in several variables is the iteration $x_{k+1} = x_k - Jf(x_k)^{-1} f(x_k)$ — it works by replacing $f$ at each step with its affine approximation $f(x_\circ) + Df_{x_\circ}(h)$ from the definition of the derivative, and solving the linear problem instead. Gradient descent moves in the direction $-\nabla f$, the direction of steepest decrease supplied by the gradient identity. The [[Thm - The Mean Value Inequality|mean value inequality]] is exactly the Lipschitz-gradient bound that convergence proofs for these methods require. The whole subject of smooth optimisation is multivariate differentiation turned into algorithms, and **convex optimisation** is the special case where the Hessian is everywhere positive semidefinite, so every critical point is a global minimum.

# Insights

**The derivative is a linear map, and that single reframing is the whole topic.** In one variable the derivative is a number, and it is easy to mistake the number for the essence. The essence is the *linear approximation*: $f(x_\circ + h) \approx f(x_\circ) + (\text{linear in } h)$. In one variable the only linear maps $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ are multiplication by a scalar, so the linear map and the number coincide and the distinction is invisible. In several variables the linear maps $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ form a rich space, and the derivative is one of them — the unique one that approximates $f$ to first order in every direction simultaneously. Once this is internalised, every theorem in the topic becomes a statement about linear maps with a remainder, and every theorem in single-variable calculus reappears as the special case $n = m = 1$. The chain rule is the cleanest example: $D(g \circ f) = Dg \circ Df$ is *obvious* for linear maps (the composite of linear approximations is the linear approximation of the composite), and the proof is just the verification that the remainders do not spoil this. The reason to insist on "linear map" rather than "Jacobian matrix" is that the matrix depends on a choice of basis and the linear map does not — and the moment you move to manifolds, where no basis is canonical, only the basis-free statement survives.

**Partial derivatives are coordinates of a candidate, not the derivative itself — and the gap between them is a strict three-step ladder.** There are three conditions, and they are genuinely different. *Partials exist*: weakest, says only that $f$ is well-behaved along the $n$ axes, and is compatible with $f$ being discontinuous. *Differentiable*: $f$ has a genuine linear approximation, valid in every direction; this implies continuity and implies the partials exist and assemble into the Jacobian, but is strictly stronger than "partials exist". *Continuously differentiable ($C^1$)*: the partials exist and vary continuously; this implies differentiability and is strictly stronger again. The two strict gaps each have a canonical witness. Between "partials exist" and "differentiable" sits $xy/(x^2+y^2)$, with both partials zero at the origin but discontinuous there. Between "differentiable" and "$C^1$" sits a function like $x^2 \sin(1/x)$ promoted to two variables — differentiable everywhere but with a partial derivative that oscillates and fails to be continuous at the origin. Knowing where each implication holds, and being able to name the counterexample for each that does not, is the precise knowledge that lets you generalise these ideas without error.

**The mean value theorem splits in two when the output is a vector, and the inequality is the half that survives.** In one variable, the mean value theorem is an *equality*: there is an exact intermediate point. This equality is a luxury of scalar outputs, and it is genuinely lost for vector-valued functions — the unit-circle curve $\gamma(t) = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$ returns to its start, so $\gamma(1) - \gamma(0) = 0$, while $\gamma'$ never vanishes, so no intermediate point can possibly satisfy a mean value equality. The reason is structural: the equality demands a single $t$ that works for all $m$ components at once, and there is no mechanism forcing the components to agree on one. What survives is the *inequality* $|f(x) - f(y)| \le \sup\|Df\| \cdot |x - y|$, and it survives precisely because an inequality is a one-sided, "for all components at once" statement that an upper bound can satisfy without any coordination. This is a recurring pattern in analysis: when an exact identity fails in a more general setting, the salvageable remnant is almost always the corresponding inequality, and the inequality is usually all the applications needed anyway. Dieudonné famously built his treatise on the inequality alone.

**Every theorem here is a single one-variable theorem transported along a line — and the transport is the chain rule.** The deep unity of the topic is that the genuinely new content is concentrated in two definitions (the total derivative and the $C^k$ classes) and one theorem (the chain rule). Everything else is single-variable calculus carried into $\mathbb{R}^n$ by the same device: given a multivariate problem, restrict $f$ to the segment $x_\circ + tv$, obtain a one-variable function $\varphi(t)$, apply the Analysis I theorem to $\varphi$, and translate back using $\varphi'(t) = Df_{x_\circ + tv}(v)$ — which is the chain rule. The mean value theorem is the one-variable mean value theorem applied to $\varphi$. Taylor's theorem is one-variable Taylor applied to $\varphi$, with the multinomial theorem sorting out the coefficients. Even Schwarz's theorem is the one-variable mean value theorem applied twice along the edges of a small square. This is why the chain rule is the load-bearing result: it is the bridge over which all of one-variable calculus walks into several variables. Master the restrict-to-a-line move and you can rederive the rest of the topic on demand.
