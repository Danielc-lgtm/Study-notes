# Notation

Throughout, $J$ is a small index category and $D : J \to \mathcal{C}$ a diagram. We write $\mathbf{Set}$ for the category of sets, $1 = \{*\}$ for a one-point (terminal) set, and $\mathcal{C}(X, Y)$ for hom-sets. For a small category $\mathcal{A}$, the [[Def - Functor Category|functor category]] is $[\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$ (also $\mathcal{D}^{\mathcal{A}}$), and the **evaluation functor** at $a \in \mathcal{A}$ is $\mathrm{ev}_a : [\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}] \to \mathcal{D}$, $F \mapsto F(a)$. A **presheaf category** is $[\mathcal{A}^{op}, \mathbf{Set}]$. The full registry is on [[Category Theory III — Limits and Colimits]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (limits in $\mathbf{Set}$).** $\mathbf{Set}$ is [[Def - Complete and Cocomplete Category|complete and cocomplete]]. For a small diagram $D : J \to \mathbf{Set}$, the limit is the set of cones with apex the terminal set, equivalently the set of **compatible families**:
> $$\lim D \;=\; \Big\{\, (x_j)_{j \in J} \in \textstyle\prod_{j} D_j \;:\; D(f)(x_j) = x_k \text{ for every } f : j \to k \text{ in } J \,\Big\} \;\subseteq\; \prod_j D_j,$$
> with limit projections $(x_j)_j \mapsto x_k$. The colimit is the quotient $\big(\coprod_j D_j\big)\big/\!\sim$ by the equivalence relation generated by $x_j \sim D(f)(x_j)$ for $f : j \to k$.

> **Theorem (limits in functor categories).** Let $\mathcal{A}$ be small and $\mathcal{D}$ a category. If $\mathcal{D}$ has all $J$-shaped limits (resp. colimits), then so does $[\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$, and they are computed **pointwise**: for a diagram $D : J \to [\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$ and each object $a \in \mathcal{A}$,
> $$(\lim_J D)(a) \;\cong\; \lim_J \big(D(-)(a)\big), \qquad (\operatorname{colim}_J D)(a) \;\cong\; \operatorname{colim}_J \big(D(-)(a)\big).$$
> Equivalently, every evaluation functor $\mathrm{ev}_a$ preserves all limits and colimits that exist in $\mathcal{D}$, and the family $(\mathrm{ev}_a)_a$ jointly creates them.

> **Corollary.** Every presheaf category $[\mathcal{A}^{op}, \mathbf{Set}]$ is complete and cocomplete, with all limits and colimits computed objectwise (pointwise) in $\mathbf{Set}$.

# Motivation

Two questions sit behind this theorem. First: where do limits *actually live* — what is the ground truth that all the abstract universal properties are shadows of? The answer is $\mathbf{Set}$. A limit in $\mathbf{Set}$ is a perfectly concrete thing, the set of compatible families, and by the [[Thm - Representable Functors Preserve Limits|representability]] of limits every limit in every category is *computed* by reducing to this set-level construction. This is the first half: $\mathbf{Set}$ is complete, and its limits are compatible families.

Second: the most important categories in practice are not $\mathbf{Set}$ but categories of *functors* — [[Def - Presheaf|presheaves]], diagrams, representations, sheaves before sheafification, simplicial sets. One needs to take limits there constantly. The miracle is that you never have to think hard: limits in a functor category are computed *one object at a time*, pointwise, reusing whatever you know about the target category. The limit of a diagram of presheaves is, evaluated at each object $a$, just the limit of the values at $a$ — a limit of sets. This "pointwise" principle is what makes functor categories tractable, and it is why presheaf categories are automatically complete and cocomplete, inheriting everything from $\mathbf{Set}$.

Together these say: the ground truth is compatible families in $\mathbf{Set}$, and the lift to functor categories costs nothing because it happens pointwise. This is the structural reason the entire subject can be grounded on $\mathbf{Set}$ and then exported everywhere by Yoneda.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The hypotheses are mild — "$\mathbf{Set}$" or "$[\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$ with $\mathcal{D}$ having the limits". The skill is recognising a category as (equivalent to) a functor category so the pointwise principle applies.

The first disguised source is **a presheaf or diagram category in disguise**. Many categories are secretly $[\mathcal{A}^{op}, \mathbf{Set}]$: graphs are presheaves on the two-object "walking edge" category, $G$-sets are functors from the one-object category $\mathbf{B}G$, simplicial sets are presheaves on the simplex category $\Delta$, quivers are presheaves on a small category. The non-obvious step is identifying the indexing category $\mathcal{A}$; once found, limits are pointwise and completeness is free. *Example problem:* compute the limit of a diagram of directed graphs by computing it separately on vertices and on edges.

The second disguised source is **a slice category or comma category over a complete base**. The forgetful functor $\mathcal{C}/c \to \mathcal{C}$ [[Def - Preservation, Reflection, and Creation of Limits|creates]] limits, and $[\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$ over complete $\mathcal{D}$ is complete; recognising "objects over $c$" or "$\mathcal{A}$-indexed families in $\mathcal{D}$" puts you in scope. The non-obviousness is that the limit's structure (the slice data, the functoriality) comes along for free from the pointwise computation. *Example problem:* show the category of pointed sets $\mathbf{Set}_*$ is complete by computing limits in $\mathbf{Set}$ and tracking the basepoint.

The third disguised source is **an algebraic category whose forgetful functor lands in $\mathbf{Set}$ and creates limits**. Groups, rings, modules: their limits are computed on underlying sets as compatible families, then equipped with the unique compatible structure. The non-obvious step is that the compatible-family description from this theorem *is* the underlying set of the limit, so the structural limit is read off the set-level one. *Example problem:* identify the inverse limit $\varprojlim \mathbb{Z}/p^n = \mathbb{Z}_p$ as the set of compatible residue sequences, then note the ring structure is forced.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "limits in $\mathbf{Set}$ are compatible families; limits in functor categories are pointwise".

Combine with **the equalizer-of-products formula**. The compatible-family description is exactly what [[Thm - Products and Equalizers Give All Limits]] yields when specialised to $\mathbf{Set}$: the product of vertices cut down by the equalizer enforcing the edges. The further result is a concrete algorithm for any limit — list the vertices, take the product, impose the edge equations — that works in $\mathbf{Set}$ and, by representability, computes hom-sets into limits everywhere.

Combine with **the Yoneda embedding**. Since the [[Def - The Yoneda Embedding|Yoneda embedding]] $\mathbf{y} : \mathcal{C} \to [\mathcal{C}^{op}, \mathbf{Set}]$ lands in a complete and cocomplete presheaf category, and preserves limits, one obtains a complete *enlargement* of any small category — the free cocompletion. The further result is that any small category embeds into a bicomplete one, the starting point for the theory of **presheaf topoi** and for defining colimits "freely". 

Combine with **pointwise preservation by evaluation**. Knowing $\mathrm{ev}_a$ preserves limits and colimits lets you transport any limit/colimit computation in a functor category to the target, object by object. The further result is that natural transformations, being families of components, interact with limits componentwise — for example a natural transformation of diagrams induces the limit map pointwise — which is the technical content behind $\lim$ being a functor on $[\mathcal{J}, \mathcal{C}]$.

# Why Is It True

**Limits in $\mathbf{Set}$.** Use the representability definition: a limit represents the functor $X \mapsto \mathrm{Cone}(X, D)$. Take $X$ to be the terminal set $1 = \{*\}$. A cone over $D$ with apex $1$ is a family of maps $(\lambda_j : 1 \to D_j)$ — but a map out of a one-point set is the choice of a single element, $\lambda_j \leftrightarrow x_j \in D_j$ — subject to the cone condition $D(f)(x_j) = x_k$. So a cone with apex $1$ *is* a compatible family. Now $\mathbf{Set}(1, S) \cong S$ naturally, so the representing object — the limit — is literally the *set* of cones with apex $1$, i.e. the set of compatible families. The projections read off the components.

> **In $\mathbf{Set}$, "a point of the limit" = "a cone with apex a point" = "a compatible family".** The limit is the set of compatible families because maps out of a point are elements.

The colimit is the dual: a cocone *out of* $D$ to a set $X$ is a family $D_j \to X$ identifying $D(f)(x_j)$ with $x_j$, so the universal such $X$ is the disjoint union of the $D_j$ with those identifications imposed — the quotient by the generated equivalence relation.

**Limits in functor categories.** A limit in $[\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$ must be a functor $L : \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{D}$ together with a natural family of projections. The key realisation is that a *natural transformation* into $L$ is exactly a family of components, one per object $a$, that is itself natural. So to make $L$ the limit it suffices to make each value $L(a)$ the limit of the values $D(-)(a)$, and to define $L$ on morphisms by the induced maps between those limits — naturality of everything is automatic because it is checked componentwise and the diagram's own naturality supplies the needed squares. In other words, the limit *cone* in $[\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$ is the pointwise limit cone, assembled into a functor. This is exactly the statement that each evaluation $\mathrm{ev}_a$ preserves limits and the family jointly creates them: a limit upstairs exists iff every pointwise limit exists, and is then their assembly.

The reason this is so clean — and limits in functor categories are *not* mysterious — is that the functor category's structure is itself defined pointwise (objects are functors, morphisms are natural = componentwise-with-squares transformations). A construction defined pointwise commutes with pointwise computation.

---

# What Makes This Hard

For the $\mathbf{Set}$ statement, the subtlety is the *colimit*: the equivalence relation must be **generated** (closed under reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity), not just the raw relation $\{(x_j, D(f)(x_j))\}$ — beginners forget the transitive closure and get the wrong quotient. For the functor-category statement, the trap is believing the limit functor $L$ is "obviously" a functor: one must check that $L$'s action on morphisms of $\mathcal{A}$, defined by universal property between pointwise limits, respects composition and identities, and that the projections are natural. The conceptual hurdle is keeping two index categories straight — $J$ (the shape of the diagram being limited) and $\mathcal{A}$ (the domain of the functors) — and remembering that the pointwise limit is over $J$, performed separately at each object of $\mathcal{A}$.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:**
For $\mathbf{Set}$, evaluate the representability definition at the terminal set and recognise cones-with-apex-a-point as compatible families. For functor categories, define the candidate limit pointwise, make it a functor by the induced maps, and check its universal property reduces to the pointwise ones.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Cones with apex $1$ are compatible families.** Show $\mathrm{Cone}(1, D) \cong \{$compatible families$\}$.
   - *Hint:* A map $1 \to D_j$ is an element of $D_j$; the cone condition becomes $D(f)(x_j) = x_k$.
   - *Why needed:* It identifies the limit set with compatible families.

2. **The limit set represents $\mathrm{Cone}(-, D)$.** Show the set of compatible families, with projections, is the limit.
   - *Hint:* $\mathbf{Set}(X, \lim D) \cong \mathrm{Cone}(X, D)$ via $x \mapsto (\pi_j(x))$; check naturality in $X$.
   - *Why needed:* It verifies the universal property, not just the underlying set.

3. **Define the pointwise limit functor.** For $D : J \to [\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$, set $L(a) = \lim_J D(-)(a)$ and $L(\alpha)$ the induced map for $\alpha : a \to a'$.
   - *Hint:* The maps $D(-)(\alpha)$ form a map of $J$-diagrams, inducing $\lim$ maps; functoriality of $L$ follows from uniqueness of induced maps.
   - *Why needed:* The limit in $[\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$ must be a functor.

4. **Universal property reduces pointwise.** Show a cone over $D$ in $[\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$ is a pointwise-compatible family of cones, factoring uniquely through $L$.
   - *Hint:* A natural transformation into $L$ is a family of components, natural and each factoring through the pointwise limit.
   - *Why needed:* It proves $L$ is the limit and that $\mathrm{ev}_a$ preserves it.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Elements of a set are maps out of a point
> **Statement:** For any set $S$, the evaluation $\phi \mapsto \phi(*)$ is a natural bijection $\mathbf{Set}(1, S) \cong S$.
>
> **Hint:** A function from a one-point set is determined by the single value it takes.
>
> **Why needed:** It converts the abstract "cone with apex $1$" into a concrete tuple of elements.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > A function $\phi : \{*\} \to S$ is determined by $\phi(*) \in S$, and every element arises this way, so $\phi \mapsto \phi(*)$ is a bijection. Naturality: for $h : S \to S'$, $(h \circ \phi)(*) = h(\phi(*))$, so the bijection commutes with $h$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Cones with apex $1$ are compatible families
> **Statement:** For $D : J \to \mathbf{Set}$, the set $\mathrm{Cone}(1, D)$ is in natural bijection with the set of compatible families $\{(x_j) \in \prod_j D_j : D(f)(x_j) = x_k\}$.
>
> **Hint:** Apply Lemma 1 leg by leg; the naturality square of the cone becomes the compatibility equation.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the explicit description of the limit set.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > A cone $\lambda : \Delta_1 \Rightarrow D$ is a family $\lambda_j : 1 \to D_j$; by Lemma 1 each is an element $x_j = \lambda_j(*) \in D_j$. The cone condition $D(f) \circ \lambda_j = \lambda_k$ evaluated at $*$ reads $D(f)(x_j) = x_k$. Conversely any compatible family defines a cone. The bijection is natural.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Evaluation functors create pointwise limits
> **Statement:** For small $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{D}$ with $J$-limits, define $L : \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{D}$ by $L(a) = \lim_J D(-)(a)$. Then $L$ is the limit of $D : J \to [\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$, and each $\mathrm{ev}_a$ sends the limit cone to the limit cone of $D(-)(a)$.
>
> **Hint:** A natural transformation into $L$ is a family of components, natural; each component factors uniquely through the pointwise limit, and the factorisations are automatically natural.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the functor-category half of the theorem.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Define $L(a) = \lim_J D(-)(a)$ with pointwise projections $\pi^a_j : L(a) \to D_j(a)$. For $\alpha : a \to a'$ in $\mathcal{A}$, the maps $D_j(\alpha)$ form a morphism of $J$-diagrams $D(-)(a) \to D(-)(a')$, inducing a unique $L(\alpha) : L(a) \to L(a')$ commuting with projections; uniqueness gives $L(\alpha'\alpha) = L(\alpha')L(\alpha)$ and $L(1_a) = 1$, so $L$ is a functor and the $\pi_j = (\pi^a_j)_a$ are natural. Given a cone $(\mu_j : G \Rightarrow D_j)$ in $[\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$, at each $a$ it is a cone over $D(-)(a)$, inducing a unique $u_a : G(a) \to L(a)$; naturality of $u = (u_a)$ follows from uniqueness of the pointwise induced maps, and $u$ is the unique cone morphism $G \to L$. Hence $L = \lim_J D$, computed pointwise. The colimit case is dual.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> **Part 1 — $\mathbf{Set}$ is complete with compatible-family limits.**
>
> *Step 0 — the candidate.* Let $D : J \to \mathbf{Set}$. Define $L = \{(x_j) \in \prod_j D_j : D(f)(x_j) = x_k \text{ for all } f : j\to k\}$ with projections $\pi_k(x) = x_k$. The product $\prod_j D_j$ exists in $\mathbf{Set}$ (functions $J \to \bigcup D_j$ with $x_j \in D_j$), and $L$ is a subset, so $L$ exists.
>
> *Step 1 — $L$ represents $\mathrm{Cone}(-,D)$.* For any set $X$, a cone $(\lambda_j : X \to D_j)$ satisfies $D(f)\lambda_j = \lambda_k$, so $x \mapsto (\lambda_j(x))_j$ lands in $L$ and defines $u : X \to L$ with $\pi_j u = \lambda_j$; conversely any $u : X \to L$ yields a cone $\pi_j u$. This bijection $\mathbf{Set}(X, L) \cong \mathrm{Cone}(X, D)$ is natural in $X$, so $L = \lim D$. (By Lemma 2 the special case $X = 1$ identifies $L$ itself as the set of compatible families.) Hence $\mathbf{Set}$ is complete.
>
> *Step 2 — cocompleteness.* Dually, $\operatorname{colim} D = (\coprod_j D_j)/\sim$ where $\sim$ is the smallest equivalence relation with $x_j \sim D(f)(x_j)$; the injections and universal property are verified by the dual argument. So $\mathbf{Set}$ is cocomplete.
>
> **Part 2 — limits in functor categories are pointwise.**
>
> By Lemma 3, if $\mathcal{D}$ has all $J$-limits then $[\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{D}]$ has all $J$-limits, computed by $(\lim_J D)(a) = \lim_J D(-)(a)$, and each $\mathrm{ev}_a$ preserves them; the family $(\mathrm{ev}_a)$ jointly reflects (a cone is a limit iff it is pointwise) hence creates them. The colimit statement is dual.
>
> **Part 3 — presheaf categories.** Taking $\mathcal{D} = \mathbf{Set}$ (complete and cocomplete by Part 1) and replacing $\mathcal{A}$ by $\mathcal{A}^{op}$, Part 2 gives that $[\mathcal{A}^{op}, \mathbf{Set}]$ is complete and cocomplete with all (co)limits computed objectwise. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Limits of representations.** A representation of a group $G$ is a functor $\mathbf{B}G \to \mathbf{Vect}_k$, so the category of representations is a functor category. By this theorem, limits and colimits of representations are computed pointwise — on the single underlying vector space — and the $G$-action is assembled automatically. The application is non-obvious because one thinks of representations as structured objects, not as functors; recognising $\mathbf{B}G$ as the domain makes completeness immediate.

**Sheaves before sheafification.** The category of [[Def - Presheaf|presheaves]] of sets on a topological space is $[\mathcal{O}(X)^{op}, \mathbf{Set}]$, hence complete and cocomplete with pointwise (open-set-wise) limits and colimits. Sheaves form a reflective subcategory; their limits agree with presheaf limits (computed objectwise) but their colimits require sheafification. The non-obvious payoff is that *limits* of sheaves are easy (pointwise) while *colimits* are subtle — a fact this theorem isolates precisely.

**Simplicial objects and the bar construction.** A simplicial object in $\mathcal{C}$ is a functor $\Delta^{op} \to \mathcal{C}$; limits and colimits of diagrams of simplicial objects are computed levelwise (pointwise over $\Delta^{op}$). This is the technical underpinning of geometric realization and the bar construction in **homotopy theory** (Chapter VI/VII). The application is non-obvious because simplicial machinery looks intricate, but its limits are governed by the pointwise principle.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Products and Equalizers Give All Limits|Products and equalizers give all limits]]** — the $\mathbf{Set}$ statement is its specialisation. The equalizer-of-products construction, read in $\mathbf{Set}$, produces exactly the subset of the product of vertices cut out by the cone equations — which is the compatible-family description. The bridge: the abstract generating construction *becomes* the concrete compatible-family set once products are cartesian and equalizers are agreement-subsets.

- **[[Thm - Representable Functors Preserve Limits|Representable functors preserve limits]]** — the reason the $\mathbf{Set}$ computation governs *all* categories. Since $\mathcal{C}(X, \lim D) \cong \lim \mathcal{C}(X, D_j)$ and the right side is a limit *in $\mathbf{Set}$*, the compatible-family description computes the hom-sets into any limit. The bridge: limits everywhere are detected by mapping in, and maps-in form a limit of sets computed as compatible families.

- **[[Def - The Yoneda Embedding|The Yoneda embedding]] and the free cocompletion** — the presheaf category $[\mathcal{C}^{op}, \mathbf{Set}]$, complete and cocomplete by this theorem, is the **free cocompletion** of $\mathcal{C}$: every small category embeds via $\mathbf{y}$ into a bicomplete category, and every functor to a cocomplete category extends along $\mathbf{y}$ to a colimit-preserving functor. The bridge: pointwise completeness of presheaves is what makes $\mathbf{y}$ a universal completion, the foundation of presheaf topoi and the functor-of-points view of schemes.

# Unlocked by This

> [!note]- Algebraic geometry background
> The **functor of points** view models a geometric object as a functor $X : \mathbf{CRing} \to \mathbf{Set}$; such functors are objects of a functor category $[\mathbf{CRing}, \mathbf{Set}]$ (equivalently presheaves on affine schemes). By this theorem that category is complete and cocomplete with limits computed pointwise: the limit of a diagram of "functors of points" is, at each ring $R$, the limit of their $R$-points in $\mathbf{Set}$. This is why fibre products of schemes can be defined pointwise — $(X \times_Z Y)(R) = X(R) \times_{Z(R)} Y(R)$, a pullback of sets — and is the technical reason the [[Def - Pullback and Pushout|pullback]] of schemes exists and is computed ring-by-ring. See [[Ex - Fibre products of schemes are pullbacks]].

> [!tip] Grothendieck Topoi and Sheaf Cohomology *(from Topos Theory)*
> A **Grothendieck topos** is a left-exact reflective subcategory of a presheaf category; this theorem provides the ambient completeness and cocompleteness, with sheaf limits inherited pointwise and sheaf colimits obtained by sheafifying. **Sheaf cohomology** is the derived functor of the (pointwise-computed) global sections limit, so the pointwise principle is the substrate on which derived functors are built.
