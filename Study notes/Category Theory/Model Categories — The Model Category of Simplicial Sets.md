# Notation Registry

The standing convention of this chapter is that **everything happens in $\mathbf{sSet} = [\Delta^{op}, \mathbf{Set}]$**, the category of [[Def - Simplicial Set|simplicial sets]], and that the model structure meant by "the model structure on $\mathbf{sSet}$" is always the **Kan–Quillen** one: cofibrations are monomorphisms, fibrations are Kan fibrations, weak equivalences are the maps whose geometric realisation is a weak homotopy equivalence. When a competing model structure (the Joyal structure, which has the *same* cofibrations but more fibrant objects) is meant, it is named explicitly. A second convention: $|{-}|$ and $\mathrm{Sing}$ always refer to geometric realisation and the singular nerve of [[Thm - Geometric Realization is Left Adjoint to the Singular Nerve]].

- $\Delta$ — the **simplex category**: objects $[n] = \{0 < 1 < \dots < n\}$, morphisms order-preserving maps; $d^i, s^i$ the cofaces and codegeneracies
- $X, Y, K, L$ — [[Def - Simplicial Set|simplicial sets]]; $X_n$ the set of $n$-simplices; $d_i, s_i$ the face and degeneracy maps
- $\Delta^n = \Delta(-, [n])$ — the **standard $n$-simplex** (representable presheaf)
- $\partial\Delta^n$ — the **boundary**, union of the $n+1$ faces; $\Lambda^n_k \subseteq \Delta^n$ — the **$k$th horn**, all faces but the $k$th
- $\Lambda^n_k$ is an **inner horn** when $0 < k < n$, an **outer horn** when $k \in \{0, n\}$
- $\mathbf{sSet}(K, X)$ — the set of simplicial maps $K \to X$; $X^K$ — the **internal hom** (function complex), $(X^K)_n = \mathbf{sSet}(K \times \Delta^n, X)$
- $X \rightarrowtail Y$ — a cofibration (here: a monomorphism); $X \twoheadrightarrow Y$ — a fibration (here: a Kan fibration); $\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }$ — a weak equivalence
- $\mathrm{LLP}(\mathcal{S})$, $\mathrm{RLP}(\mathcal{S})$ — the maps with the left / right [[Def - Lifting Property and the Retract Argument|lifting property]] against a class $\mathcal{S}$
- $I = \{\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n : n \ge 0\}$ — the **generating cofibrations**
- $J = \{\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta^n : n \ge 1,\ 0 \le k \le n\}$ — the **generating trivial cofibrations** (horn inclusions)
- $p : E \to B$ — a Kan fibration with **fibre** $F_b = p^{-1}(b)$ over a vertex $b \in B_0$
- $\pi_n(X, x)$ — the **$n$th simplicial homotopy group** of a Kan complex $X$ at a vertex $x \in X_0$
- $\sim$ — the **simplicial homotopy relation** on simplices (an equivalence relation when $X$ is Kan, fibrewise when $p$ is a Kan fibration)
- $|X|$ — the **geometric realisation**; $\mathrm{Sing}(Y)_n = \mathbf{Top}(|\Delta^n|, Y)$ — the **singular nerve**; $\simeq_Q$ — Quillen equivalence
- $\mathbf{Top}$ — a convenient category of spaces (compactly generated weak Hausdorff)

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: the category of [[Def - Simplicial Set|simplicial sets]] is the combinatorial model of the homotopy theory of spaces, and a [[Def - Model Category|model structure]] is the machine that makes "combinatorial model of" a precise, computable statement. We already know from the Higher Categories chapter that a [[Def - Topological Space|space]] can be probed by its singular simplices and that a [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complex]] is the combinatorial shadow of a space. What was missing was a framework that turns "$\mathbf{sSet}$ behaves like $\mathbf{Top}$" into a theorem with content. That framework is Quillen's, and this chapter is its first and most important worked example.

The reason simplicial sets are worth the trouble is that $\mathbf{Top}$ is, for the homotopy theorist, an awkward category. It has all the limits and colimits one wants, but its objects carry point-set pathologies (non-CW spaces, wild local behaviour) that the homotopy type cannot see and the constructions must nonetheless cope with. Simplicial sets discard the point-set baggage entirely: a simplicial set is a presheaf on a small category, so $\mathbf{sSet}$ is a presheaf topos — complete, cocomplete, cartesian closed, with a generator and a small set of "cells". Everything is finite and combinatorial in each dimension. The bet of this chapter is that this clean algebraic category nonetheless carries *exactly* the homotopy theory of spaces, and the payoff is that one can compute homotopy groups, do obstruction theory, and build mapping spaces by manipulating sets and functions.

The structural backbone of the chapter is the three-class data of the Kan–Quillen model structure:
$$\underbrace{\text{cofibrations} = \text{monomorphisms}}_{\text{generated by } \partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n} \qquad \underbrace{\text{fibrations} = \text{Kan fibrations}}_{= \mathrm{RLP}(\text{horns})} \qquad \underbrace{\text{weak equivalences}}_{=\ |{-}|\text{-weak-homotopy-equivalences}}$$
Everything below is the unpacking of this line. The cofibrations are the easiest class — a map of simplicial sets is a cofibration if and only if it is injective in every dimension, so *every* simplicial set is cofibrant (the map from the empty simplicial set is always mono). The fibrations are the [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|Kan fibrations]], the relative version of the horn-filling condition that defined Kan complexes, and the fibrant objects are exactly the Kan complexes. The weak equivalences are pinned down by transporting to $\mathbf{Top}$ and asking for a weak homotopy equivalence there — but one of the deep facts of the subject is that this can be characterised *internally*, without leaving $\mathbf{sSet}$, as the maps inducing isomorphisms on [[Def - Simplicial Homotopy Group|simplicial homotopy groups]].

Two technical pillars hold the structure up, and they occupy §2 and §3. The first is the theory of **anodyne extensions**: the trivial cofibrations turn out to be exactly the smallest class of monomorphisms containing the horn inclusions and closed under the operations a left class must be closed under (pushout, transfinite composition, retract). This is the Gabriel–Zisman characterisation, and it is what makes the lifting axioms checkable. The second is the theory of **minimal fibrations**: every [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|Kan fibration]] is fibrewise homotopy equivalent to a [[Def - Minimal Fibration|minimal]] one, and minimal fibrations are *fibre bundles* in the simplicial sense. This is the combinatorial replacement for the topological fact that fibrations are locally trivial, and it is the engine of the proof that $|{-}| \dashv \mathrm{Sing}$ is a [[Def - Quillen Adjunction and Quillen Equivalence|Quillen equivalence]] — the theorem that says $\mathbf{sSet}$ and $\mathbf{Top}$ are the same homotopy theory.

This topic assumes the reader has refreshed the Higher Categories material on [[Def - Simplicial Set|simplicial sets]], horns, [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complexes]], and the [[Thm - Geometric Realization is Left Adjoint to the Singular Nerve|realisation–singular adjunction]], and the Model Categories material on the [[Def - Model Category|axioms]], [[Def - Cofibrant and Fibrant Objects|cofibrant/fibrant objects]], [[Def - Lifting Property and the Retract Argument|lifting properties]], and [[Def - Quillen Adjunction and Quillen Equivalence|Quillen adjunctions]]. From algebraic topology, the classical notions of [[Def - Higher Homotopy Group|homotopy group]] and weak homotopy equivalence are the targets the combinatorial definitions are built to recover.

# Concept Map

## §3.1 The Kan-Quillen Model Structure

- **[[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension]]**
	- A map $p : E \to B$ of [[Def - Simplicial Set|simplicial sets]] is a **Kan fibration** if it has the right [[Def - Lifting Property and the Retract Argument|lifting property]] against every horn inclusion $\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta^n$ ($n \ge 1$, all $k$): every horn in $E$ lying over a simplex of $B$ fills. A simplicial set $X$ is a [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complex]] exactly when $X \to *$ is a Kan fibration. An **anodyne extension** is a member of the saturation of the horn inclusions — the smallest class containing them and closed under pushout, transfinite composition, and retract. Anodyne maps are precisely the maps with the left lifting property against all Kan fibrations.
	- This is a compound definition: the fibration and the trivial-cofibration class are defined together because each is the lifting-complement of the other.

- **[[Thm - Simplicial Sets Form a Model Category]]**
	- The three classes — monomorphisms (cofibrations), [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|Kan fibrations]] (fibrations), and maps whose [[Thm - Geometric Realization is Left Adjoint to the Singular Nerve|realisation]] is a weak homotopy equivalence (weak equivalences) — satisfy Quillen's axioms MC1–MC5. The fibrant objects are exactly the [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complexes]] and every object is cofibrant. The two factorisations come from the small object argument applied to the boundary inclusions $\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n$ and the horn inclusions $\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta^n$, so the structure is cofibrantly generated.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Cofibrant Generation in Practice *(from Model Categories — Cofibrantly Generated)*
> The generating sets $I = \{\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$ and $J = \{\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$ make $\mathbf{sSet}$ the prototype **cofibrantly generated model category**. Every transferred and localised model structure built on $\mathbf{sSet}$ — and there are many — inherits its generation from these two sets, which is why the small-object argument is the first tool in the subject.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Joyal Model Structure *(from Higher Category Theory)*
> Keep the cofibrations (monomorphisms) but enlarge the fibrant objects from Kan complexes to **quasi-categories** by testing only *inner* horns, and you get the **Joyal model structure**, whose homotopy theory is that of [[Def - Quasi-Category|∞-categories]] rather than of spaces. The Kan–Quillen structure is the $\infty$-groupoid special case of the Joyal one.

- **[[Ex - Monomorphisms are the cofibrations]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Identify the cofibrations with the monomorphisms by the skeletal cell-attachment construction, and deduce that every simplicial set is cofibrant. The technique is the two-inclusion saturation argument: monomorphisms are saturated and contain the boundary inclusions, while any monomorphism is built cell-by-cell as a pushout of boundary inclusions.

- **[[Ex - Kan fibrations are closed under pullback]]** (⭐)
	- Show that a [[Def - Pullback and Pushout|pullback]] of a Kan fibration is a Kan fibration by a single diagram chase, and deduce that the fibre of a Kan fibration is a Kan complex. The drill is the universal fact that right lifting classes are closed under pullback, and that fibres are pullbacks along a point.

- **[[Ex - Trivial fibrations lift against all monomorphisms]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Promote "lifts against the generating boundary inclusions" to "lifts against every monomorphism", and conclude that a trivial fibration is a Kan fibration. The technique is propagating a lifting property from the generators to the full saturated class through pushout, transfinite composition, and retract.

## §3.2 Anodyne Extensions and Simplicial Homotopy Groups

- **[[Def - Simplicial Homotopy Group]]**
	- For a [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complex]] $X$ with basepoint $x \in X_0$, the **$n$th simplicial homotopy group** $\pi_n(X, x)$ is the set of $n$-simplices $\sigma$ with totally degenerate boundary (every face $d_i\sigma$ equal to the degenerate simplex on $x$), modulo the [[Def - Homotopy|simplicial homotopy]] relation. The Kan condition is exactly what makes this relation an equivalence relation and supplies the group operation by horn-filling. The construction recovers the classical [[Def - Higher Homotopy Group|homotopy groups]]: $\pi_n(\mathrm{Sing}\,Y, y) \cong \pi_n(Y, y)$, and a map of Kan complexes is a weak equivalence if and only if it induces isomorphisms on all $\pi_n$.

- **[[Thm - Simplicial Sets Form a Model Category]]** *(continued — anodyne characterisation of the weak equivalences)*
	- Between [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complexes]], the weak equivalences are exactly the maps inducing isomorphisms on all [[Def - Simplicial Homotopy Group|simplicial homotopy groups]], so the model structure can be defined entirely inside $\mathbf{sSet}$ without reference to $\mathbf{Top}$. The trivial cofibrations are precisely the **anodyne extensions** (the Gabriel–Zisman theorem), which is what reduces "is this a trivial cofibration?" to "is this built from horn inclusions by pushout, transfinite composition, and retract?".

> [!tip] Unlocked: Fibrewise Homotopy Theory *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> The relative version of the homotopy relation — homotopy of simplices *over a base*, controlled by a [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|Kan fibration]] $p : E \to B$ — is the combinatorial form of **fibrewise (parametrised) homotopy theory**, where the long exact sequence of a fibration $\dots \to \pi_n(F) \to \pi_n(E) \to \pi_n(B) \to \pi_{n-1}(F) \to \dots$ lives. The anodyne machinery is what makes the connecting maps well-defined.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Bousfield Localization *(from Model Categories)*
> Once the weak equivalences of $\mathbf{sSet}$ are characterised by homotopy groups, one can enlarge them — invert a chosen set of maps — to obtain a **Bousfield localization**, the model-categorical way to build localised homotopy theories (rational spaces, $K(n)$-local spectra, **simplicial presheaves** for sheaf cohomology). The anodyne extensions are the fixed left class these localisations are built on top of.

- **[[Ex - The simplicial homotopy relation is transitive only for Kan complexes]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove the homotopy relation on spheroids is reflexive for any simplicial set but transitive only when the object is Kan, pinpointing the single horn-fill where fibrancy is used; with the non-Kan counterexample $\Delta^1$, whose vertex relation is not symmetric. This is why $\pi_n$ is defined only for Kan complexes.

- **[[Ex - Anodyne extensions are trivial cofibrations]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove the Gabriel–Zisman characterisation that anodyne maps are exactly the trivial cofibrations, via the [[Thm - The Retract Argument|retract argument]] applied to a small-object-argument factorisation. The decisive step is that the residual fibration is *trivial*, so the cofibration lifts against it and is exhibited as a retract of the anodyne factor.

- **[[Ex - The fundamental group of the nerve of a group]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute all simplicial homotopy groups of the [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|nerve]] $N(G)$ of a group: $\pi_1 \cong G$, all others trivial, so $|N(G)| = BG = K(G,1)$. The group operation on $\pi_1$ is read off the unique filler of the inner horn $\Lambda^2_1$ — operations as horn fillers, made concrete.

## §3.3 Minimal Fibrations and Geometric Realization

- **[[Def - Minimal Fibration]]**
	- A [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|Kan fibration]] $p : E \to B$ is **minimal** if any two simplices of $E$ that lie over the same simplex of $B$ and are fibrewise homotopic rel boundary are *equal*. Minimal fibrations are the combinatorial fibre bundles: a minimal fibration is determined up to isomorphism over $B$ by its fibre together with an action of the [[Def - Path-Product and the Fundamental Group|fundamental groupoid]], and is a fibre bundle in the sense that pullback along any simplex of $B$ gives a product. Every Kan fibration retracts onto a minimal fibration, fibrewise homotopy equivalent to it — the simplicial analogue of "compress a fibration to its essential bundle".

- **[[Thm - Geometric Realization is a Quillen Equivalence]]**
	- The adjunction $|{-}| \dashv \mathrm{Sing}$ between $\mathbf{sSet}$ and $\mathbf{Top}$ is a [[Def - Quillen Adjunction and Quillen Equivalence|Quillen equivalence]]: geometric realisation sends monomorphisms to relative cell complexes and weak equivalences to weak homotopy equivalences, $\mathrm{Sing}$ sends [[Def - Topological Space|spaces]] to [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complexes]], and the derived unit and counit are weak equivalences. Hence $\mathrm{Ho}(\mathbf{sSet}) \simeq \mathrm{Ho}(\mathbf{Top})$ — the homotopy theory of simplicial sets *is* the homotopy theory of spaces. The non-trivial input is that $|{-}|$ preserves finite products (a fact that uses [[Def - Minimal Fibration|minimal fibrations]] in Quillen's original proof) and the long exact sequence comparison.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Homotopy Hypothesis *(from Higher Category Theory)*
> The Quillen equivalence $\mathbf{sSet} \simeq_Q \mathbf{Top}$ restricted to fibrant objects says **[[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complexes]] are spaces**, the usable form of the **homotopy hypothesis**: ∞-groupoids are homotopy types. Every subsequent comparison of models for **∞-categories** is proved by a chain of Quillen equivalences anchored on this one.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Simplicial Presheaves and ∞-Topoi *(from Higher Topos Theory)*
> Because every space is modelled by a Kan complex, sheaves *valued in spaces* are modelled by **simplicial presheaves** with a localised (Bousfield) model structure, and their homotopy theories are the **∞-topoi** where nonabelian and derived cohomology live. This chapter is the bottom rung of that ladder.

- **[[Ex - Realization preserves products of standard simplices]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove $|\Delta^m \times \Delta^n| \cong |\Delta^m| \times |\Delta^n|$ by density reduction to representables plus the shuffle triangulation of the prism, and extend to $|X \times Y| \cong |X| \times |Y|$. This is the key non-formal lemma behind the Quillen equivalence, and it explains why one must work in compactly generated spaces.

- **[[Ex - A minimal Kan complex with trivial homotopy is a point]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Prove a connected [[Def - Minimal Fibration|minimal Kan complex]] with vanishing homotopy groups is isomorphic to the point. The mechanism is "homotopy from connectivity, equality from minimality": vanishing $\pi_n$ gives a homotopy to the degenerate simplex, and minimality upgrades it to equality, collapsing the complex by induction on dimension.

- **[[Ex - The unit map into the singular complex is a weak equivalence]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Deduce that the unit $X \to \mathrm{Sing}|X|$ is a weak equivalence from the (assumed) fact that the counit is, using only a triangle identity and two-out-of-three; then assemble the [[Def - Quillen Adjunction and Quillen Equivalence|Quillen equivalence]]. The exercise isolates how one half of an equivalence follows formally from the other.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The problems of this chapter chase a small, recurring set of goals. The most basic is **establishing the lifting/factorisation data of the model structure** — showing a particular class is closed under an operation, or that a map factors as anodyne-then-fibration. A second is **identifying fibrant objects**, that is, recognising when a given simplicial set is a [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complex]], because only fibrant objects have well-behaved homotopy groups and mapping spaces. A third is **computing or comparing homotopy groups**: showing two [[Def - Simplicial Homotopy Group|simplicial homotopy groups]] agree, that a map is a weak equivalence, or that $\pi_n$ of a combinatorial model matches a known topological answer. A fourth is **reducing a fibration to a bundle** — replacing a Kan fibration by a [[Def - Minimal Fibration|minimal]] one to gain rigidity. The fifth and most consequential is **proving an equivalence of homotopy theories**, of which the [[Thm - Geometric Realization is a Quillen Equivalence|realisation–singular Quillen equivalence]] is the archetype. These five — verify the structure, recognise fibrancy, compute $\pi_n$, minimise, equate homotopy theories — recur because each is a way of cashing out the slogan "$\mathbf{sSet}$ is the homotopy theory of spaces" into a checkable statement.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The assumptions are equally stereotyped. **A horn-filling hypothesis is given** — either the full Kan condition or a relative one — which is the richest source, because it instantly licenses all the homotopy-group constructions and the long exact sequence. **A map is a monomorphism** (so a cofibration), which means it can be built up by attaching cells $\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n$ and that it is the left class in any lifting problem. **A map is anodyne**, recognised because it is assembled from horn inclusions by [[Def - Pullback and Pushout|pushout]], transfinite composition, and retract; this routes immediately to "left lifting against all Kan fibrations". **An object is of the form $\mathrm{Sing}\,Y$**, hence automatically Kan, which is how a topological hypothesis enters the combinatorial world. **A simplicial set is a [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|nerve]] or a representable $\Delta^n$**, which is small, projective in the relevant sense, and where everything can be computed by hand. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a Kan hypothesis routes through the homotopy-group construction to a $\pi_n$ computation; a monomorphism routes through cell attachment to a factorisation; an anodyne map routes through the lifting characterisation to a triviality statement. The [[Model Categories — The Model Category of Simplicial Sets#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in this chapter is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each. Everything here is self-contained: a reader who knows only the [[Def - Simplicial Set|definition of a simplicial set]] should be able to follow each operation from the description.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Fill a horn.** Given a [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complex]] $X$ (or a [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|Kan fibration]] $p : E \to B$) and a horn $\Lambda^n_k \to X$, you may extend it to a simplex $\Delta^n \to X$. This is the single most-used move: composites of edges, inverses, homotopies, and the group operations on $\pi_n$ are all obtained by filling a carefully chosen horn. *Trigger:* you have $n$ of the $n+1$ faces of a simplex and need the last face plus the simplex itself. *Pattern:* "by the Kan condition, fill the horn $\Lambda^n_k$ to obtain the missing face $d_k$ and the homotopy."

2. **Build a cofibration by attaching cells.** Any monomorphism $A \rightarrowtail B$ of simplicial sets is a [[Def - Pullback and Pushout|pushout]]-composite of boundary inclusions $\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n$, one for each non-degenerate simplex of $B$ not in $A$, in increasing dimension. *Trigger:* you need to prove something about all monomorphisms, or to construct a map out of $B$ extending one out of $A$. *Pattern:* induct over the skeleta, attaching one cell at a time.

3. **Recognise and use an anodyne map.** A map is **anodyne** if it lies in the saturation of the horn inclusions. Anodyne maps are exactly the maps with the left [[Def - Lifting Property and the Retract Argument|lifting property]] against every Kan fibration, so once a map is known anodyne you may lift against any Kan fibration for free. *Trigger:* a map built from horn inclusions by pushout, transfinite composition, or retract; or one of the standard anodyne maps ($\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta^n$; $(\partial\Delta^m \times \Delta^1) \cup (\Delta^m \times \{e\}) \hookrightarrow \Delta^m \times \Delta^1$). *Pattern:* "the inclusion is anodyne, so it lifts against the Kan fibration $p$."

4. **Transpose a lifting problem across an adjunction.** A lifting problem for $i$ against $p$ is equivalent, under a Quillen adjunction, to a lifting problem for the adjunct of $i$ against the adjunct of $p$ — and under the internal-hom adjunction $-\times K \dashv (-)^K$, a lifting problem against $p$ is equivalent to one against the **pullback-hom** $p^K$. *Trigger:* a lifting problem that is hard on one side but easy after transposing. *Pattern:* push the cofibration onto the right-hand map via the pullback-hom and recognise the result as a fibration.

5. **Factor a map (small object argument).** Any map $f : X \to Y$ factors as $X \rightarrowtail Z \xrightarrow{\sim} Y$ (cofibration then trivial fibration) or $X \xrightarrow{\sim} W \twoheadrightarrow Y$ (anodyne then Kan fibration), by attaching all possible cells/horns transfinitely. *Trigger:* you need a cofibrant or fibrant replacement, or the two factorisations MC5 demands. *Pattern:* run the small object argument against $I = \{\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$ or $J = \{\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$.

6. **Replace a Kan fibration by a minimal one.** Every [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|Kan fibration]] $p : E \to B$ admits a [[Def - Minimal Fibration|minimal]] sub-fibration $E' \subseteq E$ onto which $E$ deformation-retracts fibrewise, so $E \to B$ and $E' \to B$ are fibrewise homotopy equivalent. *Trigger:* you need rigidity — to know that fibrewise-homotopic simplices are literally equal, or that the fibration is a bundle. *Pattern:* minimise, prove the cleaner statement for the bundle, transport back along the fibrewise equivalence.

7. **Pass to the singular complex.** Any homotopy-theoretic question about a [[Def - Topological Space|space]] $Y$ becomes a combinatorial question about the Kan complex $\mathrm{Sing}(Y)$, since $\pi_n(\mathrm{Sing}\,Y) \cong \pi_n(Y)$ and the unit $Y \to |\mathrm{Sing}\,Y|$ is a weak equivalence. *Trigger:* a space appears and you want to compute combinatorially. *Pattern:* replace $Y$ by $\mathrm{Sing}(Y)$, work in $\mathbf{sSet}$, realise at the end.

8. **Use that every object is cofibrant.** Because the map $\varnothing \to X$ is always a monomorphism, every simplicial set is [[Def - Cofibrant and Fibrant Objects|cofibrant]]. So cofibrant replacement is the identity and you never need to resolve the source of a map — only the target may need fibrant replacement. *Trigger:* any time the general model-category machinery asks for cofibrant replacement. *Pattern:* skip it; the object is already cofibrant.

9. **Compute via the long exact sequence of a fibration.** A [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|Kan fibration]] $F \to E \to B$ yields a long exact sequence of [[Def - Simplicial Homotopy Group|simplicial homotopy groups]] $\dots \to \pi_n F \to \pi_n E \to \pi_n B \to \pi_{n-1} F \to \dots$. *Trigger:* you know two of the three spaces' homotopy groups and want the third. *Pattern:* identify the fibration, write the sequence, chase exactness.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Defining $\pi_n(X)$ for a non-Kan simplicial set
> It is tempting to define homotopy groups of *any* simplicial set $X$ by the same formula — degenerate-boundary $n$-simplices modulo homotopy. But when $X$ is not [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan]] the simplicial homotopy relation is **not transitive**, so the quotient is not a group. The standard witness is $\Delta^1$: the two endpoints are connected by the edge $0 \to 1$, but there is no edge $1 \to 0$, so "homotopic" fails to be symmetric and $\pi_0(\Delta^1)$ as a *set of components* still works but the higher group laws collapse. The operation becomes legal exactly when $X$ is fibrant — or, for a general $X$, after taking a [[Def - Cofibrant and Fibrant Objects|fibrant replacement]] $X \xrightarrow{\sim} RX$ and setting $\pi_n(X) := \pi_n(RX)$.

> [!warning] 2. Assuming $|{-}|$ preserves all colimits *and* all finite limits because it is "geometric"
> Geometric realisation is a left adjoint, so it preserves colimits — but a left adjoint generally does *not* preserve limits, and one is tempted to assume $|X \times Y| = |X| \times |Y|$ holds for the same easy reason. It does hold, but only because of a *separate* theorem (realisation preserves finite products), which fails if one works in an inconvenient category of spaces: in plain $\mathbf{Top}$ the product $|X| \times |Y|$ may have the wrong topology, and the identity $|X \times Y| \cong |X| \times |Y|$ requires the **compactly generated** product. The repair is to work in compactly generated spaces $\mathbf{Top}$, where the product is corrected and the identity holds.

> [!warning] 3. Treating outer-horn filling as part of the Kan condition for fibrations between non-fibrant objects
> When checking that $p : E \to B$ is a Kan fibration one tests *all* horns, inner and outer. It is tempting, by analogy with [[Def - Quasi-Category|quasi-categories]] (which test only inner horns), to drop the outer horns. But a Kan *fibration* really does require all horns: dropping the outer ones gives a **left/right fibration** or an **inner fibration**, a strictly weaker and different notion belonging to the Joyal world. The operation "test only inner horns" is legal precisely when you are building the Joyal model structure for ∞-categories, not the Kan–Quillen one for spaces.

> [!warning] 4. Concluding two simplices are equal from "fibrewise homotopic"
> In a [[Def - Minimal Fibration|minimal fibration]], simplices that are fibrewise homotopic rel boundary and lie over the same base simplex *are* equal — that is the defining property. It is tempting to use this in an arbitrary Kan fibration, but there it is false: a general Kan fibration has many fibrewise-homotopic-but-distinct simplices (that redundancy is exactly what minimisation removes). The conclusion "homotopic $\Rightarrow$ equal" is legal only after replacing $p$ by its minimal model $E' \subseteq E$.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this chapter are won at the moment you decide *which world you are computing in* — the lifting world (cofibrations and fibrations and the diagrams between them), the homotopy-group world ($\pi_n$ and exact sequences), or the comparison world (moving between $\mathbf{sSet}$ and $\mathbf{Top}$). Almost every exercise lives primarily in one of these three, and recognising which dictates the route.

If the problem **asks you to verify a closure or lifting property** — that a class is closed under pullback, that a map factors a certain way, that two classes are lifting-complementary — then you are in the lifting world and your instruments are the [[Def - Lifting Property and the Retract Argument|lifting-property]] calculus and the [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|anodyne]] saturation. The route is almost always: reduce the claim about all maps in a class to a claim about the *generators* (the boundary inclusions for cofibrations, the horn inclusions for trivial cofibrations), because lifting against a class is the same as lifting against any generating set, and the generating set is finite-dimensional and explicit. The single most useful fact is that "left lifting against all Kan fibrations" *equals* "anodyne", so a triviality question becomes a question about being built from horns. When the lifting problem involves products, transpose it across the internal-hom adjunction to a pullback-hom and recognise the pieces.

If the problem **asks you to compute a homotopy group or show a map is a weak equivalence**, you are in the homotopy-group world, and the first move is always to ensure your objects are [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan]] — if they are not, [[Def - Cofibrant and Fibrant Objects|fibrantly replace]] them, because the [[Def - Simplicial Homotopy Group|simplicial homotopy group]] construction is only valid for Kan complexes. Once everything is fibrant, the group operations and the homotopy relation are all instances of horn-filling, and the productive tool is the [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|long exact sequence of a Kan fibration]]: find a fibration relating your object to ones you understand, write the sequence, and chase it. To prove a map is a weak equivalence, the internal criterion — isomorphism on all $\pi_n$ — is usually easier than realising and checking in $\mathbf{Top}$, and it is the reason the homotopy-group theory is worth building.

If the problem **asks you to move between simplicial and topological worlds**, you are in the comparison world, and the governing fact is the [[Thm - Geometric Realization is a Quillen Equivalence|Quillen equivalence]] $|{-}| \dashv \mathrm{Sing}$. The route depends on direction: to import a topological space, take $\mathrm{Sing}(Y)$, which is automatically Kan, and compute combinatorially; to export a simplicial set, realise it, using that $|{-}|$ preserves colimits and finite products. The non-obvious enabling step, when rigidity is needed, is to pass to a [[Def - Minimal Fibration|minimal fibration]]: Quillen's proof that realisation preserves products and detects weak equivalences runs by minimising a fibration to a bundle, where the fibre is constant and the computation is direct.

A meta-strategy threads through all three worlds: **when a simplicial set is hard to handle, fill or minimise.** If it is not fibrant, fibrant-replace it so horns fill; if it has homotopical redundancy, minimise it so fibrewise-homotopic simplices coincide. Both moves trade an arbitrary object for a rigid one with the same homotopy type, and the entire chapter is the systematic exploitation of that trade. Every question in this chapter is ultimately the question "**how much of the homotopy theory of spaces survives the passage to combinatorics?**" — and the answer, theorem by theorem, is "all of it."

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|The horn-filling / lifting characterisation]]**: fibrations are $\mathrm{RLP}(\text{horns})$, anodyne maps are $\mathrm{LLP}(\text{Kan fibrations})$. This is the most reusable fact because it converts every homotopical question into a *diagrammatic* one — does a lift exist? — that can be checked against a small generating set. The typical use is to prove a map is a (trivial) fibration by exhibiting lifts, or to prove a map is anodyne by building it from horns; the duality between the two is the lever. Reach for it the instant a problem mentions extending a map, filling a horn, or closure of fibrations/cofibrations.

- **[[Def - Simplicial Homotopy Group|Simplicial homotopy groups agree with topological ones]]**: $\pi_n(\mathrm{Sing}\,Y) \cong \pi_n(Y)$, and a map of Kan complexes is a weak equivalence iff it is a $\pi_n$-isomorphism. This is the bridge that lets all of classical homotopy theory be done combinatorially. The typical setup is "compute $\pi_n$ of a combinatorial model" or "show this combinatorial map is a weak equivalence"; the move is to reduce to a $\pi_n$ check on Kan complexes. It is also the *internal* definition of weak equivalence, which removes the need to leave $\mathbf{sSet}$.

- **[[Thm - Simplicial Sets Form a Model Category|Cofibrant generation by the boundary and horn inclusions]]**: the whole model structure is generated by the sets $I = \{\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$ and $J = \{\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$. The typical use is the small object argument: any map factors functorially through these two sets, giving the factorisations MC5 demands and functorial (co)fibrant replacement. Recognise its applicability whenever you need a factorisation, a replacement, or to transfer a model structure along an adjunction — the generators travel.

- **[[Def - Minimal Fibration|Minimal fibrations are bundles]]**: every Kan fibration retracts fibrewise onto a minimal one, and a minimal fibration is a fibre bundle classified by an action of the [[Def - Path-Product and the Fundamental Group|fundamental groupoid]]. The typical use is to gain rigidity in a proof — to upgrade "fibrewise homotopic" to "equal" — and it is the technical core of the proof that realisation preserves products. It is the simplicial replacement for local triviality, and the reason simplicial fibrations behave like topological ones.

- **Every object is [[Def - Cofibrant and Fibrant Objects|cofibrant]]**: $\varnothing \to X$ is always mono. This is a small fact with large consequences: cofibrant replacement is trivial, so all the asymmetry of the homotopy theory is on the fibrant side, and derived functors out of $\mathbf{sSet}$ never need a resolution of the source. Reach for it whenever the general model-category formalism asks for $QX$ — you may take $QX = X$.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic topology — homotopy groups, fibrations, and the long exact sequence.** The classical [[Def - Higher Homotopy Group|homotopy group]] $\pi_n(Y, y)$ is defined as homotopy classes of maps $(S^n, *) \to (Y, y)$; the [[Def - Simplicial Homotopy Group|simplicial homotopy group]] $\pi_n(\mathrm{Sing}\,Y, y)$ replaces the sphere $S^n$ by the boundary $\partial\Delta^{n+1}$ collapsed (an $n$-simplex with degenerate boundary) and "continuous up to homotopy" by "fillable horn". The two coincide, and the simplicial [[Def - Kan Fibration and Anodyne Extension|Kan fibration]] is the combinatorial Serre fibration: its long exact sequence of homotopy groups is the same sequence, derived here by horn-filling rather than by lifting paths. The chapter is the algebraic translation of the homotopy-group machinery of [[Def - Singular Homology|singular topology]].

2. **Homological algebra — the Dold–Kan correspondence.** Restrict attention to *abelian* simplicial objects: simplicial abelian groups. The **Dold–Kan correspondence** is an equivalence between simplicial abelian groups and non-negatively graded chain complexes, sending a simplicial abelian group $A$ to its normalised chain complex $NA$ with $\pi_n(A) \cong H_n(NA)$. Under this equivalence the Kan–Quillen weak equivalences become quasi-isomorphisms, and the model structure on $\mathbf{sSet}$ restricted to abelian objects becomes the projective model structure on chain complexes — so the simplicial homotopy groups of this chapter literally *are* the homology groups of homological algebra, and the long exact sequence of a fibration becomes the long exact sequence of a short exact sequence of complexes.

3. **Algebraic geometry — simplicial presheaves and motivic / étale homotopy.** A scheme has too few points for the Zariski topology to see its homotopy type, so one works instead with the category of presheaves of simplicial sets on a site. *(Background: a **scheme** is a space locally modelled on the prime spectrum $\mathrm{Spec}\,R$ of a commutative ring — the set of prime ideals of $R$ with the Zariski topology, glued from affine pieces; a **site** is a category equipped with a notion of covering, generalising open covers; a **presheaf** on a site is a contravariant functor to sets, and a **simplicial presheaf** is one valued in $\mathbf{sSet}$.)* The Kan–Quillen model structure of this chapter is the local building block: one puts a model structure on simplicial presheaves whose weak equivalences are "local" (stalkwise) Kan–Quillen weak equivalences, and the resulting homotopy theory — the **motivic** or **étale homotopy theory** of schemes — is where the homotopy groups of algebraic varieties live. The combinatorial homotopy theory built here is what gets sheafified to give a homotopy theory of geometric objects.

4. **Higher category theory — the homotopy hypothesis and the Joyal structure.** The fibrant objects of the Kan–Quillen structure are exactly the [[Def - Kan Complex and the Nerve|Kan complexes]], i.e. the ∞-groupoids, and the [[Thm - Geometric Realization is a Quillen Equivalence|Quillen equivalence]] with $\mathbf{Top}$ is the precise homotopy hypothesis: ∞-groupoids are spaces. Keeping the cofibrations but enlarging the fibrant objects to [[Def - Quasi-Category|quasi-categories]] (test only inner horns) gives the **Joyal model structure**, whose homotopy theory is that of $\infty$-categories rather than spaces. The Kan–Quillen structure is therefore the $\infty$-groupoid restriction of the $\infty$-category theory, and the two sit inside one combinatorial substrate, differing only in which horns the fibrant objects must fill.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a model structure on a presheaf category is "spaces, but bookkept by lifting problems."** The single perspective that organises this chapter is that the entire homotopy theory of spaces can be re-expressed as a lifting calculus on $\mathbf{sSet}$. A weak equivalence, a fibration, a homotopy group, a long exact sequence — each is, under the surface, the existence or non-existence of a lift in a diagram of simplicial sets, and each is decided against the two generating sets $I$ and $J$. This is why the subject feels so different from point-set topology: nothing is ever proved by an $\epsilon$-$\delta$ argument or a covering, everything is a diagram chase against horns and boundaries. The reward for adopting this frame is that the same machinery — generators, saturation, small object argument, lifting — applies verbatim to chain complexes, spectra, and simplicial presheaves, which is why $\mathbf{sSet}$ is studied first and hardest.

**The true name of "Kan complex" is "fibrant object", and of "weak equivalence between Kan complexes" is "$\pi_n$-isomorphism".** The horn-filling definition of a Kan complex is the right thing to *check* but not the right thing to *think*: operationally, a Kan complex is just a fibrant object of $\mathbf{sSet}$, the kind of object on which homotopy groups and mapping spaces are well-behaved, exactly as a fibrant space is one where homotopy lifting works. And while the official weak equivalences are defined by realising to $\mathbf{Top}$, the name to carry is "isomorphism on all simplicial homotopy groups" — that is what you actually verify, and the equivalence of the two definitions is one of the deep theorems that makes the combinatorial theory self-contained.

**Minimisation is the simplicial form of "compress to the essential model", and it appears whenever rigidity is needed.** A Kan fibration carries a great deal of homotopical redundancy — many distinct simplices represent the same fibrewise homotopy class. The minimal fibration strips this redundancy to the bone, leaving a fibre bundle in which fibrewise-homotopic-rel-boundary simplices are literally equal. This is the same move as passing from a homomorphism to its image (forgetting the kernel's redundancy), or from a space to its CW model: replace an object by a smaller one of the same homotopy type on which an argument becomes rigid. The trigger-reaction pattern is worth installing: *when a proof needs "homotopic implies equal", minimise the fibration first.* Quillen's proof that realisation preserves products is the canonical instance — it is false at the level of arbitrary fibrations and true at the level of minimal ones.

**The whole chapter is one long argument that combinatorics loses nothing.** It would be a priori plausible that replacing a space by a combinatorial gadget throws away some homotopy-theoretic information — that some spaces have no good simplicial model, or that simplicial homotopy groups miss something the topological ones see. The accumulated content of the chapter, culminating in the [[Thm - Geometric Realization is a Quillen Equivalence|Quillen equivalence]], is that this never happens: the unit $Y \to |\mathrm{Sing}\,Y|$ and counit $|\mathrm{Sing}\,X| \to X$ are weak equivalences, the homotopy groups agree, and the two homotopy categories are equivalent. The reason this is surprising is that simplicial sets are *so* much more rigid and finite than spaces, yet the rigidity costs nothing homotopically. This is the deepest single fact of the subject, and it is what justifies doing homotopy theory algebraically forever after.
