# Problem Statement

Show that the inclusion $\iota : \mathbb{Z} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{Q}$ of [[Def - Ring|rings]] is **both a monomorphism and an epimorphism** in the category $\mathbf{CRing}$ of commutative rings, yet is **not an isomorphism**. Conclude that "mono and epi" does not imply "iso" in a general category. (Optionally, give a second witness in $\mathbf{Top}$.)

**Recall:**

![[Def - Isomorphism, Monomorphism, Epimorphism#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Ring Homomorphism|ring homomorphism]] $\varphi : R \to S$ is a map preserving $+$, $\times$, and $1$. In $\mathbf{CRing}$ the objects are commutative rings and the morphisms are ring homomorphisms. A field such as $\mathbb{Q}$ has every nonzero element invertible, so a ring map out of $\mathbb{Q}$ is forced on fractions: $\varphi(a/b) = \varphi(a)\varphi(b)^{-1}$.

---

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** This is a "separate two notions that coincide in $\mathbf{Set}$" problem — the recurring task of showing a categorical property is genuinely weaker than its set-theoretic intuition. The route is to verify mono and epi *by their cancellation definitions* (not by injective/surjective) and then exhibit the missing inverse arrow.

**Assumption pattern:** The decisive assumption is that $\mathbb{Q}$ is the **field of fractions** of $\mathbb{Z}$: every element is a ratio of integers, and a ring map must send a ratio to the ratio of images. This "generated by $\mathbb{Z}$ under ring operations" is what makes pre-composition by $\iota$ injective, i.e. makes $\iota$ epi, even though $\iota$ is not surjective on underlying sets. Recognizing "the target is generated by the source" is the trigger for an epimorphism that is not surjective.

**Theorem routing:** Mono is checked directly (injective ring maps are left-cancellable). Epi is the interesting half: use that two ring homomorphisms agreeing on $\mathbb{Z}$ must agree on every fraction, which is right-cancellability of $\iota$. Non-iso is immediate: there is no ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}$ at all (where would $1/2$ go?), so $\iota$ has no inverse.

**Key decision point:** The non-obvious move is to check epi via the *forced values on fractions* rather than reaching for surjectivity. The natural but wrong instinct is "epi means surjective", which would (falsely) conclude $\iota$ is not epi. The whole point of the exercise is that surjectivity is sufficient but not necessary for epi in $\mathbf{CRing}$.

---

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Operation: verify mono/epi by cancellation, not by elements** (topic page, Legal Operation 2). Mono is "left-cancellable", epi is "right-cancellable"; we test these definitions against arbitrary parallel maps rather than counting points.

2. **Operation: exploit that a map out of a localization is determined by the base** (topic page, Legal Operation 5). A ring map out of $\mathbb{Q}$ is pinned down by its restriction to $\mathbb{Z}$, because fractions are forced — this is what delivers right-cancellability.

3. **Operation: refute iso by the absence of a candidate inverse arrow** (topic page, Legal Operation 3). To show no isomorphism exists, show no morphism $\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}$ exists.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Do not try to use "injective ⟹ mono, surjective ⟹ epi" for the epi part — $\iota$ is not surjective. Work from the *definition* of epimorphism: $g \circ \iota = h \circ \iota \implies g = h$ for all $g, h : \mathbb{Q} \to S$.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Two ring maps $g, h : \mathbb{Q} \to S$ that agree on the integers: what is $g(1/n)$ forced to be? Use that $g(n)g(1/n) = g(1) = 1$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For non-iso: an inverse would be a ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}$. Show none exists by asking where $1/2$ can go — its image $x$ would satisfy $2x = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}$.

# Solution

The proof has three parts. We show $\iota$ is mono (immediate from injectivity), then epi (the crux: a ring map out of $\mathbb{Q}$ is determined by its values on $\mathbb{Z}$), then not iso (no inverse ring map exists). The non-obvious move is the epi argument, which uses that $\mathbb{Q}$ is generated by $\mathbb{Z}$ under the ring operations a homomorphism must respect.

**Step 1: $\iota$ is a monomorphism.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Suppose $g, h : R \to \mathbb{Z}$ are ring homomorphisms with $\iota \circ g = \iota \circ h$. Since $\iota$ is injective (it is the inclusion), $\iota \circ g = \iota \circ h$ means $g(r) = h(r)$ as elements of $\mathbb{Q}$ for every $r$, hence as elements of $\mathbb{Z}$, so $g = h$. Thus $\iota$ is left-cancellable, i.e. a [[Def - Isomorphism, Monomorphism, Epimorphism|monomorphism]]. (More generally any injective ring homomorphism is mono, because pre-composition cannot create agreement that was not there.)

**Step 2: $\iota$ is an epimorphism.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $S$ be any commutative ring and $g, h : \mathbb{Q} \to S$ ring homomorphisms with $g \circ \iota = h \circ \iota$, i.e. $g$ and $h$ agree on every integer: $g(n) = h(n)$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. We show $g = h$ on all of $\mathbb{Q}$.
>
> Take a nonzero integer $n$. Then $g(n)$ is a unit of $S$ with inverse $g(1/n)$, since $g(n) \cdot g(1/n) = g(n \cdot \tfrac{1}{n}) = g(1) = 1$. The inverse of a unit in a ring is unique, so $g(1/n) = g(n)^{-1}$. Likewise $h(1/n) = h(n)^{-1}$. But $g(n) = h(n)$, so $g(n)^{-1} = h(n)^{-1}$, giving $g(1/n) = h(1/n)$.
>
> Now any fraction $a/b \in \mathbb{Q}$ (with $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $b \in \mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}$) satisfies
> $$g(a/b) = g(a) \cdot g(1/b) = h(a) \cdot h(1/b) = h(a/b),$$
> using multiplicativity and the two agreements just established. Hence $g = h$, so $\iota$ is right-cancellable, i.e. an [[Def - Isomorphism, Monomorphism, Epimorphism|epimorphism]]. Note we never used surjectivity — indeed $\iota$ is not surjective.

**Step 3: $\iota$ is not an isomorphism.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> An [[Def - Isomorphism, Monomorphism, Epimorphism|isomorphism]] would require a two-sided inverse ring homomorphism $j : \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}$. But no ring homomorphism $\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}$ exists at all. Suppose one did; let $x = j(1/2) \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $2x = j(2)j(1/2) = j(2 \cdot \tfrac12) = j(1) = 1$, so $2x = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}$ — impossible, as $2$ is not a unit in $\mathbb{Z}$. So no such $j$ exists, and $\iota$ is not invertible. Therefore $\iota$ is mono and epi but not iso, and $\mathbf{CRing}$ is not a balanced category.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Let $\iota : \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q}$ be the inclusion in $\mathbf{CRing}$.
>
> *Mono.* If $g, h$ satisfy $\iota g = \iota h$, then $g(r) = h(r)$ in $\mathbb{Q}$ for all $r$ (as $\iota$ is injective), so $g = h$; $\iota$ is left-cancellable.
>
> *Epi.* Let $g, h : \mathbb{Q} \to S$ with $g\iota = h\iota$, so $g|_{\mathbb{Z}} = h|_{\mathbb{Z}}$. For nonzero $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, $g(1/n) = g(n)^{-1} = h(n)^{-1} = h(1/n)$ since $g(n) = h(n)$ is a unit with unique inverse. Then $g(a/b) = g(a)g(1/b) = h(a)h(1/b) = h(a/b)$ for all $a/b \in \mathbb{Q}$, so $g = h$; $\iota$ is right-cancellable.
>
> *Not iso.* A ring map $j : \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}$ would give $x = j(1/2)$ with $2x = j(1) = 1$ in $\mathbb{Z}$, impossible. So $\iota$ has no inverse.
>
> Hence $\iota$ is mono and epi but not iso. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] A second witness, in $\mathbf{Top}$
> The continuous map $f : [0, 1) \to S^1$, $t \mapsto (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$, onto the circle $S^1 = \{(x,y) : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$, is a continuous *bijection*, hence both mono and epi in $\mathbf{Top}$ (its underlying function is injective and surjective). But it is not a homeomorphism: $f^{-1}$ is discontinuous at the join point $(1,0)$, where preimages near $t = 0$ and the "end" of $[0,1)$ are torn apart. So $f$ is mono and epi but not iso in $\mathbf{Top}$ either. The two witnesses illustrate the same gap by different mechanisms — algebraic generation versus topological tearing.

# Key Takeaways

**Epimorphism is not surjectivity outside $\mathbf{Set}$.** The single most important lesson is that "epi" is a cancellation property, and cancellation can hold for non-surjective maps when the target is *generated* by the image under the operations morphisms must respect. The trigger to recognize this: whenever the target object is "built from" the source by operations every morphism preserves — fractions from integers, a field from a subring, a completion from a dense subset — suspect that the inclusion is epi without being surjective. The general principle is that pre-composition $g \mapsto g \circ \iota$ is injective exactly when $\iota$ has dense or generating image in the relevant algebraic sense, and this is the right way to think about epimorphisms in any algebraic category.

**The inverse arrow must live in the category.** Mono and epi only ever inspect how a morphism interacts with *other morphisms of the same category*; they cannot manufacture an inverse out of thin air. The reason $\iota$ fails to be iso is not a defect of cancellability but the genuine absence of any morphism $\mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{Z}$ — the category is "too rigid" to provide one. This diagnostic recurs constantly: to decide whether a mono-epi is an iso, stop testing cancellation and instead ask directly whether a candidate inverse morphism exists in the category. In $\mathbf{Set}$ it always does (set-theoretic inverse of a bijection); in $\mathbf{CRing}$, $\mathbf{Top}$, $\mathbf{Ring}$ it often does not.

**Balanced versus unbalanced categories.** A category where mono + epi always forces iso is called *balanced*; $\mathbf{Set}$, $\mathbf{Grp}$, $\mathbf{Ab}$, and abelian categories are balanced, while $\mathbf{CRing}$, $\mathbf{Ring}$, $\mathbf{Top}$, and $\mathbf{Mon}$ are not. Knowing which side of this line a category falls on tells you immediately whether the "mono + epi" shortcut to iso is available. When working in an unbalanced category, treat iso as a genuinely stronger condition and check for an inverse explicitly. This single classificatory fact saves a great deal of false reasoning, and it is worth memorizing the standard examples on each side.
