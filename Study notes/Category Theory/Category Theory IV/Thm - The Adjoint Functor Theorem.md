# Notation

$G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ is a functor; we ask when it has a **left adjoint** $F$ with $F \dashv G$ (see [[Def - Adjunction]]). $\mathcal{D}$ is **complete** if it has all small [[Def - Limit and Colimit|limits]] (see [[Def - Complete and Cocomplete Category]]) and **locally small** if every hom-class $\mathcal{D}(X, Y)$ is a set. $G$ is **continuous** if it preserves all small limits. The comma category $(A \downarrow G)$ has objects $(B, g : A \to GB)$ and morphisms the maps $B \to B'$ in $\mathcal{D}$ commuting with the structure maps. A **set** is a small collection (not a proper class). The full symbol registry is on [[Category Theory IV — Adjunctions]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (General Adjoint Functor Theorem — Freyd).** Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a **complete, locally small** category and $G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ a functor. Then $G$ has a left adjoint **if and only if** $G$ preserves all small limits *(is continuous)* and satisfies the **solution set condition**:
>
> > **(Solution Set Condition.)** For every object $A \in \mathcal{C}$ there is a *set* (not a proper class) of objects $\{B_i\}_{i \in I}$ in $\mathcal{D}$ and morphisms $\{f_i : A \to GB_i\}_{i \in I}$ such that every morphism $f : A \to GB$ (for any $B \in \mathcal{D}$) factors as $f = Gh \circ f_i$ for some $i \in I$ and some $h : B_i \to B$ in $\mathcal{D}$.
>
> Equivalently, the solution set $\{f_i\}$ is a *jointly weakly initial set* of objects in the comma category $(A \downarrow G)$.

> **Theorem (Special Adjoint Functor Theorem — SAFT).** If, in addition, $\mathcal{D}$ is **well-powered** (each object has only a set of subobjects) and has a **cogenerating set**, then continuity of $G$ alone suffices: the solution set condition is automatic, so every continuous $G : \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C}$ has a left adjoint.

The two are companions: the GAFT requires the solution set condition by hand; the SAFT trades that for global smallness hypotheses on $\mathcal{D}$ (well-powered $+$ cogenerator) that produce a solution set automatically. Both produce a left adjoint; for right adjoints, apply the dual to $G^{op}$.

# Motivation

The chapter so far has built adjoints *by hand* — naming the free group, the tensor product, the exponential. The Adjoint Functor Theorem asks the structural question in reverse: *given a functor, when does an adjoint exist at all, even if we cannot write it down?* This is the converse of [[Thm - Right Adjoints Preserve Limits|RAPL]]. RAPL says a right adjoint must preserve limits; the AFT asks how close the converse is, and the answer is "very close — preserving limits is almost sufficient, missing only a smallness condition".

The gap that the solution set condition closes is genuinely set-theoretic, and seeing the obstruction is the point. To build a left adjoint $F$ you want, for each $A$, the *initial object* of the comma category $(A \downarrow G)$ — the universal map from $A$ into the image of $G$. Completeness of $\mathcal{D}$ lets you build initial objects as limits: the initial object of a complete category is the limit of the identity functor. But $(A\downarrow G)$ may be too big — a *proper class* of objects — and you cannot take a limit over a proper class. The solution set condition says: although there may be a proper class of objects, a mere *set* of them is "weakly initial" (everything factors through one of them), and that set is small enough to take a limit over. The limit of that set, refined to a genuine initial object, is $FA$. **The solution set condition is exactly the smallness hypothesis that rescues the construction from a size paradox.**

This is why the theorem matters in practice: it is how one *knows* the free group exists (the forgetful functor is continuous and the solution set is "groups of bounded cardinality generated by the image of $A$"), how one knows reflective localizations exist, and — in its homotopical form, Brown representability — how cohomology theories are shown to be representable. It converts the hard problem "construct the adjoint" into the often-easier problems "check continuity" and "exhibit a solution set".

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The hypotheses are "complete locally small domain, continuous functor, solution set condition", so the source question is: *what situations supply these, especially the solution set?*

The first source is **an algebraic forgetful functor on a complete category of structures**. Categories like $\mathbf{Grp}, \mathbf{Ring}, \mathbf{Mod}_R$ are complete and locally small, and forgetful functors out of them are continuous (they preserve limits by RAPL, or directly). The solution set is supplied by a *cardinality bound*: the free object on $A$ is generated by the image of $A$, so candidate $B_i$ can be taken among structures of cardinality at most some bound depending on $|A|$, of which there is only a set. *Example problem:* re-prove the existence of free groups via the GAFT, using "groups of cardinality $\leq \max(\aleph_0, |A|)$" as the solution set.

The second source is **a continuous functor between presentable or well-powered categories**, where the SAFT applies. If $\mathcal{D}$ is well-powered with a cogenerator (true for $\mathbf{Set}$, for module categories, for many "nice" categories), continuity *alone* gives the adjoint. *Example problem:* show a limit-preserving functor out of a complete well-powered category with a cogenerator has a left adjoint, with no solution set to find by hand.

The third source is **a localization / reflective subcategory problem**, where you want the reflector to exist. The inclusion $\iota : \mathcal{D}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{C}$ is continuous (it preserves limits); the AFT then produces the reflector (left adjoint) provided the solution set condition holds, which it does whenever the subcategory is closed under suitable limits and a smallness bound holds. *Example problem:* establish that sheaves form a reflective subcategory of presheaves by verifying the inclusion is continuous and the solution set condition holds (bounded by the site's cardinality).

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$G$ has a left adjoint"; combined with other facts it does more.

Combine with **uniqueness of adjoints**: the AFT gives *existence*, and [[Thm - Adjoints are Unique up to Natural Isomorphism|uniqueness of adjoints]] then says the adjoint is canonical, so any explicit construction you later find *is* the adjoint. The further result is a two-step methodology: prove existence abstractly by the AFT, then identify the adjoint with a concrete formula and know it is correct.

Combine with **the dual for right adjoints**: applying the GAFT to $G^{op}$ gives a criterion for a functor to have a *right* adjoint (cocomplete domain, cocontinuous functor, dual solution set). The further result is existence of right adjoints — for instance, that a colimit-preserving functor on a presentable category has a right adjoint, the engine behind many "this functor has a right adjoint" claims in homotopy theory.

Combine with **monadicity** (Chapter V): once the AFT supplies $F\dashv G$, the monad $GF$ exists, and the Barr–Beck theorem can be applied to ask whether $\mathcal{D}$ *is* the category of algebras. The further result is a route from "an adjoint exists" to "this category is monadic over that one".

# Why Is It True

The intuition is that **in a complete category, an initial object is a limit, and a left adjoint is a family of initial objects in comma categories**. To give $G$ a left adjoint is exactly to give, for each $A\in\mathcal{C}$, an initial object of the comma category $(A\downarrow G)$ — the universal arrow $\eta_A : A\to GFA$ (this is the universal-arrow form of [[Thm - Equivalence of the Definitions of Adjunction|the adjunction]]). So the whole problem reduces to: *does $(A\downarrow G)$ have an initial object?*

Now, $(A\downarrow G)$ inherits completeness from $\mathcal{D}$ because $G$ preserves limits — continuity of $G$ is exactly what makes $(A\downarrow G)$ a complete category. And a complete category has an initial object *provided it is not too large*: the initial object would be the limit of the identity functor on $(A\downarrow G)$, but that limit is over the whole (possibly proper-class-sized) category and may not exist. The solution set condition is the fix: it provides a *small* weakly initial family, you take the (small, hence existing) limit over just that family, and a standard argument (forming an equalizer of all endomorphisms) refines the weakly initial object into a genuine initial object.

> **The mechanism in one line:** a left adjoint is an initial object of each comma category $(A\downarrow G)$; continuity makes that category complete, and the solution set condition makes it small enough that its initial object exists as a limit.

So continuity does the *qualitative* work (making the comma category complete, so limits exist) and the solution set condition does the *quantitative* work (bounding the size so the relevant limit is over a set). Neither alone suffices: continuity without the solution set leaves a category that is complete but possibly too large (this is the source of the standard counterexamples, large complete lattices with limit-preserving functors and no adjoint); the solution set without continuity gives a small weakly initial family but no way to refine it to a true initial object.

---

# What Makes This Hard

Two things. First, the *solution set condition is the only hard hypothesis*, and it is genuinely hard — solution sets "tend to be hard to find in practice", and the whole content of any application is the cardinality argument that produces one. People stall by checking continuity (easy) and then not knowing how to bound the comma category. Second, the *necessity* of the size hypothesis is counterintuitive: it feels as if limit-preservation should be enough, and the reason it is not — that comma categories can be proper-class-large even when limits exist levelwise — is a set-theoretic subtlety invisible in small examples. The standard error is to "prove" the converse of RAPL without the solution set and produce a false theorem; the canonical counterexample (a complete poset / large lattice whose limit-preserving self-map has no adjoint) is the thing to keep in mind.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** Reduce "has a left adjoint" to "each comma category $(A\downarrow G)$ has an initial object". Use continuity of $G$ to make $(A\downarrow G)$ complete; use the solution set to get a small weakly initial family; take its limit and refine to an initial object.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Reduce to initial objects of comma categories.** Show $G$ has a left adjoint iff $(A\downarrow G)$ has an initial object for every $A$.
   - *Hint:* An initial object $(FA, \eta_A)$ of $(A\downarrow G)$ is exactly a universal arrow from $A$ to $G$; assemble these into the adjunction via [[Thm - Equivalence of the Definitions of Adjunction|the equivalence of definitions]].
   - *Why needed:* Converts the global question into a per-object existence question.

2. **Comma category is complete.** Show $(A\downarrow G)$ is complete, using that $G$ preserves limits.
   - *Hint:* Limits in $(A\downarrow G)$ are computed by taking the limit in $\mathcal{D}$ and using continuity of $G$ to lift the structure map $A\to G(\lim)$.
   - *Why needed:* Guarantees the limits used to build the initial object exist.

3. **Initial-object existence lemma.** Show a complete category with a (small) weakly initial set has an initial object.
   - *Hint:* Take the limit $W$ of the weakly initial set; then form the equalizer of *all* endomorphisms of $W$; that equalizer is initial. ("A complete category with a weakly initial set is the classic Freyd construction.")
   - *Why needed:* This is the technical core converting "weakly initial set" into "initial object".

4. **Solution set provides the weakly initial set.** Show the solution set condition is exactly "$(A\downarrow G)$ has a small weakly initial set".
   - *Hint:* The maps $\{f_i : A\to GB_i\}$ are objects of $(A\downarrow G)$, and "every $f$ factors through some $f_i$" is joint weak initiality.
   - *Why needed:* Connects the hypothesis to the input of step 3.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Left adjoint $=$ initial objects of comma categories
> **Statement:** $G : \mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{C}$ has a left adjoint iff for every $A\in\mathcal{C}$ the comma category $(A\downarrow G)$ has an initial object.
>
> **Hint:** An initial object of $(A\downarrow G)$ is a [[Def - Universal Property and Universal Arrow|universal arrow]] $\eta_A : A\to GFA$; assemble via the universal-arrow form of an adjunction.
>
> **Why needed:** Reframes the existence of an adjoint as a sequence of initial-object problems, which completeness can attack.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > An object of $(A\downarrow G)$ is a pair $(B, g : A\to GB)$; an initial object is $(FA, \eta_A)$ with a unique morphism to every $(B, g)$, i.e. a unique $h : FA\to B$ with $Gh\circ\eta_A = g$. That is exactly the universal property of a universal arrow from $A$ to $G$. By [[Thm - Equivalence of the Definitions of Adjunction|the equivalence of definitions]], a choice of such universal arrow for every $A$ is equivalent to a left adjoint $F\dashv G$. Conversely a left adjoint provides $\eta_A$ as the unit, initial in $(A\downarrow G)$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Continuity makes the comma category complete
> **Statement:** If $\mathcal{D}$ is complete and $G$ preserves small limits, then $(A\downarrow G)$ is complete, and the forgetful functor $(A\downarrow G)\to\mathcal{D}$ creates limits.
>
> **Hint:** Compute a limit in $\mathcal{D}$, then use $G(\lim) = \lim G$ to build the canonical structure map from $A$.
>
> **Why needed:** Provides the limits the initial-object construction (Lemma 3) consumes.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Given a diagram in $(A\downarrow G)$, its objects are $(B_d, g_d : A\to GB_d)$. Form $L = \lim B_d$ in $\mathcal{D}$ (exists by completeness). Since $G$ preserves limits, $GL = \lim GB_d$, so the cone $(g_d : A\to GB_d)$ — which is compatible because the diagram's morphisms commute with structure maps — induces a unique $g : A\to GL$. Then $(L, g)$ is the limit in $(A\downarrow G)$, and the projection to $\mathcal{D}$ creates it.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: A complete category with a weakly initial set has an initial object (Freyd)
> **Statement:** If $\mathcal{E}$ is complete and locally small and has a set $\{W_i\}$ that is *jointly weakly initial* (every object receives a morphism from some $W_i$), then $\mathcal{E}$ has an initial object.
>
> **Hint:** Let $W = \prod_i W_i$, then equalize *all* endomorphisms of $W$; the equalizer is initial.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the technical heart — it manufactures a genuine initial object from a mere weakly initial set, using completeness.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Form $W = \prod_i W_i$ (a small product, exists by completeness). $W$ is weakly initial: any object $X$ receives a map from some $W_i$, hence from $W$ via the projection. Let $E \to W$ be the equalizer of the set of *all* endomorphisms $W\to W$ (a set, by local smallness). Then $E$ is weakly initial (it maps to $W$, hence to everything). It is initial: given two maps $u, v : E\to X$, their equalizer $E'\to E$ receives a map $W\to E'$ (weak initiality of $W$ relative to $E'$), and composing $W\to E'\to E$ gives an endomorphism of $W$ that the equalizer $E$ already coequalizes, forcing $u = v$. So $E$ has a *unique* map to each object: it is initial.

> [!note]- Lemma 4: Solution set $=$ weakly initial set in $(A\downarrow G)$
> **Statement:** The solution set condition at $A$ is precisely the statement that $(A\downarrow G)$ has a small jointly weakly initial set.
>
> **Hint:** The solution-set maps $f_i : A\to GB_i$ are objects of $(A\downarrow G)$, and "every $f$ factors as $Gh\circ f_i$" is "every object receives a morphism from some $(B_i, f_i)$".
>
> **Why needed:** Translates the hypothesis into the input of Lemma 3.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > An object of $(A\downarrow G)$ is $(B, f : A\to GB)$. A morphism $(B_i, f_i)\to(B, f)$ is $h : B_i\to B$ with $Gh\circ f_i = f$. The solution set condition says exactly: for every $(B, f)$ there exist $i$ and $h$ with $Gh\circ f_i = f$ — i.e. a morphism $(B_i, f_i)\to (B, f)$. So $\{(B_i, f_i)\}$ is a small jointly weakly initial set.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> **($\Rightarrow$, necessity.)** If $G$ has a left adjoint, then $G$ is a right adjoint, so by [[Thm - Right Adjoints Preserve Limits|RAPL]] it preserves all small limits. The solution set condition holds with the singleton $\{\eta_A : A\to GFA\}$: every $f : A\to GB$ factors as $Gh\circ\eta_A$ for $h = \Phi^{-1}(f)$, by the universal property of the unit. So both conditions are necessary.
>
> **($\Leftarrow$, sufficiency.)** Assume $\mathcal{D}$ complete and locally small, $G$ continuous, and the solution set condition. Fix $A$.
>
> **Step 0 — reduction.** By Lemma 1, it suffices to show $(A\downarrow G)$ has an initial object.
>
> **Step 1 — the comma category is complete.** By Lemma 2, since $\mathcal{D}$ is complete and $G$ preserves limits, $(A\downarrow G)$ is complete; it is locally small because $\mathcal{D}$ is.
>
> **Step 2 — a weakly initial set.** By Lemma 4, the solution set condition gives a small jointly weakly initial set $\{(B_i, f_i)\}$ in $(A\downarrow G)$.
>
> **Step 3 — initial object.** By Lemma 3 (Freyd's initial-object construction), a complete locally small category with a weakly initial set has an initial object. Hence $(A\downarrow G)$ has an initial object $(FA, \eta_A)$.
>
> **Step 4 — assemble.** This holds for every $A$; the universal arrows $\eta_A$ assemble (by Lemma 1 and the equivalence of definitions) into a left adjoint $F\dashv G$ with unit $\eta$.
>
> **SAFT.** If $\mathcal{D}$ is additionally well-powered with a cogenerating set, one shows directly that the subobjects of a single object of $(A\downarrow G)$ form a weakly initial set: the cogenerator bounds the size, well-poweredness keeps the subobjects a set. Thus the solution set condition is automatic, and continuity of $G$ alone yields the left adjoint. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Existence of free algebras.** Use the GAFT to prove that the forgetful functor from any variety of algebras (groups, rings, modules, lattices) to $\mathbf{Set}$ has a left adjoint, with solution set "algebras of cardinality bounded by $\max(\aleph_0, |A|, |\text{signature}|)$". This recovers the existence of all free algebraic objects from one theorem and shows the cardinality bound is the real content.

**Existence of Stone–Čech compactification.** Show the inclusion of compact Hausdorff spaces into Tychonoff spaces has a left adjoint (the Stone–Čech compactification) via the SAFT: compact Hausdorff spaces are complete, well-powered, and $[0,1]$ is a cogenerator. The solution set is bounded by maps into powers of $[0,1]$. This is a striking case where the abstract theorem delivers a famously concrete construction.

**Existence of right adjoints in homotopy theory.** Apply the dual (to $G^{op}$) to show a colimit-preserving functor between presentable $\infty$-categories has a right adjoint — the **adjoint functor theorem for presentable categories**, the everyday tool for producing right adjoints (e.g. the right adjoint to a left Quillen functor, or the inclusion-of-local-objects in a Bousfield localization). This is how the AFT is actually used in modern homotopy theory.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Right Adjoints Preserve Limits|RAPL]]** — the necessity half. RAPL provides the "only if": a right adjoint preserves limits. The AFT provides the "if": limit-preservation plus a solution set is sufficient. Together they say preservation of limits *characterizes* right adjoints, up to the smallness condition the solution set supplies.

- **[[Thm - Equivalence of the Definitions of Adjunction|Universal-Arrow Form of Adjunction]]** — the reduction engine. The proof works entirely in the universal-arrow face: "$G$ has a left adjoint" becomes "each comma category has an initial object", which is the form completeness can attack. Without the equivalence of definitions there would be no reduction.

- **[[Def - Reflective Subcategory|Reflective Subcategories]]** — a prime application. The reflector of a reflective subcategory is a left adjoint to a (continuous) inclusion; the AFT is the standard tool for proving such reflectors exist, including sheafification and the Stone–Čech compactification.

- **[[Def - Complete and Cocomplete Category|Completeness]]** — the structural hypothesis. Completeness is what lets initial objects be built as limits (products and equalizers, via [[Thm - Products and Equalizers Give All Limits|the limit-construction theorem]]); the Freyd initial-object lemma is exactly that construction, guarded by the solution set.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Brown Representability *(from Algebraic Topology — Cluster 8)*
> The AFT's homotopical descendant is **Brown representability**: a functor on a nice (compactly generated, triangulated) homotopy category that sends coproducts to products and pushouts to pullbacks is representable. This is how cohomology theories are shown to be represented by **spectra** and is the working version of "limit-preservation implies adjoint/representability" in stable homotopy theory.

> [!tip] Presentable Categories and the Adjoint Functor Theorem for ∞-Categories *(from Higher Category Theory)*
> For **presentable** (and **locally presentable**) categories — those generated under colimits by a small set — the solution set condition is automatic, so the AFT simplifies to "colimit-preserving $\Rightarrow$ has a right adjoint" and "limit-preserving + accessible $\Rightarrow$ has a left adjoint". This is the everyday adjoint-existence tool in Lurie's higher category theory and the foundation of localization theory there.

> [!tip] Lawvere's Hyperdoctrines and Quantifiers as Adjoints *(from Categorical Logic)*
> The AFT guarantees the existence of the **left and right adjoints to substitution** that interpret the existential and universal quantifiers in categorical logic — $\exists \dashv \text{pullback} \dashv \forall$. That quantifiers exist as adjoints, and the conditions under which they do, is an AFT-flavored statement underpinning the semantics of first-order logic and dependent type theory.
