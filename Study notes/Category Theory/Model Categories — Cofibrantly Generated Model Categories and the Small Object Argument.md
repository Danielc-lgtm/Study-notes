# Notation Registry

This chapter works inside a cocomplete category $\mathcal{C}$ (it has all small colimits, in particular all transfinite composites), and from §3 onward inside a model category $\mathcal{M}$. The single standing convention is that $I$ and $J$ always denote **sets** of morphisms — not proper classes — because the whole point of the chapter is to generate the four model-category classes from finite data. We follow Hovey's notation $I\text{-cof}$, $I\text{-inj}$, $I\text{-cell}$ for the three closure operations attached to a set $I$.

- $\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D}$ — cocomplete categories; $\mathcal{M}, \mathcal{N}$ — model categories
- $\lambda, \kappa, \alpha, \beta, \gamma$ — ordinals; an ordinal is identified with the set of smaller ordinals, so $\beta < \lambda$ means $\beta \in \lambda$
- $\kappa$ also denotes a **cardinal** (an ordinal not in bijection with any smaller ordinal); $\mathrm{cf}(\lambda)$ is the **cofinality** of $\lambda$
- a $\lambda$-**sequence** in $\mathcal{C}$ — a colimit-preserving functor $X : \lambda \to \mathcal{C}$, written $X_0 \to X_1 \to \cdots \to X_\beta \to \cdots$
- the **transfinite composition** of $X$ — the canonical map $X_0 \to \mathrm{colim}_{\beta < \lambda} X_\beta$
- $I$ — a set of morphisms (the **generating cofibrations**); $J$ — a set of morphisms (the **generating trivial cofibrations**)
- $I\text{-inj}$ — maps with the **right lifting property** (RLP) against every map in $I$
- $I\text{-cof}$ — maps with the **left lifting property** (LLP) against every map in $I\text{-inj}$; equivalently the retracts of $I$-cell maps
- $I\text{-cell}$ — the **relative $I$-cell complexes**: transfinite composites of pushouts of maps in $I$
- $f \;\square\; g$ — "$f$ has the LLP against $g$" / "$g$ has the RLP against $f$"; the diagonal-filler relation
- $\mathrm{dom}(i)$, $\mathrm{cod}(i)$ — the domain and codomain of a morphism $i$
- $A$ is **small** relative to $I$ — $\mathcal{C}(A, -)$ commutes with the transfinite composites of $I$-cell maps (precise form below)
- $\mathcal{W}$ — the class of weak equivalences; $\mathrm{cof}, \mathrm{fib}$ — cofibrations and fibrations
- $\varnothing$ — initial object; $*$ — terminal object; $\rightarrowtail$ cofibration, $\twoheadrightarrow$ fibration, $\xrightarrow{\sim}$ weak equivalence
- $\mathbf{Top}$ — topological spaces; $\mathbf{sSet}$ — simplicial sets; $S^{n-1} \hookrightarrow D^n$, $\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n$, $\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta^n$ — boundary and horn inclusions

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: **a model structure is almost always built from a finite list of generators, and the machine that does the building is the small object argument.** In the previous chapter, the two hard axioms of a [[Def - Model Category|model category]] were lifting (MC4) and factorization (MC5), and we waved a hand at how the factorizations are produced. This chapter is that hand made rigorous. The factorization of an arbitrary map is not pulled from the air; it is constructed by transfinitely attaching cells from a small set of standard maps, exactly the way a CW complex is built by attaching disks. Once you have that construction, an astonishing economy appears: to specify a whole model structure — three proper classes of maps — you need only name two *sets* $I$ and $J$, and the four classes (cofibrations, [[Def - Fibration|fibrations]], and their trivial versions) are forced.

Why should we expect this to work? Look at the prototype. In $\mathbf{Top}$, a Serre fibration is *defined* by a lifting property — it has the right lifting property against the disk inclusions $D^n \hookrightarrow D^n \times [0,1]$ — and a cofibration is a retract of a relative cell complex, something built by gluing disks $D^n$ along their boundary spheres $S^{n-1}$. The generators $\{S^{n-1} \hookrightarrow D^n\}$ are doing all the work: cofibrations are what you build *from* them, and trivial fibrations are what lifts *against* them. The model structure on $\mathbf{Top}$ is completely determined by this one set of inclusions. The combinatorial miracle of the subject is that this is not special to spaces. Replace $\{S^{n-1} \hookrightarrow D^n\}$ by the boundary inclusions $\{\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$ and you get the model structure on [[Def - Simplicial Set|simplicial sets]]; replace it by sphere-and-disk complexes of $R$-[[Def - Module|modules]] and you get the projective model structure on chain complexes.

The structural backbone of the chapter is a single chain of three operations attached to any set of maps $I$, related by Galois adjunction along the lifting relation:

$$I \;\rightsquigarrow\; I\text{-inj} = \mathrm{RLP}(I) \;\rightsquigarrow\; I\text{-cof} = \mathrm{LLP}(I\text{-inj}) \;=\; \mathrm{retracts of}\; I\text{-cell}.$$

Read it left to right: from the generators $I$, take everything that lifts against them ($I$-inj); then take everything that lifts against *those* ($I$-cof); and the theorem we are building toward says $I$-cof is exactly the retracts of the things you can *build* from $I$ by transfinite cell attachment ($I$-cell). The small object argument is the bridge that factors any map as ($I$-cell)$\circ$($I$-inj), and the recognition theorem of Kan packages everything into a checklist: given a class $\mathcal{W}$ and two sets $I, J$ satisfying six conditions, you get a model structure, automatically [[Def - Cofibrantly Generated Model Category|cofibrantly generated]].

To run this machine you need to know that the transfinite construction *terminates*, and that is where **smallness** enters: an object $A$ is small if maps out of it cannot "escape to infinity" along a long colimit — every map $A \to \mathrm{colim}_\beta X_\beta$ factors through some bounded stage $X_\beta$. Smallness is the only genuinely set-theoretic ingredient in the subject, and it is the price of admission for the small object argument; everything else is diagram-chasing.

This chapter assumes you are fluent with the foundations of the previous one: the [[Def - Model Category|model-category axioms]], the [[Def - Lifting Property and the Retract Argument|lifting property and the retract argument]], the [[Thm - The Retract Argument|retract argument]] itself, and the [[Thm - Closure Properties of the Model Structure|lifting characterization of the four classes]]. You should be comfortable with [[Def - Limit and Colimit|colimits]] and [[Def - Pullback and Pushout|pushouts]] as universal constructions, and willing to index a colimit by an ordinal. From set theory you need only the bare definitions of ordinal, cardinal, and regular cardinal, all recalled below. No prior [[Def - Homotopy|homotopy]] theory beyond the previous chapter is assumed.

# Concept Map

## §1 Ordinals, Transfinite Composition, and Smallness

- **[[Def - Transfinite Composition and Smallness]]**
	- A $\lambda$-**sequence** is a colimit-preserving functor $X : \lambda \to \mathcal{C}$ from an ordinal, a chain $X_0 \to X_1 \to \cdots$ of length $\lambda$; its **transfinite composition** is the map $X_0 \to \mathrm{colim}_{\beta < \lambda} X_\beta$. An object $A$ is **small relative to a class $I$** if there is a cardinal $\kappa$ such that for every regular $\lambda \geq \kappa$ and every $\lambda$-sequence built from $I$-maps, the canonical map $\mathrm{colim}_\beta \mathcal{C}(A, X_\beta) \to \mathcal{C}(A, \mathrm{colim}_\beta X_\beta)$ is a bijection — every map out of $A$ into the colimit factors through a bounded stage, essentially uniquely. This is a compactness condition: in $\mathbf{Top}$ compact spaces are small, in $\mathbf{Set}$ every set is small, and smallness is the hypothesis that makes the small object argument terminate.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Locally Presentable and Combinatorial [[Def - Model Category|Model Categories]] *(from Higher Category Theory)*
> When *every* object of $\mathcal{C}$ is small (more precisely, $\mathcal{C}$ is generated under colimits by a set of small objects), $\mathcal{C}$ is a **locally presentable category**, and a cofibrantly generated model structure on a locally presentable category is a **combinatorial model category**. These are the categories where the small object argument always runs and where Jeff Smith's recognition theorem applies. Combinatorial model categories are the 1-categorical input to Lurie's presentable ∞-categories, the workhorses of higher algebra.

- **[[Ex - Compact spaces are small relative to closed inclusions]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that a compact topological space $A$ is small relative to the maps built from $\{S^{n-1} \hookrightarrow D^n\}$: a continuous map from a compact space into a transfinite colimit of cell attachments factors through a bounded stage.
- **[[Ex - Every set is small in the category of sets]]** (⭐)
	- Prove that in $\mathbf{Set}$ every object $A$ is small (relative to all maps), with $\kappa = |A|^+$, and identify where regularity of $\lambda$ is used.
- **[[Ex - Cofinality and the failure of smallness]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Exhibit an object that is *not* small relative to some class by building a $\lambda$-sequence in which a map out of $A$ genuinely fails to factor through any bounded stage, and locate the role of cofinality.

## §2 Relative Cell Complexes and the Small Object Argument

- **[[Def - Relative Cell Complex]]**
	- For a set of maps $I$, a **relative $I$-cell complex** ($I$-cell map) is a transfinite composite of pushouts of coproducts of maps in $I$ — formally, the transfinite composition of a $\lambda$-sequence each of whose stages $X_\beta \to X_{\beta+1}$ is a pushout of $\coprod I$-maps. The class $I\text{-cof}$ of **$I$-cofibrations** is $\mathrm{LLP}(I\text{-inj})$, the maps with the LLP against everything that lifts against $I$; the class $I\text{-inj} = \mathrm{RLP}(I)$ is the **$I$-injectives**. Every $I$-cell map is an $I$-cofibration, and (after the small object argument) the $I$-cofibrations are exactly the retracts of $I$-cell maps — relative cell complexes are the concrete generators, $I$-cof their retract-closure.

- **[[Thm - The Small Object Argument]]**
	- If $I$ is a set of maps whose domains are small relative to $I$, then there is a functorial factorization of every map $f$ in $\mathcal{C}$ as $f = p \circ i$ with $i \in I\text{-cell}$ (a relative $I$-cell complex) and $p \in I\text{-inj}$ (right lifting against $I$). The construction attaches, at each transfinite stage, one cell for every lifting problem against a generator, via a single large pushout, and takes a colimit over a regular cardinal large enough that smallness forces every lifting problem to be solved at a bounded stage. This is the engine that produces the factorizations axiom MC5 demands, and it is what makes $I\text{-cof} =$ retracts of $I\text{-cell}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Cellular and Left Bousfield Localization *(from Homotopical Algebra)*
> Once factorizations are generated by a set, you can *change* the weak equivalences while keeping the cofibrations and run the small object argument against an enlarged set of generating trivial cofibrations — this is **left Bousfield localization**, the construction that builds the model structures for spectra, motivic homotopy theory, and localized spaces. It requires the model category to be **cellular** (a smallness-friendly refinement of cofibrant generation, with extra control on the cells), exactly the conditions ensuring the localized factorizations still come from a set.

- **[[Ex - The small object argument in full]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Carry out the transfinite construction in detail: build the tower, prove the colimit-side map is a relative cell complex, prove the other factor is $I$-injective using smallness, and verify functoriality.
- **[[Ex - I-cof equals retracts of I-cell]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Use the small object argument plus the retract argument to prove the closure identity $I\text{-cof} =$ (retracts of $I\text{-cell}$), the structural payoff of the construction.
- **[[Ex - Pushouts of coproducts of I lie in I-cof]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove directly that $I\text{-cof}$ is closed under pushout, coproduct, transfinite composition, and retract, so that every $I$-cell map is an $I$-cofibration.

## §3 Cofibrantly Generated Model Categories

- **[[Def - Cofibrantly Generated Model Category]]**
	- A [[Def - Model Category|model category]] $\mathcal{M}$ is **cofibrantly generated** if there are sets $I$ and $J$ of maps, the **generating cofibrations** and **generating trivial cofibrations**, such that the domains of $I$ are small relative to $I\text{-cell}$ and the domains of $J$ are small relative to $J\text{-cell}$, and the four classes are recovered as $\mathrm{cof} = I\text{-cof}$, $\mathrm{triv\text{-}fib} = I\text{-inj}$, $\mathrm{triv\text{-}cof} = J\text{-cof}$, $\mathrm{fib} = J\text{-inj}$. The two smallness hypotheses license the small object argument for both $I$ and $J$, so the factorizations MC5 are *constructed*, not assumed. Essentially every model category in nature — $\mathbf{Top}$, $\mathbf{sSet}$, $\mathbf{Ch}(R)$ — is of this form.

- **[[Thm - The Recognition Theorem for Cofibrantly Generated Model Categories]]**
	- Kan's recognition theorem (Hovey 2.1.19): given a bicomplete category $\mathcal{C}$, a class $\mathcal{W}$ closed under retracts and 2-out-of-3, and two sets $I, J$ with small domains, there is a cofibrantly generated model structure with $\mathcal{W}$ as weak equivalences and $I, J$ as generators **if and only if** (i) $I$-inj $= \mathcal{W} \cap J$-inj and (ii) $J$-cell $\subseteq \mathcal{W} \cap I$-cof. In practice you verify a checklist — $\mathcal{W}$ has 2-out-of-3 and retracts, both sets have small domains, $J$-cell maps are weak equivalences, and the lifting-class matching conditions hold — and the theorem manufactures the entire model structure. It reduces "is this a model category?" to a finite verification against two sets of generators.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Projective and Injective Model Structures on Diagram Categories *(from Homological Algebra)*
> The recognition theorem is the tool that builds the **projective model structure** on a diagram category $\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{D}}$: the generators are $\{i \otimes \mathcal{D}(d, -) : i \in I, d \in \mathcal{D}\}$, and one checks Kan's conditions. This is how homotopy limits and colimits over a fixed shape are organized, and the same machine, dualized, gives the **injective model structure** used in the theory of homotopy sheaves and descent.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Transfer of Model Structures along Adjunctions *(from Homotopical Algebra)*
> Given a cofibrantly generated $\mathcal{M}$ and an adjunction $F \dashv U$ to a bicomplete $\mathcal{N}$, one can attempt to **transfer** the model structure: declare a map of $\mathcal{N}$ a weak equivalence or fibration if $U$ sends it to one, and take $FI, FJ$ as generators. The recognition theorem's conditions become the **transfer hypotheses** (Crans), and this is how model structures on categories of algebras over an [[Def - Operad|operad]], monoids in a monoidal model category, and equivariant spaces are produced.

- **[[Ex - Topological spaces are cofibrantly generated]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Identify the generating sets $I = \{S^{n-1} \hookrightarrow D^n\}$ and $J = \{D^n \hookrightarrow D^n \times [0,1]\}$ for the Quillen model structure on $\mathbf{Top}$ and verify the recognition conditions at the level needed to see $\mathbf{Top}$ is cofibrantly generated.
- **[[Ex - Simplicial sets are cofibrantly generated]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Identify $I = \{\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$ and $J = \{\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$ for the Kan–Quillen model structure on $\mathbf{sSet}$, and explain why the small domains and the horn-filling conditions make it cofibrantly generated.
- **[[Ex - Verifying Kan's recognition conditions]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Given $\mathcal{W}, I, J$ abstractly, work through the recognition theorem's checklist, showing how each condition feeds one model-category axiom, and where the two non-obvious conditions (i) and (ii) are used.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The problems of this chapter cluster around a small number of recurring goals, sharper versions of the goals from the previous chapter. The first and most common is **producing a functorial factorization** of a map as (cell complex)$\circ$(injective), which is the content of the small object argument and the way axiom MC5 is discharged in every concrete example. A second is **proving a closure identity** — that $I\text{-cof}$ equals the retracts of $I\text{-cell}$, or that $I\text{-cof}$ is closed under pushout, coproduct, and transfinite composition — because these identities are what let a *set* of generators control a *class* of cofibrations. A third is **establishing smallness** of a given object relative to a class, which is the termination certificate the small object argument requires; in practice this is always a compactness or cardinality argument. A fourth is **verifying a category is cofibrantly generated**, that is, exhibiting the two sets $I, J$ and checking they generate the four classes, which by the recognition theorem reduces to a checklist. A fifth, the capstone, is **building a model structure from scratch** via Kan's recognition theorem, given only the weak equivalences and two candidate sets of generators. These five — factor, close, certify smallness, exhibit generators, recognize a model structure — are the targets, and they recur because each is a step in compressing a homotopy theory down to finite data.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The assumptions are equally stereotyped. **A set $I$ of generators is given**, which is the richest source: it immediately defines the three derived classes $I\text{-inj}$, $I\text{-cof}$, $I\text{-cell}$ via lifting and cell attachment, and the relations among them are forced. **The domains of $I$ are small**, which is the hypothesis that unlocks the small object argument — whenever you must factor a map or run a transfinite construction, scan first for the smallness of the relevant domains. **A lifting property is available**, which by the [[Thm - The Retract Argument|retract argument]] converts into membership in a class, and which is how $I\text{-inj}$ and $I\text{-cof}$ talk to each other. **The weak equivalences satisfy 2-out-of-3 and are closed under retracts**, the two purely formal closure conditions Kan's theorem demands of $\mathcal{W}$. **A candidate $J$ of generating trivial cofibrations is given with $J\text{-cell} \subseteq \mathcal{W}$**, which is the substantive input that ties the auxiliary set $J$ to the weak equivalences. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a set of small-domain generators routes through the small object argument to a factorization; a factorization plus a lifting property routes through the retract argument to a closure identity; two sets satisfying the matching conditions (i)–(ii) route through the recognition theorem to a full model structure. The [[Model Categories — Cofibrantly Generated Model Categories and the Small Object Argument#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every argument in this chapter is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each. Everything is self-contained: a reader who has only just met cofibrant generation should be able to follow each operation from its description.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Form the three closures $I\text{-inj}$, $I\text{-cof}$, $I\text{-cell}$ of a set.** From any set of maps $I$ you immediately get the maps lifting against it ($I\text{-inj} = \mathrm{RLP}(I)$), the maps lifting against *those* ($I\text{-cof} = \mathrm{LLP}(I\text{-inj})$), and the maps built from it by transfinite cell attachment ($I\text{-cell}$). *Trigger:* a problem hands you a generating set and asks about cofibrations or fibrations. *Pattern:* translate every statement about a class into a lifting statement against $I$ — "is $f$ a cofibration?" becomes "does $f$ lift against every $I$-injective?". See [[Def - Relative Cell Complex]].

2. **Attach a cell to solve a lifting problem.** Faced with a commuting square from a generator $i \in I$ to a map $g$ with no diagonal, push out $i$ along the top map; the attached cell supplies the lift in the enlarged object. *Trigger:* you need a map to have the RLP against $I$ but it currently lacks a lift. *Pattern:* "push out the generator along the lifting problem"; iterating this universally is the small object argument. See [[Thm - The Small Object Argument]].

3. **Run the small object argument to factor a map.** When the domains of $I$ are small, factor any $f$ as $(I\text{-cell}) \circ (I\text{-inj})$ by building the transfinite tower of cell attachments. *Trigger:* you must produce a factorization (MC5), a cofibrant or fibrant replacement, or an $I$-injective approximation. *Pattern:* "by the small object argument, factor $f = p \circ i$ with $i$ a relative cell complex and $p$ right-lifting against $I$."

4. **Use the retract argument to upgrade a lifting property to class membership.** If $f$ factors as $p \circ i$ with $i \in I\text{-cell}$ and $f$ has the LLP against $p$, then $f$ is a retract of $i$, hence in $I\text{-cof}$. *Trigger:* you know $f$ lifts against the right things and want to conclude $f$ is a cofibration. *Pattern:* "factor $f$ by the small object argument, observe $f$ lifts against the injective factor, retract onto the cell factor." See [[Thm - The Retract Argument]].

5. **Certify smallness by compactness or cardinality.** To show an object $A$ is small relative to $I$, bound the size of $A$ and argue that a map out of $A$ into a transfinite colimit factors through a bounded stage. *Trigger:* the small object argument is needed and you must check its hypothesis. *Pattern:* in $\mathbf{Top}$, "$A$ is compact so its image meets finitely many cells"; in $\mathbf{Set}$ or a presheaf category, "$|A| < \kappa$ and $\lambda$ is regular, so the map factors." See [[Def - Transfinite Composition and Smallness]].

6. **Close $I\text{-cof}$ under the structural operations.** The class $I\text{-cof} = \mathrm{LLP}(I\text{-inj})$ is automatically closed under pushout, coproduct, transfinite composition, and retract — because each operation is preserved by the LLP. *Trigger:* you have built a map by gluing or stacking $I$-cofibrations and want to know the result is one. *Pattern:* "$I\text{-cof}$ is saturated, so this transfinite composite of pushouts is again an $I$-cofibration."

7. **Translate between $J$ and the trivial classes.** Set $J$ generates the trivial cofibrations: $\mathrm{triv\text{-}cof} = J\text{-cof}$ and $\mathrm{fib} = J\text{-inj}$, exactly as $I$ generates $\mathrm{cof} = I\text{-cof}$ and $\mathrm{triv\text{-}fib} = I\text{-inj}$. *Trigger:* a fibration question appears. *Pattern:* "fibration = RLP against $J$"; the two generating sets handle the two factorizations of MC5 separately. See [[Def - Cofibrantly Generated Model Category]].

8. **Verify a model structure via the recognition checklist.** Rather than checking MC1–MC5 directly, supply $\mathcal{W}, I, J$ and verify Kan's conditions: $\mathcal{W}$ has 2-out-of-3 and retracts, both sets have small domains, $J\text{-cell} \subseteq \mathcal{W}$, and the matching identities (i) $I$-inj $=\mathcal{W}\cap J$-inj, (ii) $J$-cell $\subseteq \mathcal{W}\cap I$-cof. *Trigger:* you are asked to prove a triple is a model category. *Pattern:* "by the recognition theorem it suffices to check the following finite list." See [[Thm - The Recognition Theorem for Cofibrantly Generated Model Categories]].

9. **Reduce a lifting question to the generators.** Because $I\text{-inj} = \mathrm{RLP}(I)$ and lifting against $I$ already implies lifting against all of $I\text{-cof}$, you never need to test lifting against the full class — only against the set $I$. *Trigger:* a lifting problem against a cofibration. *Pattern:* "it suffices to lift against the generators $I$, which is a set, so the test is finite per generator." This is the formal reason cofibrant generation is useful at all.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Running the small object argument without checking smallness
> It is tempting to factor any map as $(I\text{-cell})\circ(I\text{-inj})$ for any set $I$, since the transfinite tower can always be built. But without smallness of the domains of $I$, the colimit-side map $g : Z_\infty \to Y$ need **not** have the RLP against $I$: a lifting problem can "escape to infinity," its top map $\mathrm{dom}(i) \to Z_\infty$ failing to factor through any bounded stage $Z_\alpha$, so no cell was ever attached to supply the lift. The construction produces a relative cell complex on the left but garbage on the right. The operation becomes legal exactly when the domains of $I$ are [[Def - Transfinite Composition and Smallness|small relative to I]], which guarantees every lifting problem appears at a bounded stage and is solved.

> [!warning] 2. Confusing $I\text{-cell}$ with $I\text{-cof}$
> It is natural to assume the maps built from $I$ ($I\text{-cell}$, transfinite composites of pushouts) are the *same* as the cofibrations generated by $I$ ($I\text{-cof}$, the LLP-class). They are not: $I\text{-cof}$ is strictly larger, being the **retracts** of $I\text{-cell}$ maps. The retract $\mathrm{id}_X : X \to X$ of any cell complex is an $I$-cofibration that is generally not itself a relative cell complex, and a section of a cell complex need not be one. The repair is the closure identity $I\text{-cof} = (\text{retracts of } I\text{-cell})$, proved by the [[Thm - The Retract Argument|retract argument]] after the small object argument; "built from $I$" and "generated by $I$" differ exactly by retracts.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming a colimit of small objects is small, or that smallness is automatic
> It is tempting to treat smallness as a harmless technical condition that always holds. It does hold for compact spaces, finite sets, finitely presented modules, and all objects of a presheaf category — but it genuinely fails in general. An infinite discrete space is small in $\mathbf{Top}$ only relative to certain classes, and naively "the colimit of the whole tower" is generally not small even when each stage is. The standard trap is to certify smallness with the wrong cardinal $\kappa$, or to forget the regularity hypothesis on $\lambda$: if $\lambda$ is singular, a map out of $A$ can be assembled from cofinally many stages without factoring through any one of them. Smallness must be checked against a *regular* $\lambda \geq \kappa$, and $\kappa$ chosen larger than the relevant cardinality.

> [!warning] 4. Believing the two recognition conditions are redundant
> Kan's theorem requires both (i) $I$-inj $=\mathcal{W}\cap J$-inj and (ii) $J$-cell $\subseteq \mathcal{W}\cap I$-cof, and it is tempting to think one follows from the other or from the smallness hypotheses. Neither does. Condition (ii) is what makes the *trivial cofibrations actually trivial* — that $J$-built maps are weak equivalences — and dropping it lets you "generate" a triple that satisfies the lifting axioms but whose acyclic maps are not weak equivalences, so 2-out-of-3 and the factorization interact incorrectly. Condition (i) is what aligns the two factorizations so that "trivial fibration" computed via $I$ agrees with "fibration and weak equivalence" computed via $J$. The standard failure is to pick a $J$ too small (some trivial cofibration is not in $J$-cof) or too large ($J$-cell escapes $\mathcal{W}$); both break a different axiom, which is why both conditions are load-bearing.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this chapter are won or lost the moment you decide which of the five targets you are chasing, so begin by classifying. Almost every exercise is one of: produce a factorization, prove a closure identity, certify smallness, exhibit generators, or recognize a model structure — and each has a characteristic route.

If the problem **asks you to factor a map** — to discharge MC5, or to build a (co)fibrant replacement — the route is always the [[Thm - The Small Object Argument|small object argument]]. You do not invent the factorization; you build the transfinite tower $Z_0 = X, Z_1, \dots$, attaching at each stage one cell for every lifting problem against a generator, take the colimit, and read off the two factors. The colimit-side map is a relative cell complex by construction, and the other factor is $I$-injective *provided you have checked smallness*. The entire skill is recognizing that the only hypothesis you must verify is smallness of the domains of $I$; everything else is the same construction every time.

If the problem **asks you to prove a closure identity** — that $I\text{-cof}$ equals the retracts of $I\text{-cell}$, or that $I\text{-cof}$ is closed under pushout — the route runs through the [[Thm - The Retract Argument|retract argument]] combined with the small object argument. To show an arbitrary $f \in I\text{-cof}$ is a retract of a cell complex, factor $f = p \circ i$ with $i \in I\text{-cell}$ and $p \in I\text{-inj}$ (small object argument), then observe $f$ lifts against $p$ (because $f \in I\text{-cof} = \mathrm{LLP}(I\text{-inj})$ and $p \in I\text{-inj}$), and the retract argument exhibits $f$ as a retract of $i$. The closure properties under pushout, coproduct, and transfinite composition are even cleaner: they hold for *any* class defined as an LLP, because the LLP relation is preserved by these colimit operations — this is the "saturation" of $\mathrm{LLP}(S)$.

If the problem **asks you to certify smallness**, the assumption pattern is that you have an object $A$ and a class $I$, and you must produce a cardinal $\kappa$ such that maps out of $A$ into long colimits factor through bounded stages. In topological settings the route is compactness: a compact $A$ has compact image, which meets only finitely many cells, hence sits inside a bounded stage. In algebraic or combinatorial settings (sets, modules, presheaves) the route is cardinality: take $\kappa$ above $|A|$ (or the number of generators of $A$), and use regularity of $\lambda$ to assemble the factorization. The decisive move is choosing $\kappa$ correctly and remembering that $\lambda$ must be *regular*, or the cofinal-assembly counterexample bites.

If the problem **asks you to exhibit generators** — to show $\mathbf{Top}$ or $\mathbf{sSet}$ is cofibrantly generated — the route is to name $I$ and $J$ and identify the four classes. For $\mathbf{Top}$: $I = \{S^{n-1}\hookrightarrow D^n\}$ gives $I$-inj $=$ trivial fibrations and $I$-cof $=$ cofibrations; $J = \{D^n \hookrightarrow D^n\times[0,1]\}$ gives $J$-inj $=$ Serre fibrations. For $\mathbf{sSet}$: $I = \{\partial\Delta^n \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$ and $J = \{\Lambda^n_k \hookrightarrow \Delta^n\}$ (horn inclusions). The skill is recognizing that the standard generating inclusions you already know from CW theory or simplicial homotopy *are* the generators, and that their domains (spheres, boundaries, horns) are small.

If the problem **asks you to recognize a model structure from scratch**, you have $\mathcal{W}, I, J$ and want a full model structure. Run the [[Thm - The Recognition Theorem for Cofibrantly Generated Model Categories|recognition checklist]]: confirm $\mathcal{W}$ has 2-out-of-3 and is retract-closed, confirm both sets have small domains, confirm $J\text{-cell}\subseteq\mathcal{W}$, and verify the two matching conditions (i)–(ii). The non-obvious work is almost always condition (i), which equates trivial fibrations computed two ways; the standard tactic is the retract argument plus 2-out-of-3.

A meta-strategy threads through all of these: **a cofibrantly generated model category is a homotopy theory compressed to two sets, and every question reduces to a question about those sets.** Whether you are factoring, closing a class, certifying smallness, or recognizing a structure, the move is to stop reasoning about the proper classes of cofibrations and fibrations and instead reason about lifting against, and building from, the finite generating data $I$ and $J$. Internalizing this single reduction — "test against the generators, build from the generators" — resolves the majority of the chapter, because the entire apparatus exists precisely to make the generators sufficient.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - The Small Object Argument|The Small Object Argument]]**: any map factors as $(I\text{-cell})\circ(I\text{-inj})$ when the domains of $I$ are small. This is the most reusable single result in the chapter because it is what *produces* the factorizations every model structure needs — you never check MC5 by hand once you have it. Reach for it whenever you must factor a map, build a (co)fibrant replacement, or approximate a map by an $I$-injective; the only precondition to verify is smallness. It is also the engine behind the closure identity $I\text{-cof} =$ retracts of $I\text{-cell}$, so it pays compound interest: one construction discharges factorization, characterizes cofibrations, and certifies the lifting structure.

- **The lifting-class identities** $I\text{-inj} = \mathrm{RLP}(I)$ and $I\text{-cof} = \mathrm{LLP}(I\text{-inj})$: each derived class is a lifting class. **Typical use:** to recognize a map as a cofibration or fibration without computing factorizations — a map is an $I$-cofibration precisely when it lifts against every $I$-injective, and it suffices (operation 9) to test against the *set* $I$. These identities also deliver, for free, that $I\text{-cof}$ is closed under pushout, coproduct, transfinite composition, and retract, because every $\mathrm{LLP}$-class is. This is the reason a set of generators controls a whole class.

- **[[Def - Transfinite Composition and Smallness|Smallness]]**: maps out of a small object factor through a bounded stage of any long colimit. **Typical use:** the termination certificate for the small object argument, and more broadly the tool for any argument that builds an object by transfinite iteration and then needs to detect properties of the colimit at a finite stage. The trigger to reach for it is "I have a transfinite construction and need the colimit to inherit a lifting/RLP property"; the fix is always to establish smallness of the relevant domains and conclude the property is detected boundedly. It is the homotopy-theoretic analogue of compactness in analysis.

- **[[Def - Relative Cell Complex|The cell-complex picture of cofibrations]]**: cofibrations are retracts of transfinite cell attachments. **Typical use:** to *understand* what a cofibration is in any cofibrantly generated category — it is something built from the generators by gluing, up to a retract. This is the picture that makes "cofibrant object" concrete (CW complex in $\mathbf{Top}$, complex of projectives in $\mathbf{Ch}(R)$, every object in $\mathbf{sSet}$), and it is what lets inductive arguments over the cell structure replace abstract lifting arguments.

- **[[Thm - The Recognition Theorem for Cofibrantly Generated Model Categories|Kan's Recognition Theorem]]**: a checklist on $(\mathcal{W}, I, J)$ produces a cofibrantly generated model structure. **Typical use:** to *build* a model structure rather than verify one by hand, and especially to transfer a model structure along an adjunction or to put a model structure on diagrams, algebras, or localizations. Recognizing that "I want a model structure and I have candidate weak equivalences and generators" is the signal to invoke it; it converts the open-ended task of checking five axioms into a bounded verification, and it is the standard route by which new model categories enter the literature.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic topology — CW complexes and the cellular construction of spaces.** A CW complex is built exactly as a relative cell complex over the generating set $I = \{S^{n-1}\hookrightarrow D^n\}$: start with a discrete set, then transfinitely (here, countably) attach $n$-cells by pushing out $\coprod S^{n-1} \to \coprod D^n$ along attaching maps. The abstract [[Def - Relative Cell Complex|relative I-cell complex]] is the categorical distillation of this. The model-categorical statement that cofibrations are retracts of relative cell complexes is the precise form of the classical fact that cofibrations of spaces are (up to retract) inclusions of subcomplexes, and the small object argument is the abstraction of "cellular approximation": any map can be replaced by a cellular inclusion followed by a fibration. The compactness of spheres that you use to prove cellular approximation is exactly the smallness hypothesis that makes the small object argument terminate.

2. **Homological algebra — projective resolutions as cell complexes of chain complexes.** In $\mathbf{Ch}(R)$ the generating cofibrations are the inclusions $S^{n-1}(R) \hookrightarrow D^n(R)$ of sphere and disk complexes (a sphere complex is $R$ in one degree; a disk complex is $R \xrightarrow{\mathrm{id}} R$ spanning two adjacent degrees). A relative cell complex over this set is precisely a complex built by freely adjoining generators degree by degree — that is, a complex of [[Def - Module|free (hence projective) modules]] mapping to the target. The cofibrant objects are the complexes of projectives, and a cofibrant replacement is a **projective resolution**. So the small object argument, specialized to chain complexes, *is* the construction of projective resolutions, and the recognition theorem is what certifies that quasi-isomorphisms, degreewise surjections, and these cofibrations form a model structure whose homotopy category is the derived category $D(R)$.

3. **Higher category theory — combinatorial model categories and presentable ∞-categories.** A cofibrantly generated model category whose underlying category is locally presentable (generated under colimits by a *set* of small objects) is a **combinatorial model category**. These are precisely the model categories that present **presentable ∞-categories**, the central objects of Lurie's higher algebra: every presentable ∞-category is the homotopy theory of some combinatorial model category, and Quillen equivalences of combinatorial model categories correspond to equivalences of the presented ∞-categories. The smallness condition of this chapter is the 1-categorical shadow of the $\kappa$-compact-object condition that defines presentability, and Jeff Smith's recognition theorem (a streamlined form of Kan's) is the standard tool for building combinatorial model structures, including the ones underlying $\infty$-categorical localization.

4. **Algebraic geometry — model structures on sheaves and the construction of derived categories.** The derived category of quasi-coherent sheaves on a scheme is the homotopy category of a cofibrantly generated (indeed combinatorial) model structure on chain complexes of sheaves of $\mathcal{O}_X$-modules. Building it requires exactly the machinery here: a set of generators (twists of the structure sheaf, or local-to-global cell complexes), smallness of those generators (which holds because the category of sheaves on a small site is a Grothendieck topos, hence locally presentable), and the recognition theorem to certify the model structure. Left Bousfield localization, run against an enlarged set of generating trivial cofibrations, then produces the model structures for hypercomplete and other modified sheaf homotopy theories. The chapter's combinatorial control over factorizations is what makes derived algebraic geometry computable rather than merely definable.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a homotopy theory is finite data plus a machine.** It is tempting to view a model category as an unwieldy package of three proper classes of maps. The lesson of this chapter is that, in every case that arises in practice, the package is generated by two *sets* $I$ and $J$, and the three classes are recovered mechanically by lifting and cell-attachment. The homotopy theory is therefore compressed into the finite data $(\mathcal{W}, I, J)$, and the small object argument is the machine that decompresses it back into factorizations and replacements. Whenever you confront a cofibrantly generated model category, the productive question is never "what are all the cofibrations?" but "what are the generators, and what do they build and lift against?" This is the same compression that turns an infinite group into a presentation, or an infinite-dimensional space into a CW structure: name the cells and the relations, and let a construction reconstruct the whole.

**The true name of smallness is "compactness against transfinite assembly."** The official definition — $\mathcal{C}(A,-)$ commutes with the relevant transfinite colimits — is the right thing to *check* but the wrong thing to *picture*. Operationally, $A$ is small if a map out of $A$ cannot be spread across infinitely many stages of a long construction without already living at a bounded stage; its image is "compact" relative to the filtration. This is why compact spaces, finite sets, finitely presented modules, and finitely generated everything are small, and why the condition is the homotopy-theoretic descendant of compactness in analysis. The trigger-reaction is exact: when a transfinite construction needs the colimit to detect a property already visible at finite stages, reach for smallness, precisely as one reaches for compactness to pass from local to global or to rule out escape-to-infinity.

**Generated-by versus built-from: cofibrations exceed cell complexes by exactly the retracts.** A recurring confusion is that the maps you can literally build from $I$ — the relative cell complexes $I\text{-cell}$ — should be all the cofibrations. They are not: $I\text{-cof}$ is the retract-closure of $I\text{-cell}$, and the gap is genuine (an identity map is a cofibration but rarely a cell complex). The retract argument is what bridges the gap, and recognizing that "generated by a set" means "retracts of things built from the set" is a pattern that recurs across mathematics — the subgroup generated by a set, the ideal generated by a set, the saturation of a class under an operation all have this built-from-plus-closure shape. In the model-categorical case the closure is under retracts, and the small object argument is what proves the closure is exactly right.

**The recognition theorem turns existence into a checklist, and the two non-formal conditions are where the homotopy theory lives.** Of Kan's hypotheses, four are bookkeeping — $\mathcal{W}$ closed under retracts and 2-out-of-3, and the two smallness conditions — and two carry the actual content: $I$-inj $=\mathcal{W}\cap J$-inj and $J$-cell $\subseteq \mathcal{W}\cap I$-cof. These two conditions are exactly the statements that the two generating sets are *compatible* with the chosen weak equivalences: that lifting against $I$ detects trivial fibrations, and that building from $J$ produces weak equivalences. Everything subtle about a particular model structure — why these maps and not others are the weak equivalences, why this $J$ and not another — is concentrated in verifying these two. This is the cleanest illustration of the chapter's thesis: once the formal scaffolding is in place, a homotopy theory is determined by how a small amount of generating data interacts with the class of maps you have chosen to invert.
