# Problem Statement

For each of the four standard triangulated categories below, identify (i) its shift, (ii) a weak generator, (iii) whether that generator is compact, and (iv) the endomorphism ring(-spectrum) of the generator. Then state, in each case, what "modules over $\mathrm{End}(G)$" amounts to.

1. The [[Def - Chain Map and Chain Homotopy|derived category]] $D(R)$ of a [[Def - Ring|ring]] $R$.
2. The stable homotopy category $\mathcal{SH}$.
3. The stable module category $\underline{\mathbf{Mod}}_\Lambda$ over a self-injective ring $\Lambda$.
4. The bounded derived category of coherent sheaves $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$ on a smooth projective variety $X$.

**Recall:**

![[Def - Compact Weak Generator#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Triangulated Category|triangulated category]] has an invertible shift $\Sigma$ and distinguished triangles. A **compact weak generator** detects nonzero objects (via shifts) and has $[G, -]$ commuting with coproducts. By Schwede–Shipley, a single compact generator presents the category as modules over the endomorphism ring spectrum $\mathrm{End}(G)$.

> [!note]- Algebraic geometry background: coherent sheaves and $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$
> No algebraic geometry is assumed. A **variety** $X$ is a geometric space glued from affine pieces, each affine piece $\mathrm{Spec}\,R$ being the geometric avatar of a commutative ring $R$ (its points are prime ideals, its functions are ring elements). A **coherent sheaf** on $X$ is the geometric version of a finitely generated module: over each affine chart it is a finitely generated module over that chart's ring, the charts glued along overlaps. Coherent sheaves form an abelian category $\mathrm{Coh}\,X$. Taking bounded complexes of coherent sheaves and inverting quasi-isomorphisms gives the **bounded derived category** $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$, triangulated by the same mapping-cone construction as $D(R)$ — the only change is "module over a ring" becomes "module over the sheaf of rings on $X$."

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** This is a "match structure to examples" consolidation problem — the §3 target of recognizing the four canonical triangulated categories and their generators, testing whether you can apply the compact-generator framework across the board.

**Assumption pattern:** Each example supplies a natural candidate generator (the free module $R$, the sphere $\mathbb{S}$, the regular representation $\Lambda$, a tilting/spanning object on $X$), and the resource is knowing how maps out of each generator are computed (homology, homotopy groups, stable homs, derived sheaf homs).

**Theorem routing:** In each case, compute the detector $[\Sigma^n G, -]$, check generation and compactness (operation 7), identify $\mathrm{End}(G)$, and apply Schwede–Shipley. The four cases bracket the phenomenon: $D(R)$ and $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$ are "ordinary-ring-like" in the affine case, $\mathcal{SH}$ is maximally homotopical, and $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$ for non-affine $X$ may *lack* a single compact generator that is a ring (needing a tilting object or a dg-algebra).

**Key decision point:** The subtle case is $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$: a single compact generator with *ordinary* endomorphism ring exists only when $X$ admits a **tilting object**; in general the endomorphism object is a **dg-algebra**, not an ordinary ring. Recognizing that geometry forces higher homotopy (Ext groups in nonzero degrees) into $\mathrm{End}(G)$ is the decision point.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Start from the generator whose detector you know: $D(R)$ has $G = R$ with $[R[n], X] = H_n(X)$; $\mathcal{SH}$ has $G = \mathbb{S}$ with $[\Sigma^n\mathbb{S}, X] = \pi_n(X)$. In both, generation $=$ "detector vanishes $\Rightarrow$ object zero" and compactness $=$ finiteness of the generator.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> The stable module category $\underline{\mathbf{Mod}}_\Lambda$ has shift $\Omega^{-1}$ (cosyzygy) and is generated by $\Lambda$ itself (modulo projectives); $\underline{\mathrm{End}}(\Lambda)$ is the **Tate cohomology** ring $\widehat{H}^*(\Lambda)$, generally with higher-degree pieces.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$: the shift is the complex degree shift. A single compact generator with *ordinary* endomorphism ring exists iff $X$ has a **tilting object** $T$ (then $\mathrm{End}(T)$ is an ordinary algebra and $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X) \simeq D^b(\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\mathrm{End}(T))$ — e.g. Beilinson's resolution for projective space). In general $\mathrm{End}(G)$ is a dg-algebra, because $\mathrm{Ext}^i(G, G) \neq 0$ for some $i > 0$.

# Solution

The plan: tabulate the four examples by (shift, generator, compactness, $\mathrm{End}$), then read off what "modules over $\mathrm{End}(G)$" means in each, noting the spread from ordinary rings to ring spectra to dg-algebras.

**Step 1: $D(R)$ — the ordinary-ring pole.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Shift: degree shift $[1]$. Generator: $R$ (free of rank $1$), with $[R[n], X] = H_n(X)$. Compact: yes ($R$ is finitely generated/projective; homology commutes with sums). $\mathrm{End}(R) = R$, concentrated in degree $0$ — an *ordinary ring*. "Modules over $\mathrm{End}(R)$" $=$ ordinary $R$-modules, recovering $D(R) = D(\mathrm{Mod}_R)$ tautologically. (See [[Ex - R is a compact generator of its derived category]].)

**Step 2: $\mathcal{SH}$ — the ring-spectrum pole.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Shift: invertible suspension $\Sigma$. Generator: sphere spectrum $\mathbb{S}$, with $[\Sigma^n\mathbb{S}, X] = \pi_n(X)$. Compact: yes ($\mathbb{S}$ is finite). $\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{S}) = \mathbb{S}$, with $\pi_*\mathbb{S} = \pi_*^s$ nonzero in infinitely many degrees — a genuine *ring spectrum*. "Modules over $\mathbb{S}$" $=$ all spectra, which is the maximally homotopical case. (See [[Ex - The sphere spectrum is a compact generator of the stable homotopy category]].)

**Step 3: $\underline{\mathbf{Mod}}_\Lambda$ — the Tate-cohomology case.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Shift: the cosyzygy $\Omega^{-1}$ (invertible because $\Lambda$ self-injective makes projectives $=$ injectives). Generator: $\Lambda$ (the regular module, modulo projectives), which detects nonzero stable modules. Compact: yes for finite-dimensional $\Lambda$. $\underline{\mathrm{End}}(\Lambda) = \widehat{H}^*(\Lambda)$, the Tate cohomology, generally with nonzero higher-degree parts. "Modules over $\mathrm{End}(\Lambda)$" $=$ modules over the Tate cohomology ring (spectrum), the home of group-cohomological stable phenomena.

**Step 4: $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$ — the dg-algebra / tilting case.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Shift: complex degree shift. Generator: in the affine case $X = \mathrm{Spec}\,R$, the structure sheaf $\mathcal{O}_X$ corresponds to $R$, recovering case 1. For general (e.g. projective) $X$, a *single* compact generator exists (e.g. $\mathcal{O} \oplus \mathcal{O}(1) \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathcal{O}(n)$ on $\mathbb{P}^n$, Beilinson), and it is compact (perfect complexes are the compact objects of $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$ for smooth $X$). When the generator is a **tilting object** $T$ — meaning $\mathrm{Ext}^i(T, T) = 0$ for $i \neq 0$ — its endomorphism object is the *ordinary algebra* $\mathrm{End}(T)$ and $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X) \simeq D^b(\mathrm{Mod}\text{-}\mathrm{End}(T))$. When no tilting object exists, $\mathrm{End}(G)$ is a genuine **dg-algebra** with $\mathrm{Ext}^i(G, G) \neq 0$ for some $i > 0$, and "modules over $\mathrm{End}(G)$" means **dg-modules**. This is the case where geometry forces higher homotopy, and it is the entry point to noncommutative and derived algebraic geometry.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> | Category | Shift | Generator $G$ | Compact? | $\mathrm{End}(G)$ | "Modules over $\mathrm{End}(G)$" |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | $D(R)$ | $[1]$ | $R$ | yes | $R$ (degree $0$) | ordinary $R$-modules |
> | $\mathcal{SH}$ | $\Sigma$ | $\mathbb{S}$ | yes | $\mathbb{S}$, $\pi_* = \pi_*^s$ | spectra |
> | $\underline{\mathbf{Mod}}_\Lambda$ | $\Omega^{-1}$ | $\Lambda$ | yes (fin. dim.) | Tate cohomology $\widehat{H}^*\Lambda$ | modules over $\widehat{H}^*\Lambda$ |
> | $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$ | $[1]$ | tilting/spanning obj. | yes (perfect) | ordinary algebra (if tilting) or dg-algebra | (dg-)modules over $\mathrm{End}(G)$ |
>
> The four examples span the recognition theorem: $D(R)$ (ordinary ring), $\mathcal{SH}$ (ring spectrum, max homotopy), $\underline{\mathbf{Mod}}_\Lambda$ (Tate cohomology), $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$ (ordinary algebra when a tilting object exists, dg-algebra otherwise). In every case the compact generator's endomorphism object is the ring(-spectrum)(-dg-algebra) the category is modules over. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Every triangulated category in nature is "modules over its generator's endomorphism object," and the four examples show the full range of that object.** The unifying lesson is that the recognition theorem is not an isolated result but a *classification principle*: find the compact generator, compute its endomorphism object, and you have identified the entire homotopy theory. The range of endomorphism objects — ordinary ring ($D(R)$), ring spectrum ($\mathcal{SH}$), Tate cohomology ($\underline{\mathbf{Mod}}_\Lambda$), dg-algebra ($D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$) — is the range of "kinds of algebra" that stable homotopy theory encompasses. The trigger: on meeting any triangulated category, ask "what is the generator and what is its $\mathrm{End}$?"

**Higher homotopy in $\mathrm{End}(G)$ is the precise measure of how far a category is from ordinary algebra, and geometry forces it.** When $\mathrm{End}(G)$ is concentrated in degree $0$ (an ordinary ring), the category is classical module theory; nonzero higher homotopy ($\mathrm{Ext}^i(G,G) \neq 0$ or $\pi_i \neq 0$ for $i > 0$) measures the genuinely homotopical content. The transferable diagnostic: the existence of a **tilting object** (Ext-vanishing in nonzero degrees) is exactly the condition that collapses $\mathrm{End}(G)$ to an ordinary algebra, and its *absence* — typical for curved or higher-dimensional varieties — is why derived algebraic geometry needs dg-algebras and ∞-categories rather than ordinary rings.

**The compact-generator framework is the bridge from this chapter to derived algebraic geometry, noncommutative geometry, and modular representation theory.** Each of the four examples is the gateway to a major field: $D(R)$ to homological algebra, $\mathcal{SH}$ to stable homotopy and chromatic theory, $\underline{\mathbf{Mod}}_\Lambda$ to modular representation theory and support varieties, $D^b(\mathrm{Coh}\,X)$ to derived algebraic geometry and homological mirror symmetry. The conceptual payoff: mastering "identify the generator, compute its endomorphisms" gives a single uniform entry point to all of these, because in each the central object of study is a triangulated category presented by a compact generator — the homotopical Morita theorem at work across mathematics.
