# Notation Registry

- $X$ — an arbitrary set; $2^X=\mathcal{P}(X)$ — its power set
- $A^c=X\setminus A$ — complement; $A\,\triangle\,B$ — symmetric difference; $\bigsqcup$ — disjoint union
- $\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{F}$ — algebras or $\sigma$-algebras of subsets
- $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ — the $\sigma$-algebra generated by a family $\mathcal{C}$
- $\mathcal{B}(X)$ — the Borel $\sigma$-algebra of a topological space; $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, or $\mathcal{B}$ for $\mathbb{R}$
- $(X,\mathcal{A})$ — a measurable space; $(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)$ — a measure space
- $\mu,\nu$ — measures; $\widetilde\mu$ — a pre-measure; $\mu^*$ — an outer measure; $\mathbb{P}$ — a probability measure
- $[0,\infty]=[0,\infty)\cup\{\infty\}$ — the extended half-line, with $x+\infty=\infty$, $x\cdot\infty=\infty$ ($x>0$), $0\cdot\infty=0$
- $\Sigma$ — the Carathéodory $\sigma$-algebra of an outer measure
- $A_k\uparrow A$ / $A_k\downarrow A$ — increasing / decreasing sequences with union / intersection $A$
- $\lambda$ (or $\lambda_n$, $\mathrm{d}x$) — Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$; $\widetilde\lambda$ — elementary volume
- $\mathcal{B}^*(\mathbb{R}^n)$ — the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra (completion of $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$)
- $V$ — a Vitali (non-measurable) set
- $A\,\mu\text{-a.e.}$ — a property failing only on a null set

# Motivation

How do you assign a *size* — length, area, volume, probability — to subsets of a space? The naive answer, "to every subset," is impossible: [[Thm - Existence of a Non-Measurable Set|Vitali's theorem]] shows there is no countably additive, translation-invariant size defined on all of $2^{\mathbb{R}}$. So measure theory begins with a concession: we measure only a privileged collection of "nice" sets, and the collection must be closed under exactly the operations a size should respect — complement, and *countable* union. That collection is a [[Def - Algebra and σ-Algebra|σ-algebra]], and a **measure** is a countably additive, $[0,\infty]$-valued function on it.

Why *countable* additivity, and not merely finite? Because analysis is the study of limits. Finite additivity supports no limit theorem; uncountable additivity admits no nontrivial measure. Countable additivity is the unique compromise — strong enough that $\mu$ is continuous along monotone sequences of sets, weak enough that measures exist. Everything downstream — the [[Thm - Monotone Convergence Theorem|convergence theorems]], the whole superiority of Lebesgue's integral over Riemann's — is the dividend of this one choice.

The chapter then confronts the central technical problem: measures are hard to *define directly*. We know what volume a box should have; we have no formula for the volume of an arbitrary Borel set. The resolution is an extension machine. Define the size only where it is easy — on an algebra of simple sets, as a [[Def - Pre-Measure|pre-measure]] — then *inflate* it to an [[Def - Outer Measure|outer measure]] on every subset by covering from outside, and *deflate* it to a genuine measure on the [[Def - Carathéodory Measurable Sets|Carathéodory σ-algebra]] where the outer measure becomes additive. This is the [[Thm - Hahn-Carathéodory Extension Theorem|Hahn–Carathéodory theorem]], with [[Thm - Uniqueness of the Hahn-Carathéodory Extension|uniqueness]] under $\sigma$-finiteness. Run the machine on the elementary-volume pre-measure and out comes [[Def - Lebesgue Measure|Lebesgue measure]] — *the* canonical notion of volume on $\mathbb{R}^n$, translation-invariant, regular, and complete.

# Concept Map

## §1.1 Abstract Measure Theory

- **[[Def - Algebra and σ-Algebra]]**
	- A family $\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^X$ containing $X$ and closed under complement is an *algebra* if also closed under finite unions, a *$\sigma$-algebra* if closed under countable unions. The "$\sigma$" (countable) closure is the precise strength supporting limits while still admitting measures; arbitrary-union closure would force $\mathcal{A}=2^X$, on which no good measure exists. The trivial $\{\emptyset,X\}$ and discrete $2^X$ bracket all others; $(0,1]$'s half-open intervals form an algebra that is not a $\sigma$-algebra.
- **[[Def - Generated σ-Algebra]]**
	- $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal{C}$, defined as the intersection of all $\sigma$-algebras containing $\mathcal{C}$ — well-defined because $\sigma$-algebras are closed under intersection and $2^X$ is one. The construction is non-constructive: building $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ explicitly needs transfinite iteration (the Borel hierarchy). Its working form is the minimality principle: to prove $\sigma(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathcal{A}$ it suffices that $\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}$.
- **[[Def - Borel σ-Algebra]]**
	- $\mathcal{B}(X)=\sigma(\tau)$, generated by the open sets of a topological space — the canonical bridge from topology to measurability. On $\mathbb{R}^n$ it is generated equally by open balls, boxes, or (in 1D) rays $(-\infty,a)$. Every continuous map is Borel measurable; the Borel sets are minimal, hence functorial, unlike the larger Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra. Not every subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is Borel, and not every Lebesgue set is Borel.
- **[[Def - Measurable Space]]**
	- A pair $(X,\mathcal{A})$ — a set with a $\sigma$-algebra — the *stage* on which a measure will later act. The choice of $\mathcal{A}$ is substantive: it fixes which functions are measurable and (in probability) how much information is resolvable. Morphisms between measurable spaces are the measurable maps.
- **[[Def - Measure and Measure Space]]**
	- A measure $\mu:\mathcal{A}\to[0,\infty]$ satisfies $\mu(\emptyset)=0$ and $\sigma$-additivity over disjoint sequences; the triple $(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)$ is a measure space. Finite if $\mu(X)<\infty$, a probability measure if $\mu(X)=1$. Counting measure, Dirac mass $\delta_x$, and Lebesgue measure are the basic examples; densities $\nu(A)=\int_A f\,d\mu$ and pushforwards are derived ones.
- **[[Thm - Properties of Measures]]**
	- From the two axioms alone follow monotonicity, finite additivity, continuity from below ($A_k\uparrow A\Rightarrow\mu(A_k)\to\mu(A)$, unconditional), continuity from above ($A_k\downarrow A\Rightarrow\mu(A_k)\to\mu(A)$, *needs $\mu(A_1)<\infty$*), and $\sigma$-subadditivity. Continuity from below is $\sigma$-additivity re-dressed; the proofs all run on the disjointification trick. The first Borel–Cantelli lemma is a one-line corollary.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Borel Hierarchy *(from Descriptive Set Theory)*
> The non-constructive definition $\sigma(\mathcal{C})=\bigcap\{\mathcal{A}\supseteq\mathcal{C}:\mathcal{A}\text{ is a $\sigma$-algebra}\}$ has a constructive counterpart: iterate "countable union" and "complement" transfinitely starting from the open sets. The resulting stratification $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^0_1\subset\boldsymbol{\Pi}^0_1\subset\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^0_2\subset\cdots$ — open, closed, $F_\sigma$, $G_\delta$, and so on through every countable ordinal — is the **Borel hierarchy**, and it does not stabilise until level $\omega_1$. Descriptive set theory is the study of this hierarchy and its analytic and projective extensions, and it is the natural home of the question "how complicated is this set?"

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Random Variable|Random Variables]] and [[Def - Filtration|Filtrations]] *(from Probability Theory)*
> A measurable space $(X,\mathcal{A})$ is exactly the abstract structure underlying a [[Def - Probability Space|probability space]] once a probability measure is added — events live in $\mathcal{A}$ and a [[Def - Random Variable|random variable]] is a measurable function. A sub-$\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{A}$ is *partial information*, and an increasing chain $\mathcal{F}_0\subseteq\mathcal{F}_1\subseteq\cdots$ is a [[Def - Filtration|filtration]] — the central data structure of every discrete-time stochastic process. Without §1.1 there is no Kolmogorov framework.

## §1.2 Construction of Measures

- **[[Def - Pre-Measure]]**
	- A set function on an *algebra* satisfying $\widetilde\mu(\emptyset)=0$ and countable additivity *restricted to those disjoint countable unions that land back in the algebra*. The input to the extension machine: easy to define (elementary volume on boxes), and the conditional countable-additivity demand is exactly the "continuity property" whose verification, for elementary volume, needs Heine–Borel compactness.
- **[[Def - Outer Measure]]**
	- A monotone, countably subadditive $\mu^*:2^X\to[0,\infty]$ with $\mu^*(\emptyset)=0$, defined on *every* subset. Built from a [[Def - Outer Measure|cover]] $\mathcal{K}$ and a set function by $\mu^*(A)=\inf\{\sum\widetilde\mu(K_j):A\subseteq\bigcup K_j\}$ — over-estimation by economical covering. It trades additivity for total domain; recovering additivity is the job of the Carathéodory criterion.
- **[[Def - σ-Finite Measure]]**
	- $\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite if $X=\bigcup_k S_k$ with $\mu(S_k)<\infty$ — the space is exhausted by finite-measure pieces. The standing hypothesis under which finite-measure technology (subtraction, continuity from above) applies piecewise; required for uniqueness of extensions, product measures, Fubini, Radon–Nikodym. Lebesgue measure is $\sigma$-finite (tile by cubes) but not finite.
- **[[Def - Carathéodory Measurable Sets]]**
	- $A\in\Sigma$ iff $A$ splits *every* test set additively: $\mu^*(B)=\mu^*(B\cap A)+\mu^*(B\setminus A)$ for all $B$. The universal quantifier over $B$ is what forces $\Sigma$ to be a full $\sigma$-algebra. Only the inequality "$\geq$" needs checking (subadditivity gives "$\le$"); null sets always lie in $\Sigma$, so $\Sigma$ is complete.
- **[[Thm - Carathéodory's σ-Algebra]]**
	- For any outer measure, $\Sigma$ is a $\sigma$-algebra and $\mu^*|_\Sigma$ is a (complete) measure; and the cover formula does produce an outer measure. This is the engine: it manufactures a genuine measure out of a mere outer measure, and it is proved by recursively splitting test sets — the universal quantifier paying off.
- **[[Thm - Hahn-Carathéodory Extension Theorem]]**
	- Every pre-measure $\widetilde\mu$ on an algebra $\mathcal{A}$ extends to a complete measure on a $\sigma$-algebra $\supseteq\sigma(\mathcal{A})$, agreeing with $\widetilde\mu$ on $\mathcal{A}$. Carathéodory plus two compatibility facts: $\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Sigma$ (uses *finite* additivity of $\widetilde\mu$) and faithfulness (uses *countable* additivity within $\mathcal{A}$). The existence theorem of the subject.
- **[[Thm - Uniqueness of the Hahn-Carathéodory Extension]]**
	- If $\widetilde\mu$ is $\sigma$-finite, the extension is unique. Proof: any extension is $\le$ the Carathéodory one by subadditivity; equality on finite-measure windows by a subtraction argument; globalise by the $\sigma$-finite decomposition. The $\sigma$-finite hypothesis is exactly the licence to subtract; uniqueness genuinely fails without it.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Hausdorff Measure *(from Geometric Measure Theory)*
> The Carathéodory construction does not need a pre-measure on an algebra — it accepts *any* outer measure built from a gauge by infimising over covers. Choosing the gauge $h^s(\text{box})=(\text{diameter})^s$ and infimising over $\delta$-fine covers produces the **$s$-dimensional Hausdorff outer measure** $\mathcal{H}^s$, and the resulting Carathéodory $\sigma$-algebra contains every Borel set. The critical exponent at which $\mathcal{H}^s$ jumps from $\infty$ to $0$ is the **Hausdorff [[Def - Dimension|dimension]]** — a real number assigned to every metric set, agreeing with the integer dimension on submanifolds but giving $\log_3 2$ for the Cantor set. Geometric measure theory is the study of this measure and the rectifiable sets that look smooth at $\mathcal{H}^s$-almost every point.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Thm - Product Measure|Product Measures]] and Fubini *(from this topic, §3)*
> The extension machine of §1.2 is exactly the engine that builds **product measures**. Given $\sigma$-finite measure spaces $(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)$ and $(Y,\mathcal{B},\nu)$, the assignment $A\times B\mapsto \mu(A)\nu(B)$ defines a pre-measure on the algebra of finite disjoint unions of measurable rectangles; Hahn–Carathéodory extends it to the unique $\sigma$-finite measure $\mu\otimes\nu$ on the product $\sigma$-algebra. **Fubini's theorem** then licenses the exchange of iterated integrals — see [[Measure Theory III — §3–4 Product Measures and Differentiation|Measure Theory III §3]]. $\sigma$-finiteness, the very hypothesis needed for uniqueness here, is also exactly what Fubini needs.

## §1.3 The Lebesgue Measure

- **[[Def - Interval and Elementary Figure]]**
	- A box is a product of intervals; an elementary figure is a finite disjoint union of boxes; these form the smallest *algebra* on which "volume" is unambiguous. Elementary volume $\widetilde\lambda(\prod(a_k,b_k))=\prod(b_k-a_k)$, extended additively, is the pre-measure seeding Lebesgue measure. The algebra is not a $\sigma$-algebra — that gap is the reason an extension theorem is needed.
- **[[Def - Lebesgue Measure]]**
	- The Hahn–Carathéodory extension of elementary volume: the unique measure on $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ assigning each box its volume. Equivalently, the unique translation-invariant Borel measure with $\lambda([0,1]^n)=1$ — the Haar measure of $(\mathbb{R}^n,+)$. $\sigma$-finite, not finite; complete on the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra; restricts on $[0,1]^n$ to the uniform distribution.
- **[[Def - Null Set and Completion]]**
	- A null set has measure zero; a property holds *almost everywhere* if it fails only on (a subset of) a null set; a space is *complete* if every subset of a null set is measurable. The completion adjoins all such subsets — the smallest enlargement making a.e.-reasoning friction-free. The Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra is the completion of the Borel one, strictly larger.
- **[[Thm - Regularity of Lebesgue Measure]]**
	- Every Borel set is squeezed between a closed (even compact) subset and an open superset with arbitrarily small measure gap; equivalently $\lambda(A)=\inf\{\lambda(G):G\supseteq A\text{ open}\}=\sup\{\lambda(K):K\subseteq A\text{ compact}\}$. Outer regularity is just the definition of $\lambda$ as an inf over covers, re-read. The workhorse approximation principle.
- **[[Thm - Translation Invariance of Lebesgue Measure]]**
	- $\lambda(x_0+A)=\lambda(A)$ for all Borel $A$ — volume is independent of position. True on boxes by definition, carried to all Borel sets because every step of the construction commutes with translation. Conversely it *characterises* $\lambda$ among normalised Borel measures, and it is the property exploited by Vitali.
- **[[Thm - Existence of a Non-Measurable Set]]**
	- There is a set $V\subseteq(0,1]$ not in the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra: $\mathcal{B}^*(\mathbb{R})\neq 2^{\mathbb{R}}$. The proof clashes translation invariance (all rational translates of $V$ have equal measure) with countable additivity (their sum is $0$ or $\infty$) against a geometric sandwich (the sum lies in $[1,3]$). Uses the axiom of choice essentially.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Harmonic Measure *(from Complex Analysis IV)*
> The regularity statement "$\lambda(A)=\sup\{\lambda(K):K\subseteq A\text{ compact}\}=\inf\{\lambda(G):G\supseteq A\text{ open}\}$" is the prototype for a whole family of **Radon measures** — locally finite Borel measures that are inner regular by compacts and outer regular by opens. **Harmonic measure** $\omega_z$ on the boundary of a planar domain $\Omega$, defined as the distribution of the first hitting point of Brownian motion started at $z\in\Omega$, is such a Radon measure, and it is the right notion of "size" for boundary subsets in potential theory and conformal mapping — see [[Complex Analysis IV — Mapping Theory and Applications]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Radon–Nikodym Densities *(from Measure Theory III)*
> Once Lebesgue measure exists as a complete, $\sigma$-finite reference measure, every $\sigma$-finite measure $\nu$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ that vanishes on Lebesgue null sets can be written as $\nu(A)=\int_A f\,d\lambda$ for a measurable density $f\ge 0$ — the **Radon–Nikodym theorem** of [[Measure Theory III — §3–4 Product Measures and Differentiation|Measure Theory III]] (see [[Thm - Radon-Nikodym Theorem]]). This is the bridge from sets to densities, and it is what lets probability theory replace "the distribution of $X$" by "the density of $X$" whenever the distribution is absolutely continuous.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — What do we usually try to prove?** Four recurring goals. (1) *Membership* — "$A\in\mathcal{A}$", "$A\in\Sigma$": a set is measurable. Proved by closure axioms, by the [[Def - Carathéodory Measurable Sets|Carathéodory criterion]], or by exhibiting $A$ as a countable combination of known measurable sets. (2) *Measure values and inequalities* — "$\mu(A)=c$", "$\mu(A)\le\sum\mu(A_k)$": compute or bound a size. Proved by additivity on a decomposition, by continuity along a monotone sequence, by squeezing between approximants. (3) *Existence and uniqueness of a measure* — "there is a (unique) measure with property $P$": the extension theorem supplies existence, $\sigma$-finiteness supplies uniqueness. (4) *Negative/limitative results* — "no measure can do $X$", "$A$ is not measurable": proved by deriving a contradiction from the axioms, the Vitali pattern being the template.

**Sources — What assumptions do we usually leverage?** The recurring hypotheses and what each unlocks. A *$\sigma$-algebra* hypothesis gives countable closure — license to take countable unions/intersections of measurable sets. A *measure* hypothesis gives, via [[Thm - Properties of Measures]], monotonicity, both continuities, and subadditivity — the entire toolkit for relating $\mu$ of a limit to $\mu$ of approximants. A *finiteness* or *$\sigma$-finiteness* hypothesis licenses subtraction and the piecewise deployment of finite-measure arguments. A *pre-measure on an algebra* hypothesis routes straight to the extension theorem. A *monotone sequence of sets* routes to the continuity properties; a *convergent series $\sum\mu(A_k)$* routes to $\sigma$-subadditivity and Borel–Cantelli. The art is recognising, in a problem phrased without these words, that one of these source-structures is present.

# Legal Operations

1. **Check measurability via closure axioms.** To show $A$ is measurable, express it as a countable union, intersection, or complement of sets already known measurable. The input type is "$A$ built from simpler measurable sets by countably many set operations." Used in [[Ex - The first Borel-Cantelli lemma|the limsup identity]] and whenever a set is defined by a logical condition ("$x\in A_k$ infinitely often").

2. **Generate from below, prove from above.** To define the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal{C}$, take $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$; to *prove* $\sigma(\mathcal{C})\subseteq\mathcal{A}$, show only $\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}$ and invoke minimality (the family $\{B:\text{property}(B)\}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra containing the generators). This is how every "measurability holds for all Borel sets" statement is proved — never set-by-set.

3. **Disjointify.** Replace a countable union $\bigcup A_k$ by the pairwise-disjoint $\widetilde A_k=A_k\setminus\bigcup_{j<k}A_j$ with the same union and $\widetilde A_k\subseteq A_k$. Converts any union into a disjoint union so that $\sigma$-additivity applies; the engine of [[Thm - Properties of Measures|continuity from below and σ-subadditivity]].

4. **Pass to a monotone sequence.** Rewrite a set as $\bigcup A_k$ ($A_k\uparrow$) or $\bigcap A_k$ ($A_k\downarrow$) and apply continuity of $\mu$. For decreasing sequences, secure a finite-measure first term (or truncate to one). The standard route to *computing* $\mu$ of a limiting set, as in [[Ex - The Cantor set has Lebesgue measure zero|the Cantor set]].

5. **Cover from outside, infimise.** Estimate an unknown size by covering the set with countably many simple pieces and taking the cheapest cover — the definition of the [[Def - Outer Measure|outer measure]] and of [[Thm - Regularity of Lebesgue Measure|outer regularity]]. Trigger: "I need an upper bound on the size of an irregular set."

6. **Run the extension machine.** Given an intended size on an algebra, verify it is a [[Def - Pre-Measure|pre-measure]] (the work: countable-additivity-within-the-algebra, often a compactness argument), then invoke [[Thm - Hahn-Carathéodory Extension Theorem|Hahn–Carathéodory]] for existence and, if $\sigma$-finite, [[Thm - Uniqueness of the Hahn-Carathéodory Extension|uniqueness]].

7. **Sequence the tolerance.** Upgrade a family of $\varepsilon$-approximations to an exact statement by running $\varepsilon=1/k$ and taking a countable intersection (for supersets) or union (for subsets). Turns [[Thm - Regularity of Lebesgue Measure|regularity]] into the structure theorem [[Ex - Lebesgue sets are Borel modulo a null set|"Lebesgue = Borel mod null"]].

8. **Absorb null sets.** A set of measure (or outer measure) zero is invisible: it lies in every complete $\sigma$-algebra, contributes nothing to any measure, and may be discarded from any a.e. statement. Trigger: a proof produces an exceptional set whose outer measure you can bound by $0$.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Continuity from above without a finite frame
> Concluding $\mu(\bigcap A_k)=\lim\mu(A_k)$ for a decreasing sequence *without* checking $\mu(A_1)<\infty$ (or $\mu(A_{k_0})<\infty$ for some $k_0$). Counterexample: counting measure on $\mathbb{N}$, $A_k=\{k,k+1,\dots\}\downarrow\emptyset$ but $\mu(A_k)\equiv\infty$. The missing hypothesis is what licenses the subtraction in the proof; see [[Ex - Continuity from above requires finite measure]].

> [!warning] 2. Treating an outer measure as additive
> Writing $\mu^*(A\sqcup B)=\mu^*(A)+\mu^*(B)$ for arbitrary disjoint sets. An [[Def - Outer Measure|outer measure]] is only *sub*additive; additivity holds only on the [[Def - Carathéodory Measurable Sets|Carathéodory σ-algebra]]. For the [[Thm - Existence of a Non-Measurable Set|Vitali set]] additivity genuinely fails — that is the whole point of the construction.

> [!warning] 3. Unioning $\sigma$-algebras, or asserting "the" extension without $\sigma$-finiteness
> $\mathcal{A}_1\cup\mathcal{A}_2$ is almost never a $\sigma$-algebra ([[Ex - Unions and intersections of σ-algebras]]) — combining information requires *regenerating*, $\sigma(\mathcal{A}_1\cup\mathcal{A}_2)$. And speaking of "*the*" measure extending a pre-measure presupposes $\sigma$-finiteness; without it distinct extensions coexist ([[Ex - Uniqueness fails without σ-finiteness]]).

> [!warning] 4. Inferring positive measure from "large"
> An uncountable set can be null ([[Ex - The Cantor set has Lebesgue measure zero|the Cantor set]]); a positive-measure set can contain no interval ([[Ex - A fat Cantor set of positive measure|fat Cantor set]]). Measure is not cardinality and is not topological size — never substitute one for another.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this chapter fall into a few classes, and each has a near-deterministic route.

When asked to **show a set is measurable**, do not attempt to describe it explicitly. Instead express it through countably many set operations on sets already known to be in your $\sigma$-algebra — this is operation 1. If the target $\sigma$-algebra is *generated* by a family $\mathcal{C}$, never argue set-by-set: form the family of sets having the desired property, check it is a $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal{C}$, and invoke minimality (operation 2). This single move — "the good sets form a $\sigma$-algebra, so they contain the generated one" — is the most reused proof skeleton in the subject; it is how measurability of functions, of $\limsup$ sets, and of preimages is established.

When asked to **compute or bound a measure**, identify which monotone structure the set carries. A *decreasing intersection* $\bigcap A_k$ with computable $\mu(A_k)$ and a finite first term routes to continuity from above (operation 4) — this solves the Cantor set instantly. An *increasing union* routes to continuity from below, which needs no finiteness. An *irregular set you can only cover* routes to the outer-measure infimum (operation 5). A *bound* rather than an exact value, especially involving a $\limsup$ or a series, routes to $\sigma$-subadditivity: sandwich the target inside a tail union and exploit $\sum\mu(A_k)<\infty$, which is the Borel–Cantelli pattern. The recurring upgrade — operation 7 — converts "$\varepsilon$-close for every $\varepsilon$" into "equal modulo null" by sequencing $\varepsilon=1/k$.

When asked to **construct a measure** with prescribed values, you are looking at the extension machine (operation 6). Write down the intended size on the natural *algebra* of simple sets where it is unambiguous; the real work is verifying it is a pre-measure — countable additivity within the algebra — which for geometric measures means a compactness argument. Then [[Thm - Hahn-Carathéodory Extension Theorem|existence]] is automatic, and [[Thm - Uniqueness of the Hahn-Carathéodory Extension|uniqueness]] follows once you exhibit a $\sigma$-finite exhaustion.

When asked to **prove a negative result** — that no measure can satisfy certain demands, or that a set is not measurable — assume the object exists and mine the axioms for a contradiction. The [[Thm - Existence of a Non-Measurable Set|Vitali template]] is the model: find a symmetry [[Def - Group|group]] acting measure-preservingly, produce countably many disjoint translates of one set, and clash "all translates have equal measure" (a sum of a constant, hence $0$ or $\infty$) against a geometric containment that traps the total strictly between two finite numbers.

Across all classes, the meta-skill is *source recognition* (see Sources and Targets): a problem rarely says "here is a $\sigma$-finite measure and a decreasing sequence." It says something concrete, and you must see the monotone sequence, the convergent series, the pre-measure, or the symmetry hiding inside.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Properties of Measures|Continuity from below]]**: $A_k\uparrow A\Rightarrow\mu(A_k)\to\mu(A)$, *unconditionally*. This is the workhorse — it is how almost every measure of a limiting set is computed, and it needs no finiteness, unlike its decreasing counterpart. Typical use: a set is presented as an increasing union of computable pieces; the measure is the limit. It is also $\sigma$-additivity in disguise, and the set-level shadow of the [[Thm - Monotone Convergence Theorem|monotone convergence theorem]].

- **[[Thm - Properties of Measures|σ-subadditivity]]**: $\mu(\bigcup A_k)\le\sum\mu(A_k)$. The universal upper bound — it costs nothing, needs no disjointness, and is the only tool that survives when sets overlap uncontrollably. Typical use: prove a set is null by covering it with pieces of total measure $0$; bound a $\limsup$; derive the first [[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|Borel–Cantelli]] lemma. Recognise its trigger in any "$\le$" goal about a union or a series.

- **[[Thm - Hahn-Carathéodory Extension Theorem|The extension theorem]]**: a pre-measure on an algebra extends to a measure on the generated $\sigma$-algebra. The single most-used *existence* tool: it builds Lebesgue measure, Lebesgue–Stieltjes measures (hence every law on $\mathbb{R}$), product measures, and the laws of stochastic processes. Typical use: whenever a measure must be *produced*, locate the algebra where its values are obvious and check the pre-measure condition.

- **[[Thm - Regularity of Lebesgue Measure|Regularity]]**: every Borel set is approximable by open supersets and compact subsets to arbitrary measure precision. The bridge that lets one *prove things on simple sets and transport them*. Typical use: reduce a statement about general measurable sets to elementary figures or open sets; combined with tolerance-sequencing it yields the structure of the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra and underlies [[Thm - Lusin's Theorem|Lusin]]/[[Thm - Egorov's Theorem|Egorov]].

- **The minimality of $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$**: to prove a property holds for all sets in a generated $\sigma$-algebra, show the property-satisfying sets form a $\sigma$-algebra containing the generators. Typical use: measurability of functions, of preimages, of $\limsup$ sets — *every* "for all Borel sets" statement. It is the measure-theoretic induction principle.

# Bridges

1. **To probability theory.** A [[Def - Probability Space|probability space]] $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ is exactly a measure space with $\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1$ — Kolmogorov's identification. Every concept here is reread: a $\sigma$-algebra is the collection of *events*; a sub-$\sigma$-algebra is *partial information* and an increasing chain of them is a [[Def - Filtration|filtration]]; a measurable function is a [[Def - Random Variable|random variable]]; the [[Thm - Properties of Measures|continuity of measures]] is the continuity of probability; the first [[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|Borel–Cantelli lemma]] is literally $\sigma$-subadditivity. The [[Thm - Hahn-Carathéodory Extension Theorem|extension theorem]], fed the pre-measure $(a,b]\mapsto F(b)-F(a)$, constructs *every* probability law on $\mathbb{R}$ from its [[Def - Distribution Function|distribution function]].

2. **To functional analysis.** The construction of $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ as an intersection of substructures is the identical pattern as the closed linear span, and the [[Def - Outer Measure|outer measure]]'s "$\inf$ over covers" is a gauge/seminorm construction. Completion of a measure space mirrors completion of a metric space; the [[Def - Null Set and Completion|null sets]] are the analogue of the kernel one quotients by to pass from $\mathcal{L}^p$ to the Banach space [[Def - Lp Spaces|Lᵖ]]. The Riesz representation theorem will later identify measures with positive linear functionals.

3. **To topology and groups.** $\mathcal{B}(X)=\sigma(\tau)$ is the functor "forget all of topology but openness, then freely generate a $\sigma$-algebra." [[Thm - Translation Invariance of Lebesgue Measure|Translation invariance]] makes $\lambda$ the *Haar measure* of the locally compact abelian group $(\mathbb{R}^n,+)$ — the canonical invariant measure that, on compact groups, becomes the uniform probability and underlies representation theory and ergodic theory. [[Thm - Existence of a Non-Measurable Set|Vitali's theorem]] is the amenability boundary: $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ admits a finitely-additive invariant mean but no countably-additive one, and in $\mathbb{R}^3$ the non-amenable $SO(3)$ produces the Banach–Tarski paradox.

4. **To the integral.** [[Thm - Properties of Measures|Continuity from below]] for sets is the [[Thm - Monotone Convergence Theorem|monotone convergence theorem]] for indicator functions; the whole of [[Measure Theory II — Integration|Measure Theory II]] is the promotion of measures of sets to integrals of functions, with the convergence theorems as the promotion of the continuity properties.

# Insights

**The unifying frame of the chapter is "define where it's easy, extend by a theorem."** Volume is obvious on boxes and on nothing more complicated. Rather than struggle to define it directly on Borel sets, one defines a [[Def - Pre-Measure|pre-measure]] on the algebra of [[Def - Interval and Elementary Figure|elementary figures]] and lets the [[Thm - Hahn-Carathéodory Extension Theorem|Hahn–Carathéodory machine]] do the rest. This is the same pattern as extending a bilinear form from a basis, a homomorphism from generators, a bounded operator from a dense subspace. The "relations to respect" in the extension are precisely countable additivity, and the machine has three stages: a pre-measure on an algebra, inflated to an outer measure on *all* subsets (total domain, but only subadditive), deflated to a genuine measure on the Carathéodory $\sigma$-algebra (additivity recovered, domain a $\sigma$-algebra). Inflate, then deflate — that is the whole construction of measures.

**The "$\sigma$" — countability — is a deliberately chosen middle.** Finite additivity is too weak: it relates no measure to any limit, so it supports no analysis. Uncountable additivity is too strong: $\mu([0,1])=\sum_{x}\mu(\{x\})\in\{0,\infty\}$ kills every interesting measure. *Countable* additivity is the unique setting in which $\mu$ is continuous along monotone sequences yet measures still exist. Every appearance of "$\sigma$" — $\sigma$-algebra, $\sigma$-additivity, $\sigma$-finite, $\sigma$-subadditive — is the same choice being made consistently, and the entire superiority of Lebesgue's theory over Riemann's is the dividend.

**Three notions of "small" — null, countable, nowhere-dense — are mutually independent, and conflating them is the most common error.** The [[Ex - The Cantor set has Lebesgue measure zero|Cantor set]] is uncountable yet null; a [[Ex - A fat Cantor set of positive measure|fat Cantor set]] is nowhere-dense yet of positive measure; $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, null, and dense. Measure-zero is the measure-theoretic notion of negligible, and it has its own logic: it is closed under countable unions, hereditary under subsets, and — crucially — *invisible*, since [[Ex - Null sets are Carathéodory measurable|null sets pass every Carathéodory test]] and are absorbed by every complete $\sigma$-algebra. This invisibility is what makes "[[Def - Null Set and Completion|almost everywhere]]" reasoning robust and is the foundation on which $L^p$ spaces, a.e. convergence, and the convergence theorems are built.

**Symmetry plus countable additivity is a rigid constraint — rigid enough to forbid measuring everything.** [[Thm - Translation Invariance of Lebesgue Measure|Translation invariance]] is so demanding that, combined with countable additivity, it cannot be satisfied on all of $2^{\mathbb{R}}$: the [[Thm - Existence of a Non-Measurable Set|Vitali argument]] manufactures countably many disjoint translates of one set, whose total measure must be a sum of a single constant (hence $0$ or $\infty$) yet is geometrically trapped in $[1,3]$. The lesson is structural and propagates everywhere: one cannot measure every set, which is *why* $\sigma$-algebras exist; one cannot put a uniform distribution on a countable group, which is *why* probability insists on a $\sigma$-algebra of events. The negative theorem is not a pathology to be quarantined — it is the justification for the architecture of the entire subject.
