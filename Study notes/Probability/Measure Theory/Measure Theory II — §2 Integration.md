# Notation Registry

- $(X,\mathcal{A},\mu)$ — a measure space; $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ — the Borel $\sigma$-algebra
- $f,g,f_n$ — measurable functions $X\to[-\infty,\infty]$; $f^+=\max(f,0)$, $f^-=\max(-f,0)$, $|f|=f^++f^-$
- $\mathbf{1}_A$ — indicator of $A$; $\mathcal{S}^+$ — non-negative measurable simple functions
- $\sigma(f)=\{f^{-1}(B):B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\}$ — the $\sigma$-algebra generated by $f$
- $\int f\,d\mu$, $\mu(f)$ — the integral; $\int_A f\,d\mu=\int f\mathbf{1}_A\,d\mu$; $\mathbb{E}[f]$ — expectation when $\mu=\mathbb{P}$
- $\mu$-a.e. — a property failing only on a [[Def - Null Set and Completion|null set]]; $f\sim g\iff f=g$ a.e.
- $L^1(\mu)$ — integrable functions; $L^p(\mu)$, $\|f\|_p=(\int|f|^p\,d\mu)^{1/p}$, $\|f\|_\infty=\operatorname{ess\,sup}|f|$
- $f_n\uparrow f$ — increasing pointwise to $f$; $f_n\xrightarrow{\mu}f$ — [[Def - Convergence in Measure|convergence in measure]]
- $p,q$ conjugate: $\tfrac1p+\tfrac1q=1$
- $\nu\ll\mu$ — [[Def - Absolute Continuity and Density|absolute continuity]]; $\nu=f\mu$ — $\nu$ has density $f$

# Motivation

[[Measure Theory I — §1 Measure Spaces|Measure Theory I]] assigned sizes to *sets*. This chapter promotes that to an integral of *functions* — and shows the integral does what Riemann's could not: commute with limits.

The construction mirrors the construction of the measure itself: *define on an easy class, extend by approximation*. The easy class is the [[Def - Simple Function|simple functions]] — finitely many values — where the integral writes itself as $\sum\alpha_i\mu(A_i)$. Every non-negative measurable function is an increasing limit of simple functions ([[Thm - Approximation by Simple Functions]]), so its integral is defined as the supremum of the simple ones below it; signed functions split as $f^+-f^-$, integrable when $\int|f|<\infty$. Before any of this, one needs the right notion of [[Def - Measurable Function|measurable function]] — "preimages of Borel sets are measurable," the exact analogue of continuity — and the reassurance ([[Thm - Operations Preserve Measurability]]) that the class is closed under all algebraic and *limiting* operations, the pointwise-limit closure being precisely what continuity lacks.

The heart of the chapter is the **convergence theorems**. [[Thm - Monotone Convergence Theorem|MCT]] (monotone limits), [[Thm - Fatou's Lemma|Fatou]] (the universal one-sided inequality), and [[Thm - Dominated Convergence Theorem|DCT]] (general limits, under domination) together answer the question Riemann could not: *when does $\lim\int f_n=\int\lim f_n$?* The obstruction in every failure is the same — *mass escaping*, to spatial infinity, into a spike, by spreading — and each theorem is a condition forbidding escape. The [[Thm - Vitali Convergence Theorem|Vitali theorem]] then identifies the *exact* condition for $L^1$-convergence: convergence in measure plus [[Def - Absolute Continuity and Density|uniform integrability]]. Finally the [[Def - Lp Spaces|Lᵖ spaces]] package integrable functions as complete normed vector spaces — Banach spaces, $L^2$ a Hilbert space — the arena for functional analysis and probability. Two side theorems, [[Thm - Egorov's Theorem|Egorov]] and [[Thm - Lusin's Theorem|Lusin]], record that measurability is only a small perturbation of continuity: a.e. convergence is uniform off a small set, a measurable function is continuous off a small set.

# Concept Map

## §2.1 Measurable Functions

- **[[Def - Measurable Function]]**
	- $f:X\to Y$ is measurable if $f^{-1}(S)\in\mathcal{A}$ for every measurable $S$ — the exact analogue of continuity, with "measurable" replacing "open." It suffices to check preimages of a *generating* family; for real $f$, just $\{f<a\}\in\mathcal{A}$. Every continuous function is Borel measurable; in probability a measurable function is a [[Def - Random Variable|random variable]] and $\sigma(f)$ is the information it carries.
- **[[Def - Simple Function]]**
	- A measurable function taking finitely many values, $s=\sum\alpha_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i}$ — the building block on which the integral is defined as $\sum\alpha_i\mu(A_i)$. It partitions the *range*, not the domain (the decisive difference from Riemann step functions), which is what lets the integral handle discontinuous functions.
- **[[Thm - Operations Preserve Measurability]]**
	- Measurable functions are closed under $+,\cdot,\wedge,\vee,|\cdot|$ and under $\inf,\sup,\liminf,\limsup$ — hence under pointwise limits. Algebraic closure is "continuous function of a measurable pair"; limiting closure is "$\sigma$-algebras absorb countable operations." The pointwise-limit closure, which continuity lacks, is why measurable functions are the right setting for convergence theorems.
- **[[Thm - Approximation by Simple Functions]]**
	- $f\ge0$ is measurable iff there are simple $s_n\uparrow f$. The construction *dyadically slices the range* and is explicit and monotone, so it interlocks with MCT to define and compute the integral. The basis of the "standard machine": prove for indicators, extend to simple, extend to $f\ge0$, extend to signed.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Standard Machine *(across Measure Theory and Probability)*
> Simple-function approximation is the engine of the **standard machine** — the universal route for proving any identity $P(f)$ about the integral. Establish $P$ for indicators (where it is typically obvious), lift by linearity to simple functions, lift to $f\ge 0$ by MCT and monotone approximation, lift to signed $f$ via $f=f^+-f^-$. Every theorem of integration — linearity, change of variables, Fubini's theorem, properties of conditional expectation, the disintegration theorem — is proved by turning this machine. Recognising a target as "an identity stable under the four steps" reduces an apparent integration problem to the indicator case.

## §2.2 Measurability vs Continuity

- **[[Def - Almost Everywhere]]**
	- A property holds $\mu$-a.e. if it fails only on a null set. Countably many a.e.-statements combine into one (null sets are closed under countable unions), which is what makes a.e.-reasoning coherent. Functions equal a.e. have equal integrals — the integral is blind to null sets.
- **[[Thm - Egorov's Theorem]]**
	- On a finite-measure space, a.e. convergence is *uniform off a set of arbitrarily small measure*. Proof: doubly-indexed bad sets shrink to null by continuity from above ($\mu(X)<\infty$ spent here), removed on a $\delta2^{-i}$ budget. Fails on infinite-measure spaces (escape to infinity).
- **[[Thm - Lusin's Theorem]]**
	- A measurable function on a finite-measure subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is *continuous off a set of arbitrarily small measure*. Two layers: simple functions are continuous off a small set (regularity of level sets); general $f$ by simple approximation plus Egorov. Measurability is continuity up to a small set.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Density of $C_c$ in $L^p$ *(from Functional Analysis)*
> Lusin's theorem is the input that proves **continuous compactly-supported functions $C_c(\mathbb{R}^n)$ are dense in $L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for $1\le p<\infty$**. The proof: approximate any $f\in L^p$ by a bounded function with compact support (DCT-style truncation), then apply Lusin to find a continuous function agreeing off a small-measure set; the $L^p$-difference is controlled by the boundedness and the small measure. This density is the lever that reduces every $L^p$-statement to a statement about smooth functions and is the foundation of the **mollifier technique** in PDE and harmonic analysis.

## §2.3 The Integral

- **[[Def - The Integral]]**
	- Built in three steps: $\int s=\sum\alpha_i\mu(A_i)$ for simple $s\ge0$; $\int f=\sup\{\int s:s\le f\}$ for $f\ge0$; $\int f=\int f^+-\int f^-$ for $f\in L^1$ ($\int|f|<\infty$). Extends the Riemann integral, turns sums into integrals (counting measure), becomes expectation (probability measure).
- **[[Thm - Monotone Convergence Theorem]]**
	- $f_n\uparrow f\Rightarrow\int f_n\uparrow\int f$ — the integral commutes with monotone limits. The deepest property distinguishing Lebesgue from Riemann; it is continuity-of-the-measure-from-below promoted from sets to functions, proved via the catching-up sets $\{f_n\ge(1-\varepsilon)s\}$. Foundation of Fatou, DCT, Fubini, and of "$\int f=\lim\int s_n$."
- **[[Thm - Properties of the Integral]]**
	- Linearity, monotonicity, and the triangle inequality $|\int f|\le\int|f|$. Linearity is the non-trivial one: the integral of $f\ge0$ is a supremum, and sups do not add — linearity is carried up from simple functions (where it is obvious) by MCT.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Expectation of a [[Def - Random Variable|Random Variable]] *(from Probability Theory)*
> The integral against a probability measure $\mathbb{P}$ is exactly the **expectation** $\mathbb{E}[X]=\int X\,d\mathbb{P}$ of a [[Def - Random Variable|random variable]]. The whole §2.3 construction — indicators give probabilities, simple functions give weighted averages of values, monotone limits give the expectation of any non-negative random variable — is the rigorous foundation that turns probability from a calculus on densities and discrete sums into a single uniform theory. Linearity of expectation, which is taken for granted in undergraduate probability, is exactly the linearity statement here, proved by MCT for non-negative variables.

## §2.4 Convergence Theorems

- **[[Thm - Fatou's Lemma]]**
	- $\int\liminf f_n\le\liminf\int f_n$ for $f_n\ge0$ — a one-sided inequality holding with *no* hypothesis beyond non-negativity. It is MCT applied to $g_n=\inf_{k\ge n}f_k\uparrow\liminf f_n$. The universal safety net; strict exactly when mass escapes.
- **[[Thm - Dominated Convergence Theorem]]**
	- $f_n\to f$ a.e. and $|f_n|\le g\in L^1\Rightarrow\int|f_n-f|\to0$. The general limit-interchange theorem, proved by applying Fatou to $2g-|f_n-f|\ge0$. The dominating $g$ pins the mass down, forbidding escape. Differentiation under the integral sign is its main corollary.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Martingale|Martingale]] Convergence Theorems *(from Advanced Probability IV)*
> The MCT/Fatou/DCT triple promotes to the **martingale convergence theorems**: an $L^1$-bounded martingale converges almost surely ([[Thm - Almost Sure Martingale Convergence]]), and a uniformly integrable martingale converges in $L^1$ to its almost-sure limit ([[Thm - Lp and L1 Martingale Convergence]]). The proof of the $L^1$ version is literally the Vitali theorem of §2.5 applied to the martingale; the proof of the a.s. version uses Doob's upcrossing inequality together with Fatou. Without §2.4 there is no theory of stochastic processes converging at all.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Smoothness of the Fourier Transform *(from Harmonic Analysis)*
> DCT is what licenses **differentiation under the integral sign** $\partial_t \int f(x,t)\,d\mu(x) = \int \partial_t f(x,t)\,d\mu(x)$, provided the partial derivative is dominated by an integrable function uniformly in $t$. Applied to the **Fourier transform** $\hat f(\xi)=\int e^{-2\pi i x\cdot\xi}f(x)\,dx$, this gives $\partial_\xi^\alpha \hat f(\xi)= ((-2\pi i x)^\alpha f)\,\hat{}\,(\xi)$ whenever $x^\alpha f\in L^1$ — the precise *decay-controls-smoothness* duality between a function and its transform. The same DCT-under-the-integral move is the routine technique in PDE, generating functions, and analytic continuation of integral transforms.

## §2.5 Vitali's Theorem

- **[[Def - Convergence in Measure]]**
	- $\mu(\{|f_n-f|>\varepsilon\})\to0$ for all $\varepsilon$ — the bad *set* shrinks, though no point need converge. Weaker than a.e. convergence; on a probability space it is *convergence in probability*. Implied by a.e. and by $L^1$ convergence; yields an a.e.-convergent subsequence.
- **[[Def - Absolute Continuity and Density]]**
	- $\nu\ll\mu$: $\mu(A)=0\Rightarrow\nu(A)=0$ — the necessary condition for $\nu$ to have a [[Thm - Radon-Nikodym Theorem|density]]. For finite $\nu$ it is an $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ statement; applied to a *family* of functions it is **uniform integrability** — uniformly absolutely continuous integrals.
- **[[Thm - Vitali Convergence Theorem]]**
	- On a finite-measure space, $f_n\to f$ in $L^1$ **iff** $f_n\xrightarrow{\mu}f$ and $(f_n)$ is uniformly integrable. The *exact* characterisation of $L^1$-convergence: convergence in measure (closeness) plus uniform integrability (no escape). DCT is the special case "dominated $\Rightarrow$ UI."

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Uniform Integrability|Uniform Integrability]] and Martingale Closure *(from Advanced Probability IV)*
> Uniform integrability is the exact condition for an $L^1$-bounded martingale to **close at infinity** — to have an $L^1$-limit $X_\infty$ such that $X_n = \mathbb{E}[X_\infty\mid\mathcal{F}_n]$, recovering the martingale as conditional expectations of a single random variable. The criterion is decisive in option pricing and in statistical-inference proofs: a martingale that is merely $L^1$-bounded need not close, but a uniformly integrable one does. The Vitali theorem is the analytic core of this closure statement.

## §2.6 Lp Spaces

- **[[Def - Lp Spaces]]**
	- $L^p(\mu)$ — measurable functions with $\|f\|_p<\infty$, quotiented by a.e.-equality so $\|\cdot\|_p$ is a genuine norm. Complete (Banach); $L^2$ a Hilbert space; against counting measure, the sequence spaces $\ell^p$. The arena of functional analysis and of $L^p$-convergence of random variables.
- **[[Thm - Hölder and Minkowski Inequalities]]**
	- Hölder $\int|fg|\le\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$ (the $L^p$–$L^q$ pairing, Cauchy–Schwarz at $p=q=2$); Minkowski $\|f+g\|_p\le\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p$ (the triangle inequality, making $\|\cdot\|_p$ a norm). Both are convexity statements — Young's inequality, $t\mapsto t^p$ — run through the integral.
- **[[Thm - Completeness of Lp Spaces]]**
	- $L^p(\mu)$ is complete: every Cauchy sequence converges (Riesz–Fischer). Proved by extracting a rapidly-Cauchy subsequence, dominating it by the sum of its gaps, and invoking DCT. Completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ transmitted through the integral; what makes $L^p$ a Banach space.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Banach Spaces and [[Def - Dual Space|Dual Spaces]] *(from Functional Analysis)*
> The completeness of $L^p$ makes it a **Banach space** — a complete normed vector space — and $L^p$ for $1<p<\infty$ is the prototypical example. The Hölder inequality identifies the **dual space** $(L^p)^* \cong L^q$ where $\tfrac{1}{p}+\tfrac{1}{q}=1$: every continuous linear functional on $L^p$ is integration against an $L^q$ function. This $L^p$–$L^q$ duality is the structural input to the Hahn–Banach theorem in concrete form, to the weak-$*$ topology on $L^\infty$, and to every convex-duality argument in optimal transport, calculus of variations, and convex analysis.
>
> The case $p=2$ is more: $L^2$ is a **Hilbert space** under the inner product $\langle f,g\rangle = \int f\bar g\,d\mu$, with $(L^2)^*\cong L^2$ self-dually via Riesz representation. The orthogonal-projection structure of a Hilbert space *is* conditional expectation in [[Advanced Probability III — Conditional Expectation|Advanced Probability III]] — and Plancherel's theorem for the Fourier transform, the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators, and the entire theory of orthogonal polynomials live in $L^2$. $L^2$ is the central object of analysis, and §2.6 is where it is constructed.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Interpolation Theory *(from Harmonic Analysis)*
> Hölder's inequality, applied with care, gives the **log-convexity of $L^p$-norms**: $\|f\|_p$ is a log-convex function of $1/p$, so a function in two $L^p$ spaces lies in every intermediate $L^p$. This baby case is the seed of two profound theorems: the **Riesz–Thorin interpolation theorem** (a linear operator bounded on $L^{p_0}$ and on $L^{p_1}$ is automatically bounded on every $L^p$ in between, with norm controlled by log-convexity) and the **Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem** (the same conclusion from weaker hypotheses — boundedness on $L^p$-weak). These two theorems are the engine of harmonic analysis: every singular-integral or maximal-function bound is obtained by interpolating between a trivial $L^2$-estimate and a delicate weak $L^1$-estimate.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — What do we usually try to prove?** (1) *Measurability* of a function or of a set defined by a limiting condition — handled by closure axioms, the generating-set criterion, or [[Thm - Operations Preserve Measurability]]. (2) *Values and bounds of integrals* — computed via the standard machine (indicators → simple → MCT → signed), bounded via monotonicity, [[Ex - Markov's inequality|Markov]], or the convexity inequalities. (3) *Interchange of limit and integral* — the central question, routed to MCT (monotone), Fatou (one-sided, free), DCT (dominated), or Vitali (uniformly integrable). (4) *Convergence in a chosen mode* and the implications between modes. (5) *Functional-analytic structure* — that $L^p$ is a normed, complete, or Hilbert space.

**Sources — What assumptions do we leverage?** A *measurable function* hypothesis unlocks the standard machine. *Non-negativity* routes to MCT and Fatou with no further hypothesis. *A monotone sequence* routes to MCT; *a dominating $g\in L^1$* to DCT; *uniform integrability + convergence in measure* to Vitali; *finite measure* makes Egorov, Lusin, and bounded convergence available (and is automatic in probability). *$L^p$-membership* routes to Hölder/Minkowski and the completeness theorem. The recurring skill is recognising, in a concrete problem, which of these source-structures is present — most often, *building the dominating function* or *verifying uniform integrability*.

# Legal Operations

1. **The standard machine.** Prove an integral identity for indicators, extend to simple functions by [[Thm - Properties of the Integral|linearity]], to $f\ge0$ by [[Thm - Approximation by Simple Functions|simple approximation]] + [[Thm - Monotone Convergence Theorem|MCT]], to signed $f$ by $f=f^+-f^-$. The universal route for *every* theorem about integrals.

2. **Check measurability on generators.** To show $f$ measurable, verify $f^{-1}(S)\in\mathcal{A}$ only for $S$ in a generating family; the "good targets" form a $\sigma$-algebra. To show a set is measurable, build it from measurable sets by countably many operations.

3. **Slice the range.** Approximate $f\ge0$ from below by dyadic-level [[Def - Simple Function|simple functions]] $s_n\uparrow f$. The launchpad for defining and computing every integral.

4. **Pass a limit through the integral.** Identify the structure: monotone → MCT; non-negative, want only "$\le$" → Fatou; dominated → DCT; uniformly integrable + in measure → Vitali. Always name the theorem and verify *its* hypothesis.

5. **Build the dominating function.** When DCT is wanted, exhibit $g\in L^1$ with $|f_n|\le g$: a uniform bound on finite measure; the gap-sum $\sum|f_{n_{k+1}}-f_{n_k}|$ for a fast subsequence; an $L^p$-bound for $p>1$.

6. **Markov's inequality.** Convert a bound on $\int|f|^p$ into a bound on $\mu(\{|f|>\lambda\})$ — the valve from integral norms to measure/pointwise statements. Apply it to a transformed variable ($f^2$, $e^{tf}$) for Chebyshev/Chernoff bounds.

7. **Extract a fast subsequence.** From convergence in measure or $L^p$, extract a subsequence converging rapidly enough that the [[Ex - The first Borel-Cantelli lemma|first Borel–Cantelli lemma]] forces a.e. convergence. The bridge from norm/measure to pointwise; the engine of [[Thm - Completeness of Lp Spaces|completeness]].

8. **Split at a good set.** To upgrade convergence, partition $X$ into a good set (uniform convergence, via [[Thm - Egorov's Theorem|Egorov]]) and a small bad set (controlled by a uniform bound or uniform integrability). The architecture of bounded convergence and the [[Thm - Vitali Convergence Theorem|Vitali theorem]].

9. **Apply the convexity inequalities.** [[Thm - Hölder and Minkowski Inequalities|Hölder]] to bound a product or pairing, extract a power of $\mu(A)$, or compare moments; Minkowski for the triangle inequality.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Interchanging limit and integral without a hypothesis
> "$\lim\int f_n=\int\lim f_n$" is false in general — the three escaping bumps ($\mathbf{1}_{[n,n+1]}$, $n\mathbf{1}_{[0,1/n]}$, $\tfrac1n\mathbf{1}_{[0,n]}$) all have $\int f_n=1$, $\lim f_n=0$. One must invoke MCT (monotone), DCT (dominated), or Vitali (uniformly integrable) and check its hypothesis. See [[Ex - Strict inequality in Fatou's lemma]].

> [!warning] 2. Believing convergence in measure (or $L^p$) gives a.e. convergence
> It gives a.e. convergence of a *subsequence* only — the [[Ex - The typewriter sequence|typewriter sequence]] converges in measure and in every $L^p$ to $0$ yet converges a.e. nowhere. Pass to a subsequence before using pointwise arguments.

> [!warning] 3. Treating "dominated" as necessary for $L^1$-convergence
> Domination is sufficient but strictly stronger than needed. The [[Thm - Vitali Convergence Theorem|Vitali theorem]] shows the exact condition is uniform integrability; [[Ex - L1 convergence without a dominating function|wandering tall bumps]] converge in $L^1$ with no integrable dominator.

> [!warning] 4. Assuming $L^p\subseteq L^{p'}$ or the reverse, unconditionally
> The inclusion holds one way on finite-measure spaces ($L^{p'}\subseteq L^p$), the other way on counting measure ($\ell^p\subseteq\ell^{p'}$), and *neither* way on $\mathbb{R}$. Check which space you are on — see [[Ex - Lp inclusions and a power-law criterion]].

# Problem-Solving Strategy

Almost every problem in this chapter is one of two kinds: *show something is measurable*, or *evaluate / bound / pass a limit through an integral*.

For **measurability**, never describe the function explicitly. If it is built from measurable functions by algebraic operations or countable limits, [[Thm - Operations Preserve Measurability]] settles it. If a *set* defined by a limiting condition is in question, translate the condition into an equation among the measurable functions $\sup,\inf,\liminf,\limsup$ — "$\lim$ exists" becomes "$\liminf=\limsup$, finite." If the target $\sigma$-algebra is generated, check preimages of generators only.

For **integrals**, the master question is whether a limit must pass through. First identify the structure of the sequence. *Monotone and non-negative?* [[Thm - Monotone Convergence Theorem|MCT]] — unconditional, and the way every concrete integral of a non-negative function is computed (truncate to simple, integrate, take the limit). *Only a lower bound wanted, or no limit even exists?* [[Thm - Fatou's Lemma|Fatou]] — free, one-sided. *A general limit, and you can produce an integrable dominator?* [[Thm - Dominated Convergence Theorem|DCT]] — and the real work is *building* the dominator (a uniform bound on finite measure; the gap-sum of a fast subsequence; an $L^p$-bound). *No dominator, but a finite-measure space?* Check [[Def - Absolute Continuity and Density|uniform integrability]] and convergence in measure, then [[Thm - Vitali Convergence Theorem|Vitali]]. The diagnostic that decides between these is always the same: *could mass escape* — to infinity, into a spike, by spreading? If the answer is no, some convergence theorem applies; the escaping-bump examples are the mental test.

To **bound** rather than evaluate an integral, reach for monotonicity, [[Ex - Markov's inequality|Markov's inequality]] (average controls tail — and apply it to a transformed variable for Chebyshev or Chernoff bounds), or the [[Thm - Hölder and Minkowski Inequalities|convexity inequalities]]. To move between *modes of convergence* — from $L^p$ or in-measure down to a.e., or from a.e. up to $L^1$ — the two devices are *extract a fast subsequence* (downward, via Borel–Cantelli) and *split at a good set* (upward, via Egorov plus a uniform-smallness control). These two manoeuvres, together with "build the dominator," are the entire toolkit for convergence problems.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Monotone Convergence Theorem|Monotone convergence theorem]]**: $f_n\uparrow f\Rightarrow\int f_n\uparrow\int f$. The most-used theorem of the chapter — it *defines* the integral via $\int f=\lim\int s_n$, proves linearity, proves $\sigma$-additivity of density measures, and gives term-by-term integration of non-negative series for free. Reach for it whenever a non-negative monotone limit meets an integral. Typical use: compute $\int f$ by truncating $f$ to simple functions.

- **[[Thm - Dominated Convergence Theorem|Dominated convergence theorem]]**: limit and integral commute under an integrable dominator. The workhorse of *applications* — differentiation under the integral sign, continuity of integral transforms, smoothness of [[Def - Characteristic Function|characteristic functions]]. Typical use: a general (signed, non-monotone) limit, where one can cap the whole sequence by a fixed $g\in L^1$.

- **[[Ex - Markov's inequality|Markov's inequality]]**: $\mu(|f|\ge\lambda)\le\lambda^{-1}\int|f|$. The universal tail bound — average controls extreme. Typical use: pass from an integral-norm statement to a measure or pointwise statement; specialised (to $f^2$, $e^{tf}$) it becomes Chebyshev and the Chernoff bound, and it is the route from $L^p$-convergence to convergence in measure and to a.e. subsequences.

- **[[Thm - Hölder and Minkowski Inequalities|Hölder's inequality]]**: $\int|fg|\le\|f\|_p\|g\|_q$. The fundamental $L^p$–$L^q$ pairing. Typical use: bound a product or an integral against a test function; extract a power of $\mu(A)$ (giving the $L^p$ inclusions and uniform integrability for $p>1$); compare moments; Cauchy–Schwarz ($p=q=2$) bounds covariance and underlies $L^2$ geometry.

- **[[Thm - Approximation by Simple Functions|Simple approximation]]** + the **standard machine**: every measurable $f\ge0$ is $\lim s_n$ for simple $s_n\uparrow f$. Typical use: prove *any* integral identity by checking it for indicators, lifting by linearity and MCT. This is the induction principle of integration theory.

# Bridges

1. **To probability theory.** A [[Def - Random Variable|random variable]] is a measurable function on a [[Def - Probability Space|probability space]]; its integral $\int X\,d\mathbb{P}$ is the **expectation** $\mathbb{E}[X]$. The convergence theorems become the tools for interchanging limit and expectation; [[Def - Convergence in Measure|convergence in measure]] is *convergence in probability*; $L^p$-convergence is a [[Def - Modes of Convergence|mode of convergence]] of random variables; [[Thm - Vitali Convergence Theorem|Vitali]] with [[Def - Absolute Continuity and Density|uniform integrability]] is exactly the criterion for the [[Thm - Almost Sure Martingale Convergence|L¹ martingale convergence theorem]]. [[Ex - Markov's inequality|Markov/Chebyshev]] prove the [[Thm - Weak Law of Large Numbers|weak law of large numbers]]. The [[Ex - The σ-algebra generated by a function|Doob–Dynkin lemma]] — $\sigma(X)$-measurable $=$ a function of $X$ — is the foundation of [[Def - Conditional Expectation|conditional expectation]].

2. **To functional analysis.** [[Def - Lp Spaces|Lᵖ]] are the model Banach spaces; $L^2$ is the model Hilbert space, and [[Ex - The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and L2 geometry|its orthogonal-projection structure]] *is* conditional expectation. [[Thm - Completeness of Lp Spaces|Completeness]] is the property licensing fixed-point and approximation arguments; the density of $C_c$ and of simple functions in $L^p$ is the standard reduction lever. [[Thm - Hölder and Minkowski Inequalities|Hölder]] supplies the $L^p$–$L^q$ duality.

3. **To the construction of measures.** Once the integral exists, a non-negative $f$ produces a new measure $\nu(A)=\int_A f\,d\mu$ — a [[Def - Absolute Continuity and Density|density measure]] — and its $\sigma$-additivity *is* MCT. The converse, that every $\nu\ll\mu$ arises this way, is the [[Thm - Radon-Nikodym Theorem|Radon–Nikodym theorem]] of [[Measure Theory III — Product Measures and Differentiation|Measure Theory III]].

4. **To Fourier analysis and PDE.** $L^2$ is the home of the Fourier transform (Plancherel); [[Thm - Dominated Convergence Theorem|DCT]] proves the smoothness and decay of transforms; completeness of $L^p$ is what makes "the solution is the $L^p$-limit of approximants" a legitimate construction.

# Insights

**The unifying frame is again "define on an easy class, extend by approximation" — the chapter is the construction of [[Measure Theory I — §1 Measure Spaces|Lebesgue measure]] run a second time, one level up.** Measure was built from elementary figures by an extension theorem; the integral is built from [[Def - Simple Function|simple functions]] by [[Thm - Approximation by Simple Functions|simple approximation]] and [[Thm - Monotone Convergence Theorem|MCT]]. The "standard machine" — indicators, then simple functions, then $f\ge0$, then signed $f$ — is this principle made into an algorithm, and *every* theorem of integration theory (linearity, Fubini, change of variables, properties of conditional expectation) is proved by turning that machine. Recognising a goal as "an identity stable under the four steps" tells you the proof immediately.

**The single deepest fact is that the Lebesgue integral commutes with limits — and the reason is the deliberate choice to approximate $f\ge0$ from *below*.** [[Thm - Approximation by Simple Functions|Approximating from below]] is what makes the integral interact correctly with *increasing* limits, which is [[Thm - Monotone Convergence Theorem|MCT]]; MCT then yields [[Thm - Fatou's Lemma|Fatou]] (apply it to $\inf_{k\ge n}f_k$) and [[Thm - Dominated Convergence Theorem|DCT]] (apply Fatou to $2g\pm(f_n-f)$). Riemann's integral, tied to *domain* partitions and two-sided approximation, has no such theorem — and that single deficiency is why the Lebesgue integral, not Riemann's, is the foundation of every modern analytic theory. The dyadic *range*-slicing of [[Thm - Approximation by Simple Functions]] is the technical embodiment of the choice.

**Every failure of a convergence theorem is mass escaping, and there are exactly three escape routes.** Mass can flee to spatial infinity ($\mathbf{1}_{[n,n+1]}$), concentrate into an ever-thinner spike ($n\mathbf{1}_{[0,1/n]}$), or flatten out over an expanding base ($\tfrac1n\mathbf{1}_{[0,n]}$). [[Thm - Fatou's Lemma|Fatou]] is strict in exactly these cases; [[Thm - Dominated Convergence Theorem|DCT]]'s dominating function is precisely a device forbidding all three; [[Def - Absolute Continuity and Density|uniform integrability]] forbids concentration and spreading, while finite measure forbids escape to infinity. The diagnostic "can mass escape here, and how?" predicts, before any computation, whether a limit passes through an integral. This escape picture is the same one that made [[Ex - Continuity from above requires finite measure|continuity from above]] need finite measure and [[Ex - Egorov fails without finite measure|Egorov]] fail on $\mathbb{R}$ — one mechanism, recurring across the whole subject.

**A.e.-equality, convergence in measure, and the four modes of convergence form a hierarchy, and the two tools that move within it are "fast subsequence" (downward, to a.e.) and "split at a good set" (upward, to $L^1$).** A.e. and $L^p$ convergence are both stronger than convergence in measure and incomparable with each other; the [[Ex - The typewriter sequence|typewriter sequence]] separates them. From a norm or measure statement one reaches *pointwise* information by extracting a rapidly convergent subsequence and invoking [[Ex - The first Borel-Cantelli lemma|Borel–Cantelli]] — the same device that proves [[Thm - Completeness of Lp Spaces|completeness of Lᵖ]]. From a.e. or in-measure convergence one reaches $L^1$ by splitting the space, via [[Thm - Egorov's Theorem|Egorov]], into a good set where convergence is uniform and a small bad set controlled by [[Def - Absolute Continuity and Density|uniform integrability]] — the architecture of the [[Thm - Vitali Convergence Theorem|Vitali theorem]]. Knowing which mode a hypothesis gives, which mode a conclusion needs, and which of these two manoeuvres bridges them is the organising skill of the whole convergence theory.
