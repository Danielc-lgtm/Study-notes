# Notation

$(X, \tau)$ is a topological space, with $\tau \subseteq 2^X$ the collection of open sets. $\mathcal{B}(X)$ denotes the Borel $\sigma$-algebra of $X$; for $X = \mathbb{R}^n$ we write $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, and for $\mathbb{R}$ often just $\mathcal{B}$. Recall $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$, the [[Def - Generated σ-Algebra|σ-algebra generated]] by a family $\mathcal{C}$.

---

# Axiom Motivation

We have two structures on $\mathbb{R}^n$ that we would like to make compatible: the *topology*, which knows about nearness, continuity, and limits, and a *$\sigma$-algebra*, which knows about size. A measure should at the very least be able to measure open sets and closed sets — these are the geometrically natural regions, the sets analysis hands us. So we need a $\sigma$-algebra that contains every open set.

There are many such $\sigma$-algebras (the power set is one), so by the now-familiar principle we take the *smallest* one: $\sigma(\tau)$. This is the **Borel $\sigma$-algebra**. It is the canonical bridge between topology and measure theory — the unique minimal way to make every open set measurable. Because complements and countable operations are now available, the Borel sets sweep in all closed sets, all countable intersections of open sets ($G_\delta$ sets), all countable unions of closed sets ($F_\sigma$ sets), and far beyond, up the transfinite Borel hierarchy.

Why minimal rather than, say, the Lebesgue-measurable sets? Minimality buys *functoriality*: a continuous map pulls open sets back to open sets, hence Borel sets back to Borel sets, so every continuous function is automatically Borel measurable. A larger $\sigma$-algebra (like the Lebesgue sets, which is the completion) loses this — the preimage of a Lebesgue set under a continuous map need not be Lebesgue. The Borel $\sigma$-algebra is the measurable structure *intrinsic to the topology*; the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra is a measure-dependent enlargement.

# The Definition

Let $(X, \tau)$ be a topological space. The **Borel $\sigma$-algebra** of $X$ is

$$\mathcal{B}(X) = \sigma(\tau),$$

the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the open sets. Its elements are called **Borel sets**.

For $X = \mathbb{R}^n$ with the Euclidean topology, $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is generated equally well by any of several smaller families: the open balls, the open boxes $\prod_k(a_k,b_k)$, the closed boxes, the half-open boxes $\prod_k(a_k,b_k]$, or — for $n=1$ — the rays $\{(-\infty, a) : a \in \mathbb{R}\}$. Each generates $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ because each open set is a countable union of members of the family, and each family member is Borel.

---

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

$\mathcal{B}(X) = \sigma(\tau)$ is the **forgetful-then-free composite**: forget all of topology except *which sets are open*, then freely generate a $\sigma$-algebra. It is the analogue of taking the underlying group of a Lie group — keeping just enough structure for the next theory to begin. In probability, $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$ is the default $\sigma$-algebra on the value-space of a real [[Def - Random Variable|random variable]]: a random variable *is* a Borel-measurable function, and its law is a measure on $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$. The compatibility $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^m) \otimes \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n) = \mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m+n})$ links Borel structure to [[Def - Product σ-Algebra|product σ-algebras]].

# Examples / Corollaries

Every open set, every closed set, every $G_\delta$ (countable intersection of opens), every $F_\sigma$ (countable union of closeds), every singleton $\{x\} = \bigcap_n (x-\tfrac1n, x+\tfrac1n)$, every countable set, hence $\mathbb{Q}$ and the irrationals — all are Borel. The Cantor set, being closed, is Borel.

Two facts mark the *limits* of the concept. First, **not every subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is Borel**: a cardinality count gives $|\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)| = 2^{\aleph_0} = |\mathbb{R}|$, strictly less than $|2^{\mathbb{R}}| = 2^{2^{\aleph_0}}$. Second, **not every Lebesgue-measurable set is Borel**: the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{B}^*(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is the [[Def - Null Set and Completion|completion]] of $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, and it is strictly larger — every subset of a Borel null set is Lebesgue-measurable, but most are not Borel. So we have the strict chain $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^n) \subsetneq \mathcal{B}^*(\mathbb{R}^n) \subsetneq 2^{\mathbb{R}^n}$.

Calibration: (i) Is $\mathbb{Q}$ Borel? Yes — $\mathbb{Q} = \bigcup_{q \in \mathbb{Q}}\{q\}$, a countable union of singletons. (ii) Is every continuous $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ Borel measurable? Yes — $f^{-1}(\text{open})$ is open hence Borel, and the open sets generate $\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})$. (iii) Is the indicator of a Borel set Borel measurable? Yes — its preimages are among $\emptyset, A, A^c, X$.
