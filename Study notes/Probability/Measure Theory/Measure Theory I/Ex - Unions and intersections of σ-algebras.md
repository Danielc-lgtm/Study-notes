# Problem Statement

Let $X$ be a set and let $(\mathcal{A}_i)_{i\in I}$ be an arbitrary (possibly uncountable) family of $\sigma$-algebras on $X$.

**(a)** Show that the intersection $\bigcap_{i\in I}\mathcal{A}_i$ is a $\sigma$-algebra on $X$.

**(b)** Show by a concrete example on $X=\{1,2,3\}$ that the union $\mathcal{A}_1\cup\mathcal{A}_2$ of two $\sigma$-algebras need *not* be a $\sigma$-algebra.

**(c)** Conclude that for any family $\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^X$ there is a smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal{C}$, and that $\sigma(\mathcal{C}_1\cup\mathcal{C}_2)$ is generally strictly larger than $\sigma(\mathcal{C}_1)\cup\sigma(\mathcal{C}_2)$.

**Recall:**

A [[Def - Algebra and σ-Algebra|σ-algebra]] on $X$ is a family $\mathcal{A}\subseteq 2^X$ with $X\in\mathcal{A}$, closed under complement and under countable unions. The [[Def - Generated σ-Algebra|generated σ-algebra]] $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ is the intersection of all $\sigma$-algebras containing $\mathcal{C}$.

---

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** verifying (and refuting) closure properties — the most basic skill in the subject.

**Assumption pattern:** "$\mathcal{A}_i$ is a $\sigma$-algebra for each $i$" means each axiom holds in *every* $\mathcal{A}_i$ separately. An element of the intersection is an element of *all* of them, so it inherits every axiom — closure under intersection of substructures is automatic for axioms that are *universally quantified over elements of the family*.

**Theorem routing:** part (a) is the engine behind the very definition of $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$; part (b) shows why one cannot define generation by *union*.

**Key decision point:** for (b), recognising that the obstruction is closure under *union/complement across the two families* — pick $A\in\mathcal{A}_1$ and $B\in\mathcal{A}_2$ with $A\cup B$ in neither.

---

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Axiom-checking by membership chase** — to show $S\in\bigcap\mathcal{A}_i$, show $S\in\mathcal{A}_i$ for each $i$.
2. **Counterexample by minimal model** — refute a closure claim on the smallest set where it can fail.

---

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> For (a), an element of $\bigcap_i\mathcal{A}_i$ is *by definition* in every $\mathcal{A}_i$. Each axiom of a $\sigma$-algebra is a statement "if these sets are in $\mathcal{A}$ then so is that set"; apply it inside each $\mathcal{A}_i$ separately.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> For (b), take the two smallest non-trivial $\sigma$-algebras on $\{1,2,3\}$: one generated by $\{1\}$, one by $\{2\}$. Is $\{1\}\cup\{2\}=\{1,2\}$ in their union?

---

# Solution

**(a) The intersection is a $\sigma$-algebra.**

Write $\mathcal{A}=\bigcap_{i\in I}\mathcal{A}_i$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Contains $X$:* each $\mathcal{A}_i$ contains $X$, so $X\in\mathcal{A}_i$ for all $i$, hence $X\in\mathcal{A}$.
> *Complement:* let $A\in\mathcal{A}$. Then $A\in\mathcal{A}_i$ for every $i$; since each $\mathcal{A}_i$ is closed under complement, $A^c\in\mathcal{A}_i$ for every $i$; hence $A^c\in\mathcal{A}$.
> *Countable union:* let $A_1,A_2,\dots\in\mathcal{A}$. Fix $i$. Each $A_k\in\mathcal{A}_i$, and $\mathcal{A}_i$ is closed under countable unions, so $\bigcup_k A_k\in\mathcal{A}_i$. This holds for every $i$, so $\bigcup_k A_k\in\mathcal{A}$. $\square$

**(b) The union need not be a $\sigma$-algebra.**

On $X=\{1,2,3\}$ take $\mathcal{A}_1=\sigma(\{1\})=\{\emptyset,\{1\},\{2,3\},X\}$ and $\mathcal{A}_2=\sigma(\{2\})=\{\emptyset,\{2\},\{1,3\},X\}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Both are $\sigma$-algebras (four-element, of the form $\{\emptyset,A,A^c,X\}$). Their union $\mathcal{A}_1\cup\mathcal{A}_2$ contains $\{1\}$ and $\{2\}$ but **not** $\{1\}\cup\{2\}=\{1,2\}$: the only sets in the union are $\emptyset,\{1\},\{2\},\{1,3\},\{2,3\},X$, and $\{1,2\}$ is absent. So $\mathcal{A}_1\cup\mathcal{A}_2$ fails closure under union — it is not a $\sigma$-algebra. $\square$

**(c) Consequences.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> For any $\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^X$, the family of $\sigma$-algebras containing $\mathcal{C}$ is nonempty ($2^X$ is one), and by (a) its intersection $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ is a $\sigma$-algebra; it contains $\mathcal{C}$ and is contained in every $\sigma$-algebra containing $\mathcal{C}$, so it is the smallest such — the [[Def - Generated σ-Algebra|generated σ-algebra]] is well-defined.
> By (b), $\sigma(\mathcal{C}_1)\cup\sigma(\mathcal{C}_2)$ need not be a $\sigma$-algebra, so it cannot equal $\sigma(\mathcal{C}_1\cup\mathcal{C}_2)$ (which is one); with $\mathcal{C}_1=\{\{1\}\}$, $\mathcal{C}_2=\{\{2\}\}$ the left side omits $\{1,2\}$ while the right contains it. $\square$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> (a) For each axiom: $X$ lies in every $\mathcal{A}_i$ hence in the intersection; if $A$ lies in every $\mathcal{A}_i$ so does $A^c$ (each closed under complement); if each $A_k$ lies in every $\mathcal{A}_i$ then $\bigcup_k A_k$ lies in every $\mathcal{A}_i$ (each closed under countable union). So the intersection satisfies all three axioms. (b) On $\{1,2,3\}$, $\sigma(\{1\})$ and $\sigma(\{2\})$ are four-element $\sigma$-algebras whose union lacks $\{1,2\}$, violating union-closure. (c) The intersection in the definition of $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$ is over a nonempty family of $\sigma$-algebras, hence by (a) is a $\sigma$-algebra — the smallest containing $\mathcal{C}$; and (b) shows $\sigma(\mathcal{C}_1)\cup\sigma(\mathcal{C}_2)$ generally is not even a $\sigma$-algebra. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Intersections of substructures are substructures — always, for any structure defined by universally-quantified closure axioms.** This single fact powers *every* "generated by" construction in mathematics: $\sigma(\mathcal{C})$, the subgroup generated by a set, the linear span, the topological closure, the ideal generated by elements. The proof is always the same membership chase: an element of the intersection lies in each piece, so it inherits each closure axiom from each piece. Whenever you meet a new algebraic or analytic structure, the first free theorem is "intersections of these are these," and the immediate corollary is "smallest one containing a given set exists."

**Unions of substructures are almost never substructures**, because closure axioms relate *several* elements, and two elements drawn from *different* pieces of the union have no common piece to be combined in. This is why "generated by" is defined via intersection-from-above, never union-from-below, and why combining two $\sigma$-algebras (or two pieces of information, in the probabilistic reading) requires *regenerating*: $\sigma(\mathcal{F}_1\cup\mathcal{F}_2)$, not $\mathcal{F}_1\cup\mathcal{F}_2$. The same phenomenon is why the join of two sub-$\sigma$-algebras in a [[Def - Filtration|filtration]] must be generated, and why independence is a statement about *generated* $\sigma$-algebras.
