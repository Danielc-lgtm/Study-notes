# Notation

$\Omega$ — the sample space; $\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^\Omega$ — the $\sigma$-algebra of events; $\mathbb{P}$ — the probability measure. $\omega\in\Omega$ — an outcome. "a.s." — almost surely.

---

# Axiom Motivation

Probability theory needs a rigorous home for the words *outcome*, *event*, and *probability*. Kolmogorov's insight (1933) was that the home already exists: it is a [[Def - Measure and Measure Space|measure space]] of total mass $1$. Nothing new is invented — probability is measure theory with a normalisation and a change of vocabulary. But the three demands — sample space, $\sigma$-algebra of events, normalised probability measure — are not interchangeable, and the right way to see why is to drop each in turn and observe what becomes unstatable.

Consider first the **sample space $\Omega$**. This is the set of conceivable outcomes of the chance experiment — for a die toss, $\Omega = \{1, \dots, 6\}$; for a continuous-time stock price, $\Omega$ is a space of paths $[0, T] \to \mathbb{R}$; for an infinite sequence of coin flips, $\Omega = \{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$. Without $\Omega$ there is *nothing for probabilities to be probabilities of*: events are subsets of $\Omega$, random variables are functions on $\Omega$, expectations are integrals over $\Omega$. One could try to operate purely at the level of distributions on $\mathbb{R}$ — distributions in the sense of probability laws, not Schwartz distributions — and indeed for *finite-dimensional* questions about a single random variable this works (the law $\mu_X$ on $\mathbb{R}$ carries all the information). But the moment one wants to discuss *joint behaviour*, *transformations*, *coupling*, or *stochastic processes*, the underlying $\Omega$ becomes indispensable: two random variables $X, Y$ defined on the same $\Omega$ have a joint law, and only the existence of a common $\Omega$ makes it possible to say "the *same* coin toss" appears in two events. Kolmogorov's [[Thm - Product Measure|product space construction]] and [[Def - Stochastic Process|stochastic process]] theory both rest on building $\Omega$ as a path space; without it the entire apparatus has no carrier. The sample space is the substrate on which all probabilistic objects live.

Next the **$\sigma$-algebra of events $\mathcal{F}$**. Events should combine under the boolean operations one performs on them — complement ("not $A$"), countable union ("at least one of $A_1, A_2, \dots$"), countable intersection ("all of the $A_n$") — and the axiom that does the work is closure under countable union together with closure under complement, i.e. the [[Def - Algebra and σ-Algebra|$\sigma$-algebra]] axioms. Drop $\sigma$-algebra structure and a cascade of failures follows. Without closure under complement, "$A$ does not occur" is not an event, and one cannot even state $\mathbb{P}(A^c) = 1 - \mathbb{P}(A)$. Without closure under *countable* union (with mere finite-union closure) the events "$X_n$ converges", "$A_n$ infinitely often", "the random walk hits $0$ before $N$ for some $N$" are not events — they are countable boolean combinations of simpler events, and only countable-union closure makes them measurable. The [[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|Borel–Cantelli lemmas]] become unstatable: $\{A_n \text{ i.o.}\} = \bigcap_N \bigcup_{n \ge N} A_n$ requires both countable union *and* countable intersection. The notion of a [[Def - Measurable Function|measurable function]] requires $\sigma$-algebra structure on the codomain: $\{X \le t\} \in \mathcal{F}$ is the definition, and the pullback $X^{-1}(B)$ of a Borel set $B$ must lie in $\mathcal{F}$ for the law $\mu_X$ to even be defined as a measure on $\mathbb{R}$. Without $\sigma$-algebra structure, there is no integral $\int X \, d\mathbb{P}$, no expectation, no [[Def - Conditional Expectation|conditional expectation]] (which is defined by a $\mathcal{G}$-measurability-plus-averaging characterisation), no [[Def - Filtration|filtration]], no martingale, no asymptotic theorem.

Why can the $\sigma$-algebra not simply be taken to be the **full power set** $2^\Omega$? This is the question Kolmogorov's foundations were forced to answer carefully, and the answer is one of the most striking facts in foundational mathematics: on uncountable spaces, the power set is *too big* to support translation-invariant probability measures. The [[Thm - Existence of a Non-Measurable Set|Vitali construction]] picks one representative from each coset of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$ — using the axiom of choice — and the resulting set $V \subseteq [0,1)$ has the property that its countable translates by rationals in $[-1,1]$ are pairwise disjoint and cover $[0,1)$ up to a set in $[-1, 2]$. If $V$ were measurable, translation invariance of Lebesgue measure would force $\mathbb{P}(V) = \mathbb{P}(V + q)$ for each rational $q$, and countable additivity would force $\mathbb{P}([0,1)) = \sum_q \mathbb{P}(V + q)$, which is either zero or infinity, never one. The Vitali set therefore *cannot* be measurable — and the only way to honour this is to take $\mathcal{F}$ strictly smaller than $2^\Omega$. The Borel $\sigma$-algebra (and its completion to the Lebesgue $\sigma$-algebra) is the maximal $\sigma$-algebra on $\mathbb{R}$ on which Lebesgue measure exists, and the Vitali set lies *outside* it. For discrete or countable $\Omega$ one can safely take $\mathcal{F} = 2^\Omega$ — the pathology disappears — but for uncountable continuous models, $\mathcal{F}$ must be the Borel $\sigma$-algebra or a sub-$\sigma$-algebra of it. This is not a technicality; it is the precise reason Kolmogorov's axioms separate the *sets* (in $\Omega$) from the *events* (in $\mathcal{F}$): not every set is an event.

The third demand is the **probability measure** $\mathbb{P}$ with $\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1$. The bare requirement that $\mathbb{P}$ be a [[Def - Measure and Measure Space|measure]] — non-negative, $\sigma$-additive, $\mathbb{P}(\emptyset) = 0$ — is inherited verbatim from measure theory, and the motivation for each is the same as for general measures (see [[Def - Measure and Measure Space]]). What is genuinely new is the **normalisation** $\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1$. Drop normalisation and one is left with an unrestricted measure — still useful, indeed measure theory itself — but the interpretation as *probability* is gone: $\mathbb{P}(A)$ no longer represents a fraction of total possibility but a relative quantity, and conditional probability $\mathbb{P}(A | B) = \mathbb{P}(A \cap B) / \mathbb{P}(B)$ becomes a ratio rather than a probability. Without finiteness, all the *finite-measure conveniences* break: [[Thm - Egorov's Theorem|Egorov's theorem]] (a.s. convergence is uniform off a small set) requires finite measure; [[Thm - Lusin's Theorem|Lusin's theorem]] (measurable functions are continuous off a small set) requires finite measure; the [[Def - Lp Spaces|$L^p$ inclusions]] $L^q \subseteq L^p$ for $q \ge p$ require finite measure (they fail on $\mathbb{R}$ under Lebesgue, where $L^2 \not\subseteq L^1$); bounded convergence is the special case of [[Thm - Dominated Convergence Theorem|DCT]] available *only* on finite-measure spaces. And the **interpretation** is non-negotiable: probability is a fraction of total mass, $\mathbb{P}(A) \in [0,1]$ representing "the chance $A$ occurs", and the normalisation is what makes this reading legitimate. Probability theory as a *measure theory of fractions* is exactly Kolmogorov's reduction; the extra ingredient relative to general measure theory is the one number $\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1$, plus the questions it makes natural — [[Def - Independence|independence]], [[Def - Conditional Expectation|conditioning]], asymptotic limits.

What distinguishes probability from bare measure theory is therefore not the axioms but the *questions*: independence (the master hypothesis, with no measure-theoretic precursor), conditioning (the reading of a sub-$\sigma$-algebra as partial information), and limit laws (the [[Thm - Law of Large Numbers|LLN]], [[Thm - Central Limit Theorem|CLT]], and the [[Thm - Kolmogorov 0-1 Law|0–1 laws]], which extract deterministic structure from randomness in the limit). The sub-$\sigma$-algebra picture — $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ as "less information" — is the foundational shift that turns measure theory into probability and unlocks [[Def - Filtration|filtrations]], [[Def - Conditional Expectation|conditional expectation]], and the entire dynamic theory of [[Def - Martingale|martingales]]. Forward-reference: the next big definition built on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ is the [[Def - Random Variable|random variable]] as a measurable function $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$, with its [[Def - Distribution Function|law]] $\mu_X$ a [[Def - Measure and Measure Space|measure]] on $\mathbb{R}$; the chain extends through $\sigma(X)$ (the information $X$ carries), filtrations $\mathcal{F}_n$ (information accumulating in time), conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ (best $\mathcal{G}$-measurable prediction of $X$), and martingales — each a tower built on the foundation of $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$, each requiring exactly the three axioms above.

# The Definition

A **probability space** is a triple $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ where:

- $\Omega$ is a set, the **sample space** — the set of possible outcomes;
- $\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^\Omega$ is a [[Def - Algebra and σ-Algebra|σ-algebra]], whose elements are called **events**;
- $\mathbb{P}:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]$ is a [[Def - Measure and Measure Space|measure]] with $\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1$ — a **probability measure**.

Thus a probability space is exactly a measure space of total mass $1$. An event $A$ holds **almost surely** (a.s.) if $\mathbb{P}(A)=1$ — i.e. its complement is [[Def - Null Set and Completion|null]]. From the [[Thm - Properties of Measures|measure axioms]]: $\mathbb{P}(A^c)=1-\mathbb{P}(A)$, monotonicity, [[Thm - Properties of Measures|continuity]] along monotone sequences of events, and inclusion–exclusion.

# Categorical Definition

A probability space is an **object of the category $\mathbf{Meas}$ equipped with a chosen probability measure** — equivalently, an object of the category $\mathbf{Prob}$ whose definition is built from $\mathbf{Meas}$ by adding the measure as part of the data.

The vocabulary. A *category* $\mathcal{C}$ consists of objects, arrows between objects, an associative composition, and identity arrows. The category $\mathbf{Meas}$ has [[Def - Algebra and σ-Algebra|measurable spaces]] $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ as objects and [[Def - Measurable Function|measurable maps]] $f : (\Omega_1, \mathcal{F}_1) \to (\Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_2)$ — functions satisfying $f^{-1}(\mathcal{F}_2) \subseteq \mathcal{F}_1$ — as arrows. A probability space adds a third piece of data: a [[Def - Measure and Measure Space|measure]] $\mathbb{P}$ on $\mathcal{F}$ with $\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1$. There are two natural categorical formulations of "the same object with a measure attached", depending on what one wants morphisms to preserve.

**The narrow category $\mathbf{Prob}_{\text{meas}}$** takes probability spaces as objects, and arrows are **measure-preserving** measurable maps: $f : (\Omega_1, \mathcal{F}_1, \mathbb{P}_1) \to (\Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_2, \mathbb{P}_2)$ is an arrow iff it is measurable *and* $\mathbb{P}_1(f^{-1}(A)) = \mathbb{P}_2(A)$ for every $A \in \mathcal{F}_2$. This is the right category for ergodic theory and dynamical systems, where the measure is part of the structure and morphisms must respect it. **The wide category $\mathbf{Prob}_{\text{meas-map}}$** takes the same objects but lets arrows be arbitrary measurable maps; the measure on the target is then *induced* (the pushforward), not preserved. This is the category in which random variables live, since a [[Def - Random Variable|random variable]] $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ is exactly a measurable map and its *law* $\mu_X = X_* \mathbb{P}$ is the pushforward — the categorical pushforward of measures along the underlying measurable map.

The category $\mathbf{Meas}$ has products and the product carries a canonical product measure construction. Given probability spaces $(\Omega_1, \mathcal{F}_1, \mathbb{P}_1)$ and $(\Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_2, \mathbb{P}_2)$, the product space $(\Omega_1 \times \Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_1 \otimes \mathcal{F}_2)$ — the categorical product in $\mathbf{Meas}$ — supports a unique product measure $\mathbb{P}_1 \otimes \mathbb{P}_2$ such that $(\mathbb{P}_1 \otimes \mathbb{P}_2)(A_1 \times A_2) = \mathbb{P}_1(A_1)\mathbb{P}_2(A_2)$ for all rectangle events. The probability-theoretic notion of [[Def - Independence|independence]] then becomes a categorical statement: two sub-σ-algebras $\mathcal{G}_1, \mathcal{G}_2 \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ are independent iff the canonical map $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (\Omega/\mathcal{G}_1) \times (\Omega/\mathcal{G}_2)$ pushes $\mathbb{P}$ forward to the product measure of its marginals. [[Def - Conditional Expectation|Conditional expectation]] $\mathbb{E}[\cdot \mid \mathcal{G}]$, viewed categorically, is the right adjoint of the inclusion of $\mathcal{G}$-measurable functions into $\mathcal{F}$-measurable functions, with the *disintegration of measures* (when it exists) supplying the geometric content: it is a pullback-style construction expressing $\mathbb{P}$ as a $\mathcal{G}$-mixture of conditional measures.

There is a deeper categorical statement, the **Giry monad** $\mathcal{G} : \mathbf{Meas} \to \mathbf{Meas}$: it sends a measurable space $(X, \mathcal{F})$ to the space $\mathcal{G}(X)$ of all probability measures on it, with the σ-algebra generated by the evaluation maps $\mu \mapsto \mu(A)$. A probability space is then a *point* of $\mathcal{G}(\Omega)$, and a *Markov kernel* $K : (X, \mathcal{F}_X) \to (Y, \mathcal{F}_Y)$ — a measurable function from $X$ to $\mathcal{G}(Y)$ — is a morphism in the *Kleisli category* of $\mathcal{G}$. This is the categorical framework that absorbs conditional probability, conditional independence, and Markov chains under a single algebraic banner. The Giry monad is the probabilistic analogue of the powerset monad on $\mathbf{Set}$ that gives non-deterministic computation: probability is non-determinism with weights.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A probability space *is* a [[Def - Measure and Measure Space|measure space]] with $\mu(\Omega)=1$ — Kolmogorov's reduction of probability to measure theory. The dictionary: measurable set $\to$ event; [[Def - Measurable Function|measurable function]] $\to$ [[Def - Random Variable|random variable]]; [[Def - The Integral|integral]] $\to$ expectation; [[Def - Almost Everywhere|almost everywhere]] $\to$ almost surely; [[Thm - Properties of Measures|continuity of measures]] $\to$ continuity of probability. What is *genuinely new* in probability — absent from measure theory — is the reading of a *sub-$\sigma$-algebra* as a state of partial information ([[Def - Filtration|filtrations]], [[Def - Conditional Expectation|conditioning]]) and the notion of [[Def - Independence|independence]].

# Examples / Corollaries

**Discrete.** $\Omega$ countable, $\mathcal{F}=2^\Omega$, $\mathbb{P}(\{\omega\})=p_\omega\ge0$ with $\sum p_\omega=1$ — coin tosses, dice, the geometric and Poisson laws.

**The unit interval.** $\Omega=[0,1]$, $\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{B}([0,1])$, $\mathbb{P}=\lambda$ — the uniform distribution. By the [[Def - Distribution Function|quantile transform]] this single space supports *every* law on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Coin-toss space.** $\Omega=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}$ with the product $\sigma$-algebra and the $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ [[Thm - Product Measure|product measure]] — the canonical model of an infinite sequence of fair coin tosses.

Calibration: (i) Is $(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),\lambda)$ a probability space? No — $\lambda(\mathbb{R})=\infty\neq1$. (ii) Must every event have a probability? Only events in $\mathcal{F}$; the [[Thm - Existence of a Non-Measurable Set|Vitali set]] shows one cannot take $\mathcal{F}=2^\Omega$ in general. (iii) Does "a.s." mean "always"? No — it means "off a null set."
