# Notation

$(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ a [[Def - Probability Space|probability space]]; $X,Y,Z$ random variables; $\mu_X$ the law of $X$; $\sigma(X)$ the $\sigma$-algebra generated by $X$.

---

# Axiom Motivation

An *outcome* $\omega\in\Omega$ is rarely the object of interest — what we measure is some *numerical feature* of it: the number of heads, the waiting time, the position. A **random variable** is exactly such a feature: a function $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$. For probabilistic questions about $X$ to make sense, the events "$X\le t$", "$X\in B$" must be genuine events — must lie in $\mathcal{F}$. That is precisely the demand that $X$ be a [[Def - Measurable Function|measurable function]]. So a random variable *is* a measurable function; "random variable" is the probabilist's name for it.

The decisive further idea: a random variable lets us *transport* the probability measure. We rarely care about the abstract space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ — we care about the *distribution of values* of $X$. Pushing $\mathbb{P}$ forward through $X$ gives a probability measure $\mu_X$ on $\mathbb{R}$, the **law** of $X$: $\mu_X(B)=\mathbb{P}(X\in B)$. The law is all that probabilistic questions about $X$ alone depend on — and two random variables on totally different spaces are "the same" probabilistically iff they have the same law. This is why one may always replace an abstract $\Omega$ by $\mathbb{R}$ with the law: the *canonical space*.

The $\sigma$-algebra $\sigma(X)$ generated by $X$ is the third key object — "the information $X$ carries." By the [[Ex - The σ-algebra generated by a function|Doob–Dynkin lemma]], a variable is $\sigma(X)$-measurable iff it is a *function of $X$*. This is the foundation of [[Def - Conditional Expectation|conditioning]].

# The Definition

Let $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ be a probability space. A **random variable** is a [[Def - Measurable Function|measurable function]] $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ (i.e. $\{X\le t\}\in\mathcal{F}$ for all $t$); more generally an $\mathbb{R}^d$-valued random variable, or a measurable map into any measurable space.

The **law** (or **distribution**) of $X$ is the pushforward measure $\mu_X$ on $(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}))$,
$$\mu_X(B)=\mathbb{P}(X\in B)=\mathbb{P}(X^{-1}(B)),\qquad B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}),$$
a [[Def - Probability Space|probability measure]] on $\mathbb{R}$. The **$\sigma$-algebra generated by $X$** is $\sigma(X)=\{X^{-1}(B):B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}$ — the smallest $\sigma$-algebra making $X$ measurable.

Two random variables are **equal in distribution**, $X\stackrel{d}{=}Y$, if $\mu_X=\mu_Y$ — even if defined on different probability spaces.

# Categorical Definition

A random variable is precisely a **morphism in the category $\mathbf{Meas}$** of [[Def - Algebra and σ-Algebra|measurable spaces]] and [[Def - Measurable Function|measurable maps]], with the [[Def - Probability Space|source probability space]] viewed as an object equipped with extra measure-data and the target a generic measurable space.

The vocabulary. A *category* $\mathcal{C}$ consists of objects, arrows between objects, an associative composition, and identity arrows. The category $\mathbf{Meas}$ has measurable spaces $(E, \mathcal{E})$ as objects and measurable maps as arrows; composition is function composition (the composite of two measurable maps is measurable, since $f^{-1}(g^{-1}(\mathcal{E})) = (g \circ f)^{-1}(\mathcal{E})$), and identities are identity functions. A random variable on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ valued in $(E, \mathcal{E})$ is exactly an arrow $X : (\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to (E, \mathcal{E})$ in $\mathbf{Meas}$ — the measure $\mathbb{P}$ is part of the *enrichment* on the source object but plays no role in whether $X$ qualifies as a morphism.

The **law** $\mu_X = X_* \mathbb{P}$ has a clean categorical name: it is the **pushforward** of the measure along the arrow $X$. Formally, $X$ induces a map $X_* : \mathcal{M}_+(\Omega, \mathcal{F}) \to \mathcal{M}_+(E, \mathcal{E})$ between the spaces of (positive) measures on the source and target, defined by $(X_* \mu)(B) = \mu(X^{-1}(B))$ for $B \in \mathcal{E}$. The pushforward is the *covariant* functoriality of measure: composing arrows composes pushforwards, $(g \circ X)_* = g_* \circ X_*$. Through the [[Def - Probability Space#Categorical Definition|Giry monad]] $\mathcal{G}$, the law-construction $X \mapsto \mu_X$ is the unit-or-action of $\mathcal{G}$ on $X$ — it converts the measurable map $X : \Omega \to E$ together with $\mathbb{P} \in \mathcal{G}(\Omega)$ into an element $\mu_X \in \mathcal{G}(E)$, exhibiting "law of a random variable" as the categorical pushforward in the Giry framework.

The σ-algebra $\sigma(X)$ generated by $X$ is the **pullback** of $\mathcal{E}$ along $X$ — the smallest σ-algebra on $\Omega$ that makes $X$ measurable. Symbolically $\sigma(X) = X^{-1}(\mathcal{E})$, the inverse-image σ-algebra. This is the contravariant face of measurability: pullback of σ-algebras goes opposite to arrows of measurable spaces, mirroring the way pullback of open sets goes opposite to continuous maps in topology. The Doob–Dynkin lemma — "every $\sigma(X)$-measurable function factors through $X$" — is precisely the universal property of the pullback σ-algebra: $\sigma(X)$ is the *terminal* σ-algebra among those making $X$ measurable, and a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable function $\Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ factors uniquely through $X$ exactly when $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \sigma(X)$.

Finally, "equal in distribution" $X \stackrel{d}{=} Y$ is the statement that the two pushforwards $X_* \mathbb{P}_1$ and $Y_* \mathbb{P}_2$ coincide as objects of $\mathcal{G}(E)$, even when $(\Omega_1, \mathcal{F}_1, \mathbb{P}_1)$ and $(\Omega_2, \mathcal{F}_2, \mathbb{P}_2)$ are different probability spaces. This is the categorical statement that probability questions about a single random variable depend only on its image under the Giry monad — the law factors through $\mathcal{G}$, with the source space forgotten.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A random variable is a [[Def - Measurable Function|measurable function]]; its law is the **pushforward** (image) measure, the same construction as the pushforward of a measure under any measurable map. The law $\mu_X$ is built from the abstract $\mathbb{P}$ exactly as a [[Def - Lebesgue Measure|Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure]] is built — and conversely every probability measure on $\mathbb{R}$ *is* the law of some random variable (take $\Omega=\mathbb{R}$, $X=\mathrm{id}$). $\sigma(X)$ is the "information" reading of a sub-$\sigma$-algebra; in the [[Ex - The σ-algebra generated by a function|Doob–Dynkin]] picture, $\sigma(X)$-measurable means "a deterministic function of $X$."

# Examples / Corollaries

An **indicator** $\mathbf{1}_A$ ($A\in\mathcal{F}$) is a random variable, the simplest one; its law is $\mathbb{P}(A)\delta_1+\mathbb{P}(A^c)\delta_0$ (a Bernoulli law). A **discrete** random variable takes countably many values, law $\sum_k p_k\delta_{x_k}$. A variable with a [[Def - Absolute Continuity and Density|density]] $f$ has law $\mu_X=f\lambda$ — the continuous distributions.

A continuous function of a random variable, or a sum/product of random variables, is a random variable ([[Thm - Operations Preserve Measurability|operations preserve measurability]]). The law of a sum of *independent* variables is the [[Def - Convolution|convolution]] of the laws.

Calibration: (i) Is $X\equiv c$ a random variable? Yes — constant, law $\delta_c$. (ii) Does $X\stackrel{d}{=}Y$ imply $X=Y$? No — equal *laws*, not equal as functions; they may live on different spaces. (iii) Is $\sigma(X)$ all of $\mathcal{F}$? Only if $X$ is "as informative as $\Omega$"; generically $\sigma(X)\subsetneq\mathcal{F}$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Distribution function and expectation
> The law $\mu_X$ is encoded by the [[Def - Distribution Function|distribution function]] $F_X(t)=\mathbb{P}(X\le t)$; integrating against $\mu_X$ gives the [[Def - Expectation and Moments|expectation]] $\mathbb{E}[X]=\int X\,d\mathbb{P}=\int x\,d\mu_X(x)$.

> [!tip] Conditional expectation *(from [[Advanced Probability III — Conditional Expectation|Advanced Probability III]])*
> $\sigma(X)$ is the information in $X$; conditioning on it produces $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X]$, a function of $X$ by [[Ex - The σ-algebra generated by a function|Doob–Dynkin]].
