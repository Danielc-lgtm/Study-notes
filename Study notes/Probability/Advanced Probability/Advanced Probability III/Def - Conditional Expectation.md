# Notation

$(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ a [[Def - Probability Space|probability space]], $\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}$ a sub-$\sigma$-algebra, $X\in L^1$. $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ — the conditional expectation; $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Y]:=\mathbb{E}[X\mid\sigma(Y)]$.

---

# Axiom Motivation

Elementary conditional expectation, $\mathbb{E}[X \mid B] = \mathbb{E}[X \mathbf{1}_B] / \mathbb{P}(B)$, conditions on a *single* event $B$ of positive probability — it returns a *number*, the average of $X$ given that $B$ occurred. The advanced notion conditions on an entire *sub-$\sigma$-algebra* $\mathcal{G}$ — "all the information in $\mathcal{G}$" — and returns not a number but a **random variable**, $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$. The shift from a single event to an entire $\sigma$-algebra, and from a number to a random variable, is the central conceptual leap. The two-axiom characterisation pins this random variable down uniquely (up to almost-sure equality), and the right way to motivate the axioms is to drop each in turn and see what fails.

Why a random variable in the first place? Because $\mathcal{G}$ may distinguish infinitely many "scenarios," and the conditional average of $X$ depends on which scenario obtains. Think of $\mathcal{G}$ as generated by a partition of $\Omega$ (the discrete case): on each block of the partition, $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the average of $X$ over that block, so the result is a function that is *constant on each block* — i.e., a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable — taking different values on different blocks. It is "$X$ with the randomness *not visible to $\mathcal{G}$* averaged out, the randomness *visible to $\mathcal{G}$* retained." In the continuous case (e.g. $\mathcal{G} = \sigma(Y)$ with $Y$ continuous) the partition into singletons fails — each scenario has probability zero — but the *function* picture survives: $\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]$ is a function of $Y$, $h(Y)$, where $h(y)$ is the "conditional average of $X$ given $Y = y$" interpreted not as a ratio (which is $0/0$) but as the value of the unique $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable satisfying the averaging identity. The two axioms below are precisely the conditions that pin this $h(Y)$ down without ever dividing by zero.

The first axiom is **$\mathcal{G}$-measurability**: $Y = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ must be a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable, i.e. $\{Y \le t\} \in \mathcal{G}$ for every $t$. This is the precise meaning of "uses only $\mathcal{G}$-information": a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable function is, by the [[Ex - The σ-algebra generated by a function|Doob–Dynkin lemma]], a function of any random variable generating $\mathcal{G}$, so $\mathcal{G}$-measurability is exactly the constraint that $Y$ depends only on what $\mathcal{G}$ resolves. Drop this axiom and the original variable $X$ itself satisfies the second axiom trivially (with $Y = X$, the averaging identity $\mathbb{E}[X \mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X \mathbf{1}_A]$ is a tautology). Without $\mathcal{G}$-measurability the "conditional expectation" carries no extra information — it is just $X$ itself, which is not what we want. The role of $\mathcal{G}$-measurability is to *coarsen* $X$ into a function adapted to the information $\mathcal{G}$, throwing away the part of $X$'s randomness that $\mathcal{G}$ cannot see. In the partition picture this is "constant on blocks"; in the projection picture (see below) it is "lying in the [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] $L^2(\Omega, \mathcal{G}, \mathbb{P})$."

The second axiom is the **averaging identity**: for every $A \in \mathcal{G}$, $\int_A Y \, d\mathbb{P} = \int_A X \, d\mathbb{P}$, equivalently $\mathbb{E}[Y \mathbf{1}_A] = \mathbb{E}[X \mathbf{1}_A]$. This says the conditional expectation has the *same average as $X$ over every $\mathcal{G}$-event* — averaging the unseen randomness preserves expectations on the seen scale. Drop this axiom and any $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable satisfies axiom (i): the zero function, the function $42 \cdot \mathbf{1}_\Omega$, any constant, any $\mathcal{G}$-measurable transformation of $X$ — none has any reason to be the *correct* conditional expectation. Without axiom (ii) the conditional expectation is unconstrained beyond its measurability and is therefore useless: there is no way to extract numerical information about $X$ from $Y$. The averaging identity is the constraint that $Y$ correctly *predicts the integral of $X$ on each $\mathcal{G}$-event*, which is exactly the content of "best $\mathcal{G}$-measurable summary of $X$".

The two axioms together pin down a unique $Y$ up to almost-sure equality (the proof: if $Y, Y'$ both satisfy them, then $Y - Y'$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable with $\mathbb{E}[(Y - Y') \mathbf{1}_A] = 0$ for all $A \in \mathcal{G}$, hence — taking $A = \{Y - Y' > 0\}$ and $A = \{Y - Y' < 0\}$, both $\mathcal{G}$-measurable — $Y = Y'$ a.s.). Each axiom alone is insufficient; together they characterise the conditional expectation completely. The decisive subtlety is that the naive recipe — "let $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}](\omega)$ be the average of $X$ on the $\mathcal{G}$-block containing $\omega$" — *fails* when scenarios have probability zero, as they do whenever $\mathcal{G} = \sigma(Y)$ for a continuous $Y$ (each set $\{Y = y\}$ has probability zero, and one cannot divide by zero). The two-axiom characterisation circumvents this: it *defines* $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ by the properties it must satisfy and *proves existence* via [[Thm - Radon-Nikodym Theorem|Radon–Nikodym]] (taking the measure $A \mapsto \int_A X \, d\mathbb{P}$ restricted to $\mathcal{G}$, which is absolutely continuous with respect to $\mathbb{P}\restriction\mathcal{G}$, and forming its Radon–Nikodym derivative). Conditional expectation is *defined by its characterisation*, not by a formula; this is what makes it work uniformly across discrete and continuous conditioning, and what generalises the elementary $\mathbb{E}[X \mid B]$ from one event to a whole $\sigma$-algebra.

Why is this two-axiom version *preferred* to the elementary one ($\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]$ via the conditional density, when one exists)? Because the elementary version is parochial: it requires the existence of a joint density, hence absolute continuity with respect to a product reference measure, and it breaks down for mixed distributions, singular measures, or any conditioning on an event of probability zero. The two-axiom version handles all cases uniformly — discrete partitions, continuous conditioning, mixed types, sub-$\sigma$-algebras with no natural "scenario" interpretation — by characterising the answer rather than computing it. It also generalises naturally to the [[Thm - Radon-Nikodym Theorem|Radon–Nikodym derivative]] framework: $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is literally the Radon–Nikodym derivative $d\nu/d\mu \restriction_{\mathcal{G}}$ of the signed measure $\nu(A) = \int_A X \, d\mathbb{P}$ with respect to $\mathbb{P}$, restricted to $\mathcal{G}$, and this identification makes the entire abstract integration theory available. Forward-reference: this two-axiom definition is *the* central tool of [[Advanced Probability IV — Martingales in Discrete Time|martingale theory]], where the [[Def - Martingale|martingale]] identity $\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = X_n$ literally *is* conditional expectation iterated along a filtration; the tower property, conditional Jensen's inequality, the optional stopping theorem, and the convergence theorems are all calculations done in this language. Without the two-axiom characterisation working uniformly across all conditioning scenarios, the martingale theory would be limited to discrete [[Def - Filtration|filtrations]] and would not extend to continuous-time stochastic processes — where the entire theory of Brownian motion, Itô calculus, and mathematical finance lives.

# The Definition

Let $X\in L^1(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ and $\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}$ a sub-$\sigma$-algebra. The **conditional expectation of $X$ given $\mathcal{G}$** is any random variable $Y$ satisfying

1. **($\mathcal{G}$-measurability)** $Y$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable;
2. **(averaging identity)** $Y\in L^1$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y\mathbf{1}_A]=\mathbb{E}[X\mathbf{1}_A]$ for every $A\in\mathcal{G}$.

Such a $Y$ **exists and is unique up to $\mathbb{P}$-a.s. equality** ([[Thm - Existence and Uniqueness of Conditional Expectation|existence theorem]]); it is denoted $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$. For a random variable $Z$, $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z]:=\mathbb{E}[X\mid\sigma(Z)]$, and by the [[Ex - The σ-algebra generated by a function|Doob–Dynkin lemma]] $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z]=h(Z)$ for a Borel $h$; one writes $\mathbb{E}[X\mid Z=z]=h(z)$.

The **conditional probability** of an event $B$ is $\mathbb{P}(B\mid\mathcal{G}):=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}_B\mid\mathcal{G}]$.

(Condition 2 extends, by the standard machine, to $\mathbb{E}[YZ]=\mathbb{E}[XZ]$ for all bounded $\mathcal{G}$-measurable $Z$.)

# Categorical Definition

For $X\in L^2$, $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is the **orthogonal projection** of $X$ onto the closed subspace $L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})\subseteq L^2(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ — the best $\mathcal{G}$-measurable mean-square approximation of $X$. The averaging identity (2) is exactly the orthogonality condition "$X-Y\perp L^2(\mathcal{G})$." Equivalently, $\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is a positive, identity-preserving, $L^1$-contractive linear operator $L^1(\mathcal{F})\to L^1(\mathcal{G})$ — a *conditional expectation operator* in the operator-algebra sense — and it is the [[Thm - Radon-Nikodym Theorem|Radon–Nikodym derivative]] of the measure $A\mapsto\int_A X\,d\mathbb{P}$ restricted to $\mathcal{G}$.

---

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

Conditional expectation is **orthogonal projection** in the Hilbert space $L^2$ — literally the same construction as projecting a vector onto a subspace — and simultaneously a **[[Thm - Radon-Nikodym Theorem|Radon–Nikodym derivative]]** (of a restricted measure). It generalises the elementary $\mathbb{E}[X\mid B]$ by replacing one event with a whole $\sigma$-algebra, and it generalises the partition-average picture to continuous information. In statistics it is the **regression function** $\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]$ — the best predictor of $Y$ from $X$; in information theory $\mathcal{G}$ is a resolution of knowledge; in [[Advanced Probability IV — Martingales in Discrete Time|martingale theory]] iterating it along a [[Def - Filtration|filtration]] *is* a martingale.

---

# Examples / Corollaries

**Trivial $\sigma$-algebra:** $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\{\emptyset,\Omega\}]=\mathbb{E}[X]$ — no information, the conditional expectation collapses to the constant mean. **Full $\sigma$-algebra:** $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{F}]=X$ — total information, nothing to average. **Discrete conditioning:** if $\mathcal{G}=\sigma(G_1,G_2,\dots)$ for a partition, $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]=\sum_n\mathbb{E}[X\mid G_n]\mathbf{1}_{G_n}$ — constant on each block, equal to the elementary conditional expectation there. **Independence:** if $X$ is independent of $\mathcal{G}$, $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]=\mathbb{E}[X]$ — irrelevant information averages to the unconditional mean.

**Calibration check.** Verify that $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable *random variable*, not a single number — the shift from scalar to function is the central conceptual leap from the elementary definition. Verify that $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}]$ is unique only up to $\mathbb{P}$-a.s. equality (two versions can differ on null sets but no more). And verify that if $X$ is itself $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, then $\mathbb{E}[X\mid\mathcal{G}] = X$ a.s. — $X$ is "already known" to $\mathcal{G}$, so the conditional expectation has nothing to average out.

> [!tip] Martingales *(from [[Advanced Probability IV — Martingales in Discrete Time|Advanced Probability IV]])*
> A [[Def - Martingale|martingale]] is a process $(X_n)$ with $\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_n]=X_n$ — conditional expectation iterated along a [[Def - Filtration|filtration]]. The entire theory of martingales is the calculus of conditional expectation in time.

> [!tip] Conditional density and regression
> When $(X,Y)$ has a joint density, $\mathbb{E}[h(Y)\mid X]$ is computed via the [[Def - Conditional Density|conditional density]]; the map $x\mapsto\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]$ is the regression function of statistics.
