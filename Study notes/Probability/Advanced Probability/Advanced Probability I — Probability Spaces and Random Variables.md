# Notation Registry

- $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ — a [[Def - Probability Space|probability space]]: sample space, events, probability measure
- $\omega\in\Omega$ — an outcome; $A\in\mathcal{F}$ — an event; "a.s." — almost surely ($\mathbb{P}=1$)
- $X,Y,Z$ — [[Def - Random Variable|random variables]] (measurable functions $\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$)
- $\mu_X$ — the law/distribution of $X$ (pushforward of $\mathbb{P}$); $X\stackrel{d}{=}Y$ — equality in law
- $\sigma(X)$ — the $\sigma$-algebra generated by $X$; $F_X(t)=\mathbb{P}(X\le t)$ — the [[Def - Distribution Function|distribution function]]
- $\mathbb{E}[X]=\int X\,d\mathbb{P}$ — [[Def - Expectation and Moments|expectation]]; $\mathrm{Var}(X)$, $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)$, $\mathbb{E}[X^k]$
- $g$ — the quantile function $g(u)=\inf\{t:F(t)\ge u\}$
- $\bigcap,\prod$ — independence product rule; $\limsup_n A_n=\{A_n\text{ i.o.}\}$
- $\mathcal{T}=\bigcap_n\sigma(X_{n+1},\dots)$ — the tail $\sigma$-algebra
- $\mu*\nu$ — convolution of measures

# Motivation

Kolmogorov's foundational insight: probability theory is [[Measure Theory I — §1 Measure Spaces|measure theory]] on a space of total mass $1$. A [[Def - Probability Space|probability space]] $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ is a measure space with $\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1$; an *event* is a measurable set, a [[Def - Random Variable|random variable]] is a measurable function, *expectation* is the integral. Nothing is invented — the entire machinery of [[Measure Theory II — §2 Integration|integration]] transfers, and because $\mathbb{P}$ is *finite*, all finite-measure conveniences ([[Thm - Egorov's Theorem|Egorov]], bounded convergence, $L^q\subseteq L^p$ for $q\ge p$) come free.

So what *is* new? Two things. First, the **law**: a random variable transports $\mathbb{P}$ forward to a measure $\mu_X$ on $\mathbb{R}$, and every probabilistic question about $X$ alone is an integral against $\mu_X$ — encoded equivalently by the [[Def - Distribution Function|distribution function]] $F_X$, with the quantile transform realising every law as a function of one uniform variable. Second, and decisively, **independence**: the product rule $\mathbb{P}(A\cap B)=\mathbb{P}(A)\mathbb{P}(B)$, which has no counterpart in bare measure theory. Independence is the structural assumption that turns measure theory into probability — it is [[Thm - Product Measure|product-measure]] structure, and it powers everything asymptotic.

This chapter assembles the vocabulary (space, variable, law, distribution function, expectation, moments, independence) and the first genuinely probabilistic theorems: [[Thm - Jensen's Inequality|Jensen's inequality]], the [[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|Borel–Cantelli lemmas]], and the [[Thm - Kolmogorov 0-1 Law|Kolmogorov 0–1 law]] — the last asserting that the asymptotic behaviour of an independent sequence is *deterministic*, the seed of the [[Advanced Probability II — Convergence and Limit Theorems|limit theorems]].

# Concept Map

## §1.1 Probability Spaces, Random Variables, Expectation

- **[[Def - Probability Space]]**
	- A measure space $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})$ with $\mathbb{P}(\Omega)=1$ — Kolmogorov's reduction of probability to measure theory. Events form a $\sigma$-algebra; "almost surely" is "almost everywhere." Finiteness of $\mathbb{P}$ makes finite-measure technology automatic. What is new beyond measure theory: independence and the reading of sub-$\sigma$-algebras as information.
- **[[Def - Random Variable]]**
	- A measurable function $X:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$. It pushes $\mathbb{P}$ forward to its *law* $\mu_X$ on $\mathbb{R}$; probabilistic questions about $X$ depend only on $\mu_X$. $\sigma(X)$ is the information $X$ carries; $X\stackrel{d}{=}Y$ means equal laws, even on different spaces.
- **[[Def - Distribution Function]]**
	- $F_X(t)=\mathbb{P}(X\le t)$ — non-decreasing, right-continuous, limits $0,1$. In bijection with laws on $\mathbb{R}$ (the Lebesgue–Stieltjes correspondence). The quantile transform $g(U)$ realises any law as a function of a uniform variable — inverse-transform sampling.
- **[[Def - Expectation and Moments]]**
	- $\mathbb{E}[X]=\int X\,d\mathbb{P}$ — the integral against $\mathbb{P}$. Variance $\mathrm{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}X)^2$ is the squared $L^2$-norm of the centred variable; moments $\mathbb{E}[X^k]$ form a decreasing scale of integrability ($L^q\subseteq L^p$, $q\ge p$). $\mathbb{E}[h(X)]=\int h\,d\mu_X$ — moments depend only on the law.
- **[[Thm - Jensen's Inequality]]**
	- For convex $\varphi$, $\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]\ge\varphi(\mathbb{E}X)$. Proved by the supporting line at the mean. Yields moment monotonicity, $\mathrm{Var}\ge0$, non-negativity of entropy, and (in conditional form) the $L^p$-theory of martingales.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Filtrations and Information Flow *(from Advanced Probability IV)*
> A sub-$\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}$ encodes *partial information*: the $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variables are exactly the random variables that can be determined from the information $\mathcal{G}$ carries. An increasing chain $\mathcal{F}_0\subseteq\mathcal{F}_1\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{F}$ is a **filtration** — the data structure of every discrete-time stochastic process and the prerequisite for [[Def - Martingale|martingales]], [[Def - Stopping Time|stopping times]], and [[Def - Conditional Expectation|conditional expectation]] (see [[Advanced Probability IV — Martingales in Discrete Time]]). The Doob–Dynkin lemma — "$\sigma(X)$-measurable random variables are exactly measurable functions of $X$" — is the technical content that makes "information" a manipulable quantity.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Information Theory and the Non-negativity of KL-divergence *(from Information Theory)*
> Jensen's inequality applied to the convex function $\varphi(t)=-\log t$ proves the non-negativity of the **Kullback–Leibler divergence** $D(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_p[\log(p/q)] \ge 0$, with equality iff $p=q$. This single inequality is the root of information theory: it gives Gibbs's inequality, the data-processing inequality, the non-negativity of entropy, the non-negativity of mutual information, and the asymptotic-equipartition property of the entropy rate. Variational expressions for $D(p\|q)$ (Donsker–Varadhan, Gibbs) are convex-duality statements of the same Jensen fact and are the bridge to large-deviations theory.

## §1.2 Independence, Borel–Cantelli, the 0–1 Law

- **[[Def - Independence]]**
	- The product rule $\mathbb{P}(\bigcap A_i)=\prod\mathbb{P}(A_i)$, demanded for every finite sub-collection (mutual, not pairwise). For random variables: independence of the generated $\sigma$-algebras, equivalently the joint law is the product of marginals. Checkable on generating $\pi$-systems. *The* concept separating probability from measure theory.
- **[[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas]]**
	- If $\sum\mathbb{P}(A_n)<\infty$ then $\mathbb{P}(A_n\text{ i.o.})=0$ (first lemma, no independence); if $\sum\mathbb{P}(A_n)=\infty$ and the $A_n$ are independent then $\mathbb{P}(A_n\text{ i.o.})=1$ (second lemma). A zero–one dichotomy; the standard route to almost-sure statements.
- **[[Thm - Kolmogorov 0-1 Law]]**
	- For independent $(X_n)$, every tail event has probability $0$ or $1$ — asymptotic behaviour is deterministic. Proved by showing a tail event is independent of itself ($\pi$–$\lambda$). Makes the SLLN limit a constant.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Product Probability Spaces and Infinite-Sequence Constructions *(from Stochastic Processes)*
> Independence is *exactly* [[Thm - Product Measure|product-measure]] structure, and the natural habitat of an infinite independent sequence is an infinite product space $(\mathbb{R}^\infty,\mathcal{B}^{\otimes\infty},\bigotimes\mu_n)$. The **Kolmogorov extension theorem** lifts the §3.1 finite-product construction to such infinite products by checking only the finite-dimensional marginals — and this is what guarantees an i.i.d. sequence with prescribed law actually *exists* on some probability space. Every construction of Brownian motion, of a Poisson process, of an i.i.d. random field rests on this extension, and Kolmogorov's 0–1 law is its tail-behaviour companion.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Almost-Sure Convergence and the First Step of the SLLN *(from Advanced Probability II)*
> Borel–Cantelli is the standard route from a *summable* probability statement to an *almost-sure* statement: $\sum_n\mathbb{P}(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)<\infty$ for every $\varepsilon$ forces $X_n\to X$ almost surely. This converts almost-sure convergence questions into series computations and is one step of the standard proof of the **strong law of large numbers** ([[Thm - Strong Law of Large Numbers]]) — combined with truncation and Kolmogorov's three-series criterion. The same template proves a.s. convergence of martingales in [[Advanced Probability IV — Martingales in Discrete Time|Advanced Probability IV]] and of the empirical process to the true distribution function (Glivenko–Cantelli).

> [!tip] Unlocked: Tail $\sigma$-algebras and Exchangeability *(from Probability and Statistical Mechanics)*
> The tail $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{T}=\bigcap_n\sigma(X_{n+1},X_{n+2},\dots)$ is one of two canonical $\sigma$-algebras with a strong rigidity property — the other being the **exchangeable $\sigma$-algebra** $\mathcal{E}$ of events invariant under finite permutations. **De Finetti's theorem** is the exchangeable analogue of the Kolmogorov 0–1 law: an exchangeable sequence is a *mixture* of i.i.d. sequences, with the mixing measure $\mathcal{E}$-measurable. The Hewitt–Savage 0–1 law is the i.i.d. exchangeable version: every event in $\mathcal{E}$ has probability $0$ or $1$ when the sequence is i.i.d. — strictly stronger than the Kolmogorov 0–1 law. These results are foundational in **Bayesian statistics** (the parameter in a Bayesian model is exactly the de Finetti mixing measure) and in **percolation theory**, where percolation events on translation-invariant lattices are exchangeable-like and inherit a $\{0,1\}$ dichotomy.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — What do we prove?** (1) *Computations* — an expectation, a variance, a probability — reduced to integrals against the [[Def - Random Variable|law]]. (2) *Inequalities* bounding probabilities and moments — [[Ex - Markov's inequality|Markov]], Chebyshev, [[Thm - Jensen's Inequality|Jensen]], Cauchy–Schwarz. (3) *Almost-sure statements* — "$A_n$ infinitely often / eventually", "$X_n\to X$ a.s." — via [[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|Borel–Cantelli]]. (4) *Zero–one dichotomies* — tail events, the determinism of asymptotics — via the [[Thm - Kolmogorov 0-1 Law|0–1 law]]. (5) *Existence* of random variables / sequences with prescribed laws — via the [[Ex - The quantile transform|quantile transform]].

**Sources — What do we leverage?** *Finiteness of $\mathbb{P}$* gives all finite-measure theorems for free. *A law or distribution function* routes a computation to an integral on $\mathbb{R}$. *Convexity* routes to Jensen. *A summable (or divergent) series $\sum\mathbb{P}(A_n)$* routes to Borel–Cantelli. *[[Def - Independence|Independence]]* is the master hypothesis — it converts joint laws into products (Fubini computations), licenses the second Borel–Cantelli lemma and the 0–1 law, and underlies every limit theorem. *A tail event* routes to the 0–1 law.

# Legal Operations

1. **Translate to measure theory.** Read "event/random variable/expectation/almost surely" as "measurable set/function/integral/almost everywhere"; import the [[Measure Theory II — §2 Integration|integration theorems]] wholesale.

2. **Reduce to the law.** Replace any computation about $X$ alone by an integral against $\mu_X$ via [[Ex - Expectation via the law|change of variables]] $\mathbb{E}[h(X)]=\int h\,d\mu_X$ — work on $\mathbb{R}$, forget $\Omega$.

3. **Indicator decomposition.** Write a count as $\sum_i\mathbf{1}_{A_i}$ and use [[Ex - Linearity of expectation and the indicator method|linearity of expectation]] — which needs *no* independence — to get $\mathbb{E}[\text{count}]=\sum_i\mathbb{P}(A_i)$.

4. **Bound a tail by a moment.** [[Ex - Markov's inequality|Markov]] $\mathbb{P}(|X|\ge\lambda)\le\lambda^{-1}\mathbb{E}|X|$; Chebyshev (Markov on $(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2$); Chernoff (Markov on $e^{tX}$).

5. **Apply Jensen.** For convex $\varphi$, $\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]\ge\varphi(\mathbb{E}X)$ — choose $\varphi$ to manufacture the desired moment or entropy inequality.

6. **Borel–Cantelli.** To prove "finitely many $A_n$ a.s.", show $\sum\mathbb{P}(A_n)<\infty$; to prove "infinitely many a.s.", show $\sum\mathbb{P}(A_n)=\infty$ *and* manufacture independence (disjoint blocks).

7. **Use independence as product structure.** Read "$X,Y$ independent" as "$\mu_{(X,Y)}=\mu_X\otimes\mu_Y$" and compute with [[Thm - Fubini-Tonelli Theorem|Fubini]]: $\mathbb{E}[f(X)g(Y)]=\mathbb{E}f(X)\,\mathbb{E}g(Y)$, $\mu_{X+Y}=\mu_X*\mu_Y$.

8. **Identify tail events.** Recognise "insensitive to finitely many coordinates" and invoke the [[Thm - Kolmogorov 0-1 Law|0–1 law]] for an a priori $\{0,1\}$ dichotomy.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Mistaking uncorrelated for independent
> $\mathrm{Cov}(X,Y)=0$ does *not* imply independence (take $Y=X^2$, $X$ symmetric). Independence is strictly stronger; it is a statement about the joint *law*, not just the second moment.

> [!warning] 2. Checking only pairwise independence
> Pairwise independence is strictly weaker than mutual — [[Ex - Pairwise versus mutual independence|Bernstein's example]]. The second [[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|Borel–Cantelli lemma]], the [[Thm - Kolmogorov 0-1 Law|0–1 law]], and product-measure arguments need full mutual independence.

> [!warning] 3. Using the second Borel–Cantelli lemma without independence
> Divergence $\sum\mathbb{P}(A_n)=\infty$ alone gives *nothing* — the second lemma needs the $A_n$ independent. Overlapping windows are not independent; partition into disjoint blocks.

> [!warning] 4. Treating variance as linear
> $\mathrm{Var}(X+Y)=\mathrm{Var}X+\mathrm{Var}Y$ only when $X,Y$ are uncorrelated. In general there is a $+2\mathrm{Cov}$ term. Linearity is for expectation, not variance.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems split into *computation*, *estimation*, and *almost-sure / asymptotic* questions.

For a **computation** — an expectation, variance, or probability — the first move is always to *reduce to the law*: by [[Ex - Expectation via the law|change of variables]], every such quantity is an integral against $\mu_X$ (or the joint law), so one works on $\mathbb{R}$ with the density or distribution function and never touches $\Omega$. When the quantity is a *count* — expected number of fixed points, records, collisions — decompose it into [[Ex - Linearity of expectation and the indicator method|indicators]] and use linearity of expectation, which ignores dependence entirely. When several variables are involved and they are [[Def - Independence|independent]], read this as "joint law $=$ product law" and compute with [[Thm - Fubini-Tonelli Theorem|Fubini]] — factorising expectations, convolving laws of sums.

For an **estimate** — bounding a probability or a moment — the toolkit is [[Ex - Markov's inequality|Markov]], Chebyshev, [[Thm - Jensen's Inequality|Jensen]], Cauchy–Schwarz. Markov trades an average for a tail; applied to a *transformed* variable it becomes Chebyshev ($X\mapsto(X-\mathbb{E}X)^2$) or the exponential Chernoff bound ($X\mapsto e^{tX}$). Jensen relates $\mathbb{E}\varphi(X)$ and $\varphi(\mathbb{E}X)$ for convex $\varphi$ — the art is choosing $\varphi$ to produce the inequality one wants.

For an **almost-sure or asymptotic** question, the structure is fixed. If the question is "do infinitely many $A_n$ occur," it is a [[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|Borel–Cantelli]] question: compute $\sum\mathbb{P}(A_n)$; convergence forces "finitely many a.s." (no independence needed), divergence-with-independence forces "infinitely many a.s." (and one engineers independence by passing to disjoint blocks). If the question concerns a *tail* quantity — convergence of a series, a $\limsup$, a Cesàro limit — first invoke the [[Thm - Kolmogorov 0-1 Law|0–1 law]] to know the answer is $0/1$ or a constant *before* computing, then compute. To prove $X_n\to X$ a.s., the standard route is to show $\sum_n\mathbb{P}(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)<\infty$ and apply the first Borel–Cantelli lemma.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Ex - Linearity of expectation and the indicator method|Linearity of expectation]]**: $\mathbb{E}[aX+bY]=a\mathbb{E}X+b\mathbb{E}Y$, *unconditionally*. The indicator method — count $=\sum\mathbf{1}_{A_i}$, $\mathbb{E}[\text{count}]=\sum\mathbb{P}(A_i)$ — solves a vast class of "expected number of..." problems in one line, dependence notwithstanding.

- **[[Ex - Expectation via the law|The change-of-variables formula]]**: $\mathbb{E}[h(X)]=\int h\,d\mu_X$. Every computation about a single variable reduces to an integral on $\mathbb{R}$; the abstract space is dispensable. Typical use: compute moments from a density or distribution function.

- **[[Ex - Markov's inequality|Markov's inequality]]** and its descendants: the universal tail bounds. Average controls tail; applied to $X^2$, $|X|^p$, $e^{tX}$ it gives Chebyshev, moment, and Chernoff bounds — the route from "moment finite" to "tail small," hence to the [[Thm - Weak Law of Large Numbers|weak law]] and concentration.

- **[[Thm - Jensen's Inequality|Jensen's inequality]]**: $\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]\ge\varphi(\mathbb{E}X)$ for convex $\varphi$. Typical use: prove moment monotonicity, non-negativity of variance/entropy, contraction of [[Def - Conditional Expectation|conditional expectation]] — by choosing the convex $\varphi$.

- **[[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|The Borel–Cantelli lemmas]]**: convergence/divergence of $\sum\mathbb{P}(A_n)$ decides $\mathbb{P}(A_n\text{ i.o.})$. The standard machine for almost-sure occurrence and non-occurrence; the first lemma is the workhorse for proving a.s. convergence.

# Bridges

1. **To measure theory.** A probability space *is* a [[Def - Measure and Measure Space|measure space]] of mass $1$; this entire chapter is the measure theory of [[Measure Theory I — §1 Measure Spaces|Chapters I–III]] re-read with probabilistic vocabulary and questions. Expectation is the [[Def - The Integral|integral]]; the law is a [[Def - Random Variable|pushforward]]; the distribution-function correspondence is [[Thm - Hahn-Carathéodory Extension Theorem|Lebesgue–Stieltjes]]; independence is [[Thm - Product Measure|product structure]]; the first Borel–Cantelli lemma is [[Thm - Properties of Measures|σ-subadditivity]]; the 0–1 law runs on [[Thm - Dynkin's π-λ Theorem|Dynkin's theorem]].

2. **To the limit theorems.** The [[Thm - Kolmogorov 0-1 Law|0–1 law]] guarantees the limit in the [[Thm - Strong Law of Large Numbers|strong law]] is a deterministic constant; [[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|Borel–Cantelli]] is a step in proving a.s. convergence; [[Def - Independence|independence]] and the convolution structure of sums set up the [[Thm - Central Limit Theorem|central limit theorem]] — the content of [[Advanced Probability II — Convergence and Limit Theorems|Advanced Probability II]].

3. **To conditional expectation and martingales.** $\sigma(X)$ as "information," and the [[Ex - The σ-algebra generated by a function|Doob–Dynkin lemma]], are the foundation of [[Def - Conditional Expectation|conditional expectation]] ([[Advanced Probability III — Conditional Expectation|AP III]]); an increasing chain of $\sigma$-algebras is a [[Def - Filtration|filtration]] ([[Advanced Probability IV — Martingales in Discrete Time|AP IV]]); conditional [[Thm - Jensen's Inequality|Jensen]] is the engine of martingale $L^p$-theory.

4. **To information theory.** A sub-$\sigma$-algebra is a resolution of information; independence is zero mutual information; Jensen applied to $-\log$ is the non-negativity of KL-divergence and entropy — the bridge to the user's algorithmic-information work.

# Insights

**Kolmogorov's reduction is the unifying frame: probability is measure theory with mass $1$, a change of vocabulary, and *two genuinely new ideas* — the law and independence.** Everything formal — events, random variables, expectation, almost-sure — is measure theory renamed, and importing the [[Measure Theory II — §2 Integration|integration theorems]] is free. The *law* $\mu_X$ is what lets one forget the abstract $\Omega$ and compute on $\mathbb{R}$; two variables with the same law are interchangeable, and the [[Ex - The quantile transform|quantile transform]] shows a single uniform variable on $[0,1]$ already carries every law. *Independence* is the one concept with no measure-theoretic precursor — and it is not really new either, being [[Thm - Product Measure|product-measure]] structure: independent variables are coordinates of a product space, and [[Thm - Fubini-Tonelli Theorem|Fubini]] is how one computes with them.

**Independence is what makes asymptotics *deterministic* — and the [[Thm - Kolmogorov 0-1 Law|0–1 law]] is the sharpest expression of this.** Individually the $X_n$ are random; but any *tail* feature of an independent sequence — whether a series converges, the value of a $\limsup$, the limit of Cesàro averages — is a constant, fixed with probability one. The mechanism is startling: a tail event is independent of every finite past, the finite pasts generate everything, so the tail event is independent of *itself* and hence has probability $0$ or $1$. This is why the [[Thm - Strong Law of Large Numbers|strong law]] can assert convergence to a *number*, why a random power series has a deterministic radius of convergence, and why [[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|Borel–Cantelli]]'s "$A_n$ infinitely often" was always going to be $0$ or $1$. Determinism emerging from randomness, in the limit, is the deepest theme of probability — and it begins here.

**The probabilistic inequalities are all one idea — bound a hard quantity by an easy one using monotonicity or convexity.** [[Ex - Markov's inequality|Markov]] bounds a tail by an average through the pointwise inequality $\mathbf{1}_{\{X\ge\lambda\}}\le X/\lambda$; [[Thm - Jensen's Inequality|Jensen]] bounds $\varphi(\mathbb{E}X)$ by $\mathbb{E}\varphi(X)$ through the supporting line; Cauchy–Schwarz bounds a covariance through the $L^2$ geometry. Each works by replacing the quantity of interest with a *tangent* or *dominating* object that expectation handles linearly. The art — and the recurring trigger-reaction — is the substitution: apply Markov not to $X$ but to $X^2$, to $e^{tX}$, to $|X|^p$; apply Jensen with the $\varphi$ that turns the target into a moment, an entropy, or a norm. Mastering *which* transformed quantity to feed in is most of elementary probability's problem-solving.

**A single number, $\sum_n\mathbb{P}(A_n)$, governs the almost-sure occurrence of a sequence of events — the [[Thm - Borel-Cantelli Lemmas|Borel–Cantelli]] dichotomy.** Convergence of the series forces "finitely many $A_n$, almost surely," with no further hypothesis; divergence forces "infinitely many, almost surely," provided the $A_n$ are independent. This converts qualitative almost-sure questions into a quantitative series computation, and it is the standard bridge from the *summable* world of [[Measure Theory II — §2 Integration|L¹ estimates]] to the *pointwise* world of [[Def - Almost Everywhere|almost-sure]] behaviour: to prove $X_n\to X$ a.s., show the error events are summable. The two lemmas, paired with the 0–1 law's a priori $\{0,1\}$ certainty, are the complete toolkit for first-order asymptotic probability.
