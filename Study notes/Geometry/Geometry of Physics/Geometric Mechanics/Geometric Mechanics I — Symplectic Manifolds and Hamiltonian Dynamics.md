# Notation Registry

Throughout this topic, $M$ is a smooth manifold (Hausdorff, second-countable, without boundary unless said otherwise) and $Q$ is a smooth manifold playing the role of the **configuration space** of a mechanical system, with $\dim Q = n$, so phase space $T^*Q$ has dimension $2n$. **Standing convention — the sign of the canonical symplectic form.** Different sources differ on the sign of the canonical $2$-form on $T^*Q$. The convention used here, following Frankel, Marsden–Ratiu, and most modern texts, is

$$\theta = p_i\, dq^i, \qquad \omega = -d\theta = dp_i \wedge dq^i.$$

Arnold's *Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics* uses the opposite sign, $\omega = d\theta = dq^i \wedge dp_i$, and Abraham–Marsden use yet a third (the same as Frankel but writing $\omega = -d\theta$ explicitly). The choice affects only the sign of Hamilton's equations and of the Poisson bracket; with our convention $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ produces $\dot q^i = \partial H/\partial p_i$, $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q^i$, and $\{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g)$. To convert from a source using the opposite sign, flip the sign of $\omega$ and of the Poisson bracket everywhere; Hamilton's equations remain unchanged because the sign appears on both sides.

> [!warning] Convention
> Some sources (notably Arnold) use $\omega = +d\theta$ rather than $\omega = -d\theta$. This flips the sign of $X_H$, $\{\cdot,\cdot\}$, and the Liouville volume $\omega^n$. Whichever convention you adopt, **stick to it**: mixing conventions is the most common source of sign errors in symplectic computations.

- $V, W$ — finite-dimensional real vector spaces
- $\omega$ — a symplectic form (either on a vector space or on a manifold)
- $(V, \omega)$ — a symplectic vector space; $(M, \omega)$ — a symplectic manifold
- $\omega_0 = \sum_i dp_i \wedge dq^i$ — the **standard symplectic form** on $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ with coordinates $(q^1, \dots, q^n, p_1, \dots, p_n)$
- $Q$ — configuration space ($n$-dimensional smooth manifold)
- $T^*Q$ — cotangent bundle of $Q$ = **phase space** ($2n$-dimensional)
- $\pi : T^*Q \to Q$ — the cotangent bundle projection
- $(q^i, p_i)$ — canonical (Darboux) coordinates on $T^*Q$: $q^i$ are coordinates on $Q$, $p_i$ are the corresponding momentum coordinates
- $\theta = p_i\, dq^i$ — the **Poincaré (canonical, tautological) 1-form** on $T^*Q$
- $\omega = -d\theta = dp_i \wedge dq^i$ — the **canonical symplectic 2-form** on $T^*Q$
- $H : M \to \mathbb{R}$ — a **Hamiltonian function** (a smooth real-valued function on phase space)
- $X_H$ — the **Hamiltonian vector field** of $H$, defined by $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$
- $\phi^H_t$ — the **Hamiltonian flow** of $H$, the flow of $X_H$
- $L : TQ \to \mathbb{R}$ — a **Lagrangian function** (a function on the tangent bundle)
- $\{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g) = X_f(g)$ — the **Poisson bracket** of $f, g \in C^\infty(M)$
- $\dot q^i = dq^i/dt$, $\dot p_i = dp_i/dt$ — time derivatives along a trajectory
- $\omega^n = \omega \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega$ ($n$ factors) — the **Liouville volume form**, up to a factor of $n!$
- $S(C) = \int_C (p_i\, dq^i - H\, dt)$ — the **action integral** along a curve $C$ in $T^*Q \times \mathbb{R}$
- $\delta$ — variation of a curve or functional; e.g., $\delta q$ is the variation field
- $T, V$ — kinetic and potential energy; $E = T + V$ — total energy
- $g_{ij}, g^{ij}$ — components of a Riemannian metric on $Q$ and its inverse

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: **a symplectic manifold is a smooth manifold equipped with a closed, nondegenerate $2$-form, and once you have it, classical mechanics becomes a chapter in the calculus of forms.** Every notion in Hamiltonian dynamics — phase space, the Hamiltonian flow, conservation laws, Liouville's volume preservation, Poisson brackets, canonical transformations — is forced by the algebraic structure of a single $2$-form $\omega$. The classical content disappears into geometry, and the formulas you remember from physics class become consequences of two facts: $d\omega = 0$ and $\omega$ is nondegenerate.

To see why a $2$-form is the right object, return to the empirical content of Newtonian mechanics. A configuration of a system is a point in a manifold $Q$ (positions of all particles, angles of a pendulum, etc.). The dynamics is second-order — Newton's law is $\ddot q = F/m$ — so the state of the system at one instant requires both position and velocity, a point in the **tangent bundle** $TQ$. But velocities transform tensorially in only one way under a change of configuration variables, while the natural objects associated with a Lagrangian transform as **covectors**: the momentum $p_i = \partial L/\partial \dot q^i$ is a $1$-form on $Q$, not a vector. Switching from velocities to momenta moves us from $TQ$ to the **cotangent bundle** $T^*Q$, and this is **phase space**. The advantage is structural: $T^*Q$ carries a canonical $1$-form $\theta = p_i\, dq^i$ — the **Poincaré (tautological) 1-form** — which exists with no extra choices, no metric, no Lagrangian. Its exterior derivative $\omega = -d\theta = dp_i \wedge dq^i$ is the **canonical symplectic form**, and it is the geometry that does all the work.

Once you have $\omega$, the recipe for converting between observables and dynamics is automatic. A function $H : T^*Q \to \mathbb{R}$ — the Hamiltonian, typically the total energy — defines a unique vector field $X_H$ on $T^*Q$ by the equation $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$. The existence and uniqueness of $X_H$ is exactly the nondegeneracy of $\omega$: the map $X \mapsto \iota_X\omega$ from vector fields to $1$-forms is an isomorphism. The integral curves of $X_H$ are the trajectories of the mechanical system, and writing $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ in the coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$ recovers Hamilton's equations

$$\dot q^i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}$$

with no further input. The closedness $d\omega = 0$ is what makes the geometry consistent across the whole manifold, and via Cartan's magic formula it gives **Liouville's theorem** in one line: $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d\iota_{X_H}\omega + \iota_{X_H}d\omega = d(dH) + 0 = 0$, so the symplectic form (and its top wedge power $\omega^n$, the symplectic volume) is preserved by the flow. The conservation of phase-space volume is a corollary of $d^2 = 0$.

The chapter is organized around the structural backbone

$$\text{symplectic vector space} \subset \text{symplectic manifold} \xrightarrow{\text{Darboux}} \text{locally } (\mathbb{R}^{2n}, \omega_0),$$

where the central existence theorem — [[Thm - Darboux's Theorem|Darboux's theorem]] — says that every symplectic manifold looks locally like $(\mathbb{R}^{2n}, \sum dp_i \wedge dq^i)$. This is dramatic: it means there are no local invariants of symplectic manifolds, no symplectic analogue of Riemannian curvature. Two symplectic manifolds of equal dimension are locally indistinguishable, and the entire geometry is global. The proof, by **Moser's trick**, is the flagship technique of symplectic geometry: deform the given $2$-form to the standard one, find a time-dependent vector field whose flow realizes the deformation, and integrate. The Moser method recurs throughout the subject — it is what proves the uniqueness of symplectic structures up to isotopy.

The other half of the chapter is the variational picture. Newton's law arises from two equivalent variational principles. On $TQ$, **Hamilton's principle** $\delta \int L\, dt = 0$ with $L = T - V$ produces the Euler–Lagrange equations. On $T^*Q$, **Hamilton's principle in phase space** $\delta \int (p\dot q - H)\, dt = 0$ produces Hamilton's equations. The two are related by the **Legendre transform** $p_i = \partial L/\partial \dot q^i$, $H = p_i\dot q^i - L$, which converts a regular Lagrangian into a Hamiltonian and vice versa. Finally, **Jacobi's principle of least action** — for an autonomous system with energy $E$, trajectories are geodesics in the conformal metric $g^J_{ij} = 2(E - V)g_{ij}$ — turns Hamiltonian trajectories into geometry: dynamics is the geodesic flow of a metric that encodes both the kinetic-energy tensor and the potential. This is the bridge to [[Riemannian Geometry II — Geodesics, the Exponential Map, and Variational Principles|Riemannian Geometry II]] and the reason the geodesic flow on a Riemannian manifold is the prototype of every Hamiltonian system.

**Audience-assumption paragraph.** You should be fluent with smooth manifolds and the cotangent bundle ([[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases]], [[Differential Geometry VI — Vector Bundles and the Cotangent Bundle]]), with vector fields, flows, and the Lie bracket ([[Differential Geometry V — Vector Fields, Flows, and the Lie Bracket]]), and especially with the calculus of differential forms — exterior derivative $d$, interior product $\iota_X$, Lie derivative $\mathcal{L}_X$, [[Thm - Cartan's Magic Formula|Cartan's magic formula]] $\mathcal{L}_X = d\iota_X + \iota_X d$ ([[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]]). The variational calculations in the second half draw on the variation-of-arc-length calculation from [[Riemannian Geometry II — Geodesics, the Exponential Map, and Variational Principles|Riemannian Geometry II]] and the basic linear algebra of bilinear forms ([[Def - Bilinear Form]], [[Def - Symmetric and Alternating Bilinear Form]]). On the physics side, familiarity with Newton's equations of motion and the basics of Lagrangian mechanics is helpful but not strictly required — the variational principles are derived from scratch.

# Concept Map

## §1.1 Symplectic Manifolds

- **[[Def - Symplectic Vector Space]]**
	- A **symplectic vector space** is a finite-dimensional real vector space $V$ equipped with a bilinear form $\omega : V \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ that is **antisymmetric** ($\omega(u,v) = -\omega(v,u)$) and **nondegenerate** ($\omega(u, \cdot) = 0 \Rightarrow u = 0$). The standard example is $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ with $\omega_0((q,p),(q',p')) = \sum_i(p_i q'^i - p'_i q^i)$, written as the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & I_n \\ -I_n & 0\end{pmatrix}$ in the $(q,p)$-basis. Nondegeneracy of an antisymmetric form forces $\dim V$ to be even, and the **linear Darboux theorem** says every symplectic vector space admits a basis in which $\omega$ has the standard form. The single antisymmetric counterpart to an inner product gives a complete classification by dimension.

- **[[Def - Symplectic Manifold]]**
	- A **symplectic manifold** is a smooth manifold $M$ equipped with a $2$-form $\omega \in \Omega^2(M)$ that is **closed** ($d\omega = 0$) and **nondegenerate** at every point (so $(T_pM, \omega_p)$ is a symplectic vector space for all $p$). Nondegeneracy forces $\dim M$ to be even, $\dim M = 2n$, and produces an isomorphism $TM \cong T^*M$ by $X \mapsto \iota_X\omega$ — the symplectic analogue of the musical isomorphism in [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds|Riemannian geometry]], but with antisymmetry replacing symmetry. The top wedge $\omega^n = \omega \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega$ is a volume form, so every symplectic manifold is canonically oriented. Examples: $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ with $\omega_0$; every cotangent bundle $T^*Q$; every oriented surface with its area form; closed orientable surfaces of genus $g \geq 1$ admit symplectic structures but never bound as cotangent bundles.

- **[[Def - The Canonical Symplectic Form on a Cotangent Bundle]]**
	- The cotangent bundle $T^*Q$ of any smooth manifold $Q$ carries a canonical $1$-form $\theta$, the **Poincaré (tautological) 1-form**, defined intrinsically by $\theta_\alpha(v) = \alpha(d\pi_\alpha(v))$ for $\alpha \in T^*Q$ and $v \in T_\alpha(T^*Q)$, where $\pi : T^*Q \to Q$ is the projection. In canonical coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$ it reads $\theta = p_i\, dq^i$. Its exterior derivative $\omega = -d\theta = dp_i \wedge dq^i$ is the **canonical symplectic form** on $T^*Q$, automatically closed (it is exact) and nondegenerate (the matrix of $\omega$ in $(q,p)$-coordinates is the standard symplectic matrix). The construction requires no metric and no extra data — every cotangent bundle is canonically a symplectic manifold.

- **[[Thm - Darboux's Theorem]]**
	- Every symplectic manifold $(M^{2n}, \omega)$ admits, around every point, local coordinates $(q^1, \dots, q^n, p_1, \dots, p_n)$ — **Darboux coordinates** — in which $\omega = \sum_i dp_i \wedge dq^i$. Two symplectic manifolds of equal dimension are therefore locally indistinguishable: **there are no local invariants of symplectic geometry**. The proof, by Moser's trick, deforms the given $\omega$ to the standard $\omega_0$ along a path $\omega_t$ of symplectic forms and integrates the time-dependent vector field $X_t$ defined by $\iota_{X_t}\omega_t = -\sigma$ where $d\sigma = \omega - \omega_0$, producing a diffeomorphism that pulls $\omega$ to $\omega_0$. The contrast with Riemannian geometry — which has rich local invariants (curvature) — is striking and is the defining structural fact about the subject.

- **[[Def - Lagrangian Submanifold]]**
	- A submanifold $L \subset (M^{2n}, \omega)$ is **Lagrangian** if it is isotropic ($\omega$ restricts to zero on $L$) and of maximal dimension among isotropic submanifolds, equivalently $\dim L = n$ and $\omega|_L = 0$. Examples: the zero section of $T^*Q$; the graph of a closed $1$-form $\beta$ on $Q$ inside $T^*Q$ (it is Lagrangian if and only if $d\beta = 0$); the cotangent fibre $T^*_qQ$ at any point. Lagrangians are the symplectic analogue of "half-dimensional planes orthogonal to themselves"; the slogan **"everything is a Lagrangian"** — graphs of canonical transformations, generating functions, supports of WKB solutions, branes — captures the principle that almost every interesting object in symplectic geometry is or arises from a Lagrangian submanifold.

- **[[Ex - Harmonic Oscillator in Phase Space]]** (⭐)
	- For the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator $H(q, p) = \tfrac{1}{2}(p^2 + \omega_0^2 q^2)$ on $T^*\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^2$, write down the canonical symplectic form, derive the Hamiltonian vector field $X_H$, solve Hamilton's equations explicitly, and verify the trajectories are ellipses in $(q, p)$-space confined to level sets of $H$. Compute the symplectic area enclosed by one period of the orbit and recover Planck's quantum $h$ (in suitable units) as the natural unit of phase-space area.

- **[[Ex - Pendulum on a Sphere (Symplectic Treatment)]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Set up the phase space $T^*S^2$ for a particle constrained to the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^3$ under gravity. Express the canonical symplectic form in spherical coordinates, derive Hamilton's equations, identify the conserved Hamiltonian (energy) and conserved angular momentum about the vertical axis, and use these conservation laws to reduce to a one-dimensional problem in the polar angle. This is the prototypical example of **symplectic reduction by a circle action**.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Symplectic Reduction *(from Geometric Mechanics II / Hamiltonian Group Actions)*
> When a Lie group $G$ acts on a symplectic manifold $(M, \omega)$ preserving $\omega$ with a **moment map** $\mu : M \to \mathfrak{g}^*$ (a $G$-equivariant map encoding the infinitesimal symmetry), the **Marsden–Weinstein quotient** $M//_\mu G := \mu^{-1}(0)/G$ inherits a symplectic structure of dimension $\dim M - 2\dim G$. This is the geometric content of **Noether's theorem**: continuous symmetries produce conserved quantities (the components of $\mu$), and "passing to the quotient" lets you forget the symmetry by reducing to a smaller symplectic manifold where the dynamics is still Hamiltonian. The reduced phase space is the right setting for almost every problem with continuous symmetry: a rigid body, the $n$-body problem with rotational invariance, gauge theories with the Gauss law constraint.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Gromov Non-Squeezing *(from Symplectic Topology)*
> Darboux's theorem rules out *local* invariants, but symplectic manifolds carry deep **global** invariants. **Gromov's non-squeezing theorem** (1985): a ball $B^{2n}(r) \subset \mathbb{R}^{2n}$ cannot be symplectically embedded into the cylinder $B^2(R) \times \mathbb{R}^{2n-2}$ unless $r \leq R$. A volume-preserving diffeomorphism can squeeze a ball into an arbitrarily thin cylinder, but a *symplectic* one cannot — symplectic capacity, measured by $\pi r^2$, is preserved. The proof uses pseudo-holomorphic curves, opening the door to **Hofer's geometry of symplectomorphisms**, **Floer homology**, and the modern subject of symplectic topology.

## §1.2 Hamiltonian Vector Fields and Flows

- **[[Def - Hamiltonian Function]]**
	- A **Hamiltonian function** on a symplectic manifold $(M, \omega)$ is any smooth function $H : M \to \mathbb{R}$. In mechanics $H$ is typically the total energy $H = T + V$, kinetic plus potential, expressed as a function on phase space. The Hamiltonian generates dynamics via the assignment $H \mapsto X_H$ where $X_H$ is determined uniquely by $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$. Two Hamiltonians differing by a constant generate the same dynamics; conversely, the dynamics determines $H$ up to a constant. The Hamiltonian is the central observable, but in the symplectic framework *every* smooth function on $M$ has equal status as a "Hamiltonian for its own flow", and this democratization is what underlies the Poisson-algebra structure on $C^\infty(M)$.

- **[[Def - Hamiltonian Vector Field]]**
	- For a function $H \in C^\infty(M)$ on a symplectic manifold $(M, \omega)$, the **Hamiltonian vector field** $X_H$ is the unique vector field satisfying $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$, equivalently $\omega(X_H, \cdot) = dH$. Existence and uniqueness follow from the nondegeneracy of $\omega$: the map $X \mapsto \iota_X\omega$ is a fibrewise linear isomorphism $TM \to T^*M$, and $X_H$ is its inverse applied to $dH$. In canonical coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$ on $T^*Q$ this reads $X_H = (\partial H/\partial p_i)\partial_{q^i} - (\partial H/\partial q^i)\partial_{p_i}$, and its integral curves are exactly the solutions of Hamilton's equations $\dot q^i = \partial H/\partial p_i$, $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q^i$. The flow $\phi^H_t$ of $X_H$ is the **Hamiltonian flow** and gives the dynamical evolution of the system.

- **[[Thm - Hamiltonian Flows are Symplectomorphisms]]**
	- For any Hamiltonian $H \in C^\infty(M)$ on a symplectic manifold $(M, \omega)$, the flow $\phi^H_t$ preserves $\omega$: $(\phi^H_t)^*\omega = \omega$ for all $t$ for which the flow is defined. The proof is a one-line Cartan computation: $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d\iota_{X_H}\omega + \iota_{X_H}d\omega = d(dH) + \iota_{X_H}(0) = 0$, using $d^2 = 0$ and closedness of $\omega$. The corollary is that Hamiltonian dynamics is a flow in the group of **symplectomorphisms** (canonical transformations), and the full symplectomorphism group is "infinite-dimensionally Lie" with Lie algebra essentially $C^\infty(M)$ under the Poisson bracket.

- **[[Thm - Liouville's Theorem on Phase Space Volume]]**
	- Every Hamiltonian flow preserves the **Liouville volume form** $\omega^n/n! = dq^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dq^n \wedge dp_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dp_n$ on $T^*Q$ (and the analogous $\omega^n$ on any symplectic manifold). The proof is immediate from $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$ and the Leibniz rule for the Lie derivative: $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega^n = n\,\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega \wedge \omega^{n-1} = 0$. The dynamical content is fundamental: an ensemble of states (a region in phase space) evolves under Hamiltonian flow as an **incompressible fluid**. This is the geometric heart of statistical mechanics — the microcanonical ensemble, the equal-probability postulate on energy shells, and ultimately the foundation of equilibrium statistical mechanics all rest on Liouville's theorem.

- **[[Def - Symplectomorphism (Canonical Transformation)]]**
	- A diffeomorphism $\varphi : (M_1, \omega_1) \to (M_2, \omega_2)$ is a **symplectomorphism** (classically a **canonical transformation**) if $\varphi^*\omega_2 = \omega_1$. The set of symplectomorphisms of $(M, \omega)$ to itself forms an infinite-dimensional Lie group $\mathrm{Symp}(M, \omega)$. Hamiltonian flows produce a distinguished subgroup, the **Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms** $\mathrm{Ham}(M, \omega)$, which sits inside $\mathrm{Symp}(M, \omega)$ as a kernel-like normal subgroup; the quotient $\mathrm{Symp}/\mathrm{Ham}$ is captured by the first de Rham cohomology $H^1_{dR}(M; \mathbb{R})$. Canonical transformations are the symmetries of the symplectic structure; they are what the substitutions in classical-mechanics textbooks "preserving the form of Hamilton's equations" are reaching for.

- **[[Ex - Geodesic Flow on a Riemannian Manifold is Hamiltonian]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Given a Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$, define $H : T^*M \to \mathbb{R}$ by $H(q, p) = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{ij}(q)\,p_i p_j$ — half the squared norm of the cotangent vector. Show that the Hamiltonian vector field $X_H$ on $T^*M$ with the canonical symplectic structure has integral curves whose projections to $M$ are exactly the geodesics of $g$, parametrized by arc length. This identifies the geodesic flow as the Hamiltonian flow of the kinetic-energy function — and is the reason Jacobi's principle equates Hamiltonian trajectories with geodesics in a conformal metric.

- **[[Ex - The Lagrange Submanifold of a Magnetic Field]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- For a charged particle in an electromagnetic field with magnetic potential $A = A_i\, dq^i$, the "twisted" symplectic form $\omega = \omega_0 + \pi^*F$ on $T^*Q$, where $F = dA$ is the magnetic field $2$-form, gives Hamiltonian dynamics that recovers the Lorentz force law. Show that the graph $\{(q, eA(q)) : q \in Q\} \subset T^*Q$ is a Lagrangian submanifold for the standard $\omega_0$ if and only if $F = 0$, and verify that the dynamics on $(T^*Q, \omega)$ with Hamiltonian $H = \tfrac{1}{2}|p|^2$ matches that on $(T^*Q, \omega_0)$ with Hamiltonian $H = \tfrac{1}{2}|p - eA|^2$. This is the geometric statement of **minimal coupling** in electromagnetism.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Moment Map *(from Hamiltonian Group Actions)*
> When a Lie group $G$ acts on $(M, \omega)$ preserving $\omega$, each $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$ produces an infinitesimal generator vector field $X_\xi$ on $M$. If $X_\xi$ is *Hamiltonian* — i.e., $X_\xi = X_{\mu^\xi}$ for some smooth function $\mu^\xi$ — and the assignment $\xi \mapsto \mu^\xi$ is $\mathfrak{g}$-equivariant, then the assembled map $\mu : M \to \mathfrak{g}^*$ defined by $\mu(p)(\xi) = \mu^\xi(p)$ is the **moment map**. It is the geometric incarnation of Noether's theorem: $\mu$ is constant along the Hamiltonian flow of any $G$-invariant Hamiltonian, so its components are conserved quantities. For rotational symmetry $G = SO(3)$, $\mu$ is the angular momentum vector; for translation, $\mu$ is the linear momentum; in field theory, $\mu$ gives the Noether charges of internal symmetries.

## §1.3 Poisson Brackets and Conservation Laws

- **[[Def - Poisson Bracket]]**
	- The **Poisson bracket** on a symplectic manifold $(M, \omega)$ is the bilinear operation $\{\cdot, \cdot\} : C^\infty(M) \times C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$ defined by $\{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g) = X_f(g) = -X_g(f)$. In canonical coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$, $\{f, g\} = \sum_i \big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q^i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q^i}\big)$. The fundamental brackets are $\{q^i, q^j\} = 0$, $\{p_i, p_j\} = 0$, $\{q^i, p_j\} = \delta^i_j$ — recognizable as the classical analogues of the canonical commutation relations $[\hat q^i, \hat p_j] = i\hbar\delta^i_j$ in quantum mechanics. The Poisson bracket is the algebraic structure that promotes the symplectic manifold from a geometric object to a **Poisson algebra** of observables.

- **[[Thm - Poisson Bracket is a Lie Algebra Structure on Smooth Functions]]**
	- The Poisson bracket makes $C^\infty(M)$ into an (infinite-dimensional) real **Lie algebra**: $\{\cdot,\cdot\}$ is bilinear, antisymmetric ($\{f,g\} = -\{g,f\}$), and satisfies the **Jacobi identity** $\{f,\{g,h\}\} + \{g,\{h,f\}\} + \{h,\{f,g\}\} = 0$. Moreover it satisfies the **Leibniz rule** $\{f, gh\} = \{f, g\}h + g\{f, h\}$, making $C^\infty(M)$ a **Poisson algebra**. The Lie-algebra map $f \mapsto X_f$ is a homomorphism into vector fields: $X_{\{f,g\}} = -[X_f, X_g]$ (with the sign depending on convention). The Jacobi identity for $\{\cdot,\cdot\}$ is equivalent to $d\omega = 0$ — the closedness of $\omega$ is exactly what makes the Poisson algebra associative-up-to-homotopy and the Lie algebra structure consistent.

- **[[Ex - Poisson Bracket of Angular Momentum Components]]** (⭐⭐)
	- On $T^*\mathbb{R}^3$ with canonical coordinates $(q^1, q^2, q^3, p_1, p_2, p_3)$, define the angular momentum components $L^i = \epsilon^{ijk}q^j p_k$. Compute the Poisson brackets $\{L^i, L^j\}$ directly from the canonical brackets and show $\{L^i, L^j\} = \epsilon^{ijk}L^k$ — the angular momentum components form a Lie subalgebra of $(C^\infty, \{\cdot,\cdot\})$ isomorphic to $\mathfrak{so}(3)$. Verify that $L^2 = \sum_i (L^i)^2$ Poisson-commutes with each $L^i$ and is therefore a **Casimir** of the angular-momentum subalgebra.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Completely Integrable Hamiltonian Systems *(from Arnold–Liouville Theory)*
> A Hamiltonian system on a $2n$-dimensional symplectic manifold is **completely integrable** if it admits $n$ functionally independent conserved quantities $f_1 = H, f_2, \dots, f_n$ in **involution** (i.e., $\{f_i, f_j\} = 0$ for all $i, j$). The **Arnold–Liouville theorem** then says the common level sets $\{f_i = c_i\}$ are tori (in the compact, connected case), and there exist **action–angle variables** $(I_1, \dots, I_n, \theta^1, \dots, \theta^n)$ — with $I_i$ the actions, $\theta^i$ angular coordinates on the torus — in which Hamilton's equations become $\dot I_i = 0$, $\dot\theta^i = \partial H/\partial I_i = \text{constant}$. The dynamics is **quasi-periodic motion on tori**: rotation at constant angular velocity in each angle. Classical integrable systems include the Kepler problem, the harmonic oscillator, the rigid body, the Toda lattice, and the Calogero–Moser system. **KAM theory** (Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser) studies what happens to these tori under a small perturbation that breaks integrability — most survive (the "KAM tori"), with quantitative measure-theoretic estimates that ultimately underlie the long-term stability of the solar system.

## §1.4 Variational Principles in Mechanics

- **[[Def - The Lagrangian Function]]**
	- A **Lagrangian** for a mechanical system on configuration space $Q$ is a smooth function $L : TQ \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, typically $L(q, \dot q, t)$, that produces the dynamics via Hamilton's principle. In the most common setting $L = T - V$ where $T = \tfrac{1}{2}g_{ij}(q)\dot q^i \dot q^j$ is the kinetic energy (determined by a Riemannian metric on $Q$ and the masses) and $V(q)$ is the potential energy. A Lagrangian is **regular** if the Hessian matrix $\partial^2 L/\partial \dot q^i \partial \dot q^j$ is invertible at every point; this is exactly the condition for the **Legendre transform** $TQ \to T^*Q$ to be a local diffeomorphism. Lagrangians live on $TQ$ and treat velocities directly; Hamiltonians live on $T^*Q$ and treat momenta. The Legendre transform is the bridge between the two pictures.

- **[[Def - The Legendre Transform]]**
	- For a regular Lagrangian $L : TQ \to \mathbb{R}$, the **Legendre transform** is the map $\mathbb{F}L : TQ \to T^*Q$ defined fibrewise by $\mathbb{F}L(q, \dot q) = (q, p)$ where $p_i = \partial L/\partial \dot q^i$. The associated **Hamiltonian** on $T^*Q$ is $H(q, p) = p_i \dot q^i - L(q, \dot q)$, with $\dot q$ expressed in terms of $(q, p)$ via the inverse of $\mathbb{F}L$. The transformation is involutive: applying it to $H$ recovers $L$. For $L = \tfrac{1}{2}g_{ij}\dot q^i \dot q^j - V(q)$ the Hamiltonian comes out to $H = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{ij}p_i p_j + V(q) = T + V$, the **total energy**. This is the precise statement that "energy is the Legendre dual of the Lagrangian", and it is what connects the variational and Hamiltonian pictures of mechanics.

- **[[Thm - Hamilton's Principle in TQ Gives Euler-Lagrange Equations]]**
	- A curve $q : [a, b] \to Q$ is a stationary point of the action $S[q] = \int_a^b L(q, \dot q, t)\, dt$ among all variations with fixed endpoints $q(a), q(b)$ if and only if it satisfies the **Euler–Lagrange equations**

	$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i} - \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i} = 0.$$
	
	The proof is integration by parts on the variation $\delta S = 0$, using the vanishing of $\delta q$ at the endpoints to discard the boundary term, and the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations to conclude the integrand must vanish identically. For $L = T - V$ this recovers Newton's law $m\ddot q = -\nabla V$ in any coordinate system on $Q$, with the metric structure on $Q$ providing the covariant derivative that makes $\ddot q$ tensorially meaningful.

- **[[Thm - Equivalence of Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Formalisms]]**
	- For a regular Lagrangian $L$ with associated Hamiltonian $H = p\dot q - L$ via the Legendre transform, the following two variational principles are equivalent and produce equivalent equations of motion:
	
	$$\delta\int_a^b L(q, \dot q, t)\, dt = 0 \quad \text{on } TQ \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \delta\int_a^b \big(p_i\dot q^i - H(q, p, t)\big)\, dt = 0 \quad \text{on } T^*Q.$$
	
	The first gives the Euler–Lagrange equations; the second gives Hamilton's equations $\dot q^i = \partial H/\partial p_i$, $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q^i$. The two are not just dual statements about the same dynamics — they are the same statement about the same trajectory, viewed first on $TQ$ and then on $T^*Q$, with the Legendre transform providing the bijection. In the phase-space form, the integrand $p\, dq - H\, dt$ is a $1$-form on $T^*Q \times \mathbb{R}$, and the variation can be computed using the Lie derivative directly — this is the structural advantage of the Hamiltonian picture.

- **[[Ex - Liouville Volume in the n-Dimensional Harmonic Oscillator]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For the $n$-dimensional harmonic oscillator $H = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_i(p_i^2 + \omega_i^2 q_i^2)$, compute the volume of the energy shell $\{H \leq E\}$ in phase space directly, and verify it grows as $E^n/(n!\prod_i \omega_i)$ — recovering the classical density of states. Use Liouville's theorem to argue that this volume is preserved under the dynamics, and connect the result to the quantum-mechanical degeneracy $\binom{n+E/\hbar\omega - 1}{n-1}$ of the energy levels in the isotropic case via Weyl's correspondence.

- **[[Ex - Jacobi's Principle for a Particle in a Central Potential]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- For a particle in a spherically symmetric potential $V(r)$ with conserved energy $E$, Jacobi's principle reduces the dynamics to geodesic motion in the conformal Jacobi metric $g^J_{ij} = 2(E - V(r))g_{ij}$ on the classically allowed region $E > V(r)$. Use spherical symmetry to derive Binet's equation for the orbit shape, and recover the Kepler ellipses $V(r) = -k/r$ as the geodesics of the Jacobi metric in this case. Discuss what happens at turning points where $E = V(r)$ and the conformal metric degenerates.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Hamilton–Jacobi Theory *(from Geometric Mechanics II / Classical Mechanics)*
> The variational principle leads naturally to the **Hamilton–Jacobi equation** $\partial S/\partial t + H(q, \partial S/\partial q, t) = 0$, a first-order PDE for a generating function $S(q, t)$ that produces the dynamics by canonical transformation. A complete solution $S(q, \alpha, t)$ depending on $n$ parameters $\alpha$ converts integration of Hamilton's equations into algebraic differentiation. The Hamilton–Jacobi equation is the **classical limit of the Schrödinger equation**: writing $\psi = e^{iS/\hbar}$ and taking $\hbar \to 0$ produces it as the lowest-order term, with quantum corrections appearing at order $\hbar$. It is the foundational equation behind the WKB approximation, geometric optics, and the eikonal equation.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Hofer's Geometry of Symplectomorphisms *(from Symplectic Topology)*
> The set of Hamiltonian symplectomorphisms $\mathrm{Ham}(M, \omega)$ carries a remarkable **bi-invariant metric** introduced by Hofer: the distance between two symplectomorphisms is the infimum of the "energy" $\int_0^1 (\max_M H_t - \min_M H_t)\, dt$ over all Hamiltonians $H_t$ generating a path between them. This is the only known bi-invariant metric on a group of diffeomorphisms — in stark contrast to the Lie group of all diffeomorphisms, where no bi-invariant Finsler metric exists. The diameter of $(\mathrm{Ham}(M), d_H)$ is infinite for many symplectic manifolds, and Hofer's distance is a deep symplectic invariant tied to **Floer homology** and the **Arnold conjecture** on the number of fixed points of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises and applications of this topic fall into a small handful of recurring goals, and recognizing which goal you face determines which piece of the symplectic apparatus to reach for. The most common is **deriving the equations of motion** of a specific mechanical system: given a configuration space $Q$, a kinetic energy $T$, and a potential $V$, write down the Lagrangian, perform the Legendre transform, identify the Hamiltonian as the total energy, and produce Hamilton's equations either in canonical coordinates or as the abstract relation $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$. A second is **identifying conservation laws**: given a Hamiltonian system, find quantities $f$ that satisfy $\{f, H\} = 0$, since these are exactly the constants of motion. A third is **computing or estimating phase-space volumes** — the volume of an energy shell, the symplectic area enclosed by an orbit, the volume of a region accessible to the dynamics — using Liouville's theorem to argue that the answer is invariant under evolution. A fourth is **verifying that a given map is a symplectomorphism** (a "canonical transformation" in classical language), typically by checking $\varphi^*\omega = \omega$ directly, by checking that $\varphi$ is the time-$t$ map of some Hamiltonian flow, or by writing $\varphi$ in terms of a generating function. A fifth is **recognizing a Lagrangian submanifold**: graphs of closed $1$-forms, level sets of Poisson-commuting functions, the support of asymptotic WKB solutions — Lagrangian submanifolds are everywhere in symplectic geometry, and identifying them often converts a hard problem into a routine one. Underlying all five is the meta-target of **converting a classical-mechanics calculation into a coordinate-free symplectic statement**, because the symplectic statement automatically inherits the structural theorems (Liouville, Noether via the moment map, Darboux, Hamilton–Jacobi) that finish problems in one line.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The assumptions in these problems are equally stereotyped. **A Lagrangian or Hamiltonian is given in canonical coordinates** — this is the workhorse source, because it lets Hamilton's equations be written immediately in coordinates and the Poisson brackets computed by the standard formula $\{f,g\} = \partial_{q^i}f\,\partial_{p_i}g - \partial_{p_i}f\,\partial_{q^i}g$. **A symmetry group acts on the configuration space** $Q$, lifting canonically to $T^*Q$ — this is the source for Noether's theorem in symplectic form, with the moment map producing conserved quantities, and (when the action is sufficiently free) for symplectic reduction to a smaller phase space. **The Hamiltonian is autonomous** ($\partial H/\partial t = 0$) — this is the source for energy conservation, $H$ itself becoming a constant of motion, and confines the dynamics to energy shells $\{H = E\}$; **time-dependent** Hamiltonians are handled by adding $\mathbb{R}$ to the phase space and using the contact form $p\,dq - H\,dt$. **The system is integrable** — meaning $n$ functionally independent conserved quantities in involution exist on a $2n$-dimensional phase space — this is the strongest source, producing action–angle coordinates and quasi-periodic motion on tori via the Arnold–Liouville theorem. **A Riemannian metric is given on $Q$** — this is the source for the kinetic-energy Hamiltonian $H = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{ij}p_ip_j$, whose Hamiltonian flow is the geodesic flow, and is the input to Jacobi's principle equating Hamiltonian trajectories with geodesics in a conformal metric. The recurring move is to **route a source to a target**: a Hamiltonian in coordinates routes through Hamilton's equations to the equations of motion; a symmetry routes through the moment map to a conservation law; a regular Lagrangian routes through the Legendre transform to the Hamiltonian picture; an integrable system routes through Arnold–Liouville to action–angle variables. The [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves that almost every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. The list is the operational distillation of the chapter.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Compute $X_H$ from $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ in canonical coordinates.** Given $H(q, p)$ on $T^*Q$ with $\omega = dp_i \wedge dq^i$, expand $X_H = A^i \partial_{q^i} + B_i \partial_{p_i}$ and impose $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$. The interior product gives $\iota_{X_H}(dp_i \wedge dq^i) = B_i\, dq^i - A^i\, dp_i$, and matching to $dH = (\partial H/\partial q^i)dq^i + (\partial H/\partial p_i)dp_i$ yields $B_i = \partial H/\partial q^i$ and $A^i = -\partial H/\partial p_i$, but with our sign convention the Hamiltonian vector field is $X_H = (\partial H/\partial p_i)\partial_{q^i} - (\partial H/\partial q^i)\partial_{p_i}$. *Trigger:* any concrete $H$ given in coordinates whose flow you need to write down. *Pattern:* mechanically extract $X_H$ from $H$ via the symplectic isomorphism.

2. **Use Cartan's magic formula to compute $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}$ of anything.** The Lie derivative along a Hamiltonian vector field can be computed without ever finding the flow, via $\mathcal{L}_{X_H} = d\iota_{X_H} + \iota_{X_H}d$. The two most important specializations: $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d(dH) + \iota_{X_H}(0) = 0$ (proving symplectic invariance, hence Liouville); $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}f = X_H(f) = \{f, H\}$ on functions (giving the time-derivative of an observable along the flow). [[Thm - Cartan's Magic Formula|Cartan's formula]] is the universal one-step shortcut. *Trigger:* a Lie derivative or invariance question about a form or function under a Hamiltonian flow.

3. **Check $\{f, H\} = 0$ to verify a conservation law.** Any $f \in C^\infty(M)$ whose Poisson bracket with $H$ vanishes is constant along trajectories: $df/dt = X_H(f) = -\{H, f\} = \{f, H\} = 0$. *Trigger:* a candidate conserved quantity needs verification, or a symmetry is suspected and you want to find the conserved quantity it produces. *Pattern:* compute $\{f, H\}$ in canonical coordinates and check it is identically zero on the manifold; if so, $f$ is conserved.

4. **Recognize $\omega = -d\theta$ on a cotangent bundle to make $\omega$ exact.** When the phase space is a cotangent bundle $T^*Q$, the canonical symplectic form is **exact**: $\omega = -d\theta$ with $\theta = p_i\, dq^i$. This means $\int_S \omega = \int_S d(-\theta) = -\int_{\partial S}\theta$ for any oriented surface $S$ in phase space — Stokes' theorem converts $2$-dimensional symplectic-area integrals into $1$-dimensional contour integrals. *Trigger:* you need a "symplectic area" or a flux of $\omega$ through some surface, and the surface is bounded by a known loop.

5. **Use Darboux coordinates to localize.** Around any point of any symplectic manifold, [[Thm - Darboux's Theorem|Darboux's theorem]] gives coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$ in which $\omega = \sum dp_i \wedge dq^i$, and every computation can be performed in this standard form. The slogan: "modulo a local diffeomorphism, every symplectic problem is a problem on $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ with the standard $\omega_0$." *Trigger:* a question is local in character and the abstract symplectic form is awkward; switch to Darboux coordinates and compute.

6. **Apply the Legendre transform to switch between Lagrangian and Hamiltonian pictures.** Given a regular $L(q, \dot q)$, set $p_i = \partial L/\partial \dot q^i$, solve for $\dot q = \dot q(q, p)$, and form $H(q, p) = p_i\dot q^i - L$. The Euler–Lagrange equations on $TQ$ transmute into Hamilton's equations on $T^*Q$ and vice versa. *Trigger:* a problem is more natural in one picture than the other (Lagrangian for constrained systems and field theories, Hamiltonian for conservation laws and statistical mechanics). *Pattern:* both formalisms describe the same dynamics; pick the cheaper.

7. **Identify a Lagrangian submanifold to encode a canonical transformation.** A symplectomorphism $\varphi : (M, \omega_M) \to (N, \omega_N)$ is encoded by the **graph** $\Gamma_\varphi \subset M \times N$, which is a Lagrangian submanifold of $(M \times N, \omega_N - \omega_M)$ if and only if $\varphi$ is a symplectomorphism. **Generating functions** $S(q, Q)$ for canonical transformations are the practical computational realization. *Trigger:* you need to specify a canonical transformation, or check that a given map preserves $\omega$, or construct a Hamiltonian flow from a target.

8. **Use $\omega^n$ as a volume form for phase-space integration.** The top wedge power $\omega^n/n!$ is the **Liouville (symplectic) volume form**, invariant under all Hamiltonian flows. Integrals against it — number of microstates with energy below $E$, partition functions in statistical mechanics, expectation values of observables — are automatically invariant. *Trigger:* a phase-space integral or volume needs to be computed or estimated. *Pattern:* express the integrand against $\omega^n/n!$ and use invariance to evaluate on a convenient simplification of the integration region.

9. **Reduce by a symmetry via the moment map.** When a Lie group $G$ acts on $(M, \omega)$ preserving $\omega$ and a $G$-invariant Hamiltonian $H$, the **moment map** $\mu : M \to \mathfrak{g}^*$ produces conserved quantities and the **Marsden–Weinstein quotient** $\mu^{-1}(c)/G_c$ inherits a symplectic structure on which the reduced dynamics lives. *Trigger:* a continuous symmetry of the system; *pattern:* identify $\mu$, restrict to a level set, quotient by the isotropy of $c$, and reduce dimension by $2\dim G$.

10. **Use the Jacobi identity to discover new conservation laws.** If $\{f, H\} = 0$ and $\{g, H\} = 0$, then by Jacobi $\{\{f, g\}, H\} = -\{\{g, H\}, f\} - \{\{H, f\}, g\} = 0$, so $\{f, g\}$ is *also* conserved. *Trigger:* you have two conserved quantities and want a third for free. *Pattern:* compute the Poisson bracket of any two known conserved quantities; the result is conserved.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating $\omega$ as a metric: lowering and raising indices symmetrically
> The symplectic form $\omega$ produces an isomorphism $TM \to T^*M$ by $X \mapsto \iota_X\omega$, looking superficially like the musical isomorphism $\flat$ from a [[Def - Riemannian Metric|Riemannian metric]]. The temptation is to treat $\omega$ as a metric, using it to "raise and lower indices" the way one does in Riemannian geometry — and to expect symmetric, positive-definite intuitions. But $\omega$ is **antisymmetric**, with the consequence that $\omega(X, X) = 0$ for **every** vector $X$: there is no notion of "length" of a vector and no positive-definite quadratic form attached to $\omega$. Specifically, "the contravariant version of $\iota_X\omega$" using any Riemannian metric on $M$ is *orthogonal to $X$*, not parallel. The fix: when manipulating index expressions involving $\omega$, treat it strictly as an antisymmetric form, and never assume the formulas you would use for a metric.

> [!warning] 2. Pushing forward $X_H$ along an arbitrary diffeomorphism and expecting Hamiltonian dynamics
> If $\varphi : M \to M'$ is a diffeomorphism and $X_H$ is the Hamiltonian vector field for $H$ on $(M, \omega)$, then $\varphi_*X_H$ is in general **not** a Hamiltonian vector field on $(M', \omega')$ — even if $\varphi$ is a smooth diffeomorphism and $\omega'$ is symplectic. The vector field $\varphi_*X_H$ is the Hamiltonian vector field of $\varphi_*H$ with respect to $\varphi_*\omega$, which equals $\omega'$ only if $\varphi$ is a [[Def - Symplectomorphism (Canonical Transformation)|symplectomorphism]] $\varphi^*\omega' = \omega$. The standard counterexample: $\varphi(q, p) = (q, p^3)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$, smoothly invertible, transforms the harmonic oscillator $H = \tfrac{1}{2}(q^2 + p^2)$ into something whose Hamiltonian flow (with respect to the *same* canonical $\omega$) generates non-elliptic curves — because $\varphi$ is not a symplectomorphism. The fix: only symplectomorphisms preserve Hamiltonian dynamics; check $\varphi^*\omega = \omega$ before any such transport.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming nondegenerate antisymmetric $\Rightarrow$ exists in odd dimensions
> The condition that an antisymmetric bilinear form be nondegenerate is highly restrictive: the matrix $(\omega_{ij})$ must be invertible, hence have nonzero determinant. But an antisymmetric matrix of odd size has $\det(\omega) = (-1)^{2n+1}\det(\omega) = -\det(\omega)$, forcing $\det = 0$. So **no nondegenerate antisymmetric form can exist in odd dimension** — symplectic manifolds are always even-dimensional. The illegal-but-tempting move is to try to define a "symplectic structure" on $S^3$ or $\mathbb{R}^3$ or any odd-dimensional manifold. The fix: odd-dimensional analogues of symplectic structures exist (**contact manifolds** with a maximally non-integrable hyperplane field) but require different machinery; do not try to force a symplectic form where dimension forbids it.

> [!warning] 4. Forgetting closedness: thinking nondegenerate is enough
> A nondegenerate $2$-form $\omega$ alone suffices to define $X_H$ via $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ — but **does not** make the resulting dynamics preserve volume or admit Poisson brackets satisfying Jacobi. The closedness $d\omega = 0$ is precisely what makes $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d\iota_{X_H}\omega + \iota_{X_H}d\omega = d(dH) + 0 = 0$ vanish (Liouville's theorem) and what makes the Poisson bracket satisfy the Jacobi identity. Manifolds with a nondegenerate but **non-closed** $2$-form are called **almost-symplectic**; they generally lack Liouville's theorem, lack Darboux's theorem, and lack Jacobi for the bracket. The fix: never drop the closedness axiom — it is what makes the geometry into a calculus.

> [!warning] 5. Claiming every closed nondegenerate $2$-form arises as $-d\theta$ for some $1$-form $\theta$
> On a cotangent bundle the canonical $\omega = -d\theta$ is *exact*, with $\theta$ the Poincaré $1$-form. The temptation is to expect every symplectic form to be exact. **It is not.** On a closed (compact, no boundary) symplectic manifold $M^{2n}$, $\omega^n$ is a nowhere-vanishing top form, so $\int_M \omega^n \neq 0$. If $\omega = d\theta$ were exact, then $\omega^n = d(\theta \wedge \omega^{n-1})$ would also be exact, and by Stokes $\int_M \omega^n = \int_{\partial M}(\cdots) = 0$ — contradiction. So **no closed symplectic manifold has an exact symplectic form** — including $S^2$ with its area form, $T^{2n}$ with its product form, the complex projective spaces $\mathbb{CP}^n$ with their Fubini–Study form. The fix: exactness is the case for cotangent bundles only; for general symplectic manifolds, $\omega$ defines a nonzero class in $H^2_{dR}(M)$.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this topic split into a small number of types, and recognizing the type is the opening move. The classification of the problem determines whether you reach for canonical coordinates and grind, or whether one of the structural theorems finishes the problem in a line.

If the problem **gives you a mechanical system and asks for its equations of motion**, then you are in the most common class. Begin by identifying the configuration manifold $Q$ — the space whose points label configurations of the system, taking constraints into account. Build the Lagrangian $L = T - V$ in any convenient coordinates on $Q$, with $T = \tfrac{1}{2}g_{ij}(q)\dot q^i\dot q^j$ where $g$ is the kinetic-energy metric (often Euclidean for unconstrained particles, but in general it encodes both the geometry of $Q$ and the masses). Apply the Legendre transform $p_i = \partial L/\partial \dot q^i$ to obtain the Hamiltonian $H(q, p) = T + V$, the total energy in $T^*Q$ coordinates. Then write Hamilton's equations directly: $\dot q^i = \partial H/\partial p_i$, $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q^i$. The detail to watch is whether the Legendre transform is regular (Hessian invertible); for $T = \tfrac{1}{2}g_{ij}\dot q^i\dot q^j$ the Hessian is $g_{ij}$ itself, regular if and only if $g$ is nondegenerate, which is automatic for a Riemannian metric.

If the problem **asks for conserved quantities of a given Hamiltonian system**, the strategy has two prongs. First, identify any visible symmetries — translations, rotations, time-translation — and apply Noether's theorem in its symplectic form (the moment map): each $1$-parameter symmetry group produces a function on phase space whose Poisson bracket with $H$ vanishes, hence a conserved quantity. The time-translation symmetry (when $H$ does not depend on $t$) gives back $H$ itself as conserved — this is **energy conservation**. Spatial translations give momentum components; spatial rotations give angular momentum components. Second, once you have a list of conserved quantities, **take Poisson brackets** to discover more: by the Jacobi identity, $\{f, g\}$ is conserved whenever both $f$ and $g$ are. For the angular momentum vector $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{q} \times \mathbf{p}$, the Poisson-bracket relations $\{L_i, L_j\} = \epsilon_{ijk}L_k$ give the $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ Lie algebra, and $L^2 = |\mathbf{L}|^2$ Poisson-commutes with each component — a **Casimir**, conserved on every $\mathfrak{so}(3)$-orbit. The systematic version of this is the moment-map / symplectic-reduction package: continuous symmetries produce conserved quantities, and quotienting by the symmetry produces a smaller phase space on which the reduced dynamics lives.

If the problem **involves Liouville's theorem or phase-space volume**, the strategy is always the same: invoke $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}(\omega^n/n!) = 0$ as a single move, and use the invariance of the Liouville volume to argue that the volume of any time-evolved region equals its initial volume. Concretely, compute the initial volume by some convenient means (often a Gaussian integral, a counting argument, or a direct geometric calculation) and use invariance to claim the same value at every later time. The most important application is in **statistical mechanics**, where the volume of an energy shell $\{H \leq E\}$ is computed once and stays valid throughout the dynamics. The standard trap is to forget that volume-preservation does not preclude *shape* deformation: a Hamiltonian flow can stretch and twist a phase-space region arbitrarily violently, as long as the volume is preserved. The KAM tori and stochastic regions of phase space coexist precisely because Liouville's theorem constrains only the integral, not the local structure.

If the problem **asks whether two pictures of the same system agree** — say a Lagrangian formulation versus a Hamiltonian formulation, or two different choices of canonical coordinates — the strategy is to use the **Legendre transform** (when crossing the $TQ \leftrightarrow T^*Q$ divide) or to verify a **symplectomorphism** (when comparing two coordinate systems on the same $T^*Q$). The latter usually goes: write down the supposed symplectomorphism $\varphi$ explicitly, compute $\varphi^*\omega$, and check it equals $\omega$. When the transformation is generated by a function $S(q, Q)$ — the **generating function** of the canonical transformation — the check reduces to the consistency of the partial derivatives $p_i = \partial S/\partial q^i$, $P_i = -\partial S/\partial Q^i$. The slogan: **the equivalence of the formalisms is essentially a one-line consequence of the Legendre transform**.

If the problem **involves a variational principle** — Hamilton's principle on $TQ$, Hamilton's principle in phase space, Jacobi's principle of least action — then the strategy is to set up the variation $\delta\int(\cdots)dt$ in canonical or generalized coordinates, integrate by parts to convert $\delta\dot q$ into $-d\delta q/dt$ inside the integral, and discard the boundary terms using the fixed-endpoint condition. The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations then forces the Euler–Lagrange equations. For Jacobi's principle, the calculation is more subtle because the *time* of motion varies — different paths take different times to traverse, and the variation must be done with the energy constraint $H = E$ enforced; the result is geodesic motion in the conformal metric $g^J = 2(E - V)g$.

Threading through all these is one meta-strategy: **whenever a calculation in classical mechanics looks ugly, try the symplectic formulation; whenever the symplectic formulation looks abstract, compute in Darboux coordinates.** The dance is between the coordinate-free statements ($\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$, $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$, $\{f,g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g)$) and the canonical-coordinate computations ($\dot q = \partial_p H$, $\dot p = -\partial_q H$, $\{f,g\} = \partial_q f\,\partial_p g - \partial_p f\,\partial_q g$). The two should be held simultaneously; whichever side is easier for the problem in front of you, that is the side to use. The unifying question of the chapter is: **what does the closed nondegenerate $2$-form $\omega$ on this manifold force the dynamics to look like?**

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Hamiltonian Flows are Symplectomorphisms|Hamiltonian flows preserve ω]]**: $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$, hence $(\phi^H_t)^*\omega = \omega$. This is the most-used single fact in the chapter because it is *free* — it costs one line of Cartan's formula and applies to *every* Hamiltonian flow. **Typical use:** any time a Hamiltonian flow appears, conclude that $\omega$ (and hence $\omega^n$, the Liouville volume) is invariant, without computing the flow. This is the structural reason that statistical mechanics, ergodic theory, and KAM theory can speak about long-time dynamics: the symplectic structure is preserved exactly, with no error term, by every flow generated by an energy function.

- **[[Thm - Liouville's Theorem on Phase Space Volume|Liouville's theorem]]**: phase-space volume is conserved by Hamiltonian dynamics. **Typical use:** any volume integral over a region in $T^*Q$ is invariant under the dynamics, so it can be computed at any convenient time. This is the foundational fact behind the microcanonical ensemble in statistical mechanics — equal a-priori probability on the energy shell is consistent with the dynamics because the flow preserves the volume measure on the shell. It also rules out attractors and dissipative behavior in Hamiltonian systems: no region in phase space can shrink under the flow.

- **[[Thm - Darboux's Theorem|Darboux's theorem]]**: every symplectic manifold is locally $(\mathbb{R}^{2n}, \sum dp_i \wedge dq^i)$. **Typical use:** to compute or check a local property of a symplectic manifold, pass to Darboux coordinates and reduce to the standard case. There are no local invariants to be careful about — every local computation can be done in canonical coordinates. This makes symplectic geometry profoundly different from Riemannian geometry, where curvature is a local invariant.

- **The Poisson bracket as a Lie-algebra structure on $C^\infty(M)$**: $\{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g)$, satisfying antisymmetry, Leibniz, and Jacobi. **Typical use:** detecting conservation laws ($\{f, H\} = 0 \Leftrightarrow f$ is conserved); discovering new conservation laws from old ones (Jacobi); proving the algebra of observables is a Lie algebra, hence promoting Hamiltonian mechanics to a representation-theoretic framework that connects directly to quantum mechanics. The Poisson algebra is the classical limit of the operator algebra of quantum mechanics, with $[\hat A, \hat B]/i\hbar \to \{A, B\}$.

- **The Legendre transform Lagrangian $\leftrightarrow$ Hamiltonian**: $p_i = \partial L/\partial \dot q^i$, $H = p_i\dot q^i - L$. **Typical use:** switching between the variational ($TQ$) and the Hamiltonian ($T^*Q$) picture of any mechanical system. The Lagrangian picture is preferred for constraints, field theories, and gauge theories (where the constraints have a natural variational description); the Hamiltonian picture is preferred for conservation laws, statistical mechanics, and quantization (where the Poisson algebra is the right structure). The transform exchanges them at no cost when $L$ is regular.

# Bridges

1. **[[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms|DG VIII — the calculus of forms]].** Every operation on a symplectic manifold is an operation in the calculus of differential forms. The symplectic form $\omega$ is a closed $2$-form ($d\omega = 0$); the canonical $1$-form $\theta$ on $T^*Q$ relates to $\omega$ by $\omega = -d\theta$; the Hamiltonian vector field is defined by $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$, a single application of the [[Def - Interior Product (Contraction with a Vector Field)|interior product]] from DG VIII; Liouville's theorem is one application of [[Thm - Cartan's Magic Formula|Cartan's magic formula]]; the Liouville volume $\omega^n$ is the $n$-fold wedge product. The entire chapter is what happens when the calculus of forms is applied to phase space — symplectic geometry is the calculus of $d$, $\wedge$, $\iota_X$, $\mathcal{L}_X$ specialized to manifolds equipped with a distinguished closed nondegenerate $2$-form.

2. **[[Riemannian Geometry II — Geodesics, the Exponential Map, and Variational Principles|RG II — geodesics as Hamiltonian flow]].** The geodesic equation on a Riemannian manifold $(Q, g)$ is the Hamilton's equation for the Hamiltonian $H : T^*Q \to \mathbb{R}$, $H(q, p) = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{ij}(q)p_ip_j$. The geodesic flow on $TQ$ pulls back via the metric isomorphism to the Hamiltonian flow of $H$ on $T^*Q$, with the canonical symplectic structure $\omega = -d\theta$. This identification is the geometric content of [[Ex - Geodesic Flow on a Riemannian Manifold is Hamiltonian]]. It is the simplest and most important example of a Hamiltonian system: the kinetic-energy Hamiltonian on a curved configuration space. **Jacobi's principle of least action** completes the bridge in the other direction: for an autonomous system with potential $V$ and energy $E$, the trajectories projected to $Q$ are geodesics in the conformal **Jacobi metric** $g^J_{ij} = 2(E - V)g_{ij}$. So mechanics with potential is hiding inside Riemannian geometry — every problem with energy conservation is, up to a metric conformal change, a geodesic flow.

3. **Electromagnetism and the magnetic symplectic form.** A charged particle in an electromagnetic field with vector potential $A$ has Hamiltonian $H = \tfrac{1}{2}|p - eA|^2 + e\phi$ on $T^*Q$ with the canonical $\omega$, or equivalently Hamiltonian $H = \tfrac{1}{2}|p|^2 + e\phi$ with the **twisted symplectic form** $\omega = \omega_0 + e\pi^*F$, where $F = dA$ is the field-strength $2$-form. The two are related by the gauge-equivalent symplectomorphism $(q, p) \mapsto (q, p - eA(q))$. The field strength $F$ is closed ($dF = 0$ is the homogeneous Maxwell equation), so the twisted $\omega$ is still closed and symplectic; if $F$ is exact ($F = dA$ globally) the two forms are symplectomorphic. The **Aharonov–Bohm effect** is the statement that when $F$ vanishes locally but $A$ has a nontrivial holonomy around a loop (in a non-simply-connected region), classical dynamics misses a topological invariant that quantum mechanics picks up — and the symplectic-geometric statement is that the twisted symplectic structure is symplectomorphic to the canonical one only up to a choice of trivialization with global obstructions.

4. **Quantum mechanics — geometric quantization and the canonical commutation relations.** The Poisson bracket $\{\cdot, \cdot\}$ on $C^\infty(T^*Q)$ is the **classical limit** of the commutator $[\hat A, \hat B]/i\hbar$ in quantum mechanics: $\{f, g\} = \lim_{\hbar \to 0} [\hat f, \hat g]/i\hbar$. The canonical commutation relations $[\hat q^i, \hat p_j] = i\hbar\delta^i_j$ are the quantization of $\{q^i, p_j\} = \delta^i_j$. The systematic program of **geometric quantization** attempts to build a Hilbert space and operator algebra from a symplectic manifold by choosing a **polarization** (a Lagrangian foliation), a **prequantum line bundle** (a complex line bundle with connection whose curvature is $\omega/\hbar$), and a **half-density structure**. The obstruction to a prequantum line bundle existing is precisely the **integrality of $[\omega/2\pi\hbar]$ in $H^2(M; \mathbb{Z})$** — the **Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization condition** in modern form.

5. **Statistical mechanics — the microcanonical ensemble and ergodicity.** Liouville's theorem on phase-space volume is the structural foundation of equilibrium statistical mechanics. The **microcanonical ensemble** assigns equal a-priori probability to all states on the energy shell $\Sigma_E = \{H = E\}$, with the probability measure being the **Liouville measure** $\omega^n/n! \wedge dH/|dH|$ restricted to $\Sigma_E$ and normalized. Liouville's theorem is precisely what guarantees that this measure is invariant under the dynamics — without it, the microcanonical assumption would be inconsistent. **Ergodicity** is the statistical-mechanical hypothesis that the time-average of an observable along a generic trajectory equals its Liouville-space-average on the energy shell; it is a much stronger assumption than Liouville's theorem (which is automatic) and only proved for specific systems (the Sinai billiard, the hard-sphere gas).

6. **Algebraic topology — symplectic capacity and Floer homology.** While Darboux's theorem says there are no *local* symplectic invariants, the global symplectic structure carries deep topological information. The simplest invariant beyond the dimension and the cohomology class $[\omega] \in H^2_{dR}(M)$ is the **Gromov width** (the largest $r$ such that $B^{2n}(r)$ symplectically embeds into $M$), which is **not** preserved by general volume-preserving maps — Gromov's non-squeezing theorem rules this out. The full theory of **symplectic capacities** (Hofer–Zehnder, Ekeland–Hofer, etc.) refines this into a hierarchy of invariants, and **Floer homology** (Lagrangian Floer, Hamiltonian Floer, symplectic Floer) produces graded modules whose ranks are global symplectic invariants — generalizing Morse homology by replacing functions on $M$ with action functionals on the loop space. These are at the frontier of symplectic topology and connect to **mirror symmetry**, string theory's deepest mathematical legacy.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a symplectic structure is "the geometry of conjugate variables".** The whole subject is forced by the observation that position and momentum are not interchangeable, despite both being functions on phase space — they are **conjugate** in the precise sense that $\{q^i, p_j\} = \delta^i_j$, with all other canonical brackets vanishing. The antisymmetric structure $\omega = dp_i \wedge dq^i$ is the geometric encoding of this conjugacy. Once you accept the asymmetry of $q$ and $p$ as the fundamental feature, everything else follows: nondegeneracy gives the bijection $H \mapsto X_H$; closedness gives volume preservation and the Jacobi identity; Darboux says the structure is locally rigid. The classical-mechanics formalism is the geometry of an antisymmetric pairing on phase space.

**The true name of $\omega$ is "the geometric encoding of energy-to-flow".** The textbook definition of a symplectic form — closed nondegenerate $2$-form — is the right thing to *check* but the wrong thing to *think*. The operational meaning is that $\omega$ is the structure that converts energy functions into vector fields: $H \mapsto X_H$ via $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$. So when you see "$(M, \omega)$ is symplectic", do not picture a $2$-form; picture the assignment $C^\infty(M) \to \text{Vect}(M)$ that takes each function to "the dynamics it generates". The whole subject — Hamiltonian flows, Poisson brackets, Noether's theorem, integrable systems — is the study of this assignment and its algebraic properties. The defining equation $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ is the symplectic structure made dynamical.

**Closedness $d\omega = 0$ is the structural reason everything works, and exactness on $T^*Q$ is the structural reason cotangent bundles are the natural phase spaces.** Closedness gives Liouville's theorem (via Cartan's formula), Jacobi for the Poisson bracket (a one-line computation reducing to $d\omega = 0$ in disguise), and Darboux's theorem (Moser's argument relies essentially on $d\omega_t = 0$ throughout the deformation). Exactness on $T^*Q$ — $\omega = -d\theta$ — gives extra structure: the symplectic-area integrals $\int_S \omega$ reduce by Stokes to contour integrals $-\int_{\partial S}\theta$, the action integral $\int p\, dq$ acquires geometric meaning as a contour integral of $\theta$, and the variational principle on $T^*Q$ uses $p\, dq - H\, dt$ as a $1$-form whose closedness on the dynamical trajectories *is* Hamilton's equations.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: whenever a symmetry exists, compute its moment map.** Continuous symmetries in classical mechanics produce conserved quantities by Noether's theorem, but the symplectic version makes this systematic. The reaction has three steps: (a) identify the Lie group $G$ acting on $M$ preserving $\omega$, (b) for each $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$, find the function $\mu^\xi$ whose Hamiltonian vector field generates the action of $\xi$, and (c) assemble $\mu : M \to \mathfrak{g}^*$ as the moment map. Then $\{\mu^\xi, H\} = 0$ for any $G$-invariant $H$, and the components of $\mu$ are conserved. Translations give linear momentum; rotations give angular momentum; gauge symmetries give the Gauss-law constraints. The moment map is the universal conservation-law-producing machine of symplectic mechanics.

**Inheritance: symplectic structure on $T^*Q$ inherits from the structure of $Q$ being a manifold (no extra data needed).** Most differential-geometric structures require an additional choice: a metric for Riemannian geometry, a connection for parallel transport, an orientation for integration. But the symplectic structure on the cotangent bundle $T^*Q$ inherits entirely from $Q$ being a smooth manifold: the tautological $1$-form $\theta$ is canonically defined by the universal property $\theta_\alpha(v) = \alpha(d\pi(v))$, no choices made. So **every smooth manifold's cotangent bundle is canonically symplectic, with no input beyond the smooth structure**. This is why cotangent bundles are the universal phase spaces of mechanics — they are forced by the configuration space alone.

**The symplectic-vs-Riemannian dichotomy: local flexibility versus local rigidity.** Riemannian geometry has rich local invariants — sectional curvature, Ricci tensor, scalar curvature — and two Riemannian manifolds are locally isomorphic only when their curvature tensors agree pointwise. Symplectic geometry has **no local invariants** — by Darboux, every $(M^{2n}, \omega)$ is locally $(\mathbb{R}^{2n}, \omega_0)$. The interesting structure is entirely *global*: cohomology class of $\omega$, symplectic capacity, Floer homology. This is the structural reason that symplectic topology (Gromov non-squeezing, Floer theory, Lagrangian intersections) looks more like algebraic topology than like differential geometry — it is, fundamentally, a topological subject with an underlying differential structure rather than a geometric subject with topology layered on top.
