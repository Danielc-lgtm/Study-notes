# Notation

$(M, \omega)$ is a symplectic manifold of dimension $2n$. $\omega^n := \omega \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega$ ($n$ factors) is the top wedge power; $\omega^n/n!$ is the **Liouville volume form** (the $n!$ normalization makes the formula compatible with the standard Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ in Darboux coordinates). $H$ is a Hamiltonian, $X_H$ the Hamiltonian vector field, $\phi^H_t$ the Hamiltonian flow.

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Liouville).** Let $(M, \omega)$ be a symplectic manifold of dimension $2n$ and let $H : M \to \mathbb{R}$ be a smooth (possibly time-dependent) Hamiltonian. The Hamiltonian flow $\phi^H_t$ preserves the **Liouville volume form** $\omega^n/n!$:
> $$\big(\phi^H_t\big)^* \frac{\omega^n}{n!} = \frac{\omega^n}{n!} \quad \text{for all } t.$$
> Equivalently, $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}(\omega^n/n!) = 0$.
>
> **In Darboux coordinates** $(q^i, p_i)$ where $\omega = \sum_i dp_i \wedge dq^i$, the Liouville volume is $\omega^n/n! = dq^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dq^n \wedge dp_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dp_n$, the standard Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$. The theorem says that any region $R \subset M$ evolves under the Hamiltonian flow with **constant volume**: $\mathrm{Vol}(\phi^H_t(R)) = \mathrm{Vol}(R)$ for all $t$.

---

# Motivation

Liouville's theorem is the **foundational geometric fact of classical statistical mechanics**. It says that an ensemble of states — a region in phase space — evolves under Hamiltonian dynamics as if it were an **incompressible fluid**: the volume is preserved, even though the shape can deform wildly. This is the geometric meaning of "Hamiltonian systems are conservative", and it is the foundation of the microcanonical ensemble, of the equal-probability postulate on energy shells, and of equilibrium statistical mechanics.

The role of Liouville's theorem in classical mechanics is structural and universal:

1. **Phase-space volume is conserved** — there are no attractors in Hamiltonian dynamics. This rules out the kinds of asymptotic simplification (steady states, limit cycles) that dissipative systems exhibit. Hamiltonian dynamics maintains its full $(2n)$-dimensional richness for all time.

2. **The microcanonical ensemble is consistent.** Assigning equal a-priori probability to all states on an energy shell $\{H = E\}$ requires the resulting measure to be invariant under the dynamics — otherwise the ensemble would evolve in time, contradicting the equilibrium assumption. Liouville guarantees this invariance.

3. **Poincaré recurrence.** Volume preservation on a compact phase space implies that almost every orbit returns arbitrarily close to its initial point infinitely often — the **Poincaré recurrence theorem**. The recurrence is dynamical, not statistical, and it underlies many of the foundational paradoxes (Loschmidt's reversibility, Zermelo's recurrence) and resolutions in statistical mechanics.

4. **Symplectic integrators.** Numerical simulation of Hamiltonian systems benefits from discretizations that preserve a discrete analogue of the Liouville volume — **symplectic integrators**. They have superior long-time stability because they automatically respect volume preservation, while non-symplectic methods accumulate spurious drift.

Liouville's theorem is named after Joseph Liouville, who proved it in 1838 for the special case of $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ with the standard symplectic form. The modern symplectic formulation — valid on any symplectic manifold, with the elegant proof via Cartan's magic formula — is due to the synthesis of symplectic geometry in the 20th century.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening).**

The hypothesis is "you have a Hamiltonian flow on a symplectic manifold". Several non-obvious situations turn out to be Hamiltonian, hence to satisfy Liouville's theorem.

**Source: any incompressible flow on $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ with $\omega_0 = \sum dp_i \wedge dq^i$.** A divergence-free vector field on $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ (in the Euclidean-divergence sense) is the same as a vector field preserving the Lebesgue measure. The bridge: in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ the standard volume form is also the Liouville form. If furthermore the divergence-free vector field is *Hamiltonian* (which on simply connected $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ is automatic for any symplectic vector field), then Liouville's theorem applies — and the Hamiltonian is the "stream function" of the flow. *Example use:* 2D incompressible inviscid fluid flow is Hamiltonian, with the stream function as the Hamiltonian and Liouville's theorem as the conservation of vortex line transport.

**Source: any flow on a cotangent bundle generated by the geodesic Hamiltonian $H = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{ij}p_ip_j$.** This includes the **geodesic flow** on a Riemannian manifold, viewed as a Hamiltonian flow on the cotangent bundle. Liouville's theorem then says the geodesic flow preserves the Liouville measure on $T^*M$, with consequences for ergodic theory of geodesic flows (e.g., on negatively curved manifolds, the geodesic flow is ergodic with respect to this measure by Anosov's theorem). *Example use:* the **billiard system on a planar region** is essentially a geodesic flow on a manifold with corners, and Liouville's theorem applied to it gives the equal-probability distribution that statistical-mechanics calculations of billiard properties rest on.

**Source: any time-dependent Hamiltonian flow.** Liouville's theorem holds even for $H = H(q, p, t)$ — the time-dependence does not affect symplectic-form preservation, hence does not affect volume preservation. *Example use:* a charged particle in a time-varying electromagnetic field has time-dependent Hamiltonian dynamics; Liouville's theorem still applies, with consequences for kinetic theory in plasma physics.

**Source: any moment-map-reduced symplectic system.** When a Hamiltonian system has a symmetry group $G$ with moment map $\mu$, the reduced phase space $M//G = \mu^{-1}(0)/G$ inherits a symplectic structure and the reduced dynamics is Hamiltonian. Liouville's theorem applies to the reduced flow, giving conservation of the reduced phase-space volume. *Example use:* rotational symmetry reduction of a planetary system reduces by 2 dimensions (energy + angular momentum), and Liouville's theorem on the reduced space gives the statistical mechanics of constrained planetary motions.

**Targets (Output Amplification).**

The conclusion "phase-space volume is preserved by the flow" has wide-ranging consequences when combined with other facts.

**Target + ergodic hypothesis = equal-time and equal-energy averages.** If a Hamiltonian system is **ergodic** on each energy shell (an additional assumption beyond Liouville), then time averages equal Liouville-space averages on the energy shell. This is the central postulate of equilibrium statistical mechanics: the microcanonical ensemble (equal probability on energy shells) gives the same predictions as time-averaging an actual trajectory. Liouville alone does not give ergodicity, but it gives the invariant measure with respect to which ergodicity is asked. *Combination use:* derive the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution from microscopic dynamics via the microcanonical-canonical relationship.

**Target + Birkhoff ergodic theorem = recurrence and statistical predictions.** Birkhoff's ergodic theorem applied to the Liouville measure (assuming finite volume) gives the precise statement that time-averages equal space-averages for almost every initial condition, provided the system is ergodic. *Combination use:* justification of statistical-mechanics predictions for systems where ergodicity can be proven (the hard-sphere gas in some regimes; the Sinai billiard).

**Target + Poincaré recurrence = recurrence on bounded regions.** Volume preservation on a compact region (or, more generally, on a region of finite Liouville measure) forces orbits to return arbitrarily close to their initial conditions infinitely often. *Combination use:* explanation of (in fact resolution of) the apparent contradiction between microscopic time-reversibility and macroscopic irreversibility.

**Target + KAM theory = persistence of invariant tori.** For an integrable Hamiltonian system, the phase space is foliated by invariant tori (action-angle picture), and Liouville's theorem ensures the measure-preserving structure of this foliation. **KAM theory** studies what happens to these tori under a small perturbation of the Hamiltonian: most survive (in a measure-theoretic sense) with the perturbed dynamics remaining quasi-periodic. The Liouville volume is the underlying invariant measure with respect to which KAM "most tori survive" is formulated. *Combination use:* long-term stability of the solar system, with the planetary orbits being slightly perturbed KAM tori.

# Why Is It True

**The mechanism in one sentence:** *Liouville's volume preservation follows from the symplectic form preservation by taking the top wedge power, and the symplectic form preservation follows from Cartan's magic formula and the closedness $d\omega = 0$.*

Here is the calculation. We have already shown ([[Thm - Hamiltonian Flows are Symplectomorphisms]]) that $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$ via Cartan's formula. To get the volume conservation, take the top wedge power:

$$\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega^n = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \omega^i \wedge \mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega \wedge \omega^{n - i - 1} = 0,$$

using the Leibniz rule for $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$. So $\omega^n$ is preserved by the flow. Dividing by the constant $n!$ does not affect preservation: $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}(\omega^n/n!) = 0$, and $(\phi^H_t)^*(\omega^n/n!) = \omega^n/n!$.

**In Darboux coordinates,** $\omega^n/n!$ takes the explicit form $dq^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dq^n \wedge dp_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dp_n$ — the standard Lebesgue volume on $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$. So Liouville's theorem in Darboux coordinates is the statement that **the Lebesgue measure on phase space is preserved by Hamilton's equations**. This is the most concrete formulation, and it is what is typically taught in physics courses.

**Why the $1/n!$?** The wedge $\omega^n = (dp_1 \wedge dq^1 + \dots + dp_n \wedge dq^n)^n$ expands by the multinomial theorem with $n!$ terms all equal to $dp_1 \wedge dq^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dp_n \wedge dq^n$. So $\omega^n = n! \,dp_1 \wedge dq^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dp_n \wedge dq^n$, and dividing by $n!$ gives the standard volume form. The $1/n!$ is the conventional normalization that makes $\omega^n/n!$ equal to the standard Euclidean volume (up to the sign convention for ordering coordinates).

**For divergence-free flows in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$:** the Lebesgue measure is preserved iff $\mathrm{div}\,X = 0$. For a Hamiltonian vector field $X_H = (\partial_p H)\partial_q - (\partial_q H)\partial_p$, the divergence is $\partial_q(\partial_p H) - \partial_p(\partial_q H) = 0$ (by equality of mixed partials, Schwarz's theorem). So **Hamiltonian vector fields are divergence-free**, which is the direct (and most elementary) proof of Liouville's theorem in coordinates. The geometric proof via Cartan's formula is more general (works on any symplectic manifold) and reveals the deeper structural reason: closedness of $\omega$ + nondegeneracy + definition of $X_H$ all conspire to give volume preservation.

**Why does volume preservation matter physically?** Because phase-space volume is the right notion of "size of a region in state space", and a dynamics that preserves it cannot have asymptotic simplification: the trajectory must "fill up" any region it visits (in an averaged sense, by recurrence). This is the fundamental reason Hamiltonian dynamics is **conservative** — energy, volume, and a whole host of invariants are simultaneously preserved.

# What Makes This Hard

The proof is trivial once Cartan's formula and the closedness of $\omega$ are in hand — it's essentially a corollary of [[Thm - Hamiltonian Flows are Symplectomorphisms]]. The conceptual difficulty is appreciating the *consequences*: that volume preservation rules out attractors, forces recurrence, justifies the microcanonical ensemble, and underlies symplectic integrators. The other common stumbling block is computational: writing out $\omega^n/n!$ in coordinates and verifying it equals the standard Lebesgue volume requires careful bookkeeping of wedge signs. Many students initially confuse the $1/n!$ normalization or get the sign of the volume form wrong.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Hamiltonian flows preserve $\omega$
> **Statement:** $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$ for any smooth $H : M \to \mathbb{R}$ on $(M, \omega)$.
>
> **Hint:** Cartan's magic formula: $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d\iota_{X_H}\omega + \iota_{X_H}d\omega = d(dH) + 0 = 0$.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the foundation. Without it, $\omega^n$ would not be preserved either.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > See [[Thm - Hamiltonian Flows are Symplectomorphisms]] for the proof. The computation: by Cartan, $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d(\iota_{X_H}\omega) + \iota_{X_H}(d\omega) = d(dH) + \iota_{X_H}(0) = 0$, using the defining property $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$, the closedness $d\omega = 0$, and $d^2 = 0$.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Leibniz rule for Lie derivative
> **Statement:** For vector field $X$ and forms $\alpha, \beta$, $\mathcal{L}_X(\alpha \wedge \beta) = \mathcal{L}_X\alpha \wedge \beta + \alpha \wedge \mathcal{L}_X\beta$.
>
> **Hint:** The Lie derivative is a derivation of degree $0$ on the wedge algebra.
>
> **Why needed:** Lets us extend $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$ to $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega^n = 0$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > The Lie derivative is defined via the flow: $\mathcal{L}_X\omega := (d/dt)|_{t=0}\phi_t^*\omega$. Pullback is a homomorphism of the wedge algebra: $\phi_t^*(\alpha \wedge \beta) = \phi_t^*\alpha \wedge \phi_t^*\beta$. Differentiating in $t$ at $t = 0$ and using the product rule:
> > $$\mathcal{L}_X(\alpha \wedge \beta) = \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t = 0}(\phi_t^*\alpha \wedge \phi_t^*\beta) = \mathcal{L}_X\alpha \wedge \beta + \alpha \wedge \mathcal{L}_X\beta.$$
> > (No sign issues because $\mathcal{L}_X$ is degree-preserving.)

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Leibniz iterated gives $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega^n = 0$
> **Statement:** $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega^n = 0$.
>
> **Hint:** Iterate Leibniz $n - 1$ times; each term involves $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$.
>
> **Why needed:** Gives the Liouville-volume-form preservation.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > By induction on $n$. For $n = 1$: $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$ from Lemma 1. For $n \geq 2$: by Lemma 2,
> > $$\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega^n = \mathcal{L}_{X_H}(\omega \wedge \omega^{n-1}) = \mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega \wedge \omega^{n-1} + \omega \wedge \mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega^{n-1} = 0 \wedge \omega^{n-1} + \omega \wedge 0 = 0,$$
> > using Lemma 1 and the inductive hypothesis.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $(M, \omega)$ be a symplectic manifold of dimension $2n$ and let $H : M \to \mathbb{R}$ be a smooth function with Hamiltonian vector field $X_H$.
>
> **Step 0 — Well-posedness.** Verify that $\omega^n/n!$ is well-defined and nonvanishing. Since $\omega$ is a nondegenerate $2$-form, $\omega^n$ is a nowhere-zero top form (this is the standard fact that "$\omega^n$ is a volume form on every symplectic manifold"). Dividing by $n!$ is just a constant scaling. So $\omega^n/n!$ is a well-defined volume form on $M$, called the **Liouville volume**.
>
> **Step 1 — Symplectic preservation.** By [[Thm - Hamiltonian Flows are Symplectomorphisms]] (Lemma 1), $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$. The proof is the three-line Cartan calculation:
> $$\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d(\iota_{X_H}\omega) + \iota_{X_H}(d\omega) = d(dH) + 0 = 0.$$
>
> **Step 2 — Top wedge preservation.** By Lemma 3, $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega^n = 0$. The proof is induction on $n$ using Leibniz for $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}$.
>
> **Step 3 — Liouville form preservation.** Dividing by $n!$:
> $$\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\frac{\omega^n}{n!} = \frac{1}{n!}\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega^n = 0.$$
>
> **Step 4 — Flow preservation.** Since $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}(\omega^n/n!) = 0$, the standard relation between Lie derivative and flow gives
> $$(\phi^H_t)^*\frac{\omega^n}{n!} = \frac{\omega^n}{n!}$$
> for all $t$ for which the flow is defined. (Proof: $(d/dt)(\phi^H_t)^*(\omega^n/n!) = (\phi^H_t)^*\mathcal{L}_{X_H}(\omega^n/n!) = 0$, so the pullback is constant in $t$, equal to its value at $t = 0$.)
>
> **Step 5 — Geometric interpretation.** For any measurable region $R \subset M$,
> $$\mathrm{Vol}(\phi^H_t(R)) = \int_{\phi^H_t(R)} \frac{\omega^n}{n!} = \int_R (\phi^H_t)^*\frac{\omega^n}{n!} = \int_R \frac{\omega^n}{n!} = \mathrm{Vol}(R),$$
> using the change-of-variables formula and the preservation result. So the **volume of any region is preserved** by the Hamiltonian flow.
>
> **Step 6 — Coordinate verification.** In Darboux coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$ where $\omega = \sum dp_i \wedge dq^i$, compute $\omega^n/n!$ explicitly. By the multinomial expansion (noting that the only non-vanishing terms have one factor from each summand),
> $$\omega^n = n!\,dp_1 \wedge dq^1 \wedge dp_2 \wedge dq^2 \wedge \cdots \wedge dp_n \wedge dq^n = n!\,(-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\,dq^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dq^n \wedge dp_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dp_n,$$
> where the sign comes from reshuffling $(dp_1, dq^1, dp_2, dq^2, \dots)$ into the standard ordering $(dq^1, \dots, dq^n, dp_1, \dots, dp_n)$. So $\omega^n/n! = (-1)^{n(n-1)/2}dq^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dq^n \wedge dp_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dp_n$ — the **standard Lebesgue volume** on $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ (up to the sign, which is a choice of orientation). In this language Liouville's theorem reads: **Hamilton's equations preserve the standard Lebesgue measure on phase space**.
>
> $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Statistical mechanics: equipartition and ergodic theory.** Liouville's theorem is the geometric foundation of the microcanonical ensemble. Combine it with **Birkhoff's ergodic theorem**: if the Hamiltonian flow is ergodic on the energy shell $\Sigma_E = \{H = E\}$ (which is a nontrivial additional assumption), then time-averages of observables along generic trajectories equal phase-space averages with respect to the **Liouville measure on $\Sigma_E$**, defined as $\omega^n/n! \wedge dH/|dH|$ restricted to $\Sigma_E$. This is the precise statement of the **equal a-priori probability postulate** of equilibrium statistical mechanics: equal probability per unit Liouville-area on the energy shell. For specific systems (Sinai billiard, hard-sphere gas in some regimes) ergodicity has been proved; for most physically relevant systems it is assumed.

**Plasma physics: Vlasov equation.** The Vlasov equation $\partial_t f + v \cdot \nabla_x f + (q/m)(E + v \times B)\cdot \nabla_v f = 0$ governs the one-particle distribution function $f(x, v, t)$ of a collisionless plasma. It can be reformulated as a **continuity equation in phase space** for the volume preserved by the single-particle Hamiltonian flow. Liouville's theorem is exactly what makes the Vlasov equation a continuity equation — i.e., what allows the phase-space density $f$ to be defined and to satisfy $df/dt = 0$ along Hamiltonian trajectories.

**Numerical analysis: symplectic integrators preserve discrete volume.** A symplectic integrator preserves the symplectic form exactly (at each timestep), hence preserves the discrete Liouville volume. **Verlet's leap-frog scheme** is the simplest example: it preserves $\omega$ in the sense that the discrete-time map $(q_n, p_n) \to (q_{n+1}, p_{n+1})$ is a symplectomorphism in the canonical $\omega$. This means long-time numerical simulation does not exhibit spurious volume drift, with major consequences for the simulation of integrable systems, KAM tori, and the long-term solar-system stability problem.

**Information theory: entropy in Hamiltonian systems.** Define the entropy of a probability distribution $\rho$ on phase space by $S[\rho] = -\int \rho \log \rho \, dV_L$ with $dV_L = \omega^n/n!$. **Liouville's theorem implies entropy is conserved** under Hamiltonian flow: $dS/dt = 0$ if $\rho$ evolves by the Hamiltonian dynamics. This is the **classical version** of the unitarity of quantum mechanics (which preserves von Neumann entropy). The apparent contradiction with the second law of thermodynamics (entropy increase) is resolved by **coarse-graining**: when phase space is divided into macroscopic cells and the entropy is computed from the coarse-grained density, that entropy can increase even though the fine-grained (Liouville) entropy is conserved.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Hamiltonian Flows are Symplectomorphisms]]**: this is the immediate ancestor. Liouville's theorem is the corollary obtained by taking the top wedge power $\omega^n$ and using the Leibniz rule for the Lie derivative. The bridge: symplectic-form preservation ($\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$) implies symplectic-volume preservation ($\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega^n = 0$). The deeper statement is symplectic preservation; volume preservation is a derived corollary.

- **[[Thm - Darboux's Theorem]]**: in Darboux coordinates, $\omega^n/n!$ becomes the standard Lebesgue measure $dq^1 \cdots dq^n dp_1 \cdots dp_n$. So Liouville's theorem in Darboux coordinates is the elementary fact "Hamilton's equations are divergence-free, so they preserve the Lebesgue measure". The bridge: Darboux's theorem says the *local form* of any Hamiltonian dynamics is Hamilton's equations on $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$, and Liouville on that local form is a one-line divergence calculation.

- **Birkhoff's ergodic theorem** (from ergodic theory): for an ergodic measure-preserving transformation, time-averages equal space-averages almost everywhere. Applied to the Liouville-measure-preserving Hamiltonian flow on an energy shell (when ergodic), Birkhoff gives the central postulate of statistical mechanics. The bridge: Liouville provides the *invariant measure* needed to even state ergodicity; Birkhoff then provides the consequence (time = space averages) modulo the *additional* assumption of ergodicity.

- **Poincaré recurrence theorem**: volume preservation on a compact phase space (or on a region of finite measure) forces almost every orbit to return arbitrarily close to its initial point. The proof: cover the region by small balls; orbits emanating from one ball, by volume preservation, cannot all "escape forever" without filling up another ball, so they must return. **The recurrence theorem is the dynamical consequence of Liouville's theorem on finite-measure regions.**

- **Convergence of statistical-mechanics ensembles**: in the canonical ensemble at temperature $T$, the equilibrium distribution is the Boltzmann distribution $\rho \propto e^{-H/k_B T}$. This is preserved by the Hamiltonian flow: $d\rho/dt = -\rho \{H/k_B T, H\}/\rho = 0$, by antisymmetry of the Poisson bracket. So the canonical ensemble is an invariant distribution of the dynamics — consistent with equilibrium. Liouville's theorem is what makes "the Boltzmann distribution is an equilibrium" a meaningful statement: without volume preservation, even an explicitly time-independent density would not be a dynamical equilibrium.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Poincaré Recurrence Theorem *(from Ergodic Theory)*
> Volume preservation on a region of finite Liouville measure (in particular, on a compact region) implies the **Poincaré recurrence theorem**: for any open neighbourhood $U$ of a point $p$ and any time $T$, there exists $t > T$ such that $\phi^H_t(p) \in U$ for almost every $p$ in any positive-measure region. Almost every orbit returns arbitrarily close to its initial point infinitely often. The proof: if the orbit of $p$ never returned to $U$ for $t > T$, the images $\phi^H_T(U), \phi^H_{2T}(U), \dots$ would all be disjoint, but they all have the same finite measure (by Liouville), violating finiteness of the total measure. **Recurrence times are typically astronomical** ($e^N$ for $N$-particle systems), so recurrence is consistent with practical irreversibility on human timescales, but the principle is exact.

> [!tip] No Attractors in Hamiltonian Dynamics *(from Dynamical Systems)*
> Volume preservation rules out attractors: a region cannot have smaller volume than its basin of attraction (which would be required for an attractor). So Hamiltonian dynamics cannot exhibit the asymptotic simplification of dissipative systems — no fixed-point attractors, no limit cycles, no strange attractors with fractional dimension less than the ambient. This makes Hamiltonian dynamics simultaneously more "conservative" (preserves more) and more "complex" (no asymptotic simplification). The deep dynamical structures that *do* arise in Hamiltonian dynamics — KAM tori, stable and unstable manifolds of hyperbolic orbits, chaotic regions, Arnold diffusion — all coexist within the volume-preserving constraint.

> [!tip] Symplectic Integrators in Computational Physics *(from Numerical Analysis)*
> Numerical simulation of Hamiltonian systems benefits enormously from discretizations that preserve a discrete analogue of the symplectic form (hence of Liouville volume). **Verlet's leap-frog**, the **Forest–Ruth fourth-order symplectic integrator**, and the **Yoshida sixth-order method** are widely used. They have the structural property that the *exact* solution of a slightly perturbed Hamiltonian system gives the *numerical* trajectory — so all qualitative features (KAM tori, energy conservation up to a tiny perturbation, recurrence) are preserved over exponentially long times. Non-symplectic integrators (general Runge–Kutta methods) accumulate spurious energy drift and qualitatively distort the dynamics over long times. This is why N-body simulations of the solar system, plasma simulations, and molecular dynamics universally use symplectic integrators — Liouville's theorem is built into the discretization.
