# Notation Registry

A standing convention for this topic: **a connection on a principal $G$-bundle $P \to M$ is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form $\omega \in \Omega^1(P; \mathfrak{g})$ on the total space, not on the base.** This is the cleanest, most invariant formulation, and it is what makes the theory globally well defined. The "gauge potential" $A_\alpha$ used by physicists is the *pullback* of $\omega$ to the base along a local section $s_\alpha : U_\alpha \to P$, so $A_\alpha = s_\alpha^* \omega$ is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form on the open set $U_\alpha \subseteq M$, depending on the choice of section. Different sections give different $A_\alpha$, related by the gauge-transformation law $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$. A second convention concerns the bracket on $\mathfrak{g}$-valued forms: we follow Frankel and write the **curvature 2-form** as $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$. For matrix groups, $\tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega] = \omega \wedge \omega$ (the matrix wedge product), so the formula reads $\Omega = d\omega + \omega \wedge \omega$ — both versions appear in the literature and they agree on matrix groups. A third convention: the **Maurer-Cartan equation** has the convention-dependent sign $d\theta_G + \tfrac{1}{2}[\theta_G, \theta_G] = 0$ for left-invariant $\theta_G$; the right-invariant version differs by a sign.

> [!warning] Convention — left-invariance and the Maurer-Cartan sign
> The Maurer-Cartan form $\theta_G = g^{-1}dg$ is the **left-invariant** Maurer-Cartan form on $G$, satisfying $d\theta_G + \tfrac{1}{2}[\theta_G, \theta_G] = 0$. The right-invariant form $\tilde\theta_G = dg\,g^{-1}$ satisfies $d\tilde\theta_G - \tfrac{1}{2}[\tilde\theta_G, \tilde\theta_G] = 0$ — opposite sign. Frankel and Kobayashi-Nomizu use left-invariance throughout, and so do we.

- $M, N$ — smooth manifolds; $\dim M = n$
- $G$ — a Lie group with [[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group|Lie algebra]] $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$
- $P \to M$ — a principal $G$-bundle with right action $R_g : P \to P$, $p \mapsto p \cdot g$
- $\pi : P \to M$ — the projection
- $V_p P = \ker(d\pi_p) \subset T_p P$ — the **vertical subspace** at $p \in P$ (tangent to the fibre)
- $H_p \subset T_p P$ — the **horizontal subspace** at $p$; in general not canonical, depends on the connection
- $\xi^* \in \mathfrak{X}(P)$ — the **fundamental vector field** associated to $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$: $\xi^*_p = \tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} p \cdot \exp(t\xi)$
- $\mathrm{Ad} : G \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$, $\mathrm{Ad}_g(\xi) = g\xi g^{-1}$ — the [[Def - Adjoint Representation|adjoint representation]] of $G$ on $\mathfrak{g}$
- $\theta_G = g^{-1}dg \in \Omega^1(G; \mathfrak{g})$ — the **left-invariant Maurer-Cartan form** on $G$
- $\Omega^p(M; \mathfrak{g}) = \Omega^p(M) \otimes \mathfrak{g}$ — the space of **$\mathfrak{g}$-valued $p$-forms** on $M$ (equivalently, $\Omega^p(M; \mathfrak{g}) = \Gamma(\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes (M \times \mathfrak{g}))$)
- $[\alpha, \beta] \in \Omega^{p+q}(M; \mathfrak{g})$ — the **bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$-valued forms**: $[\alpha \otimes \xi, \beta \otimes \eta] = (\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes [\xi, \eta]$
- $d : \Omega^p(M; \mathfrak{g}) \to \Omega^{p+1}(M; \mathfrak{g})$ — the **exterior derivative** on $\mathfrak{g}$-valued forms, $d(\alpha \otimes \xi) = (d\alpha) \otimes \xi$
- $\omega \in \Omega^1(P; \mathfrak{g})$ — a **connection 1-form** on $P$
- $A_\alpha = s_\alpha^*\omega \in \Omega^1(U_\alpha; \mathfrak{g})$ — the **local connection 1-form** (gauge potential) corresponding to a section $s_\alpha : U_\alpha \to P$
- $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega] \in \Omega^2(P; \mathfrak{g})$ — the **curvature 2-form** on $P$
- $F_\alpha = s_\alpha^*\Omega = dA_\alpha + \tfrac{1}{2}[A_\alpha, A_\alpha] \in \Omega^2(U_\alpha; \mathfrak{g})$ — the **local curvature** (field strength)
- $\rho : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$ — a representation of $G$ on a vector space $V$
- $P \times_\rho V$ or $E_\rho$ — the **associated vector bundle** with fibre $V$
- $\mathrm{Ad}\,P = P \times_{\mathrm{Ad}} \mathfrak{g}$ — the **adjoint bundle**, the associated bundle for the adjoint representation
- $\Omega^r(M; E) = \Gamma(\Lambda^r T^*M \otimes E)$ — the space of **$E$-valued $r$-forms** (also called **$r$-form sections** of $E$)
- $d_\nabla : \Omega^r(M; E) \to \Omega^{r+1}(M; E)$ — the **exterior covariant derivative** on $E$-valued forms
- $d_\omega$ or $d_A$ — the same operator written for principal/local connections

The base notation for manifolds, forms, bundles, Lie groups, and the Lie bracket lives on the source topic pages [[Differential Geometry V — Vector Fields, Flows, and the Lie Bracket|DG V]], [[Differential Geometry VI — Vector Bundles and the Cotangent Bundle|DG VI]], [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms|DG VIII]], and [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|DG XI]]; only the gauge-theoretic additions are listed above.

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: a connection on a principal $G$-bundle is the data needed to differentiate fields valued in any representation of $G$ in a way that respects the symmetry $G$ — and the curvature of the connection is the field strength.

To see why this matters, recall the picture from [[Gauge Theory I — Connections on Vector Bundles and the Electromagnetic Connection|Gauge Theory I]]. There the gauge field $A$ appeared as a $\mathfrak{u}(1) \cong i\mathbb{R}$-valued 1-form on the base $M$, the electromagnetic potential, with the field strength $F = dA$ a $\mathfrak{u}(1)$-valued 2-form. That worked beautifully for electromagnetism because $U(1)$ is abelian — the gauge transformation $A \mapsto A + d\chi$ involves only the abelian group structure, and the curvature $F = dA$ is exact and gauge-invariant in a clean way. But the moment one tries to do **non-abelian gauge theory** — the strong nuclear interaction with structure group $SU(3)$, the weak interaction with $SU(2)$, the unified electroweak with $SU(2) \times U(1)$, or Yang-Mills with any non-abelian $G$ — this picture breaks. Two new phenomena appear that have no counterpart in the abelian case. First, the gauge potential $A$ takes values in $\mathfrak{g}$, a non-commutative Lie algebra, and the gauge transformation law acquires an extra **non-linear term** $g^{-1}Ag$ from the conjugation action of $G$ on $\mathfrak{g}$ — the Adjoint representation. Second, the field strength is no longer $dA$ alone but $F = dA + \tfrac{1}{2}[A, A] = dA + A \wedge A$ for matrix groups, where the wedge of the Lie-algebra-valued 1-form with itself is in general nonzero. The extra term $A \wedge A$ is what couples the gauge field to itself: gluons interact with gluons, $W$ and $Z$ bosons interact with each other. Photons do not, because $U(1)$ is abelian and $[A, A] = 0$ identically.

The clean way to organise all of this — abelian and non-abelian uniformly, and with the gauge transformation law made geometrically transparent — is to **move from the base $M$ to the total space $P$ of a principal $G$-bundle**, and to define the connection as a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form on $P$, not on $M$. The local potentials $A_\alpha$ are then *consequences* of $\omega$: they are the pullbacks of $\omega$ along local sections. Different sections give different pullbacks, and the gauge transformation law is automatic from this — it is the statement of how a pullback changes under change of section. This is the **Ehresmann perspective** on connections, the global geometric reformulation that has been the standard since the 1950s and that is essential for all modern gauge theory.

The plan of the chapter follows a precise four-stage arc. Section §3.1 develops the machinery of **Lie-algebra-valued differential forms**: how to take their exterior derivative, how to bracket two of them, and the canonical example of the [[Def - The Maurer-Cartan Form|Maurer-Cartan form]] on a Lie group, which satisfies the [[Thm - Maurer-Cartan Equation|Maurer-Cartan equation]] $d\theta + \tfrac{1}{2}[\theta, \theta] = 0$. This last equation is *the* template — every curvature formula in gauge theory is a deformation of it. Section §3.2 defines the central object: a [[Def - Connection 1-Form on a Principal Bundle|connection 1-form]] $\omega$ on a principal $G$-bundle, the two axioms (equivariance and verticality) that define it, and the equivalent [[Def - Horizontal Subspace|horizontal-distribution]] picture (Ehresmann's geometric perspective). We extract the local potentials $A_\alpha$ as pullbacks under sections and derive the [[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Local Connection 1-Forms|gauge transformation law]] $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$ as a clean consequence — the same formula that appeared by direct manipulation in [[Gauge Theory I — Connections on Vector Bundles and the Electromagnetic Connection|Gauge Theory I]] now derived from a single global geometric source. Section §3.3 defines the [[Def - Curvature 2-Form on a Principal Bundle|curvature 2-form]] $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$ — the [[Thm - Cartan Structural Equation for Principal Connections|Cartan structural equation]] — and proves the [[Thm - Bianchi Identity for Principal Connections|Bianchi identity]] $d_\omega\Omega = 0$. Section §3.4 returns to representations: any representation $\rho : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$ produces an **associated vector bundle** $P \times_\rho V$, and the principal connection $\omega$ [[Thm - Principal Connection Induces a Connection on Every Associated Bundle|induces a connection]] on every such bundle. The most important special case is the [[Def - Adjoint Bundle|adjoint bundle]] $\mathrm{Ad}\,P = P \times_{\mathrm{Ad}} \mathfrak{g}$, in which the curvature lives as a 2-form section: the field strength $F \in \Omega^2(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)$ is the global, gauge-invariant home of the local field strengths $F_\alpha$.

$$
\text{principal bundle } P \to M \;\xrightarrow{\text{pick }\omega}\; \text{connection 1-form on } P \;\xrightarrow{\text{pullback}}\; \text{gauge potentials } A_\alpha \;\xrightarrow{\Omega = d\omega + \frac{1}{2}[\omega,\omega]}\; \text{curvature } F \in \Omega^2(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)
$$

The unifying frame is that **the gauge field is the connection, the field strength is its curvature, and gauge invariance is the change-of-section freedom in the principal bundle**. Once one believes this picture, everything in classical Yang-Mills is forced: the dynamical equations come from extremising an action built from $F$, the conserved currents come from Noether's theorem applied to the gauge symmetry, and the topological structure (instantons, monopoles, $\theta$-vacua) comes from the bundle topology one is allowed to take. The full Yang-Mills dynamics — equations of motion, instantons, the BPST solution — belongs to [[Gauge Theory IV — Yang–Mills Fields and Instantons|Gauge Theory IV]]; this chapter delivers the *kinematics*, the geometric setup on which the dynamics runs.

The reader is assumed to have worked through [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms|DG VIII]] (differential forms and the exterior derivative), [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|DG XI]] (Lie groups, Lie algebras, the adjoint representation, the exponential map), and the prior Gauge Theory chapters: [[Gauge Theory I — Connections on Vector Bundles and the Electromagnetic Connection|Gauge Theory I]] (vector-bundle connections and the abelian case), [[Gauge Theory II — Principal Bundles, Representations, and Bundle Classification|Gauge Theory II]] (the bundles themselves, frame bundles, structure groups). Familiarity with [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation|Riemannian Geometry I]] is useful because the Levi-Civita connection is the prototype of all this machinery — it is the principal connection on the $O(n)$-frame bundle of a Riemannian manifold, and the [[Thm - Cartan Structural Equation for Principal Connections|Cartan structural equation]] specialises exactly to the structural equations of Riemannian geometry.

# Concept Map

## §3.1 Lie-Algebra-Valued Forms and the Maurer-Cartan Form

- **[[Def - Lie-Algebra-Valued Differential Form]]**
	- A $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $p$-form on a manifold $M$ is a smooth section of $\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes \mathfrak{g}$, equivalently an element of $\Omega^p(M) \otimes \mathfrak{g}$; if $\{E_R\}$ is a basis of $\mathfrak{g}$, every such form decomposes as $\varphi = E_R \otimes \varphi^R$ where each $\varphi^R$ is an ordinary $p$-form. Evaluated on tangent vectors, $\varphi(X_1, \ldots, X_p)$ is an element of $\mathfrak{g}$. The exterior derivative extends linearly: $d(E_R \otimes \varphi^R) = E_R \otimes d\varphi^R$, since the basis $E_R$ is constant. The space $\Omega^p(M; \mathfrak{g})$ is the natural home of every gauge-theoretic object in this chapter.

- **[[Def - Bracket of g-Valued Forms]]**
	- The bracket of two $\mathfrak{g}$-valued forms combines the Lie bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$ with the wedge product of forms: $[\alpha \otimes \xi, \beta \otimes \eta] = (\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes [\xi, \eta]$, extended bilinearly. Symmetry is graded: $[\beta, \alpha] = -(-1)^{pq}[\alpha, \beta] \cdot (\text{sign from antisymmetry of }\mathfrak{g}\text{-bracket})$, which works out to $[\beta, \alpha] = (-1)^{pq+1}[\alpha, \beta]$. For matrix groups, $\tfrac{1}{2}[\alpha, \alpha]$ equals the matrix wedge $\alpha \wedge \alpha$ when $\alpha$ is a 1-form, the key identity that lets the Cartan structural equation be written as $\Omega = d\omega + \omega \wedge \omega$ in matrix notation. Leibniz: $d[\alpha, \beta] = [d\alpha, \beta] + (-1)^p[\alpha, d\beta]$.

- **[[Def - The Maurer-Cartan Form]]**
	- On a Lie group $G$, the **left-invariant Maurer-Cartan form** $\theta_G \in \Omega^1(G; \mathfrak{g})$ is the unique $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form satisfying $\theta_G(X)|_g = (dL_{g^{-1}})_g(X)$ for $X \in T_g G$ — it left-translates each tangent vector back to the identity, where it becomes an element of $\mathfrak{g}$. Equivalently, if $X^R$ are the left-invariant vector fields obtained by left-translating a basis $E_R$ of $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$, and $\sigma^R$ are the dual left-invariant 1-forms ($\sigma^R(X^S) = \delta^R_S$), then $\theta_G = E_R \otimes \sigma^R$. For matrix groups, $\theta_G = g^{-1}dg$ — the notation that gives the form its other common name. The right-invariant version $dg\,g^{-1}$ satisfies an analogous equation with the opposite bracket sign.

- **[[Thm - Maurer-Cartan Equation]]**
	- The Maurer-Cartan form $\theta_G$ on a Lie group $G$ satisfies $d\theta_G + \tfrac{1}{2}[\theta_G, \theta_G] = 0$. This is the universal template for the structural equation of every connection: the curvature 2-form of the trivial flat connection on $G \to \mathrm{pt}$ is precisely $\Omega = d\theta_G + \tfrac{1}{2}[\theta_G, \theta_G]$, which the Maurer-Cartan equation says vanishes. Every curvature in gauge theory is a *deformation* of this template — the Cartan structural equation $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$ has the same shape, but for a general connection on a non-trivial bundle the right-hand side need not be zero. The proof is a direct computation from $d\sigma^R = -\tfrac{1}{2}C^R_{ST}\sigma^S \wedge \sigma^T$ (the dual structure constant identity) together with the definition of the bracket of forms.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Cartan Geometry *(from Differential Geometry)*
> A **Cartan geometry** modelled on a homogeneous space $G/H$ is a principal $H$-bundle equipped with a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form that pointwise mimics the Maurer-Cartan form. The Maurer-Cartan equation $d\theta + \tfrac{1}{2}[\theta, \theta] = 0$ holds in the flat (Klein geometry) case; its failure for a general Cartan geometry is the curvature. This unifies Riemannian geometry (Cartan geometry on $\mathbb{R}^n = ISO(n)/O(n)$), conformal geometry, projective geometry, and parabolic geometries into a single framework. **Élie Cartan's** mid-twentieth-century program was the unification of these geometries under the single rubric of Maurer-Cartan-style structural equations.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Lie Algebra Cohomology *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> The Maurer-Cartan equation defines the cohomology of $\mathfrak{g}$ with coefficients in a $\mathfrak{g}$-module — the **Chevalley-Eilenberg complex**. The de Rham cohomology of a compact Lie group $G$ equals the cohomology of left-invariant forms, equals the cohomology of the Maurer-Cartan complex on $\mathfrak{g}^*$. So computing $H^\bullet(G; \mathbb{R})$ reduces to pure Lie algebra. This is the bridge to the **Chern-Weil theory** that constructs characteristic classes from invariant polynomials on $\mathfrak{g}$ — the topological invariants of principal bundles.

## §3.2 Principal Connections

- **[[Def - Fundamental Vector Field of a Principal Bundle]]**
	- For a principal $G$-bundle $P \to M$ with right action $R_g$ and an element $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$, the **fundamental vector field** $\xi^* \in \mathfrak{X}(P)$ is defined by $\xi^*_p = \tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} R_{\exp(t\xi)}(p) = \tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} p \cdot \exp(t\xi)$. Geometrically it is the infinitesimal generator of the right-translation flow by $\exp(t\xi)$, and is tangent to the fibre of $\pi$ at every point — that is, $\xi^*_p \in V_p P = \ker(d\pi_p)$. The map $\xi \mapsto \xi^*_p$ is a linear isomorphism $\mathfrak{g} \xrightarrow{\sim} V_p P$ — the **vertical-space isomorphism** — because the right action is free and transitive on each fibre. This identification is what makes the verticality axiom $\omega(\xi^*) = \xi$ in the definition of a connection well posed.

- **[[Def - Connection 1-Form on a Principal Bundle]]**
	- A **connection** on a principal $G$-bundle $P \to M$ is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form $\omega \in \Omega^1(P; \mathfrak{g})$ satisfying two axioms. **(i) Equivariance:** $R_g^*\omega = \mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\omega$ for all $g \in G$, that is, right-translating a tangent vector by $g$ and then evaluating $\omega$ gives the same result as evaluating $\omega$ first and then conjugating the answer by $g^{-1}$. **(ii) Verticality:** $\omega(\xi^*) = \xi$ for every $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$ — the form reproduces the Lie-algebra element that generates each fundamental vector field. Together these axioms make $\omega$ an "isomorphism on vertical directions, equivariant on the rest"; the kernel $H_p := \ker\omega_p$ is the **horizontal subspace**, and the decomposition $T_p P = V_p P \oplus H_p$ is the geometric content of the connection.

- **[[Def - Horizontal Subspace]]**
	- For a connection $\omega$ on a principal $G$-bundle $P$, the **horizontal subspace** at $p \in P$ is $H_p = \ker\omega_p \subseteq T_p P$. The collection $\{H_p\}_{p \in P}$ is a **horizontal distribution**: a smooth $G$-equivariant rank-$n$ ($n = \dim M$) subbundle of $TP$ that is everywhere transverse to the vertical: $T_p P = V_p P \oplus H_p$. Equivariance means $(R_g)_* H_p = H_{p \cdot g}$ — the right action carries horizontal vectors to horizontal vectors. The projection $d\pi_p$ restricts to an isomorphism $H_p \xrightarrow{\sim} T_{\pi(p)} M$, so a horizontal vector is *uniquely* determined by its image on the base and the basepoint $p$. This is the geometric heart of a connection: it gives a canonical way to lift each tangent vector on the base to a horizontal vector on $P$, the **horizontal lift**.

- **[[Thm - Principal Connection is Equivalent to a Horizontal Distribution]]**
	- Specifying a connection 1-form $\omega \in \Omega^1(P; \mathfrak{g})$ satisfying equivariance and verticality is *equivalent* to specifying a $G$-equivariant horizontal distribution $H \subset TP$ — a smooth assignment of an $n$-dimensional subspace $H_p \subset T_p P$ at each $p$, transverse to the vertical and carried into itself by the right action. The bijection is $\omega \mapsto \ker\omega$ in one direction and "let $\omega$ vanish on $H$ and be the vertical-space isomorphism on $V$" in the other. This is the bridge between the **algebraic** (1-form) and **geometric** (distribution) perspectives on connections: the 1-form perspective is computationally clean and the bracket/curvature formulas drop out; the distribution perspective is conceptually clean and parallel transport becomes "lift the curve horizontally". Modern accounts use both interchangeably.

- **[[Def - Local Connection 1-Form (Gauge Potential)]]**
	- For a connection $\omega$ on $P \to M$ and a local section $s_\alpha : U_\alpha \to P$ over an open set $U_\alpha \subseteq M$, the **local connection 1-form** (also called the **gauge potential** in physics) is $A_\alpha := s_\alpha^*\omega \in \Omega^1(U_\alpha; \mathfrak{g})$. This is the down-on-the-base avatar of the global connection: it is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form on the open patch $U_\alpha$, depending explicitly on the choice of section. Two different sections $s_\alpha, s_\beta$ over the same open set produce different $A_\alpha, A_\beta$, related by the gauge transformation law $A_\beta = g^{-1}A_\alpha g + g^{-1}dg$ where $g : U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \to G$ is the transition function $s_\beta = s_\alpha \cdot g$. The collection $\{A_\alpha\}$ on a trivialising open cover plus the transition data is equivalent to the connection $\omega$ on the total space.

- **[[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Local Connection 1-Forms]]**
	- For a connection $\omega$ on $P \to M$ and two local sections $s_\alpha, s_\beta$ over an open set $U \subseteq M$ related by $s_\beta(x) = s_\alpha(x) \cdot g(x)$ for a smooth $g : U \to G$, the corresponding gauge potentials $A_\alpha = s_\alpha^*\omega, A_\beta = s_\beta^*\omega$ are related by the **gauge transformation law** $A_\beta = g^{-1} A_\alpha g + g^{-1}dg = \mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}} A_\alpha + g^* \theta_G$. The "inhomogeneous" term $g^{-1}dg$ is the pullback of the Maurer-Cartan form along $g$; the "homogeneous" term is the adjoint action of $g^{-1}$. This is the master gauge-transformation law that appears in physics, and it explains *why* the gauge potential $A$ is not itself a tensor: its inhomogeneous transformation is what makes the covariant derivative $\nabla = d + A$ transform correctly.

> [!tip] Unlocked: BRST Cohomology *(from Quantum Field Theory)*
> In the quantisation of non-abelian gauge theory, gauge transformations are reinterpreted as **BRST symmetry**: a fermionic nilpotent operator $Q$ with $Q^2 = 0$ whose action on $A$ reproduces $g^{-1}dg$ at the infinitesimal level. The BRST cohomology classifies physical observables — those that are $Q$-closed modulo $Q$-exact — and the master gauge transformation law $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$ is the integrated form of $Q A = -d c - [A, c]$ for a ghost field $c$. The Faddeev-Popov ghosts of quantum gauge theory live in the adjoint bundle and exist precisely because $A$ transforms inhomogeneously — they are the price of gauge-fixing.

## §3.3 Curvature and the Bianchi Identity

- **[[Def - Curvature 2-Form on a Principal Bundle]]**
	- The **curvature 2-form** of a connection $\omega$ on a principal $G$-bundle $P \to M$ is $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega] \in \Omega^2(P; \mathfrak{g})$ — the **Cartan structural equation**. For matrix groups this reads $\Omega = d\omega + \omega \wedge \omega$. Two key properties: $\Omega$ is **horizontal** ($\Omega(X, Y) = 0$ if either $X$ or $Y$ is vertical) and **equivariant** ($R_g^*\Omega = \mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\Omega$). These properties together mean $\Omega$ descends to a 2-form section of the adjoint bundle, $F \in \Omega^2(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)$ — the global, geometrically invariant **field strength**. Local computation: $F_\alpha = s_\alpha^*\Omega = dA_\alpha + \tfrac{1}{2}[A_\alpha, A_\alpha]$. The Maurer-Cartan equation is the special case "flat connection on a trivial bundle".

- **[[Thm - Cartan Structural Equation for Principal Connections]]**
	- For a connection $\omega$ on a principal $G$-bundle $P \to M$, the curvature 2-form $\Omega := d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$ is horizontal and equivariant, hence descends to a section $F \in \Omega^2(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)$. In any local trivialisation determined by a section $s : U \to P$, $F|_U = s^*\Omega = dA + \tfrac{1}{2}[A, A]$, with $A = s^*\omega$. This is the **non-abelian field strength** of Yang-Mills theory. The two properties (horizontality, equivariance) are derived from the connection axioms by direct computation, and they are precisely what is needed for $\Omega$ to be a tensor on the base — unlike $\omega$ itself, which is not, since its pullback $A$ transforms inhomogeneously. The structural equation is consistent with the [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation#Concept Map|vector-bundle structural equation]] $\Omega^a{}_b = d\omega^a{}_b + \omega^a{}_c \wedge \omega^c{}_b$ of [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation|Riemannian Geometry I]] via the associated bundle construction: the connection on the associated bundle $P \times_\rho V$ has curvature $d\rho(\Omega)$ in matrix form, where $d\rho : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ is the differential of $\rho$.

- **[[Thm - Bianchi Identity for Principal Connections]]**
	- For a connection $\omega$ on a principal $G$-bundle $P \to M$ with curvature $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$, the **Bianchi identity** holds: $d_\omega\Omega = d\Omega + [\omega, \Omega] = 0$. Equivalently, $d_\nabla F = 0$ as 3-form section of $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$, where $\nabla$ is the induced connection on $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$ and $d_\nabla$ is the exterior covariant derivative. In components for a matrix group: $dF + A \wedge F - F \wedge A = 0$, which expands to $d_\mu F_{\nu\rho} + d_\rho F_{\mu\nu} + d_\nu F_{\rho\mu} = 0$ in covariant-derivative language. The abelian special case $G = U(1)$ reduces to $dF = 0$ — half of Maxwell's equations (the "geometric" half: $\nabla \cdot B = 0$ and Faraday's law). For non-abelian $G$, the extra $[\omega, \Omega]$ term couples the gauge field to its own curvature. The proof is a one-line computation: differentiate the structural equation, use $d^2 = 0$, use the graded Leibniz of the bracket.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Yang-Mills Equations *(from Yang-Mills Theory)*
> The Yang-Mills equation $d_\omega \star \Omega = 0$ is the *second* half of gauge theory: it is the dynamical equation, derived from extremising the Yang-Mills action $S[\omega] = -\tfrac{1}{2}\int_M \mathrm{tr}(F \wedge \star F)$ on the space of connections. Together with the Bianchi identity $d_\omega \Omega = 0$ (which is a *geometric identity*, true for every connection), $d_\omega \star \Omega = 0$ generalises Maxwell's equations to non-abelian gauge theory. For self-dual connections in four dimensions ($\Omega = \star\Omega$), Bianchi implies Yang-Mills automatically — this is the **instanton equation**, the central object of [[Gauge Theory IV — Yang–Mills Fields and Instantons|Gauge Theory IV]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Chern-Simons Forms *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> For a $U(n)$- or $SU(n)$-connection in three dimensions, the **Chern-Simons 3-form** $CS(\omega) = \mathrm{tr}(\omega \wedge d\omega + \tfrac{2}{3}\omega \wedge \omega \wedge \omega)$ has the property that $d\,CS(\omega) = \mathrm{tr}(\Omega \wedge \Omega)$ — the Chern-Weil characteristic class. The Chern-Simons functional $\int_{M^3} CS(\omega)$ is a gauge-invariant action (modulo integers) and defines a 3-dimensional topological field theory whose partition function on $M^3$ is the **Witten invariant** of the 3-manifold, related to the Jones polynomial of knots. The same Chern-Simons forms appear in the **WZW model** of 2D conformal field theory and in the construction of **Chern characters** in K-theory.

- **[[Ex - Computing the Curvature in Two Different Gauges]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For a non-abelian connection $A = A_a^\mu \sigma_a\,dx^\mu$ on Minkowski space, compute $F = dA + \tfrac{1}{2}[A, A]$ in components: $F^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A^a_\nu - \partial_\nu A^a_\mu + \varepsilon^a{}_{bc}A^b_\mu A^c_\nu$. Then perform a finite gauge transformation by a smooth map $g : \mathbb{R}^4 \to SU(2)$ and verify that $F$ transforms in the adjoint representation, while $A$ transforms inhomogeneously.

- **[[Ex - Bianchi Identity for the Electromagnetic Field is dF = 0]]** (⭐)
	- Specialise the principal Bianchi identity $d_\omega\Omega = 0$ to the abelian case $G = U(1)$, and verify it reduces to $dF = 0$, which in components $F = \tfrac{1}{2}F_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu \wedge dx^\nu$ on Minkowski space gives Faraday's law $\nabla \times E + \partial_t B = 0$ together with $\nabla \cdot B = 0$.

## §3.4 Induced Connections on Associated Bundles

- **[[Def - Exterior Covariant Derivative on Associated Bundles]]**
	- For an associated vector bundle $E = P \times_\rho V$ with induced connection $\nabla = d + d\rho(A)$ in any local trivialisation, the **exterior covariant derivative** $d_\nabla : \Omega^r(M; E) \to \Omega^{r+1}(M; E)$ is the unique extension of $\nabla$ to $E$-valued forms satisfying the graded Leibniz rule $d_\nabla(\alpha \wedge \psi) = d\alpha \wedge \psi + (-1)^p \alpha \wedge d_\nabla\psi$ for $\alpha \in \Omega^p(M)$ and $\psi \in \Omega^q(M; E)$. Locally, $d_\nabla \psi = d\psi + d\rho(A) \wedge \psi$ — the same formula as the ordinary $\nabla$, with the wedge replacing the usual product. For $E = \mathrm{Ad}\,P$, $d\rho(A) = \mathrm{ad}(A) = [A, \cdot]$, and the exterior covariant derivative is $d_\nabla \psi = d\psi + [A, \psi]$ — the formula that appears in the Bianchi identity. The square is $d_\nabla^2 \psi = d\rho(F) \wedge \psi$ — curvature obstructs $d_\nabla$ from being a chain map, an exact analogue of $R(X, Y) = \nabla_X \nabla_Y - \nabla_Y \nabla_X - \nabla_{[X, Y]}$ on the level of forms.

- **[[Def - Adjoint Bundle]]**
	- The **adjoint bundle** of a principal $G$-bundle $P \to M$ is the associated vector bundle $\mathrm{Ad}\,P := P \times_{\mathrm{Ad}} \mathfrak{g}$, where $G$ acts on $\mathfrak{g}$ via the adjoint representation $\mathrm{Ad}_g \xi = g\xi g^{-1}$. Fibres are copies of $\mathfrak{g}$; transition functions are $\mathrm{Ad}(c_{\alpha\beta}) : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$. The adjoint bundle is the *intrinsic* home of the curvature: $\Omega$ defines a global 2-form section $F \in \Omega^2(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)$ even though the local $A_\alpha$ do not glue into a global object. For $G = U(1)$, the adjoint representation is trivial ($U(1)$ is abelian), so $\mathrm{Ad}\,P = M \times \mathbb{R}$ is the trivial line bundle and the electromagnetic field strength is a globally defined ordinary 2-form. For non-abelian $G$, $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$ is generically a non-trivial vector bundle, and the field strength is genuinely a section of a non-trivial bundle.

- **[[Thm - Principal Connection Induces a Connection on Every Associated Bundle]]**
	- Given a principal connection $\omega$ on $P \to M$ and a representation $\rho : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$, there is a canonical induced connection $\nabla^\rho$ on the associated vector bundle $E = P \times_\rho V$. In any local trivialisation (determined by a section $s_\alpha$ of $P$), $\nabla^\rho$ acts on sections $\psi : U_\alpha \to V$ by $\nabla^\rho \psi = d\psi + d\rho(A_\alpha)\psi$, where $d\rho : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(V) = \mathrm{End}(V)$ is the Lie-algebra representation differential of $\rho$. The curvature of $\nabla^\rho$ is $d\rho(F)$, where $F$ is the curvature of $\omega$. For $\rho = \mathrm{Ad}$ this gives the connection on $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$, with covariant derivative $\nabla\psi = d\psi + [A, \psi]$; for the defining representation of $U(1)$ on $\mathbb{C}$ it gives the electromagnetic covariant derivative $\nabla\psi = d\psi + iA\psi$; for the spinor representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ it gives the **spinor covariant derivative** used in the Dirac equation on a curved spin manifold (see [[Spinors and the Dirac Equation]]).

> [!tip] Unlocked: Holonomy of a Principal Connection *(from Differential Geometry)*
> The **holonomy group** $\mathrm{Hol}(\omega) \subseteq G$ of a connection $\omega$ at a point $p \in P$ is the subgroup of $G$ obtained by parallel-transporting $p$ along all loops in $M$ based at $\pi(p)$ — the result is $p \cdot g$ for some $g \in G$, and these $g$'s form a subgroup. The **Ambrose-Singer theorem** states that the Lie algebra of $\mathrm{Hol}^0(\omega)$ (the connected component) is the linear span of all curvature values $\Omega(X, Y)$ at all points: holonomy is generated by curvature. This is the most precise expression of the slogan "curvature is the obstruction to parallel transport being path-independent". **Flat connections** ($\Omega = 0$) have discrete holonomy, classified by representations $\pi_1(M) \to G$ — this is the bridge to Galois theory in covering-space form.

- **[[Ex - Adjoint Bundle of a U(1)-Bundle is Trivial]]** (⭐)
	- For an arbitrary $U(1)$-bundle $P \to M$, show that the adjoint bundle $\mathrm{Ad}\,P = P \times_{\mathrm{Ad}} \mathfrak{u}(1)$ is the trivial line bundle $M \times i\mathbb{R}$, since $U(1)$ acts trivially on its own Lie algebra (abelian groups have trivial adjoint action). Conclude that the electromagnetic field strength $F$ is a globally defined real 2-form on $M$ — a feature that fails completely in the non-abelian case.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring exercises and theorems in this topic pursue a small handful of canonical goals. The first is **to compute a local connection 1-form** (gauge potential) in coordinates, given either a global $\omega$ and a section, or a specific physical situation (electromagnetic field, spin connection on a curved manifold, principal bundle reduction). The second is **to compute the curvature** $F = dA + \tfrac{1}{2}[A, A]$ in components, typically in matrix-group form $F^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A^a_\nu - \partial_\nu A^a_\mu + f^a{}_{bc}A^b_\mu A^c_\nu$, and to recognise that the non-abelian term $f^a{}_{bc}A^b A^c$ is what makes the theory self-interacting. The third is **to verify a Bianchi identity** $d_\omega F = 0$ — either as a sanity check or as the abelian special case $dF = 0$ that gives Maxwell's geometric equations. The fourth is **to identify the appropriate associated bundle** for a given matter field (the defining rep of $U(1)$ gives a complex line bundle for the wave function; the defining rep of $SU(3)$ gives a colour-triplet bundle; the spinor rep gives a spinor bundle), and to write down the induced covariant derivative. The fifth is **to compute the effect of a gauge transformation** on $A$ and $F$ — verifying that $F$ transforms in the adjoint representation while $A$ acquires the inhomogeneous $g^{-1}dg$ term. The unifying observation is that *every concrete calculation in classical gauge theory is some version of "pull back $\omega$ along a section, then transform"*, and the work is in keeping track of which side of $P \to M$ you are on.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses split into a small number of stereotyped patterns. **A principal bundle is given**, often constructed from physical data: the frame bundle of a manifold, the bundle of phases of a quantum wave function, the bundle of internal-symmetry frames. **A representation $\rho$ of the structure group is given**, dictating which matter fields can be coupled (the strong force uses the defining rep of $SU(3)$, the weak force the defining rep of $SU(2) \times U(1)$, gravity the spin rep of $\mathrm{Spin}(1,3)$). **A local trivialisation is given**, via a section $s_\alpha$, allowing the global $\omega$ to be pulled back to a concrete $A_\alpha$ on a chart. **Two local trivialisations are given**, related by a transition $g : U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \to G$, allowing the gauge transformation law to be invoked. **A connection 1-form $\omega$ is given on $P$**, in either the algebraic (a 1-form satisfying the two axioms) or the geometric (a horizontal distribution) form, allowing the curvature and the Bianchi identity to be computed. **A flat connection is given** ($\Omega = 0$ identically), which by Ambrose-Singer has discrete holonomy and is classified by a representation $\pi_1(M) \to G$ — the bridge to algebraic topology and to instanton number / topological charge. The routing is always: source data → identify which side of $P \to M$ → choose local trivialisation → reduce to ordinary differential forms on $M$ valued in $\mathfrak{g}$ → use the matrix-group bracket identity to compute $dA + A \wedge A$.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in the topic routes through. When stuck, scan and try each one.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Pull back a global connection or curvature along a section.** Given $\omega \in \Omega^1(P; \mathfrak{g})$ and a local section $s : U \to P$, the local gauge potential is $A = s^*\omega \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{g})$, and the local field strength is $F = s^*\Omega = dA + \tfrac{1}{2}[A, A]$. *Trigger:* you have a global geometric object on $P$ and want a coordinate-friendly expression on $M$. *Pattern:* substitute the explicit section $s(x) = (x, g(x))$ in a local trivialisation, then $s^*\omega = g^{-1}\omega_{\text{can}}g + g^{-1}dg$ where $\omega_{\text{can}}$ is the canonical horizontal 1-form on the trivial bundle.

2. **Apply the gauge transformation law $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$.** This converts between two choices of section. *Trigger:* a problem becomes hard in one gauge but easy in another (e.g., compute curvature where $A = 0$ — this is the "Coulomb gauge" for non-abelian theories). *Pattern:* identify the symmetry of the problem, then choose $g$ to align with it; reverse-engineer the gauge transformation that takes $A$ to a simpler form, then invert at the end.

3. **Use the structural equation $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$.** *Trigger:* you need the curvature, either to compute it from $\omega$ or to use its known horizontality and equivariance. *Pattern:* in matrix-group form, $\Omega = d\omega + \omega \wedge \omega$; in components, $F^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A^a_\nu - \partial_\nu A^a_\mu + f^a{}_{bc}A^b_\mu A^c_\nu$. The non-abelian term $f^a{}_{bc}A^b A^c$ vanishes iff $\mathfrak{g}$ is abelian (electromagnetism) or $A$ commutes with itself component-wise.

4. **Use the Bianchi identity $d_\omega\Omega = 0$.** *Trigger:* you have curvature in hand and want to extract a differential constraint. *Pattern:* for abelian, $dF = 0$ — the geometric Maxwell equations. For non-abelian, $dF + [A, F] = 0$ — equivalently, $d_\nabla F = 0$ as a 3-form section of $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$. The Bianchi is a *geometric identity*, not a dynamical equation; it constrains every connection.

5. **Decompose a tangent vector on $P$ into vertical and horizontal parts.** Given a connection $\omega$, every $X \in T_p P$ splits as $X = X^V + X^H$ with $X^V = \omega(X)^*$ (the fundamental vector field of the Lie-algebra value) and $X^H \in \ker\omega_p$. *Trigger:* anything involving parallel transport, horizontal lifts, holonomy, or geometric versus algebraic computations. *Pattern:* "the horizontal part of $X$ is $X - \omega(X)^*$" — write it down once and use it repeatedly.

6. **Horizontally lift a curve $\gamma$ on $M$ to a horizontal curve $\tilde\gamma$ on $P$.** Given $\gamma : I \to M$ and a starting point $p_0 \in \pi^{-1}(\gamma(0))$, there is a unique curve $\tilde\gamma : I \to P$ with $\pi \circ \tilde\gamma = \gamma$, $\tilde\gamma(0) = p_0$, and $\dot{\tilde\gamma}(t) \in H_{\tilde\gamma(t)}$ for all $t$. *Trigger:* parallel transport (the value $\tilde\gamma(1)$ is the parallel transport of $p_0$ along $\gamma$, in $P$ — and on associated bundles via the representation). *Pattern:* solve the linear ODE $\dot g(t) = -A(\dot\gamma(t)) g(t)$ with $g(0) = e$ in a local trivialisation, then $\tilde\gamma(t) = s(\gamma(t)) \cdot g(t)$.

7. **Use the induced connection $\nabla^\rho = d + d\rho(A)$ on an associated bundle.** *Trigger:* a matter field $\psi$ valued in a representation $V$ appears; its dynamics requires a covariant derivative. *Pattern:* identify $\rho$, compute $d\rho : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ on a basis, and write $\nabla\psi = d\psi + d\rho(A)\psi$. For the adjoint representation, $d\rho = \mathrm{ad}$ and $\nabla\psi = d\psi + [A, \psi]$.

8. **Recognise an object as a horizontal equivariant form, hence a form on the base valued in an associated bundle.** A $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $r$-form $\varphi$ on $P$ that is horizontal ($\varphi(X) = 0$ for $X$ vertical in any slot) and equivariant ($R_g^*\varphi = \mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\varphi$) descends to a section of $\Omega^r(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)$. *Trigger:* any naturally-defined $\mathfrak{g}$-valued form on $P$. *Pattern:* check the two properties, then write "this descends to an $r$-form on $M$ with values in $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$" without doing the pullback explicitly — the descent is canonical.

9. **Use the affine structure of the space of connections.** The space $\mathcal{A}(P)$ of connections is affine over $\Omega^1(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)$: pick any reference connection $\omega_0$, then every other connection is $\omega = \omega_0 + \pi^*\eta$ for a unique $\eta \in \Omega^1(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)$. *Trigger:* parametrising variations of connections (e.g., for Yang-Mills action variations, for moduli space analysis, for instanton constructions). *Pattern:* the curvature of $\omega_0 + \pi^*\eta$ is $F_0 + d_{\omega_0}\eta + \tfrac{1}{2}[\eta, \eta]$ — quadratic in $\eta$, with the linear term being the covariant derivative of $\eta$.

10. **Specialise the abelian case $G = U(1)$ as a sanity check.** For $G = U(1)$, $\mathfrak{g} = i\mathbb{R}$ is abelian, $[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,] = 0$, the structural equation collapses to $F = dA$, gauge transformations become $A \mapsto A + d\chi$, and the adjoint bundle is trivial. *Trigger:* a general principal-bundle formula needs verification. *Pattern:* set $G = U(1)$, drop all bracket terms, and confirm the result reduces to the (correct) electromagnetic equation. If it does not, the general formula has an error.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating the local gauge potential $A_\alpha$ as a tensor on $M$
> The temptation is to think of $A_\alpha$ as a globally defined $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form on $M$, since it locally looks like one. It is *not*: under change of section $s' = s \cdot g$, $A$ transforms as $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$, with the inhomogeneous term $g^{-1}dg$ destroying any tensorial transformation rule. The mistake is to ignore the inhomogeneous term — e.g., to write "$A$ is a 1-form section of $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$", which is false. What *is* a section of $\Omega^1(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)$ is the *difference* $A_1 - A_2$ of two connections (the inhomogeneous term cancels), or the curvature $F$ (the inhomogeneous term contributes nothing to a 2-form built from $dA + A \wedge A$). The operation becomes legal exactly when the bundle is *globally trivialisable* and a global section is chosen (e.g., $P = M \times G$); then $A$ is genuinely a 1-form on $M$ valued in $\mathfrak{g}$, but this is the trivial-bundle case, not the general one.

> [!warning] 2. Writing $F = dA$ for a non-abelian connection
> The abelian formula $F = dA$ that worked for electromagnetism is wrong for non-abelian $G$: the correct formula is $F = dA + \tfrac{1}{2}[A, A] = dA + A \wedge A$ for matrix groups. The extra non-linear term is what gives non-abelian gauge theory its self-interaction (gluons couple to gluons; in QED, photons do not couple to photons). The temptation comes from the wish to write $F$ as an exact form globally — this is impossible because $A$ is not globally defined (illegal operation 1), and even locally $F$ is not exact unless $A \wedge A = 0$. The operation becomes legal when $G$ is abelian (so $[A, A] = 0$) or in a gauge where $A = 0$ locally (which is always possible for a flat connection but never globally for a non-trivial flat connection).

> [!warning] 3. Confusing the connection 1-form $\omega$ on $P$ with the gauge potential $A$ on $M$
> The names are similar and the symbols often interchanged, but they live in different places: $\omega \in \Omega^1(P; \mathfrak{g})$ is a 1-form on the total space, globally defined and invariantly attached to the bundle; $A \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{g})$ is a 1-form on an open patch of the base, defined only after choosing a section. Mixing them produces nonsense (e.g., "the curvature 2-form $d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$ is the field strength on $M$" — false, it is a 2-form on $P$, only its pullback to $M$ via a section is the field strength). The fix: always be explicit about whether you are working on $P$ or $M$, and use distinct symbols $\omega$ versus $A$. Physicists use $A$ exclusively because they always work in a fixed gauge; mathematicians need both because they care about gauge invariance.

> [!warning] 4. Forgetting the equivariance axiom
> The temptation is to define a connection just as "a 1-form satisfying $\omega(\xi^*) = \xi$" (verticality only), dropping equivariance. This is wrong — without equivariance, the horizontal distribution $\ker\omega$ is not $G$-equivariant, the connection does not behave well under change of trivialisation, and the gauge transformation law $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$ does not hold. The mistake leads to "connections" whose curvature does not descend to a section of $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$, breaking the whole formalism. The fix: always check both axioms. Equivariance is the non-trivial half — verticality is essentially a normalisation.

> [!warning] 5. Using the Bianchi identity to derive Yang-Mills equations
> The Bianchi identity $d_\omega F = 0$ is a *geometric identity* — it holds for every connection, by construction. The Yang-Mills equation $d_\omega \star F = 0$ is a *dynamical equation* — it holds only for connections that extremise the Yang-Mills action. They look formally similar (both involve $d_\omega$ acting on a curvature-related quantity), but the Bianchi identity is automatic and contains no physics, while Yang-Mills is the actual gauge-field equation of motion. Conflating them produces "derivations" of Yang-Mills from pure geometry, which is impossible — geometry gives the kinematics (the Bianchi identity), but the dynamics requires a Lagrangian. The operation becomes legal once one is explicit: "Bianchi is identity; Yang-Mills is dynamics; they are dual via Hodge star".

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic are won at the moment you decide which side of the projection $P \to M$ you are working on. Geometric questions about the connection itself (horizontality, equivariance, curvature, holonomy) live on $P$. Computational questions about specific gauge potentials and field strengths live on $M$, after choosing a section. The bridge between the two is the pullback $A = s^*\omega$, and most of the work in any concrete problem is in keeping track of this pullback and how it changes under change of section.

If the problem **asks to compute a curvature in components**, the route is mechanical: identify the gauge potential $A$ in a chosen gauge, then evaluate $F^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A^a_\nu - \partial_\nu A^a_\mu + f^a{}_{bc} A^b_\mu A^c_\nu$ (operation 3, [[Thm - Cartan Structural Equation for Principal Connections|structural equation]]). The two structural pieces are the abelian part $\partial_\mu A^a_\nu - \partial_\nu A^a_\mu$ (the same as in electromagnetism) and the non-abelian self-coupling $f^a{}_{bc}A^b A^c$ (zero for abelian groups). If the gauge potential vanishes in your gauge ($A = 0$), then $F = 0$ in that gauge, hence $F = 0$ everywhere by gauge covariance — this is the trivial-connection shortcut, useful for sanity checking.

If the problem **asks for the effect of a gauge transformation**, the route is to apply the [[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Local Connection 1-Forms|master law]] $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$ for the connection (operation 2). The field strength transforms in the adjoint representation: $F' = g^{-1}Fg = \mathrm{Ad}_{g^{-1}}F$. The trick is to remember that the gauge transformation acts on the *section*, not on the connection — the connection is intrinsic, only the local gauge potential changes. For specific calculations, finding a "good gauge" (one in which $A$ takes a simple form, e.g., Lorenz gauge $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$, axial gauge $A_0 = 0$, Coulomb gauge for $U(1)$) makes computations tractable; the gauge transformation law is the mechanism for moving between gauges.

If the problem **asks for parallel transport or holonomy**, the route is through the horizontal-lift construction (operations 5–6). Given a curve $\gamma$ on $M$ and a starting fibre point $p_0$, solve the linear ODE $\dot g(t) + A(\dot\gamma(t))\,g(t) = 0$ (matrix-group case) with $g(0) = e$ — this gives the parallel transport in $G$, and the formal solution is the *path-ordered exponential* $g(t) = \mathcal{P}\exp\left(-\int_0^t A(\dot\gamma(\tau))\,d\tau\right)$. For closed loops, the holonomy depends only on the homotopy class of the loop *up to curvature*; the Ambrose-Singer theorem then identifies the Lie algebra of holonomy with the span of curvature values. The path-ordered exponential is the universal formula — it specialises to the abelian $\exp\left(-i\oint A\right)$ that gives the Aharonov-Bohm phase, and to the Wilson loop of non-abelian gauge theory.

If the problem **involves an associated bundle and a matter field**, the route is through the induced connection $\nabla^\rho = d + d\rho(A)$ (operation 7). Identify the representation $\rho$ that determines the bundle, compute its differential $d\rho : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ on a basis, and write the covariant derivative of a section explicitly. The Dirac equation on a curved spacetime, the Schrödinger equation in an electromagnetic field, the colour-charge equation for a quark — all are of the form "free equation, but $\partial$ replaced by $\nabla^\rho$". The trigger is always "field $\psi$ in representation $V$ minimally coupled to gauge field $A$".

If the problem **is a structural question about the space of connections** — Yang-Mills action variations, instanton moduli, gauge-fixing slices — the route is through the affine structure (operation 9): write $\omega = \omega_0 + \pi^*\eta$ for a reference $\omega_0$ and a 1-form $\eta \in \Omega^1(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)$. The curvature expansion $F_{\omega} = F_{\omega_0} + d_{\omega_0}\eta + \tfrac{1}{2}[\eta, \eta]$ is then the cornerstone of perturbative gauge theory and instanton calculus.

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle: **the gauge field is the connection, the field strength is its curvature, and gauge invariance is the freedom of section choice in a principal bundle**. Every question in this chapter is some version of "given a principal bundle and a representation, what is the right covariant derivative, what does its curvature look like, and how does it transform under gauge changes?". The geometric setup on $P$ is invariant; everything we compute is a local shadow of it on $M$.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Cartan Structural Equation for Principal Connections|Cartan structural equation]]**: $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$. **Typical use:** this is the master formula for curvature in *every* gauge theory — abelian, non-abelian, Riemannian, conformal, Cartan-geometric. It is what one writes down once and computes from forever after. The non-abelian self-interaction term $\tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$ is the geometric origin of all gluon-gluon coupling, all $W$-$Z$ self-coupling, all instanton calculus. Recognise the structural equation as the deformation of the Maurer-Cartan equation: the Maurer-Cartan equation says the curvature of the flat trivial connection is zero; the structural equation says the curvature of a general connection is the obstruction to its being flat-trivial.

- **[[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Local Connection 1-Forms|Gauge transformation law]]**: $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$. **Typical use:** this is the law that all of gauge theory — physics and mathematics alike — is the consequence of. It explains why $A$ is not a tensor (the inhomogeneous $g^{-1}dg$), why $F$ is a tensor (the $g^{-1}dg$ cancels in $dA + \tfrac{1}{2}[A, A]$), why gauge-fixing is non-trivial (one must work modulo this group action), and why the difference of two connections is a 1-form section of $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$ (the inhomogeneous terms agree and cancel). It is the geometric content of the slogan "gauge invariance is a redundancy in description".

- **[[Thm - Bianchi Identity for Principal Connections|Bianchi identity]]**: $d_\omega F = 0$. **Typical use:** the geometric identity that *every* connection satisfies. In the abelian case it is half of Maxwell ($dF = 0$); in the non-abelian case it is the kinematic constraint $dF + [A, F] = 0$ that complements the dynamical Yang-Mills equation $d_\omega \star F = 0$. Recognising "this equation is a Bianchi identity" tells you it is automatic, contains no physics, and need not be imposed — it is true for free, by the structure of the connection. Distinguishing Bianchi from Yang-Mills is one of the most common conceptual errors and the most important to internalise.

- **[[Thm - Principal Connection Induces a Connection on Every Associated Bundle|Induced connection on associated bundles]]**: $\nabla^\rho = d + d\rho(A)$. **Typical use:** this is the bridge from the principal bundle (the abstract symmetry data) to the associated bundles (where the matter fields live). It is what makes the principal-bundle formalism *useful*: you do not need a separate connection for every matter field; the principal connection induces all of them through their representations. The same $\omega$ gives the electromagnetic covariant derivative on charged scalars, the connection on $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$ where the field strength lives, the spinor covariant derivative on the spinor bundle, and so on — one geometric object, many physical applications.

- **[[Def - Adjoint Bundle|The adjoint bundle]] $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$**: the canonical vector bundle in which the curvature lives. **Typical use:** $F \in \Omega^2(M; \mathrm{Ad}\,P)$ is the global, gauge-invariant home of the local field strengths $F_\alpha = s_\alpha^* \Omega$. Recognising this lets one talk about the field strength without choosing a gauge — essential for moduli-space arguments and for instanton calculus. For abelian $G$, $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$ is trivial and $F$ is an ordinary 2-form on $M$; for non-abelian $G$, $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$ is typically non-trivial and the topology of $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$ contributes to the topological invariants of the bundle.

# Bridges

1. **[[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation|Riemannian geometry]] — the Levi-Civita connection is the principal connection on the $O(n)$-frame bundle.** The orthonormal frame bundle $F^O(M) \to M$ of a Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$ is a principal $O(n)$-bundle, and the Levi-Civita connection — defined in [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation|Riemannian Geometry I]] as the unique torsion-free metric-compatible connection on $TM$ — is precisely the principal connection on this bundle whose pullback under any orthonormal local frame gives the matrix of Cartan connection 1-forms $\omega^a{}_b$. The curvature 2-forms $\Omega^a{}_b$ on the base are then the local pullback of the principal curvature $\Omega \in \Omega^2(F^O M; \mathfrak{o}(n))$, and the structural equation $\Omega^a{}_b = d\omega^a{}_b + \omega^a{}_c \wedge \omega^c{}_b$ of Riemannian geometry is exactly the matrix form of the principal-bundle structural equation $\Omega = d\omega + \omega \wedge \omega$ for the defining representation of $O(n)$. This identification is the *origin* of the analogy "gravity is a gauge theory of the Lorentz group"; the analogy is precise because the principal-bundle formalism is the unifying framework for both.

2. **[[Gauge Theory I — Connections on Vector Bundles and the Electromagnetic Connection|Electromagnetism]] — $U(1)$-gauge theory is the abelian special case.** The electromagnetic potential $A_\mu$ and field strength $F_{\mu\nu}$ of [[Gauge Theory I — Connections on Vector Bundles and the Electromagnetic Connection|Gauge Theory I]] are exactly the local connection 1-form and curvature 2-form of a $U(1)$-principal connection, with the abelian special case $[A, A] = 0$ collapsing the structural equation to $F = dA$, the Bianchi identity to $dF = 0$ (Maxwell's geometric half), and the gauge transformation law to $A \mapsto A + d\chi$. The Aharonov-Bohm effect is the statement that the holonomy $\oint A$ around a non-contractible loop is gauge-invariant modulo $2\pi$ and physically observable — pure geometry, encoded in the holonomy of the principal connection. The Dirac monopole is the choice of a non-trivial $U(1)$-bundle over $S^2$ (Hopf bundle), with the quantisation of magnetic charge $g e \in \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}$ coming from the integrality of the first Chern class.

3. **[[Gauge Theory IV — Yang–Mills Fields and Instantons|Yang-Mills theory]] — the dynamics on top of this kinematics.** The connection $\omega$ and its curvature $F$ developed here are the *kinematic objects* of Yang-Mills theory; the *dynamical equation* is $d_\omega \star F = 0$ (or with matter source $d_\omega \star F = j$), derived from extremising the action $S[\omega] = -\tfrac{1}{2}\int_M \mathrm{tr}(F \wedge \star F)$ on the affine space $\mathcal{A}(P)$ of connections. Bianchi $d_\omega F = 0$ is geometric kinematics; Yang-Mills $d_\omega \star F = 0$ is dynamics. For **self-dual connections** in four dimensions, $F = \star F$, Bianchi implies Yang-Mills automatically — these are the **instantons** of [[Gauge Theory IV — Yang–Mills Fields and Instantons|Gauge Theory IV]], and they are the absolute minima of the Yang-Mills action in each topological sector classified by $c_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$.

4. **[[Algebraic Topology III — Higher Homotopy and Chern Forms|Algebraic topology]] — Chern-Weil theory and characteristic classes.** Given a connection $\omega$ on $P$ and an $\mathrm{Ad}$-invariant polynomial $p$ on $\mathfrak{g}$, the form $p(\Omega) \in \Omega^{2k}(M; \mathbb{R})$ is closed (by Bianchi) and its de Rham class is independent of the choice of $\omega$ — this is the **Chern-Weil construction** of characteristic classes. For $U(n)$, the invariants are traces of powers of $\Omega$, giving the **Chern classes** $c_k(P) \in H^{2k}(M; \mathbb{Z})$; for $O(n)$, traces of even powers give the **Pontryagin classes**, and the **Pfaffian** of $\Omega$ for $SO(2k)$ gives the **Euler class**. These classes are *topological invariants of the bundle*, completely independent of the choice of connection, and they obstruct triviality of $P$. The integral $\int_M c_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ for an $SU(2)$-bundle over $S^4$ is the **instanton number** of [[Gauge Theory IV — Yang–Mills Fields and Instantons|Gauge Theory IV]] — pure topology, computable from any connection on the bundle.

5. **Lie theory — the Maurer-Cartan equation as universal template.** The Maurer-Cartan equation $d\theta_G + \tfrac{1}{2}[\theta_G, \theta_G] = 0$ on a Lie group $G$ is the universal template for the structural equation of every connection. Indeed, the trivial principal bundle $G \to \mathrm{pt}$ over a point has a canonical flat connection $\omega = \theta_G$, and its curvature $d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega] = 0$ *is* the Maurer-Cartan equation. The curvature of a general connection is the deformation of the Maurer-Cartan equation: the obstruction to the connection being locally trivial. This frames every curvature formula in gauge theory as "how far from Maurer-Cartan is $\omega$?" — a unifying perspective that connects gauge theory to **Cartan geometry**, **Klein geometry**, and the differential structure of Lie groups themselves.

6. **Connections on associated bundles vs. vector-bundle connections.** A vector-bundle connection $\nabla$ on $E \to M$ (the topic of [[Gauge Theory I — Connections on Vector Bundles and the Electromagnetic Connection|Gauge Theory I]] and [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation|Riemannian Geometry I]]) is *equivalent* to a principal connection on the corresponding frame bundle $\mathrm{Fr}(E) \to M$, which is the principal $\mathrm{GL}(K)$-bundle of bases of fibres. The vector-bundle data $(E, \nabla)$ recovers the principal data $(\mathrm{Fr}(E), \omega)$ uniquely, and vice versa via the associated bundle $E = \mathrm{Fr}(E) \times_{\mathrm{GL}(K)} \mathbb{R}^K$. So vector-bundle connections and principal connections are two windows on the same geometric object — one localised to the vector bundle, one globalised to the frame bundle. The choice of window is a matter of convenience: vector-bundle connections are computationally direct; principal connections are conceptually clean and necessary for non-trivial structure-group reductions (e.g., orthonormal frame reductions, spin-structure reductions).

7. **Gauge theory and the Standard Model.** The Standard Model of particle physics is a gauge theory with structure group $SU(3) \times SU(2) \times U(1)$ — the strong force ($SU(3)$, eight gluon gauge bosons in $\mathfrak{su}(3)$), the weak force and electromagnetism unified ($SU(2) \times U(1)$, four bosons broken by the Higgs mechanism to one massless photon and three massive $W^\pm, Z$). All of it is built on the principal-bundle formalism of this chapter: each force has its principal bundle, its connection 1-form, its gauge potential in some gauge, its curvature giving the field strengths, its associated bundles where the matter fields (quarks, leptons, Higgs) live. The Higgs mechanism, spontaneous symmetry breaking, anomaly cancellation — all are formulated in this language. The reader who masters this chapter has the geometric kinematics for the entire Standard Model; the dynamics and the renormalisation are the rest of QFT.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a connection is a $G$-equivariant horizontal distribution on the total space of a principal bundle, equivalently a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form on the total space satisfying equivariance and verticality.** Every gauge theory — abelian or non-abelian, electromagnetic or chromodynamic or gravitational — is the same geometric story: a principal bundle, a connection on it, a curvature 2-form measuring the failure of horizontal-distribution closure, a gauge group acting by change of section. The local gauge potential $A$ is a shadow of $\omega$ that depends on a choice of section; the local field strength $F$ is a shadow of $\Omega$ that descends globally to a section of $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$ because $\Omega$ is horizontal and equivariant. Once one believes this picture, the formulas of gauge theory are no longer ad hoc but inevitable — they are forced by the geometry of principal bundles plus the algebra of Lie algebras.

**The true name of the gauge potential $A$ is "the pullback of a connection 1-form on the principal bundle along a local section".** The formal definition — "a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form on $U$ subject to the gauge transformation law" — is the right thing to compute with but the wrong thing to think. The operational picture, the one that explains why every formula looks the way it looks, is that on the total space $P$ there is a global object $\omega$, and what we call "the gauge potential in a chart" is its pullback under a section. Different sections give different pullbacks, and the gauge transformation law is the cocycle that records this dependence. Once one sees $A$ as a *shadow* of $\omega$, the inhomogeneous transformation $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$ is no longer a mysterious rule — it is the natural consequence of pulling back under a different section. Physicists work with $A$ exclusively because they always work in a fixed gauge; mathematicians keep both $A$ and $\omega$ in the picture to make gauge invariance manifest.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: "compute curvature" → "structural equation $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$, then pull back".** The single most-used calculation in gauge theory is "given $A$, compute $F$". The reaction is mechanical: $F = dA + \tfrac{1}{2}[A, A]$ in invariant form, or $F^a_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A^a_\nu - \partial_\nu A^a_\mu + f^a{}_{bc} A^b_\mu A^c_\nu$ in components. The abelian piece is the same as electromagnetism; the non-abelian piece $f^a{}_{bc}A^b A^c$ is the self-interaction term that vanishes only when $\mathfrak{g}$ is abelian. Recognising this pattern lets one compute the field strength for any gauge theory in a few lines.

**A second trigger-reaction pattern: "matter field $\psi$ in representation $V$ minimally coupled to gauge field $A$" → "covariant derivative $\nabla\psi = d\psi + d\rho(A)\psi$".** This is the second most-used calculation in gauge theory, and the universal rule for "covariantising" any equation in the presence of a gauge field. The minimally-coupled Dirac equation $i\gamma^\mu(\partial_\mu - ieA_\mu)\psi = m\psi$ is the case $\rho =$ defining rep of $U(1)$ on $\mathbb{C}$; the minimally-coupled Klein-Gordon equation for a colour-charged scalar is $\rho =$ defining rep of $SU(3)$ on $\mathbb{C}^3$; the spinor covariant derivative in curved spacetime is $\rho =$ spinor rep of $\mathrm{Spin}(1,3)$; and so on uniformly. Recognising the pattern is the recipe for writing down the equations of motion for any matter field in any gauge theory.

**Inheritance — every property of the local gauge potential is inherited from the global connection on $P$.** Smoothness of $A$ is inherited from smoothness of $\omega$; the equivariance of the gauge transformation law is inherited from the equivariance axiom of $\omega$; the descent of $F$ to a section of $\mathrm{Ad}\,P$ is inherited from the horizontality of $\Omega$; the Bianchi identity is inherited from the algebraic identity $d^2 = 0$ together with the Jacobi identity for $[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]$ on $\mathfrak{g}$. Whenever a fact about $A$ or $F$ seems mysterious, the question is "what is the corresponding fact about $\omega$ or $\Omega$ on $P$?" — and the answer is almost always a clean geometric statement of which the local fact is a shadow.

**Indefiniteness of curvature is not a defect — it is the field strength.** Just as the indefiniteness of the Lorentzian metric is the physics of relativity (causal structure, light cones, time direction), the non-vanishing of curvature $F = dA + A \wedge A$ is the physics of gauge fields (force, self-interaction, instantons). A vanishing curvature would mean a *flat connection*, which physically corresponds to "no gauge field present, no force". Curvature is the gauge-field analogue of force; the Bianchi identity is the analogue of "force balance is automatic"; the Yang-Mills equation is the analogue of "force has dynamics". The geometric content of gauge theory is precisely that the force is curvature.

**The unifying observation of all gauge theory: the gauge field is the connection, the field strength is its curvature, and gauge invariance is the change-of-section freedom in a principal bundle.** This single sentence captures the entire conceptual content of the chapter. It explains why all gauge theories look formally similar (they share a single geometric structure); why the dynamics is so constrained (the action must be gauge-invariant, so it must be a function of $F$ and its derivatives); why topological terms appear (they are characteristic classes of the bundle, gauge-invariant by Chern-Weil); and why the quantisation is delicate (one is quantising on the quotient $\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{G}$ of connections modulo gauge transformations, an infinite-dimensional moduli space with non-trivial topology). The reader who carries this sentence forward to [[Gauge Theory IV — Yang–Mills Fields and Instantons|Gauge Theory IV]] and beyond will find that every subsequent development is a refinement of it.
