# Notation

For a real oriented vector bundle $E \to M$ of rank $k$, the **Euler class** is denoted $e(E) \in H^k(M; \mathbb{Z})$ (or $H^k(M; \mathbb{R})$ via the de Rham isomorphism). When $k$ is odd, $e(E) = 0$. For the tangent bundle of a closed oriented manifold, $\int_M e(TM) = \chi(M)$. The Pfaffian-of-curvature representative is denoted $\mathrm{Pf}(\Omega^\nabla)/(2\pi)^{k/2}$, where $\Omega^\nabla$ is the curvature 2-form of any metric connection $\nabla$ on $E$. See [[Gauge Theory II — Principal Bundles, Representations, and Bundle Classification]] for the registry.

# Axiom Motivation

The Euler class is the **topological obstruction to the existence of a nowhere-vanishing section of a real oriented vector bundle of rank equal to the dimension of the base**. If $E \to M$ has rank $k = \dim M$ (the "top" rank, where the dimension of the bundle and the base match), then a global section $s : M \to E$ generically intersects the zero section in a $0$-dimensional submanifold of $M$ — a finite collection of points. The Poincaré dual of this zero locus is a cohomology class in $H^k(M)$, and this class is the **Euler class** $e(E)$. When $e(E) = 0$, the zero locus can be perturbed away (the section can be deformed to a nowhere-vanishing section); when $e(E) \neq 0$, no such deformation is possible, and the integer $\int_M e(E)$ counts the algebraic number of zeros of any generic section.

Why **vector bundles need a metric to define $e(E)$** in this de Rham presentation? Because the Pfaffian polynomial $\mathrm{Pf}$ is only $\mathrm{SO}(k)$-invariant, not $\mathrm{GL}(k)$-invariant. To apply it to the curvature, we need to reduce the structure group of $E$ from $\mathrm{GL}(k)$ to $\mathrm{SO}(k)$ — i.e., put a Riemannian metric and an orientation on $E$. Any two metrics give the same Euler class (the construction is metric-independent in cohomology), but the *intermediate* form $\mathrm{Pf}(\Omega^\nabla)$ depends on the metric and connection.

Why **odd-rank bundles** have $e(E) = 0$? Because the Pfaffian of an odd-sized skew-symmetric matrix vanishes identically. Geometrically: an odd-rank oriented vector bundle always admits a nowhere-vanishing section (away from a codimension-$k$ subset, but $k$ odd makes the algebraic-count obstruction zero in $H^k$). For odd-rank tangent bundles, this corresponds to $\chi(M) = 0$ on odd-dimensional manifolds (Poincaré duality), confirming the topological computation.

Why is the **Pfaffian-of-curvature** the de Rham representative? This is the content of **Chern–Weil theory** specialized to the orthogonal group. The general construction: for any compact Lie group $G$ and any $G$-invariant polynomial $P$ on $\mathfrak{g}$, evaluating $P$ on the curvature 2-form $\Omega \in \Omega^2(P; \mathfrak{g})$ of any principal $G$-connection produces a closed differential form on the base whose cohomology class is independent of the connection. The Pfaffian is the $\mathrm{SO}(2n)$-invariant polynomial of degree $n$ (the *only* one that is not in the polynomial ring generated by the elementary symmetric polynomials), and the resulting cohomology class is the Euler class. The independence from the connection is exactly why the integer $\int_M e(TM) = \chi(M)$ is a topological invariant — it does not depend on the metric used to compute the curvature.

Why is the **integral over a closed manifold an integer**? This is the topological side: $e(E) \in H^k(M; \mathbb{Z})$ is integral (it is a cohomology class with $\mathbb{Z}$ coefficients, not just $\mathbb{R}$), so its pairing with the fundamental class $[M]$ is an integer. The de Rham representative $\mathrm{Pf}(\Omega)/(2\pi)^{k/2}$ is a *real* differential form, but its integral over the integral fundamental class is automatically an integer. This is the deep content of Gauss-Bonnet-Chern: the analytic side (curvature integral) lands in $\mathbb{Z}$ because the topological side does.

What goes wrong if we **forget orientation**? Then we cannot reduce $\mathrm{GL}(k) \to \mathrm{SO}(k)$, only $\mathrm{GL}(k) \to \mathrm{O}(k)$, and the Pfaffian is only $\mathrm{O}(k)$-equivariant *up to sign* (it transforms by $\det g = \pm 1$). The Euler class of a non-orientable bundle is defined only modulo $2$ — it becomes the top Stiefel-Whitney class $w_k(E) \in H^k(M; \mathbb{Z}/2)$. The integer-valued Euler class requires orientation.

# The Definition

Let $E \to M$ be a real oriented vector bundle of rank $k$.

**If $k$ is odd**, $e(E) := 0 \in H^k(M; \mathbb{Z})$.

**If $k = 2n$ is even**, the **Euler class** $e(E) \in H^{2n}(M; \mathbb{Z})$ is defined as follows. Choose any Riemannian metric on $E$ (compatible with the orientation), reducing the structure group from $\mathrm{GL}(2n, \mathbb{R})$ to $\mathrm{SO}(2n)$, and choose any metric connection $\nabla$ on $E$. The curvature 2-form $\Omega^\nabla$ takes values in $\mathfrak{so}(2n)$ (skew-symmetric matrices). The differential form
$$\mathrm{Pf}(\Omega^\nabla) / (2\pi)^n \;\in\; \Omega^{2n}(M)$$
is closed (Bianchi identity); the **Euler class** is its de Rham cohomology class:
$$e(E) := \left[\frac{\mathrm{Pf}(\Omega^\nabla)}{(2\pi)^n}\right] \;\in\; H^{2n}(M; \mathbb{R}).$$
By Chern-Weil theory this class is independent of the choice of metric and connection. The class lifts canonically to integer cohomology $H^{2n}(M; \mathbb{Z})$ (via the topological definition as the Poincaré dual of the zero locus of a generic section, or equivalently as a pullback from the universal Euler class on $BSO(2n)$).

Equivalently and combinatorially: $e(E)$ is the **Poincaré dual of the zero locus of any generic smooth section** $s : M \to E$ that meets the zero section transversally. The intersection $s(M) \cap o(M) \subset E$ is a smooth submanifold of $M$ of codimension $2n$, and its Poincaré dual is $e(E)$.

For the **tangent bundle** $E = TM$ of a closed oriented manifold $M^{2n}$,
$$\int_M e(TM) \;=\; \chi(M),$$
the **Euler characteristic** — see [[Thm - Gauss-Bonnet-Chern Theorem]].

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The Euler class is **the obstruction to a global section**. For a rank-$k$ bundle over a $k$-dimensional base, $e(E) = 0$ in $H^k(M)$ is precisely the condition that the bundle admits a nowhere-vanishing section. This generalizes the **Poincaré-Hopf theorem**: $\chi(M) = e(TM) \cdot [M] =$ sum of indices of any vector field — when $\chi(M) = 0$ there exists a nowhere-vanishing vector field, when $\chi(M) \neq 0$ no such vector field exists.

The Euler class is the **top Chern class of a complex vector bundle, viewed as a real oriented bundle**. For a complex rank-$n$ bundle $E_\mathbb{C}$ (= real rank-$2n$ oriented), $e(E_\mathbb{C}) = c_n(E_\mathbb{C}) \in H^{2n}(M; \mathbb{Z})$. So Euler classes specialize Chern classes; the Pfaffian formula in the real case becomes the determinant formula $c_n(E) = \det(\Omega/2\pi i)^{n}/n!$ in the complex case (the $n$-th elementary symmetric polynomial of the eigenvalues of $\Omega/2\pi i$).

The Euler class is the **pullback of the universal Euler class on $BSO(2n)$**. The classifying space $BSO(2n)$ has $H^{2n}(BSO(2n); \mathbb{Z})$ containing a distinguished generator (the Euler class), and any oriented rank-$2n$ bundle $E$ over $M$ classifies to a map $M \to BSO(2n)$; pulling back the universal Euler class gives $e(E)$. This is the topologist's definition; the Chern-Weil definition (Pfaffian of curvature) is the geometer's.

**True name:** the Euler class is **the Poincaré dual of the zero locus of any generic section of $E$**. Operationally: when you want to compute $e(E)$, pick a generic section, count its zeros (with signs from the orientation), and Poincaré-dualize. This is the geometric content that makes $\int_M e(TM) = \chi(M) = \sum j_v(p_\alpha)$ (Gauss-Bonnet-Poincaré).

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: $e(TS^2) = 2 \cdot [\mathrm{pt}] \in H^2(S^2; \mathbb{Z})$.** Compute via Gauss-Bonnet: $\int_{S^2} K\,dA = 4\pi$ for the round metric ($K \equiv 1$), so $e(TS^2)/(2\pi) = 2$, giving $\int_{S^2} e(TS^2) = 2 = \chi(S^2)$. Alternatively: any vector field on $S^2$ has zeros summing to index $2$ (e.g., the vector field tangent to a meridian has a zero at each pole, both of index $+1$).

**Is an instance: $e(TT^2) = 0 \in H^2(T^2; \mathbb{Z})$.** The 2-torus is parallelizable (admits a nowhere-vanishing vector field), so $e(TT^2) = 0$, matching $\chi(T^2) = 0$.

**Is an instance: $e(T\mathbb{CP}^n) = (n+1) \cdot h^n \in H^{2n}(\mathbb{CP}^n)$.** With $h \in H^2(\mathbb{CP}^n; \mathbb{Z})$ the hyperplane class. So $\int_{\mathbb{CP}^n} e(T\mathbb{CP}^n) = n + 1 = \chi(\mathbb{CP}^n)$. See [[Ex - Computing the Euler Class of the Tangent Bundle of CP^n]].

**Is an instance: $e(\text{Möbius bundle}) = 0$ — but the Möbius bundle is not oriented, so $e$ is not defined.** This non-instance highlights the orientation requirement.

**Is an instance: $e(TS^{2n+1}) = 0$.** Any odd-dimensional manifold has $\chi = 0$ (Poincaré duality), matching the vanishing of $\mathrm{Pf}$ in odd dimensions. Concretely, $S^{2n+1}$ admits the nowhere-vanishing vector field $-x_2\partial_1 + x_1\partial_2 - x_4\partial_3 + \ldots$ (paired-coordinate rotation).

**Is NOT an instance: a non-orientable bundle's Euler class.** The Möbius line bundle over $S^1$ is non-orientable; the integer Euler class is not defined. Its mod-2 Stiefel-Whitney class $w_1 \in H^1(S^1; \mathbb{Z}/2) = \mathbb{Z}/2$ is nonzero, and this is the relevant invariant.

**Is NOT an instance: a complex line bundle's Euler class is the first Chern class.** A complex line bundle is a real rank-2 oriented bundle; $e(L_{\mathbb{R}}) = c_1(L) \in H^2(M; \mathbb{Z})$. The Hopf bundle over $S^2$ has $c_1 = -1$, so $e = -1$.

**Corollary (Whitney sum formula).** $e(E \oplus F) = e(E) \smile e(F)$ for oriented bundles $E, F$ (provided ranks are compatible). This is the multiplicative property of the Euler class.

**Corollary ($e(E) = 0$ if $E$ admits a nowhere-vanishing section).** A nowhere-vanishing section is a global frame for a rank-$1$ subbundle, splitting $E = L \oplus E'$ with $L$ trivial; then $e(L) = 0$ and $e(E) = e(L) \smile e(E') = 0$.

**Corollary (Gauss-Bonnet-Chern).** $\int_M e(TM) = \chi(M)$ for closed oriented $M^{2n}$, with $\chi(M)$ the Euler characteristic. See [[Thm - Gauss-Bonnet-Chern Theorem]].

**Calibration check.** Verify (i) $e(TS^2)$ evaluates to $2$ via Gauss-Bonnet for the round metric; (ii) $e(TT^2) = 0$ by exhibiting a nowhere-vanishing vector field on the torus; (iii) $e(E) = 0$ for any odd-rank bundle by the Pfaffian-vanishes argument.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Gauss-Bonnet-Chern Theorem *(from Differential Geometry / Index Theory)*
> The integral formula $\int_M e(TM) = \chi(M)$ is the Gauss-Bonnet-Chern theorem, the first instance of an index theorem. It generalizes Gauss-Bonnet for surfaces to all even-dimensional closed orientable Riemannian manifolds. See [[Thm - Gauss-Bonnet-Chern Theorem]].

> [!tip] Euler Class as Obstruction to a Section *(from Obstruction Theory)*
> The Euler class is the *primary obstruction* to constructing a nowhere-vanishing section of a rank-$k$ vector bundle on a $k$-dimensional base. More generally, in obstruction theory the sequence of obstruction classes $o_0, o_1, o_2, \ldots$ to extending sections to higher and higher skeleta lives in cohomology with coefficients in the homotopy groups of fibres; the Euler class is the first nontrivial obstruction in this sequence. The systematic theory is part of [[Algebraic Topology III — Higher Homotopy and Chern Forms]].

> [!tip] Index Theorem Interpretation *(from Atiyah-Singer)*
> Gauss-Bonnet-Chern $\int_M e(TM) = \chi(M)$ is the index theorem for the operator $D = d + d^* : \Omega^{\mathrm{even}}(M) \to \Omega^{\mathrm{odd}}(M)$: the analytical index $\dim\ker D - \dim\mathrm{coker}\,D = \chi(M)$ (by Hodge theory) equals the topological index $\int_M e(TM)$ (by Chern-Weil). This is the prototype of the **Atiyah-Singer index theorem**, the master theorem of which the Euler-class-Gauss-Bonnet identity is the simplest instance.
