# Problem Statement

Consider an infinitely long, infinitely thin solenoid along the $z$-axis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ with magnetic flux $\Phi$ confined to the interior (a $\delta$-function tube of flux). Outside the solenoid (i.e., on $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{z\text{-axis}\}$), the magnetic field vanishes: $\vec B = 0$.

**(a)** Show that $A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}\,d\phi$ is a valid vector potential outside the solenoid (where $\phi$ is the azimuthal angle in cylindrical coordinates), satisfying $\vec B = \nabla \times A = 0$ on this domain.

**(b)** Consider an electron passing on opposite sides of the solenoid. Show that the holonomy of the $U(1)$-electromagnetic connection around a closed loop encircling the solenoid once is
$$\boxed{\exp\Bigl(\tfrac{ie}{\hbar}\oint A\Bigr) = \exp\Bigl(\tfrac{ie\Phi}{\hbar}\Bigr).}$$

**(c)** Conclude that the interference pattern of the two electron paths shifts by an amount depending only on the magnetic flux $\Phi$, even though the electron *never* enters a region of non-zero $B$. This is the **Aharonov-Bohm effect**, and it proves that the electromagnetic potential $A$ is a physical field in quantum mechanics, not merely a mathematical convenience.

**(d)** Verify gauge invariance: show that the phase $e^{ie\Phi/\hbar}$ is well-defined modulo $2\pi$, even though $A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d\phi$ is *not* a globally defined 1-form (since $\phi$ is multi-valued).

The **holonomy** of a $U(1)$-connection $\omega = -(ie/\hbar)A$ around a closed loop $\gamma$ is $\exp((ie/\hbar)\oint_\gamma A)$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** This is a flagship calculation in gauge-theoretic quantum mechanics, demonstrating that the [[Def - Wilson Line and Holonomy of a Connection|holonomy]] of the EM $U(1)$-connection is *physically observable* even on a region where the curvature (field strength) vanishes. The exercise sits at the boundary between standard quantum mechanics (Schrödinger equation, double-slit interference) and modern differential geometry (connections on bundles, holonomy as a gauge-invariant observable), and is the simplest experimental confirmation that the vector potential is more fundamental than the field strength.

**Assumption pattern:** The solenoid creates a configuration where $\vec B$ is confined to the interior, so on the configuration space $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{z\text{-axis}\}$ (where the electron lives), $F = 0$. The vector potential $A$, however, is *non-zero* on this domain — it is the closed 1-form $\frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d\phi$ whose line integral around any loop encircling the axis is $\Phi$. The configuration space is *not simply connected* ($\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}$, generated by loops around the axis), and the closed-but-not-exact $A$ encodes this topology.

**Theorem routing:** (i) Verify $\vec B = dA = 0$ outside the solenoid by direct computation. (ii) Compute $\oint_\gamma A = \Phi$ for any loop $\gamma$ encircling the axis once — direct angular integration. (iii) The holonomy formula $\exp((ie/\hbar)\oint A)$ then gives the Aharonov-Bohm phase. (iv) Gauge invariance follows because the integral $\oint A$ is gauge-invariant modulo $2\pi$ (a gauge transformation $A \to A + df$ for *single-valued* $f$ preserves $\oint A$; for multi-valued $f$ winding $n$ times, $\oint A$ shifts by $2\pi n$, but the exponential is unchanged modulo $2\pi$).

**Key decision point:** The non-obvious choice is to interpret $A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d\phi$ as a *connection on a non-trivial $U(1)$-bundle over the non-simply-connected domain* rather than a single-valued function. The angle $\phi$ itself is multi-valued (defined modulo $2\pi$), so $A$ is technically a connection on a bundle that is *flat* (zero curvature) but has *non-trivial monodromy* — the holonomy $\exp(ie\Phi/\hbar)$ around the generating loop of $\pi_1$. This is the cleanest geometric framing of the Aharonov-Bohm effect.

# Solution

The proof has four steps. Step 1 verifies the vector potential. Step 2 computes the holonomy as a closed-loop integral. Step 3 interprets it as an observable phase shift in interferometry. Step 4 reconciles the multi-valuedness of $\phi$ with the well-definedness of the physical phase.

**Step 1: The vector potential $A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d\phi$ satisfies $\vec B = 0$ outside the solenoid.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> In cylindrical coordinates $(r, \phi, z)$, the 1-form $d\phi$ corresponds to the vector field $\frac{1}{r}\hat\phi$ via the metric $dr^2 + r^2 d\phi^2 + dz^2$:
> $$d\phi = (d\phi)^\flat = (\text{vector with components } (0, 1/r, 0) \text{ in } (r, \phi, z) \text{ basis}).$$
> So $A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d\phi$ corresponds to $\vec A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi r}\hat\phi$.
>
> Compute the curl: in cylindrical coordinates,
> $$\nabla \times A = \left(\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial A_z}{\partial\phi} - \frac{\partial A_\phi}{\partial z}\right)\hat r + \left(\frac{\partial A_r}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial r}\right)\hat\phi + \frac{1}{r}\left(\frac{\partial(rA_\phi)}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial A_r}{\partial\phi}\right)\hat z.$$
> Plug in $A_r = 0$, $A_\phi = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi r}$, $A_z = 0$:
> $$\nabla \times A = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r \cdot \frac{\Phi}{2\pi r})}{\partial r}\hat z = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(\Phi/(2\pi))}{\partial r}\hat z = 0.$$
>
> So $\vec B = \nabla \times A = 0$ on $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{z\text{-axis}\}$. ✓
>
> Alternatively in form-language: $F = dA = d(\frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d\phi) = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d(d\phi) = 0$ on $r > 0$ (since $d\phi$ is closed). Same conclusion.

**Step 2: Holonomy around a loop encircling the solenoid is $\exp(ie\Phi/\hbar)$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $\gamma$ be a closed loop encircling the $z$-axis once counterclockwise. The line integral of $A$ around $\gamma$:
> $$\oint_\gamma A = \oint_\gamma\frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d\phi = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}\oint_\gamma d\phi = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}\cdot 2\pi = \Phi.$$
>
> The integral $\oint d\phi$ is $2\pi$ because the angle $\phi$ changes by $2\pi$ as you traverse the loop once. (Alternatively, parameterize $\gamma$ as $\phi : [0, 2\pi] \to [0, 2\pi]$ and use the fundamental theorem of calculus.)
>
> The holonomy of the $U(1)$-connection $\omega = -(ie/\hbar)A$ is
> $$\mathrm{Hol}_{\mathrm{EM}}(\gamma) = \exp\Bigl(-\oint_\gamma\omega\Bigr) = \exp\Bigl(\frac{ie}{\hbar}\oint_\gamma A\Bigr) = \exp\Bigl(\frac{ie\Phi}{\hbar}\Bigr).$$
>
> This is the Aharonov-Bohm phase.

**Step 3: Physical interpretation — the interference shift.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Consider a double-slit experiment: an electron source emits electrons, which pass through two slits and recombine on a detector screen. Without the solenoid, the wave function at the screen is a sum of two contributions, one from each slit, and the interference pattern is governed by their relative phase.
>
> With the solenoid inserted between the slits (containing flux $\Phi$, with $\vec B = 0$ on the electron paths), the wave function acquires an additional phase along each path. By the Feynman path-integral picture, the contribution from path $\gamma$ is multiplied by $\exp\bigl((ie/\hbar)\int_\gamma A\bigr)$. For paths on the two sides of the solenoid, the relative phase shift is
> $$\Delta\phi_{\mathrm{AB}} = \frac{e}{\hbar}\Bigl(\int_{\gamma_1}A - \int_{\gamma_2}A\Bigr) = \frac{e}{\hbar}\oint_{\gamma_1 - \gamma_2}A = \frac{e\Phi}{\hbar}.$$
>
> The interference pattern on the screen shifts by a constant amount proportional to $\Phi$ — *with no change to the local electromagnetic environment of either electron path*. The shift was first observed experimentally by Chambers in 1960 and confirmed many times since (Tonomura et al. 1986 with electron holograms). This proves that the *potential* $A$ has physical effects beyond what $F = dA$ alone can produce — the AB effect is *the* canonical demonstration of "$A$ is more fundamental than $F$" in quantum mechanics.

**Step 4: Gauge invariance and multi-valuedness.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> The angle $\phi$ is multi-valued on $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{z\text{-axis}\}$ — defined only modulo $2\pi$. Hence $A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d\phi$ is not technically a globally defined 1-form — it is a connection on a non-trivial $U(1)$-bundle.
>
> However, the *exponential* of the line integral is single-valued. A gauge transformation $A \to A + df$ for a multi-valued $f$ winding $n$ times around the $z$-axis adds $2\pi n$ to $\oint A$, but $\exp((ie/\hbar)(2\pi n)) = \exp(2\pi i n e/\hbar)$. For the wave function to be well-defined (single-valued at each point of the configuration space), we'd need this to equal $1$ — i.e., $ne/\hbar \in \mathbb{Z}$, i.e., $e/\hbar = 1$ for integer $n$, which is satisfied for any value of $e$ (since the wave function picks up the same phase under any gauge transformation, and physical observables depend only on relative phases).
>
> In the standard formulation: the Aharonov-Bohm phase $e\Phi/\hbar$ is *modulo $2\pi$* — fluxes differing by $2\pi\hbar/e = \Phi_0$ (the **flux quantum** in Gaussian-c=1 units) give the same observable phase shift. For each value $\Phi \in [0, \Phi_0)$, the AB effect produces a *distinct* interference pattern; flux values are physically distinguishable up to $\Phi_0$.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **(a) Vector potential.** In cylindrical coordinates on $\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{z\text{-axis}\}$, the 1-form $A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d\phi$ corresponds to $\vec A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi r}\hat\phi$. Computing $\nabla \times A$ in cylindrical coordinates gives $\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial(r A_\phi)}{\partial r}\hat z = 0$, since $rA_\phi = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}$ is constant. Hence $\vec B = 0$ on $r > 0$. Equivalently, $F = dA = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}d(d\phi) = 0$ on the domain.
>
> **(b) Holonomy.** For a loop $\gamma$ encircling the $z$-axis once: $\oint_\gamma A = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}\oint d\phi = \frac{\Phi}{2\pi}\cdot 2\pi = \Phi$. The holonomy of $\omega = -(ie/\hbar)A$ is $\exp(-\oint\omega) = \exp((ie/\hbar)\Phi) = e^{ie\Phi/\hbar}$.
>
> **(c) Interference shift.** Two electron paths on opposite sides of the solenoid form a closed loop encircling the axis once, with relative phase $e^{ie\Phi/\hbar}$. This shifts the interference pattern by a fixed amount depending only on $\Phi$, even though $\vec B = 0$ on both paths. Observed experimentally; proves the EM potential is physical in quantum mechanics.
>
> **(d) Gauge invariance.** The exponential $e^{ie\Phi/\hbar}$ is single-valued in $\Phi$ modulo $2\pi\hbar/e = \Phi_0$ (the flux quantum). The multi-valuedness of $\phi$ in $A$ does not contaminate physical observables: the wave function is a section of a $U(1)$-bundle (not a single-valued function), and the holonomy is the gauge-invariant observable.
> ▪

> [!warning] Verification via Stokes' theorem
> The line integral $\oint A = \Phi$ can be derived alternatively by Stokes' theorem applied to a surface $\Sigma$ bounded by $\gamma$, *including the solenoid*: $\oint A = \int_\Sigma F$, and $F$ has a $\delta$-function contribution $\Phi$ concentrated on the $z$-axis. So $\int_\Sigma F = \Phi$, matching the direct calculation. This is the physical content: the line integral "sees" the enclosed flux even though $F = 0$ everywhere on the actual path of the electron. (The non-simple-connectedness of the configuration space allows this.)

> [!warning] Independent route: solving the Schrödinger equation directly
> One can also verify the AB phase by solving the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\partial_t\psi = \frac{1}{2m}(-i\hbar\nabla - eA)^2\psi$ directly, finding the wave function around the solenoid in cylindrical-coordinate separation of variables, and reading off the phase shift between paths. The result is the same $e^{ie\Phi/\hbar}$, confirming the holonomy interpretation from the bundle-geometric perspective.

# Key Takeaways

**The Aharonov-Bohm effect proves that the electromagnetic potential $A$ is a physical field in quantum mechanics, not just a mathematical convenience.**

Classical electromagnetism treats $A$ as a gauge artifact — only $F = dA$ matters for the Lorentz force on a charged particle. Quantum mechanics changes this: the wave function transforms under gauge transformations as $\psi \to e^{(ie/\hbar)f}\psi$, and the Schrödinger equation explicitly depends on $A$. The AB effect shows the dependence is *observable* — even when $F = 0$ throughout the electron's trajectory, the *holonomy* $\oint A$ can produce a measurable phase shift. The lesson is structural: $A$ is *the connection on a $U(1)$-bundle*, and connections live globally; only locally are they exhaustively determined by their curvatures. Gauge theory is the language for this distinction.

**Holonomy is the universal gauge-invariant observable of a connection on a bundle.**

For abelian connections (like EM), the holonomy is $\exp(-\oint\omega) = \exp((ie/\hbar)\oint A)$. For non-abelian connections (Yang-Mills), it is the path-ordered exponential $\mathcal{P}\exp(-\oint\omega)$, with the trace $\mathrm{tr}\,W_\gamma$ being a gauge-invariant function on the loop space. In quantum field theory, **Wilson loops** $\langle\mathrm{tr}\,W_\gamma\rangle$ are the basic non-perturbative observables — confining gauge theories show "area-law" decay $\langle\mathrm{tr}\,W_\gamma\rangle \sim e^{-\sigma\,\mathrm{Area}}$, and the AB effect is the simplest physical manifestation. Always think of holonomy as "the physically meaningful integrated information in a connection".

**The non-simple-connectedness of the configuration space is what enables the AB effect.**

If the configuration space were simply connected (say $\mathbb{R}^3$), then every loop would bound a disk; by Stokes, $\oint A = \int F = 0$ when $F = 0$ on the bounding disk; no holonomy. The AB effect requires a domain with non-trivial topology — $\pi_1(\mathbb{R}^3 \setminus \{z\text{-axis}\}) = \mathbb{Z}$ generated by loops around the axis. The flux $\Phi$ parameterizes the *space of flat $U(1)$-connections modulo gauge* on this domain: $\mathrm{Hom}(\pi_1, U(1)) = \mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}, U(1)) = U(1)$. The Aharonov-Bohm phase is the holonomy representation evaluated on the generator. This pattern generalizes: every non-trivial topology of configuration space can produce analogous "topological" phase effects in quantum mechanics (e.g., Berry phases, topological insulators).

**Cross-link to the Dirac monopole:** See [[Ex - Dirac Monopole as a Non-Trivial Bundle over S^2]] and [[Thm - Dirac Quantization Condition]]. The Dirac monopole is the *complementary* topological effect: a non-trivial $U(1)$-bundle over a simply-connected base $S^2$, classified by an integer Chern number. The AB effect is a flat connection on a non-trivial $\pi_1$, classified by a continuous parameter in $U(1)$. Together they exhibit the two ways a $U(1)$-gauge field can carry global topological information.
