# Notation Registry

Throughout this topic $M$ is a smooth oriented manifold of dimension $n$, equipped with either a Riemannian metric $g$ (positive definite, signature $(n, 0)$) or a Lorentzian / pseudo-Riemannian metric (signature $(n - s, s)$ with $s \geq 1$). The closed-Riemannian case — $M$ compact, without boundary, $g$ positive definite — is the setting in which the full Hodge decomposition theorem holds, and is the default unless explicitly stated otherwise.

**Standing convention — the sign of $\star\star$.** Two sources of sign ambiguity tangle through this chapter and are worth pinning down before notation is fixed. The first is the *orientation convention*: we use the standard "positively-oriented orthonormal frame yields volume $+1$" convention, so $\operatorname{vol}_n = \sigma^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \sigma^n$ on a positively oriented orthonormal coframe. The second is the *metric-signature sign* in $\star\star$. On a $\mathrm{Riemannian}$ $n$-manifold, $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)}$ on $k$-forms. On a pseudo-Riemannian manifold of signature $(n-s, s)$, $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k) + s}$. The two formulas agree at $s = 0$. The sole consequence for Lorentzian 4D spacetime (signature $(3, 1)$, so $s = 1$): $\star\star = -(-1)^{k(4-k)}$, and in particular $\star\star = -1$ on $2$-forms — this is the sign that makes $\star F = F$ legitimate on Minkowski self-dual electromagnetic fields.

> [!warning] Convention: codifferential sign
> The codifferential $\delta = d^* = -\,\mathrm{sgn}\,\star d \star$ has *two* common sign conventions in the literature. Frankel writes the Riemannian case as $d^* = (-1)^{n(p+1)+1}\star d \star$ acting on $p$-forms; Warner and Lee write the same formula. Some sources (de Rham, certain physics texts) absorb a sign in $\star$ and write $\delta = -\star d \star$. We follow Frankel: $\delta = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d \star$ in the Riemannian case, and the sign is whatever makes $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle = \langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle$ hold on a closed oriented manifold. Converting to the other convention only flips the sign of $\delta$, which does not affect harmonicity ($\delta\omega = 0 \iff -\delta\omega = 0$) nor the Hodge Laplacian ($\Delta = d\delta + \delta d$ is unchanged since $\delta$ appears bilinearly).

- $\Omega^k(M)$ — smooth differential $k$-forms on $M$; $\Omega^0(M) = C^\infty(M)$
- $d : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k+1}(M)$ — the [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold|exterior derivative]], satisfying $d^2 = 0$
- $\wedge$ — the [[Def - The Wedge Product on a Manifold|wedge product]]
- $g$ — the [[Def - Riemannian Metric|Riemannian metric]] (or Lorentzian metric); $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_g$ — induced pointwise inner product on tensors
- $\operatorname{vol}_n$ or $\operatorname{vol}_g$ — the [[Def - Riemannian Volume Form|Riemannian volume form]] determined by $g$ and the [[Def - Orientation of a Smooth Manifold|orientation]]
- $\star : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{n-k}(M)$ — the **Hodge star operator**, characterized by $\alpha \wedge \star\beta = \langle\alpha, \beta\rangle\,\operatorname{vol}_n$
- $\delta = d^*$ — the **codifferential**, $\delta : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k-1}(M)$, the formal $L^2$-adjoint of $d$
- $\Delta = d\delta + \delta d$ — the **Hodge Laplacian** (or Laplace–de Rham operator); $\Delta : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^k(M)$
- $\nabla^2 := -\Delta$ — occasionally used so that $\nabla^2 f$ agrees in sign with the usual Riemannian [[Def - Riemannian Metric|Laplace–Beltrami]] operator
- $\mathcal{H}^k(M) = \{\omega \in \Omega^k(M) : \Delta\omega = 0\}$ — the space of **harmonic $k$-forms**
- $\langle\alpha, \beta\rangle_{L^2} = \int_M \alpha \wedge \star\beta = \int_M \langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_g\, \operatorname{vol}_n$ — the **$L^2$ inner product** on forms; finite when $M$ is compact or supports are compact
- $\|\omega\|_{L^2}^2 = \langle\omega, \omega\rangle_{L^2}$ — the $L^2$ norm of $\omega$
- $H^k_{dR}(M) = \ker d / \operatorname{im} d$ — the [[Def - de Rham Cohomology|de Rham cohomology]] in degree $k$
- $b_k(M) = \dim H^k_{dR}(M)$ — the $k$-th **Betti number** of $M$
- $\operatorname{Ric}$ — the Ricci tensor of $g$; $\operatorname{Ric}(X, X) = R_{ij}X^i X^j$
- $\nabla$ — the [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Statement)|Levi-Civita connection]] of $g$; $\nabla^*$ — its formal $L^2$-adjoint on tensor fields
- $G : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^k(M)$ — the **Green operator**, inverse of $\Delta$ on the orthogonal complement of $\mathcal{H}^k$

The wider differential-geometry conventions are in [[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases]]. The exterior-form notation is in [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]].

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: **every de Rham cohomology class on a closed Riemannian manifold contains a unique form of minimum $L^2$ norm, and that form is the harmonic representative.** The theorem extracts, out of a topological invariant defined by an enormous and unwieldy quotient, a single canonical representative satisfying a second-order elliptic PDE — and that PDE is the natural geometric Laplacian on forms. Hodge theory is what happens when you add a Riemannian metric to the de Rham complex and ask what extra structure appears.

Three problems motivate the chapter, and each is solved by a different facet of the same machinery. The first is **how do you choose a canonical representative for a cohomology class?** A class $[\omega] \in H^k_{dR}(M)$ is, formally, an equivalence class of closed forms modulo exact forms — an infinite-dimensional family, parameterized by the choice of primitive $\eta$ in $\omega + d\eta$. There is no algebraic procedure to single out one. But once $M$ carries a Riemannian metric, every $L^2$ class contains a unique form orthogonal to all exact forms, and that form turns out to satisfy $d\omega = 0 = \delta\omega$ — both closed and coclosed. The selection is canonical: it depends only on $g$. The PDE $\Delta\omega = 0$ that this form satisfies is elliptic, and its kernel is finite-dimensional — so on a closed manifold the Betti numbers $b_k$ are computed as [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]] of solution spaces of an elliptic equation. The whole topological invariant becomes analytic.

The second problem is **how do you split a form into "interesting" and "uninteresting" parts?** A general $k$-form $\beta$ contains exact information ($d\beta$), coexact information ($\delta\beta$), and a [[Def - Residue|residue]] that is both closed and coclosed. The Hodge decomposition

$$\Omega^k(M) = \mathcal{H}^k(M) \oplus d\Omega^{k-1}(M) \oplus \delta\Omega^{k+1}(M)$$

makes this triple decomposition orthogonal in $L^2$ and exhaustive. It is the form-version of the Helmholtz decomposition of vector fields on $\mathbb{R}^3$ — a vector field splits uniquely as gradient plus curl plus harmonic — and that classical result reappears as the $n = 3$, $k = 1$ case. The decomposition's deepest use is in elliptic PDE theory: Poisson's equation $\Delta\alpha = \rho$ has a solution iff $\rho$ is orthogonal to the kernel $\mathcal{H}^k$, and the solution is unique modulo $\mathcal{H}^k$ — the Fredholm alternative for the [[Def - Hodge Laplacian|Hodge Laplacian]], derived not from spectral theory but from the geometry of the splitting.

The third problem is **how do curvature and topology constrain each other?** Bochner's theorem is the prototype: a closed Riemannian manifold with positive Ricci curvature has $H^1_{dR}(M; \mathbb{R}) = 0$. The proof is a calculation, not an existence argument: take a harmonic $1$-form $h$, compute $\Delta\langle h, h\rangle$ using the Weitzenböck identity $\Delta = \nabla^*\nabla + \operatorname{Ric}$, integrate over $M$, and use positivity of $\operatorname{Ric}$ to force $h \equiv 0$. The Weitzenböck identity is the key — it splits the Hodge Laplacian into a "rough" piece $\nabla^*\nabla$ (which is manifestly nonnegative) and a curvature piece. When the curvature piece is also nonnegative (or positive), harmonic forms are forced to be parallel (or zero), and Hodge theory then converts this to a topological obstruction. The Bochner technique is the prototype of every modern result connecting curvature to topology — it is how positive scalar curvature obstructs spin cobordism in the Lichnerowicz formula, how Ricci flow controls cohomology, how the Atiyah–Singer index theorem refines into geometric inequalities.

The structural backbone of the chapter is the orthogonal triple

$$\Omega^k(M) \;=\; \underbrace{\mathcal{H}^k(M)}_{\text{closed and coclosed}} \;\oplus\; \underbrace{d\Omega^{k-1}(M)}_{\text{exact}} \;\oplus\; \underbrace{\delta\Omega^{k+1}(M)}_{\text{coexact}}$$

with the three summands mutually orthogonal in the $L^2$ inner product $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle = \int_M \alpha \wedge \star\beta$. Everything in the chapter is either the construction of this decomposition (the Hodge star, the [[Def - The Codifferential|codifferential]], the Hodge Laplacian, and Hodge's theorem), its corollaries (canonical harmonic representatives, Poincaré duality), or its applications (Bochner's theorem, the Weitzenböck identity, Maxwell's equations on a curved spacetime).

**Audience-assumption paragraph.** The reader should be comfortable with smooth manifolds and differential forms ([[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases]], [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]]), with the exterior derivative, wedge product, and pullback ([[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold]], [[Def - The Wedge Product on a Manifold]], [[Def - Pullback of a Differential Form on a Manifold]]), with orientation, integration, and Stokes' theorem ([[Differential Geometry IX — Orientation, Integration, and Stokes' Theorem]], [[Thm - Stokes' Theorem on Manifolds]]), and with de Rham cohomology ([[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius]], [[Def - de Rham Cohomology]]). The Riemannian-metric side — the metric tensor, the musical isomorphism, the volume form, and the Levi-Civita connection — is in [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds]] and [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation]]. The Lorentzian-signature parts of the chapter additionally use [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric]] from special relativity. The Bochner technique requires familiarity with the Ricci tensor, scheduled in [[Riemannian Geometry III — Riemann Curvature and Topology]].

# Concept Map

## §1.1 The Hodge Star Operator

- **[[Def - The L2 Inner Product on Differential Forms]]**
	- The pointwise inner product of two $k$-forms on a (pseudo-)Riemannian manifold is $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_g(p) = \alpha_I(p)\beta^I(p)$ summed over increasing multi-indices, with indices raised by the metric; it is positive definite in the Riemannian case and indefinite in the pseudo-Riemannian case. Integrating against the volume form gives the **$L^2$ inner product** $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_{L^2} = \int_M \langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_g\, \operatorname{vol}_n$, well-defined for compactly supported forms (and for all smooth forms when $M$ is compact). On a closed Riemannian manifold, $\Omega^k(M)$ becomes a pre-Hilbert space under this inner product, and the Hodge star, codifferential, and Laplacian are all defined relative to it. Completion gives the space $L^2 \Omega^k(M)$ of square-integrable $k$-forms.

- **[[Def - The Hodge Star Operator]]**
	- The **Hodge star** $\star : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{n-k}(M)$ is the unique $C^\infty(M)$-linear map satisfying $\alpha \wedge \star\beta = \langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_g\,\operatorname{vol}_n$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in \Omega^k(M)$. On an oriented orthonormal coframe $(\sigma^1, \dots, \sigma^n)$ with $\sigma^I = \sigma^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \sigma^{i_k}$ for an increasing multi-index $I$, $\star\sigma^I = \pm\sigma^J$ where $J$ is the complementary multi-index and the sign is the orientation sign; in particular $\star 1 = \operatorname{vol}_n$ and $\star\operatorname{vol}_n = 1$ on a Riemannian manifold. The star is an isometry $\Omega^k \to \Omega^{n-k}$ for the pointwise inner product. The double star is $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)}$ on a Riemannian manifold and $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)+s}$ in signature $(n-s, s)$; in 4D Lorentzian spacetime $\star\star = -1$ on $2$-forms, the sign that makes self-duality $\star F = \pm F$ consistent.

- **[[Thm - Properties of the Hodge Star]]**
	- The Hodge star $\star : \Omega^k \to \Omega^{n-k}$ is a pointwise $C^\infty(M)$-linear isomorphism that satisfies (i) $\star 1 = \operatorname{vol}_n$ and $\star \operatorname{vol}_n = \pm 1$ (sign by signature); (ii) $\langle \star\alpha, \star\beta\rangle = \pm \langle \alpha, \beta\rangle$ (sign by signature); (iii) $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)+s}\,\mathrm{id}$; (iv) on functions $\star f = f\operatorname{vol}_n$; (v) in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the Euclidean metric, $\star(dx) = dy \wedge dz$, $\star(dx \wedge dy) = dz$, and the same with cyclic permutation — so $\star$ is the source of the cross product and the curl. The defining identity is exactly bookkeeping: the metric is needed to identify $\Omega^k$ with $\Omega^{n-k}$ as vector spaces of equal dimension $\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n}{n-k}$, and $\star$ is the natural pointwise isometry under this identification.

- **[[Def - Self-Dual and Anti-Self-Dual Forms]]**
	- On a $4$-manifold with Riemannian signature, $\star\star = 1$ on $2$-forms, so the eigenvalues of $\star$ acting on $\Omega^2$ are $\pm 1$ and one has the orthogonal decomposition $\Omega^2(M) = \Omega^2_+(M) \oplus \Omega^2_-(M)$ into self-dual ($\star\omega = \omega$) and anti-self-dual ($\star\omega = -\omega$) forms. On Lorentzian $4$D, $\star\star = -1$ on $2$-forms, so the eigenvalues of $\star$ are $\pm i$ and self-duality is a complex condition: one decomposes $\Omega^2(M; \mathbb{C}) = \Omega^2_+(M; \mathbb{C}) \oplus \Omega^2_-(M; \mathbb{C})$ with $\star = \pm i$ on each piece. The self-dual / anti-self-dual decomposition is the entire substrate of instanton theory in **Yang–Mills theory**: a connection's curvature $F$ decomposes as $F = F_+ + F_-$, and the **(anti-)self-dual** connections satisfying $F_\pm = 0$ minimize the Yang–Mills energy in their topological class.

- **[[Ex - Hodge Star on R^3 Recovers Cross Product and Scalar Triple Product]]** (⭐)
	- Compute the Hodge star on every basis $k$-form in Euclidean $\mathbb{R}^3$ and verify that $\star(a \wedge b)$ for $1$-forms $a, b$ equals the $1$-form dual to the cross product $\vec a \times \vec b$, and that $\star(a \wedge b \wedge c)$ is the scalar triple product.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Yang–Mills Self-Duality *(from Gauge Theory)*
> The decomposition $\Omega^2 = \Omega^2_+ \oplus \Omega^2_-$ is the algebraic shell of the entire **Yang–Mills self-duality** story. A connection $A$ on a principal $G$-bundle over a Riemannian $4$-manifold has curvature $F_A \in \Omega^2(M; \mathrm{ad}\,P)$; the Yang–Mills energy is $\|F_A\|^2 = \|F_+\|^2 + \|F_-\|^2$, and the topological charge (second Chern number) is $\|F_+\|^2 - \|F_-\|^2$. So energy is bounded below by the absolute value of the charge, and equality holds iff $F_- = 0$ ($A$ self-dual) or $F_+ = 0$ ($A$ anti-self-dual). These are the **[[Def - Instanton|instantons]]** of [[Gauge Theory IV — Yang–Mills Fields and Instantons]]; the BPST instanton on $\mathbb{R}^4$ is the explicit minimizer.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Hodge Theory on Complex Manifolds *(from Complex Geometry)*
> On a Kähler manifold the Hodge star refines: $d = \partial + \bar\partial$ and one has a separate **$\bar\partial$-Laplacian** $\Delta_{\bar\partial} = \bar\partial\bar\partial^* + \bar\partial^*\bar\partial$ whose harmonic forms compute **Dolbeault cohomology** $H^{p,q}_{\bar\partial}(M)$. On a compact Kähler manifold the three Laplacians agree up to a factor of 2: $\Delta_d = 2\Delta_\partial = 2\Delta_{\bar\partial}$. This gives the **Hodge decomposition** $H^k(M; \mathbb{C}) = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(M)$ with **Hodge symmetry** $h^{p,q} = h^{q,p}$, the central structure theorem for compact Kähler geometry and the gateway to the **Hodge conjecture**.

## §1.2 The Codifferential and the Hodge Laplacian

- **[[Def - The Codifferential]]**
	- The **codifferential** is the operator $\delta = d^* : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k-1}(M)$ defined by $\delta = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d\star$ on a Riemannian $n$-manifold (with an extra sign in the pseudo-Riemannian case, by signature). It satisfies $\delta^2 = 0$, since $\delta^2 = \pm\star d\star\star d\star = \pm\star d^2\star = 0$ via $d^2 = 0$. On a closed oriented Riemannian manifold $\delta$ is the formal $L^2$-adjoint of $d$, meaning $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle = \langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle$ for all $\alpha \in \Omega^{k-1}, \beta \in \Omega^k$ — the integration by parts has no boundary term. In coordinates the codifferential of a $k$-form $\beta = \beta_{i_1\cdots i_k}\,dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k}$ is essentially the divergence: $(\delta\beta)_K = -\nabla^j\beta_{jK}$, justifying the name "divergence on forms".

- **[[Thm - Codifferential is the Adjoint of d]]**
	- On a closed oriented Riemannian manifold $M$ and forms $\alpha \in \Omega^{k-1}(M)$, $\beta \in \Omega^k(M)$, the codifferential $\delta = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d\star$ is the formal $L^2$-adjoint of $d$: $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle_{L^2} = \langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle_{L^2}$. The proof is one application of Stokes' theorem: $d(\alpha \wedge \star\beta) = d\alpha \wedge \star\beta + (-1)^{k-1}\alpha \wedge d\star\beta$, the integral over $M$ vanishes by [[Thm - Stokes' Theorem on Manifolds|Stokes' theorem]] on a manifold without boundary, and rewriting $\alpha \wedge d\star\beta$ as $\alpha \wedge \star(\star^{-1} d\star\beta)$ identifies the sign convention. On a manifold with boundary the same calculation gives $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle - \langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle = \int_{\partial M}\alpha \wedge \star\beta$, with the boundary term controlled by tangential / normal boundary conditions.

- **[[Def - Hodge Laplacian]]**
	- The **Hodge Laplacian** (or **Laplace–de Rham operator**) is $\Delta = d\delta + \delta d : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^k(M)$. It is a second-order elliptic operator on a Riemannian manifold (degenerate in pseudo-Riemannian signature — on Minkowski space, $\Delta$ on functions becomes the d'Alembertian $\Box = \partial_t^2 - \nabla^2$, which is hyperbolic, not elliptic). On functions it agrees with the negative of the Laplace–Beltrami operator: $\Delta f = -\nabla^2 f = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_i(\sqrt{|g|}\,g^{ij}\partial_j f)$ — the sign convention makes $\Delta$ a nonnegative operator on a closed Riemannian manifold, with eigenvalues $\geq 0$. The Hodge Laplacian is self-adjoint, $\langle\Delta\omega, \eta\rangle = \langle\omega, \Delta\eta\rangle$, and commutes with $d$, $\delta$, and $\star$ (Problem 14.2.2 in Frankel).

- **[[Def - Harmonic Form]]**
	- A form $\omega \in \Omega^k(M)$ is **harmonic** if $\Delta\omega = 0$. The space of harmonic $k$-forms is denoted $\mathcal{H}^k(M)$. On a *closed* Riemannian manifold, harmonic is equivalent to "closed and coclosed": $\Delta\omega = 0 \iff d\omega = 0$ and $\delta\omega = 0$. The proof is an inner-product calculation: $0 = \langle\Delta\omega, \omega\rangle = \langle d\omega, d\omega\rangle + \langle\delta\omega, \delta\omega\rangle$, both nonnegative, so both must vanish. This is *false* on a noncompact manifold or one with boundary: the function $f(x, y) = \mathrm{Re}\,e^{x+iy} = e^x\cos y$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is harmonic (in the function sense $\nabla^2 f = 0$, equivalently $\Delta f = 0$ for the Hodge Laplacian) but not constant. Closedness/coclosedness is the working condition on a closed manifold.

- **[[Ex - Maxwell's Equations Use the Codifferential]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that the source-free Maxwell equations on a Lorentzian $4$-manifold reduce to $dF = 0$ and $\delta F = 0$ for the electromagnetic $2$-form $F$, and verify that this is equivalent to $dF = 0$ and $d\star F = 0$. Recover the classical $\nabla \cdot \vec E = 0$, $\nabla \times \vec B - \partial_t \vec E = 0$, $\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$, $\nabla \times \vec E + \partial_t \vec B = 0$ by component expansion.

- **[[Ex - Computing the Hodge Star on S^2]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Using the round metric on $S^2$ in spherical coordinates $(\theta, \varphi)$, compute $\star 1$, $\star d\theta$, $\star d\varphi$, $\star(d\theta \wedge d\varphi)$, and verify $\star\star = (-1)^{k(2-k)}\,\mathrm{id}$. Use the result to compute the Laplace–Beltrami operator on a function in $(\theta, \varphi)$ via $\Delta f = -\star d\star df$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Elliptic Operator Theory *(from Functional Analysis and PDE)*
> The Hodge Laplacian is the prototype of an **elliptic differential operator**: a second-order linear operator on a vector bundle whose principal symbol is invertible. Every fact about $\mathcal{H}^k$ — finite-dimensionality of the kernel, smoothness of harmonic forms (elliptic regularity), the Fredholm alternative — comes from the general theory of elliptic operators on closed manifolds. The Hodge theorem is the canonical example in any elliptic-operator-theory course, and the proof (via the parametrix construction or via heat kernels) is the model for every subsequent index-theoretic argument. The **Atiyah–Singer index theorem** computes the index of a general elliptic operator topologically, with the Hodge Laplacian's index ($\chi(M)$, the Euler characteristic) as the foundational example.

## §1.3 The Hodge Decomposition

- **[[Def - The de Rham Cohomology Pairing]]**
	- On a closed oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold $M$, the integration pairing $H^k_{dR}(M) \times H^{n-k}_{dR}(M) \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by $([\alpha], [\beta]) \mapsto \int_M \alpha \wedge \beta$ is well-defined (Stokes makes the answer independent of representatives) and **nondegenerate**. Nondegeneracy is **Poincaré duality** in its de Rham form: every cohomology class in $H^k$ pairs nontrivially with some class in $H^{n-k}$. The Hodge star realizes the pairing concretely: the harmonic representative of $[\beta]$ is paired with $[\alpha]$ via $\int \alpha \wedge \star\,\text{harmonic}(\beta)$, which equals the $L^2$ inner product of $\alpha$ with the harmonic representative of $\beta$.

- **[[Def - Green Operator (Hodge)]]**
	- The **Green operator** $G : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^k(M)$ on a closed oriented Riemannian manifold is the bounded inverse of $\Delta$ restricted to the orthogonal complement of $\mathcal{H}^k$. Explicitly: for any $\beta \in \Omega^k(M)$, write the Hodge decomposition $\beta = h + d\delta\alpha + \delta d\alpha$ (with $h \in \mathcal{H}^k$); then $G\beta := \alpha - \langle\alpha, h_\alpha\rangle$ where the harmonic part of $\alpha$ is subtracted off (so $G\beta$ is itself orthogonal to $\mathcal{H}^k$). The Green operator satisfies $\Delta G\beta = \beta - H\beta$ where $H$ is the $L^2$-projection onto $\mathcal{H}^k$, equivalently $\beta = H\beta + \Delta G\beta$ — this is the Hodge decomposition with the exact and coexact summands packaged as $\Delta G\beta = d\delta G\beta + \delta d G\beta$.

- **[[Thm - Hodge Decomposition Theorem]]**
	- *(flagship)* On a closed oriented Riemannian manifold $M$, every $k$-form $\beta$ decomposes uniquely as $\beta = h + d\alpha + \delta\gamma$ with $h \in \mathcal{H}^k(M)$, $\alpha \in \Omega^{k-1}(M)$, $\gamma \in \Omega^{k+1}(M)$, and the three summands are mutually $L^2$-orthogonal. Equivalently, $\Omega^k(M) = \mathcal{H}^k(M) \oplus d\Omega^{k-1}(M) \oplus \delta\Omega^{k+1}(M)$ as an $L^2$-orthogonal direct sum. The space $\mathcal{H}^k(M)$ of harmonic $k$-forms is finite-dimensional. The proof requires elliptic operator theory: the deep ingredient is that $\Delta : \Omega^k \to \Omega^k$ has closed image with finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel (Fredholm), at which point pure $L^2$-orthogonality arguments split off the kernel and identify the image as exact-plus-coexact forms.

- **[[Thm - Harmonic Forms Represent de Rham Cohomology]]**
	- On a closed oriented Riemannian manifold $M$, the natural map $\mathcal{H}^k(M) \to H^k_{dR}(M)$ sending a harmonic form $h$ to its cohomology class $[h]$ is a vector-space isomorphism. Equivalently, every de Rham cohomology class contains a unique harmonic representative. The proof is the Hodge decomposition applied to a closed form: $\beta = h + d\alpha + \delta\gamma$, closedness forces $d\delta\gamma = 0$, taking $L^2$-pairing with $\delta\gamma$ forces $\delta\gamma = 0$, so $\beta = h + d\alpha$ — exact part absorbed into the cohomology class, leaving a single harmonic representative. Uniqueness is from the orthogonal decomposition: two harmonic forms in the same class differ by an exact form, but that difference must lie in $\mathcal{H}^k \cap d\Omega^{k-1} = \{0\}$.

- **[[Thm - Poincare Duality via Hodge Star]]**
	- On a closed oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold $M$, the Hodge star induces a vector-space isomorphism $\star : \mathcal{H}^k(M) \to \mathcal{H}^{n-k}(M)$ (since $\star$ commutes with $\Delta$). By [[Thm - Harmonic Forms Represent de Rham Cohomology]], this descends to a canonical isomorphism $H^k_{dR}(M) \cong H^{n-k}_{dR}(M)$; in particular, the Betti numbers satisfy $b_k = b_{n-k}$. This is **Poincaré duality** for closed orientable manifolds, derived metric-dependently from Hodge theory but topologically intrinsic. The pairing $H^k \times H^{n-k} \to \mathbb{R}$, $([\alpha], [\beta]) \mapsto \int_M \alpha \wedge \beta$, is the nondegenerate bilinear form behind the duality, and the harmonic representative of $\star[\beta]$ is the metric realization of $\beta$'s dual class.

- **[[Ex - Harmonic 1-Forms on the Torus]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that on the flat $n$-torus $T^n = \mathbb{R}^n / \mathbb{Z}^n$, the space of harmonic $1$-forms is exactly the constant-coefficient $1$-forms $\sum_i c_i\, dx^i$ with $c_i \in \mathbb{R}$. Conclude $\dim \mathcal{H}^1(T^n) = n = b_1(T^n)$, verifying the Hodge theorem in this case and confirming Poincaré duality $b_k = b_{n-k}$ via $\dim \mathcal{H}^k(T^n) = \binom{n}{k}$.

- **[[Ex - Self-Duality of the Electromagnetic Tensor in Minkowski]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Compute $\star F$ for the electromagnetic $2$-form $F = E_i\, dx^i \wedge dt + B_{ij}\, dx^i \wedge dx^j$ in Minkowski space, and show that the Maxwell equations $dF = 0$, $d\star F = 0$ split into the four classical equations (Gauss, Ampere, no monopoles, Faraday). Discuss why $\star\star = -1$ on $2$-forms in Lorentzian $4$D makes "$F$ is self-dual" ($\star F = F$) inconsistent over $\mathbb{R}$ but consistent over $\mathbb{C}$ (with eigenvalues $\pm i$), and how this preview's the self-dual / anti-self-dual decomposition that becomes consistent over $\mathbb{R}$ on a Riemannian $4$-manifold ([[Gauge Theory IV — Yang–Mills Fields and Instantons]]).

> [!tip] Unlocked: Heat-Kernel Proof of the Hodge Theorem *(from Geometric Analysis)*
> The original proof of the Hodge theorem (de Rham–Kodaira, refined by Warner) constructs the Green operator via a parametrix for the elliptic operator $\Delta$. An alternative — and conceptually deeper — proof uses the **heat semigroup** $e^{-t\Delta}$ on forms. As $t \to \infty$, $e^{-t\Delta}\omega$ converges to the harmonic projection of $\omega$ in $L^2$; the operator $\int_0^\infty (e^{-t\Delta} - H)\,dt$ is the Green operator $G$. The heat-kernel approach generalizes directly to noncompact manifolds (with controlled geometry) and is the foundation of the **heat-equation proof of the Atiyah–Singer index theorem** — the supertrace of the heat kernel as $t \to 0$ gives the topological index, as $t \to \infty$ gives the analytic index, and both must be equal.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Hodge Theory on Manifolds with Boundary *(from PDE Theory)*
> On a compact Riemannian manifold with boundary, the Hodge decomposition splits according to boundary conditions: imposing **tangential boundary conditions** $i^*\omega = 0$ gives "relative" harmonic forms representing $H^k(M, \partial M)$; imposing **normal boundary conditions** $i^*(\star\omega) = 0$ gives "absolute" harmonic forms representing $H^k(M)$. These two cohomology [[Def - Group|groups]] are related by **Lefschetz duality** $H^k(M) \cong H^{n-k}(M, \partial M)$, the manifold-with-boundary analogue of Poincaré duality. The boundary-value version of Hodge theory is the foundation of physical applications: incompressible fluid flow in a vessel, electrostatic equilibrium with conducting boundaries, magnetostatics — every "harmonic field with prescribed boundary behavior" problem.

## §1.4 Bochner Technique and Curvature Bounds

- **[[Def - Hodge Number]]**
	- The **$k$-th Hodge number** of a closed Riemannian manifold is $h^k(M) := \dim \mathcal{H}^k(M)$. By [[Thm - Harmonic Forms Represent de Rham Cohomology|the Hodge theorem]], $h^k(M) = b_k(M)$, the $k$-th Betti number, so the Hodge numbers are topological invariants in the real-Riemannian setting. The notation $h^k$ becomes essential in the **Kähler / complex** case, where it bifurcates into **Hodge numbers** $h^{p,q}(M) = \dim H^{p,q}(M)$ for the Dolbeault cohomology, refining the Betti numbers via $b_k = \sum_{p+q=k} h^{p,q}$. The Hodge diamond of $h^{p,q}$ for a compact Kähler manifold satisfies Hodge symmetry $h^{p,q} = h^{q,p}$ and Serre duality $h^{p,q} = h^{n-p,n-q}$, and is the central invariant of complex projective varieties.

- **Weitzenböck formula for the Hodge Laplacian on $1$-forms**
	- For a $1$-form $\omega$ on a Riemannian manifold, $\Delta\omega = \nabla^*\nabla\omega + \operatorname{Ric}(\omega^\sharp, \cdot)$, where $\nabla^*\nabla$ is the **rough Laplacian** (the negative trace of the Hessian, $-g^{ij}\nabla_i\nabla_j\omega$) and the curvature term $\operatorname{Ric}(\omega^\sharp, \cdot)$ contracts the Ricci tensor against the metric dual of $\omega$. The formula was discovered independently by Weitzenböck and Bochner. Higher-degree analogues exist (Lichnerowicz formula), with the curvature term involving the full Riemann curvature tensor, but the $1$-form case is the cleanest and the one used in Bochner's theorem. The Weitzenböck formula splits the Hodge Laplacian into a manifestly nonnegative part ($\nabla^*\nabla$ has nonnegative $L^2$-pairing by $\langle\nabla^*\nabla\omega, \omega\rangle = \|\nabla\omega\|^2 \geq 0$) and a curvature part, so positivity of $\operatorname{Ric}$ forces $\Delta\omega = 0 \Rightarrow \omega = 0$.

- **[[Thm - Bochner's Theorem]]**
	- A closed Riemannian manifold $M^n$ with positive Ricci curvature (i.e., $\operatorname{Ric}(X, X) > 0$ for all $X \neq 0$) has $H^1_{dR}(M; \mathbb{R}) = 0$, so the first Betti number is $b_1(M) = 0$. The proof is a calculation: take a harmonic $1$-form $h$, expand $\frac{1}{2}\Delta|h|^2 = -\operatorname{Ric}(h^\sharp, h^\sharp) - |\nabla h|^2$ via the Weitzenböck formula, integrate over $M$; the left side vanishes ($\int_M \Delta|h|^2 = 0$ on a closed manifold by Stokes), and both terms on the right are nonpositive — they must each vanish, so $h^\sharp = 0$ pointwise. With nonnegative (rather than positive) Ricci, the same calculation forces harmonic $1$-forms to be **parallel** ($\nabla h = 0$), constraining $b_1 \leq n$ with equality forcing $M$ to be a flat torus quotient.

- **[[Ex - First Betti Number Bounds via Bochner's Theorem]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Show that the round sphere $S^n$ has $b_1 = 0$ by computing its Ricci curvature ($\operatorname{Ric} = (n-1)g$) and invoking Bochner's theorem, then verify directly via the de Rham complex on $S^n$. Show by contrast that the flat torus $T^n$ has $\operatorname{Ric} = 0$ and $b_1 = n$, achieving Bochner's nonnegativity bound. Discuss why the hyperbolic space $\mathbb{H}^n$ (with $\operatorname{Ric} = -(n-1)g$) is not covered by Bochner — indeed, closed hyperbolic manifolds have arbitrarily large $b_1$, showing positivity of $\operatorname{Ric}$ is necessary.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Morse Theory and Critical Points *(from Differential Topology)*
> The Hodge decomposition admits a parallel formulation in **Morse theory**: for a Morse function $f$ on a closed manifold, the Betti numbers $b_k$ are bounded by the number of critical points of index $k$ (the **Morse inequalities**), and equality holds for **perfect Morse functions**. The de Rham complex is replaced by the Morse–Smale chain complex generated by critical points, with the differential counting gradient flow lines; the resulting **Morse homology** is isomorphic to singular homology, and is the finite-dimensional combinatorial analogue of the infinite-dimensional Hodge theory. Witten's heat-kernel deformation $d_t = e^{-tf}\,d\,e^{tf}$ interpolates between the two pictures, providing an analytic proof of the Morse inequalities and laying the groundwork for **Floer homology** in infinite-dimensional settings.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Vanishing Theorems in Algebraic Geometry *(from Complex / Algebraic Geometry)*
> The Bochner technique generalizes to **Kodaira vanishing** for ample line bundles: on a projective complex manifold $X$ with an ample line bundle $L$, $H^q(X, \Omega^p_X \otimes L) = 0$ for $p + q > \dim X$. The proof is a Weitzenböck calculation on the bundle-valued Hodge Laplacian, with the curvature of $L$ playing the role of the Ricci curvature. **Kodaira vanishing, Akizuki–Nakano vanishing, Kodaira–Nakano vanishing**, and their many refinements are all Bochner-technique theorems — every vanishing theorem in modern algebraic geometry is a curvature-positivity input to a Weitzenböck-type calculation, with the kernel of an elliptic operator forced to be zero.

> [!tip] Unlocked: $L^2$-Hodge Theory on Noncompact Manifolds *(from Geometric Analysis)*
> On a noncompact Riemannian manifold the Hodge decomposition no longer holds for $\Omega^k$ — the image of $\Delta$ is not closed, and harmonic forms need not represent cohomology classes. The fix is to work with **$L^2$-cohomology**: complete $\Omega^k_c(M)$ in the $L^2$ norm, study the Hodge Laplacian on this Hilbert space, and define $L^2$-harmonic forms as those in $\ker\Delta \cap L^2$. The **Atiyah $L^2$-index theorem** computes the analytic $L^2$-index for actions of discrete groups, with applications to **growth of Betti numbers in coverings** (the Lück approximation theorem). $L^2$-Hodge theory is the analytic foundation of much of modern geometric group theory.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

Hodge-theoretic exercises cluster around five recurring goals, and recognizing which goal you face determines which tool routes to it. The most common is **computing the harmonic representative of a cohomology class**, or equivalently computing $\mathcal{H}^k(M)$ as a vector space — this happens on tori, spheres, Lie groups, and any manifold whose symmetry group acts transitively, because then averaging over the group makes the harmonic projection explicit. A second target is **verifying a form is harmonic**, which on a closed manifold reduces to the joint check $d\omega = 0 = \delta\omega$ via the equivalence on the closed-manifold side. A third is **computing the codimension or rank of an elliptic operator** by Hodge theory: solving Poisson's equation $\Delta\alpha = \rho$ on a closed manifold needs $\rho \perp \mathcal{H}^k$, so the obstruction is exactly $\mathcal{H}^k$'s dimension, which is a Betti number. A fourth is **deriving topological constraints from curvature** via the Bochner technique: input is a curvature inequality (positive Ricci, nonnegative scalar curvature, etc.), output is a Betti number bound or a parallel-forms statement. A fifth is **applying Poincaré duality** to relate cohomology groups in complementary degrees, computing $b_k$ from $b_{n-k}$ when the latter is easier. These five — compute $\mathcal{H}^k$, verify harmonicity, solve Poisson's equation, derive Betti bounds from curvature, apply duality — are the recurring targets, and each is a way of pinning down a cohomological invariant via the metric.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses in Hodge-theoretic problems are equally stereotyped. **A closed (compact, without boundary) oriented Riemannian manifold** — this is the precondition for everything in Hodge theory, since it is when the integration-by-parts producing $\delta = d^*$ has no boundary term, the $L^2$ inner product is nondegenerate, and $\Delta$ has finite-dimensional kernel. **An explicit metric in local coordinates** — to compute $\star$ or $\delta$ or $\Delta$ concretely one needs $g_{ij}$ in coordinates, often spherical / cylindrical / coordinate-symmetric for the manifold in hand. **A symmetry group acting on $M$** — if a connected Lie group $G$ acts on $M$ by isometries, averaging any form over $G$ produces a $G$-invariant form, and on a homogeneous space the harmonic forms are exactly the $G$-invariant ones. **A curvature bound** — positive or nonnegative Ricci, scalar, or sectional curvature — feeds the Weitzenböck formula and produces vanishing or rigidity results. **An explicit closed form whose harmonic representative is sought** — typically built from coordinates or from a known cohomology generator, with the harmonic decomposition extracted by orthogonal projection. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a closed manifold with a symmetry group routes through equivariant averaging to a description of $\mathcal{H}^k$; a curvature bound routes through Weitzenböck to a Betti number bound; an explicit metric routes through the coordinate formulas for $\star$ and $\delta$ to a concrete Laplacian computation; a closed form routes through Hodge decomposition to its harmonic representative.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves that almost every problem in this chapter is assembled from. When stuck, scan and try each one. The chapter is unusual in that nearly every operation requires the closed-Riemannian-manifold hypothesis to be valid — the operations that hold without it are explicitly flagged.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Apply the defining identity $\alpha \wedge \star\beta = \langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\,\operatorname{vol}_n$ to convert wedges into inner products.** This is the working definition of $\star$ and the single most-used identity in Hodge-theoretic computation. *Trigger:* an integral $\int_M \alpha \wedge \star\beta$ appears, or a pointwise wedge product needs to be unpacked as an inner product. *Pattern:* rewrite the wedge as $\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\,\operatorname{vol}_n$, recognize the result as an $L^2$ inner product, and apply orthogonality / Cauchy–Schwarz / adjoint relations.

2. **Use $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)+s}$ to peel off a Hodge star.** *Trigger:* a formula contains $\star\star\omega$ explicitly, or you suspect a double-star can simplify a chain of $\star$s. *Pattern:* the sign is $+1$ unless $k(n-k)$ is odd or you are in pseudo-Riemannian signature with $s$ odd. On Riemannian $4$-manifolds and $2$-forms ($k = n - k = 2$, sign $+1$), this is the algebraic shell for self-duality.

3. **Use $\delta = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d\star$ to compute $\delta\omega$.** *Trigger:* you need $\delta\omega$ explicitly and you already know $\star\omega$, $d(\star\omega)$, and $\star d \star\omega$ in coordinates. *Pattern:* apply the three operators in sequence, track signs carefully. Often $\star d\star$ is easier than computing $\delta$ via the divergence formula $(\delta\beta)_K = -\nabla^j\beta_{jK}$, especially in low dimensions.

4. **Integrate by parts using $\langle d\alpha,\beta\rangle = \langle\alpha,\delta\beta\rangle$ on a closed manifold.** *Trigger:* an $L^2$ inner product of the form $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle$ or $\langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle$ appears, especially in a variational or extremization argument. *Pattern:* the identity has no boundary term on a closed manifold (Stokes kills it); use it to move $d$ off one factor and onto the adjoint $\delta$ on the other. This is the workhorse of Hodge-theoretic proofs.

5. **Check harmonic = closed + coclosed on a closed manifold.** $\Delta\omega = 0 \iff d\omega = 0$ and $\delta\omega = 0$, on a closed Riemannian manifold. *Trigger:* you want to verify $\omega$ is harmonic without computing $\Delta\omega$ directly; or you have $\omega$ harmonic and want to use both $d\omega = 0$ and $\delta\omega = 0$ separately. *Pattern:* on a closed manifold the equivalence is automatic, but on noncompact manifolds and manifolds with boundary it is not — Frankel calls this difference fundamental, and harmonic functions on $\mathbb{R}^n$ (which need not be coclosed in the form sense) illustrate.

6. **Decompose a form via Hodge's theorem.** Any $\beta \in \Omega^k(M)$ on a closed Riemannian manifold splits uniquely as $\beta = h + d\alpha + \delta\gamma$ with $h \in \mathcal{H}^k$, $L^2$-orthogonally. *Trigger:* you have a form and need to extract its harmonic part, or you need to argue that a form is exact / coexact by ruling out the harmonic part. *Pattern:* use orthogonality with known harmonic forms to nail down $h$, then $h$ being subtracted leaves a sum of exact and coexact pieces that can be further pinned down by tests against $d$ and $\delta$.

7. **Solve Poisson's equation $\Delta\alpha = \rho$ via the Fredholm alternative.** $\Delta\alpha = \rho$ has a solution iff $\rho \perp \mathcal{H}^k$, and any two solutions differ by a harmonic form. *Trigger:* a PDE of Laplacian type, with the unknown being a form. *Pattern:* check the orthogonality condition first; if satisfied, the Green operator $G$ gives the unique solution orthogonal to $\mathcal{H}^k$ via $\alpha = G(\rho - H\rho) = G\rho$ (since $H\rho = 0$).

8. **Apply Poincaré duality $\star : \mathcal{H}^k \to \mathcal{H}^{n-k}$.** *Trigger:* you want $b_k$ but $b_{n-k}$ is easier, or vice versa; or you need to identify a particular harmonic form in complementary degree. *Pattern:* if $h$ is harmonic in degree $k$, then $\star h$ is harmonic in degree $n - k$ — apply $\Delta$ and use $[\Delta, \star] = 0$. On a closed orientable $n$-manifold, $b_k = b_{n-k}$ unconditionally.

9. **Average over a symmetry group to extract the harmonic part.** If a compact Lie group $G$ acts on $M$ by [[Def - Isometry|isometries]], then $\omega \mapsto \int_G g^*\omega\,d\mu(g)$ produces a $G$-invariant form, and on a homogeneous space $G/H$ the $G$-invariant forms are exactly the harmonic ones. *Trigger:* $M$ has a transitive isometry group, or even just a continuous one — tori, spheres, projective spaces, Lie groups. *Pattern:* the averaging projector is the harmonic projection in disguise. This is how one computes $\mathcal{H}^*(T^n) = $ constant-coefficient forms, $\mathcal{H}^*(S^n) = $ degree-$0$ and degree-$n$ constants, etc.

10. **Apply the Weitzenböck formula $\Delta = \nabla^*\nabla + \mathrm{curvature}$ to derive curvature-Betti inequalities.** *Trigger:* the problem inputs a curvature condition and asks for a topological output, or vice versa. *Pattern:* compute $\langle\Delta\omega, \omega\rangle$ on a harmonic form, expand via Weitzenböck, use that $\langle\nabla^*\nabla\omega, \omega\rangle = \|\nabla\omega\|^2 \geq 0$, and let the curvature term's sign decide. Positive curvature kills harmonic forms; nonnegative curvature forces them parallel.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating $\Delta$ on functions as the usual Riemannian Laplace–Beltrami operator without sign-checking
> The Hodge Laplacian on $\Omega^0(M) = C^\infty(M)$ is $\Delta f = -\nabla^2 f = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{|g|}}\partial_i(\sqrt{|g|}g^{ij}\partial_j f)$ — note the **negative sign**. Many sources write $\nabla^2 := -\Delta$ to recover the sign that makes the operator nonpositive (so that eigenvalues are $\leq 0$, mirroring the Euclidean $\Delta = \sum \partial_i^2$). The Hodge convention reverses this: $\Delta$ is nonnegative with eigenvalues $\geq 0$. Mixing conventions silently — using $\Delta$ in a formula derived for $-\nabla^2$ — produces sign errors that can flip "positive curvature kills harmonic forms" to "positive curvature is consistent with harmonic forms" and break Bochner's theorem entirely. The operation becomes legal exactly when you fix one convention up-front and stick with it; we adopt $\Delta = d\delta + \delta d$ throughout.

> [!warning] 2. Using "harmonic = closed and coclosed" on a noncompact manifold or one with boundary
> The equivalence $\Delta\omega = 0 \iff d\omega = 0$ and $\delta\omega = 0$ is *false* without compactness and without boundarylessness. The Euclidean harmonic function $e^x\cos y$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ satisfies $\nabla^2 f = 0$ (so $\Delta f = 0$ for the Hodge Laplacian on $0$-forms) but is not coclosed in any nontrivial sense, since $\delta f = 0$ automatically on $0$-forms. The deeper failure is for higher-degree forms: harmonic $1$-forms on $\mathbb{R}^2$ are gradients of harmonic functions, but they need not be coclosed unless we *redefine* harmonicity. The operation becomes legal exactly when $M$ is closed Riemannian; on bounded domains, Frankel reserves the term "**harmonic field**" for forms satisfying $d\omega = 0 = \delta\omega$, distinguishing them from solutions of $\Delta\omega = 0$.

> [!warning] 3. Forgetting that $\star\star = -1$ on $2$-forms in Lorentzian $4$D
> The Riemannian formula $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)}$ gives $\star\star = 1$ on $2$-forms in $4$D, which makes self-duality $\star F = \pm F$ an honest decomposition over $\mathbb{R}$. The Lorentzian formula has an extra factor of $(-1)^s = -1$, so $\star\star = -1$ on $2$-forms in Minkowski space, and "self-duality" over $\mathbb{R}$ becomes inconsistent ($\star F = F$ implies $F = \star\star F = -F$, so $F = 0$). The standard counterexample-temptation: trying to define an instanton on Minkowski space the same way as on Euclidean space, and finding the equations have no nontrivial solutions. The operation becomes legal exactly when you allow complex coefficients ($\star^2 = -1$ has eigenvalues $\pm i$, giving a complex self-dual / anti-self-dual decomposition); or, more usefully, when you Wick-rotate Minkowski to Euclidean signature.

> [!warning] 4. Confusing the Hodge Laplacian on $1$-forms with the rough Laplacian
> A naive guess: $\Delta\omega$ for a $1$-form $\omega = \omega_i dx^i$ is obtained by taking $\nabla^2$ of each component $\omega_i$ — i.e. $(\Delta\omega)_i = \nabla^j\nabla_j\omega_i$, equivalently $\Delta = \nabla^*\nabla$. This is *wrong* on a general Riemannian manifold: the correct formula is $\Delta = \nabla^*\nabla + \operatorname{Ric}$ (Weitzenböck), with an extra curvature term. The two agree only in flat space, where $\operatorname{Ric} \equiv 0$. The standard counterexample: on the round $S^2$, the function $f$ with $\Delta f = 2f$ has $df$ not equal to $\nabla^*\nabla df$ unless one accounts for the Ricci correction $\operatorname{Ric}(df^\sharp) = df^\sharp$. The operation becomes legal exactly when $M$ is Ricci-flat (which is rare), or when the curvature term is being tracked explicitly via Weitzenböck.

> [!warning] 5. Believing the Hodge decomposition holds on a noncompact Riemannian manifold
> On $\mathbb{R}^n$, the decomposition $\Omega^k(\mathbb{R}^n) = \mathcal{H}^k \oplus d\Omega^{k-1} \oplus \delta\Omega^{k+1}$ is *false* for technical functional-analytic reasons: the image of $\Delta$ is not closed in $L^2$, so orthogonal projection onto $\ker\Delta$ does not give the harmonic representative of an $L^2$-class. The Helmholtz decomposition $\vec F = \nabla\phi + \nabla\times \vec A + \vec H$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ holds only with growth conditions on $\vec F$ at infinity; the "harmonic" part $\vec H$ is the part of $\vec F$ with both vanishing curl and vanishing divergence and is not in $L^2$ unless it is zero. The operation becomes legal in the noncompact case exactly when one passes to $L^2$-Hodge theory, working with a closed extension of $\Delta$ on the Hilbert space $L^2\Omega^k$ and accounting for additional cohomology contributions from "infinity" (reduced $L^2$-cohomology).

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this chapter divide into computations (compute $\mathcal{H}^k$, compute $\star\omega$, compute $\delta\omega$, verify a form is harmonic), structural results (prove Hodge decomposition for a specific case, derive Poincaré duality on a specific manifold), and curvature-topology bridges (apply Bochner). Begin by classifying which camp you are in.

If the problem **asks you to compute $\mathcal{H}^k(M)$** for a specific manifold, your route depends on the manifold's symmetry. On a *homogeneous space* $G/H$ with $G$ acting by isometries, the harmonic forms are exactly the $G$-invariant forms — averaging projects a form onto its harmonic part, and a $G$-invariant form is automatically harmonic (because $\Delta$ commutes with isometries and $G$ acts transitively, so any $G$-invariant function or form constant on each orbit is the harmonic projection). On a torus $T^n = \mathbb{R}^n/\mathbb{Z}^n$, the constant-coefficient forms $\sum c_I dx^I$ are translation-invariant and harmonic; in fact they are *all* the harmonic forms, since any other harmonic form would have a Fourier expansion with positive eigenvalue. On a Lie group $G$ with a bi-invariant metric, the harmonic forms are the bi-invariant forms, which can be computed from the Lie algebra cohomology $H^*(\mathfrak{g})$. The general workflow: identify the symmetry, average over it, check the resulting invariant [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] is the kernel of $\Delta$ by comparison with the known Betti number.

If the problem **asks you to compute $\star\omega$ or $\delta\omega$ explicitly**, your route is coordinate computation. The defining identity $\alpha \wedge \star\beta = \langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\,\operatorname{vol}_n$ lets you read off $\star\beta$ component-by-component by testing against a basis of $\alpha$'s. Alternatively, in an orthonormal coframe $(\sigma^I)$ on a (pseudo-)Riemannian manifold, $\star\sigma^I = \pm\sigma^J$ with $J$ the complementary multi-index and the sign tracked by the orientation. For $\delta$, use the formula $\delta = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d\star$ — three operations whose composition is often easier than the coordinate divergence formula $(\delta\beta)_K = -\nabla^j\beta_{jK}$, especially in low dimensions or on a coordinate-symmetric manifold (sphere, hyperbolic space, torus). Verify the result by checking the adjoint identity $\langle d\alpha,\beta\rangle = \langle\alpha,\delta\beta\rangle$ on a simple test form $\alpha$.

If the problem **asks you to verify a form is harmonic**, the routine on a closed Riemannian manifold is the two-step check $d\omega = 0$ and $\delta\omega = 0$, far simpler than computing $\Delta\omega$ directly. The first step is the de Rham condition, often immediate from the form's construction (e.g., constant-coefficient on a torus, the volume form, $dz/z$ on $S^1$ as a complex-coordinate form). The second step is the coclosedness condition — apply $\star$, take $d$ of the result, check it is zero (equivalent to $\delta\omega = 0$ since $\delta\omega = \pm\star d\star\omega$ and $\star$ is invertible). On a non-closed manifold, this shortcut fails: one must compute $\Delta\omega$ directly via $\Delta = d\delta + \delta d$.

If the problem **asks you to find a harmonic representative of a cohomology class**, the Hodge theorem guarantees one exists uniquely, and the procedure is to start with any closed representative $\omega$ and subtract off the exact piece via the Green operator: the harmonic representative is $h = \omega - d\delta G\omega$. In practice, on a manifold with explicit symmetry, the easier route is direct: write down a closed form with the right cohomology class, check by inspection (or by symmetry averaging) that it is harmonic. The torus $T^n$ is the canonical example — the cohomology class $[\sigma^I] \in H^k(T^n)$ has harmonic representative $\sigma^I = dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx^{i_k}$, a constant-coefficient form, which is harmonic by symmetry.

If the problem **involves a curvature-Betti inequality** (Bochner-style), the route runs through the Weitzenböck formula. Take a harmonic form, compute the Laplacian of its pointwise squared length, expand via Weitzenböck into a rough-Laplacian piece (which always integrates to $-\|\nabla h\|_{L^2}^2$ on a closed manifold) and a curvature piece. The curvature piece's sign determines the conclusion: positive Ricci forces $h \equiv 0$ and so $b_1 = 0$; nonnegative Ricci forces $h$ parallel and so $b_1 \leq n$ with rigidity. The same template, with the Riemann tensor in higher degrees, gives generalized Bochner theorems: positive scalar curvature on a spin manifold obstructs harmonic spinors (Lichnerowicz), positive sectional curvature has Synge-type implications. The pattern is always: **harmonic + curvature bound + Weitzenböck + integration on closed manifold $\Rightarrow$ pointwise vanishing or parallelism**.

The meta-strategy threading through everything: **every question in this chapter is the question "what does choosing a metric do to a topological invariant?"** De Rham cohomology is topological — the same Betti numbers regardless of how $M$ is metricized. But the metric selects a *canonical representative* in each class (the harmonic form), and that canonical representative satisfies an elliptic PDE whose spectrum, Sobolev properties, and curvature dependence become geometric invariants of the metric. The topological invariant is unchanged, but the metric structures it. Every Bochner-type theorem is the observation that the metric structuring *constrains* the topological invariant via curvature; every Poincaré-duality theorem is the observation that the structuring *symmetrizes* it via $\star$; every Hodge-decomposition theorem is the observation that the structuring *splits* it into algebraically meaningful pieces.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Hodge Decomposition Theorem|Hodge Decomposition Theorem]]**: $\Omega^k(M) = \mathcal{H}^k(M) \oplus d\Omega^{k-1}(M) \oplus \delta\Omega^{k+1}(M)$ as an $L^2$-orthogonal direct sum on a closed oriented Riemannian manifold. **Typical use:** the master organizational tool for the chapter. Every existence/uniqueness statement in form-valued PDEs on closed Riemannian manifolds factors through this decomposition. Reach for it whenever you have a form and need to extract its harmonic, exact, or coexact part; whenever you need to solve $\Delta\alpha = \rho$ via the Fredholm alternative; or whenever you want to identify a cohomology group with a space of forms satisfying a PDE. Its most-used corollary is the canonical-harmonic-representative theorem, the structural payoff of the whole machinery.

- **[[Thm - Codifferential is the Adjoint of d|δ is the L² adjoint of d]]**: on a closed oriented Riemannian manifold, $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle = \langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle$ for all forms $\alpha, \beta$. **Typical use:** integration by parts on forms. Reach for it whenever you have an integral involving $d$, want to move the derivative onto the other factor, and the manifold is closed (or supports are compact). The disguised use is *negative* — when you want to prove $\delta\omega = 0$ from a closed-manifold condition like minimizing $L^2$ norm in a cohomology class, take the first variation $\delta = d\alpha$, the variational equation reads $\langle\omega, d\alpha\rangle = \langle\delta\omega, \alpha\rangle = 0$ for all $\alpha$, which forces $\delta\omega = 0$.

- **Weitzenböck formula** $\Delta = \nabla^*\nabla + \operatorname{Ric}$ on $1$-forms: **Typical use:** the bridge between curvature and analytic properties of harmonic forms. Reach for it whenever you have a curvature condition (positive / nonnegative Ricci, or general sign information on the Riemann tensor in higher degrees) and want a Betti-number conclusion. The disguised use is in higher Lichnerowicz formulas — for the Dirac operator, the curvature term involves scalar curvature, giving positive-scalar-curvature obstructions to harmonic spinors; for the bundle-valued Laplacian on a Hermitian holomorphic line bundle, the curvature term involves the Chern curvature of the bundle, giving Kodaira vanishing.

- **[[Def - The Hodge Star Operator|Hodge star ⋆]]** as the metric isomorphism $\Omega^k \cong \Omega^{n-k}$: **Typical use:** every Poincaré-duality argument runs through $\star$ either explicitly or via the integration pairing $\int_M \alpha\wedge\star\beta = \langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$. Reach for it whenever you want to swap a degree-$k$ object for a degree-$(n-k)$ one — for the cohomology pairing, for the codifferential's definition, for the self-dual decomposition in $4$D. The disguised use: $\star$ is what makes the form-version of the cross product / curl on $\mathbb{R}^3$ identical to the form $\star d\star$, unifying classical vector calculus identities into a single statement.

- **Harmonic = $G$-invariant on a homogeneous space**: on a homogeneous Riemannian manifold $G/H$ with $G$ acting by isometries, the harmonic forms are exactly the $G$-invariant ones. **Typical use:** computing $\mathcal{H}^*$ on tori, spheres, Lie groups, projective spaces, Grassmannians — every space where a transitive isometry group is available. The disguised use: identifies $H^*(G/H; \mathbb{R})$ with $H^*(\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{h})$ (relative Lie algebra cohomology), turning a smooth-topology computation into a finite-dimensional algebraic one.

# Bridges

1. **Algebraic topology — every de Rham class has a harmonic representative, identifying $H^k_{dR}$ with $\mathcal{H}^k$.** The [[Algebraic Topology I — Singular Homology and the de Rham Theorem|de Rham theorem]] identifies $H^k_{dR}(M)$ with the singular cohomology $H^k(M; \mathbb{R})$, a purely topological invariant. Hodge theory does one better on a closed Riemannian manifold: it picks a canonical representative — the unique harmonic form in each class — and converts the abstract quotient $\ker d / \operatorname{im} d$ into a concrete space of forms $\mathcal{H}^k$ satisfying a second-order elliptic PDE. The dimension count is therefore a Betti number ($\dim\mathcal{H}^k = b_k$), but the harmonic representative itself depends on the choice of metric, so Hodge theory is a metric-enrichment of de Rham theory. Singular homology underlies the entire construction: the harmonic projection of a closed form $\omega$ has the same periods $\int_S \omega$ over each homology cycle $S$ as $\omega$ does, because the difference $\omega - h$ is exact and exact forms integrate to zero over cycles by Stokes.

2. **Riemannian geometry — the Weitzenböck formula uses the Levi-Civita connection and its formal adjoint.** The Weitzenböck formula $\Delta = \nabla^*\nabla + \operatorname{Ric}$ on $1$-forms is the bridge from Hodge theory to [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation|Riemannian geometry]] and to [[Riemannian Geometry III — Riemann Curvature and Topology|the Ricci curvature]]. The rough Laplacian $\nabla^*\nabla$ is built from the [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Statement)|Levi-Civita connection ∇]] and its formal $L^2$-adjoint $\nabla^*$, defined so that $\langle\nabla^*\eta, \omega\rangle = \langle\eta, \nabla\omega\rangle$ for $\omega \in \Omega^1$ and $\eta$ a section of $T^*M \otimes T^*M$ (i.e., a $(0,2)$-tensor). The Ricci tensor appears via a commutator of covariant derivatives: $[\nabla_i, \nabla_j]\omega_k = -R_{ijk}{}^l\omega_l$, and tracing gives the Ricci-dependent piece of the Hodge Laplacian. Higher-degree Weitzenböck formulas (the Lichnerowicz formula on $\Omega^k$) involve the full Riemann tensor, but the $1$-form case is the cleanest and is the prototype for Bochner's theorem.

3. **Gauge theory and Yang–Mills — the self-dual / anti-self-dual decomposition on $4$-manifolds.** On a closed oriented Riemannian $4$-manifold, $\star\star = 1$ on $\Omega^2$, so $\Omega^2 = \Omega^2_+ \oplus \Omega^2_-$ splits orthogonally by sign of the $\star$-eigenvalue. The Yang–Mills functional on a connection $A$ on a principal $G$-bundle is $\mathrm{YM}(A) = \int_M |F_A|^2\operatorname{vol}_g$, and this decomposes as $\|F_+\|^2 + \|F_-\|^2$ via the orthogonal $\star$-eigenspace splitting of the curvature. The topological charge (second Chern number) is $\|F_+\|^2 - \|F_-\|^2 = c_2(P)[M]$, so $\mathrm{YM}(A) \geq |c_2(P)[M]|$ with equality precisely when $F_- = 0$ (self-dual instanton) or $F_+ = 0$ (anti-self-dual). The self-dual decomposition of $\Omega^2$ is therefore the algebraic substrate for the whole theory of [[Gauge Theory IV — Yang–Mills Fields and Instantons|Yang–Mills instantons]], including the BPST instanton on $\mathbb{R}^4$ and the **Donaldson** invariants of $4$-manifolds.

4. **Electromagnetism on curved spacetime — Maxwell's equations in form-language are $dF = 0$ and $\delta F = J$.** In Frankel's chapter, the electromagnetic field is a $2$-form $F$ on a Lorentzian $4$-manifold, the source-free Maxwell equations are $dF = 0$ (homogeneous: no monopoles, Faraday) and $d\star F = 0$ (inhomogeneous: Gauss, Ampere). With a current $3$-form $S$, the inhomogeneous equation becomes $d\star F = 4\pi S$, equivalently $\delta F \propto J$ for the current $1$-form $J$ dual to $S$. The point of the form-language version is that **it transports unchanged from Minkowski to curved spacetime**: the operators $d$ and $\delta$ are defined on any Lorentzian $4$-manifold, and Maxwell's equations on a curved background are exactly the same equations on the curved $M^4$. The classical $\vec E$, $\vec B$ decomposition is coordinate-dependent and breaks under general changes of frame; the $2$-form $F$ is coordinate-independent and survives. This is the prototype of [[Gauge Theory I — Connections on Vector Bundles and the Electromagnetic Connection|gauge-theoretic electromagnetism]] in [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds|relativistic geometry]].

5. **Special relativity — the Lorentzian Hodge star satisfies $\star\star = -1$ on $2$-forms in $4$D Minkowski.** In [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|Minkowski space]], the Lorentzian Hodge star has $\star\star = -1$ on $2$-forms (since $k(n-k) + s = 2(4-2) + 1 = 5$, odd). The consequence is that real self-duality $\star F = F$ forces $F = 0$ — there are no nontrivial real-self-dual electromagnetic fields on Minkowski space. The complex self-duality $\star F = iF$ (eigenvalue $+i$ for the operator $\star$) is consistent over $\mathbb{C}$ and packages $F$ into $F + i\star F$ as a complex self-dual $2$-form; this is the "complex Maxwell tensor" $F^{\mathrm{cmplx}} = \vec E + i\vec B$ familiar from physics. The sign-flip is also why Wick-rotating to Euclidean signature ($s = 0$, $\star\star = +1$ on $2$-forms in $4$D) makes self-duality consistent over $\mathbb{R}$ and enables the Euclidean-signature instanton calculations of Yang–Mills theory.

6. **Complex / Kähler geometry — Dolbeault cohomology and Hodge symmetry.** On a Kähler manifold, the exterior derivative splits as $d = \partial + \bar\partial$ (and the conjugate), and each piece has its own Laplacian: $\Delta_\partial = \partial\partial^* + \partial^*\partial$, $\Delta_{\bar\partial} = \bar\partial\bar\partial^* + \bar\partial^*\bar\partial$, $\Delta_d = d d^* + d^* d$. The **Kähler identities** force $\Delta_d = 2\Delta_\partial = 2\Delta_{\bar\partial}$, and $\Delta_{\bar\partial}$-harmonic $(p,q)$-forms represent **Dolbeault cohomology** $H^{p,q}_{\bar\partial}(M)$. The Hodge decomposition refines to $H^k(M;\mathbb{C}) = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(M)$ with **Hodge symmetry** $h^{p,q} = h^{q,p}$ and **Serre duality** $h^{p,q} = h^{n-p, n-q}$. These constraints make the Hodge diamond of Hodge numbers $h^{p,q}$ the principal invariant of compact Kähler manifolds — and the **Hodge conjecture** is one of the seven Millennium Problems, asking which cohomology classes in $H^{p,p}$ are represented by algebraic cycles.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: Hodge theory metrizes de Rham theory.** De Rham cohomology gives you an equivalence class — closed forms modulo exact forms — and asks no more. Hodge theory takes this same equivalence class and, using only the additional input of a Riemannian metric, picks out a canonical representative: the unique harmonic form in the class. The picking is by a variational principle (minimum $L^2$ norm), and the canonical representative satisfies a second-order elliptic PDE ($\Delta\omega = 0$). The whole machinery is what happens when you ask "what is the natural inner product on a quotient vector space, and which representative does it select?" The answer is "the orthogonal complement to the things being quotiented out," and on $\Omega^k / d\Omega^{k-1}$ that complement is exactly $\ker\delta$, the coclosed forms — together with closedness, this is harmonicity. Every theorem in the chapter is a consequence of this metrization: the Hodge decomposition is the explicit triple-orthogonal split of $\Omega^k$; Poincaré duality is the metric's compatibility with the integration pairing; Bochner's theorem is the curvature dependence of the harmonic representative.

**The true name of "harmonic form" is "$L^2$-minimum representative."** The textbook definition $\Delta\omega = 0$ is the right thing to compute but the wrong thing to think. The operational viewpoint is that a harmonic form is the *unique* form in its cohomology class with smallest $L^2$ norm. The PDE $\Delta\omega = 0$ is the Euler–Lagrange equation for the constrained minimization "minimize $\|\omega\|_{L^2}^2$ subject to $\omega$ closed and $[\omega] = c$ a fixed cohomology class". Every fact about harmonic forms — that they exist (Hodge), are unique (orthogonal-complement uniqueness), satisfy $d\omega = 0 = \delta\omega$ on a closed manifold (the variational equation), are preserved by isometries — is a fact about this minimization. The PDE viewpoint and the variational viewpoint are equivalent (a critical point of $\|\omega\|^2$ on the affine space of closed forms with fixed class is precisely a coclosed closed form), but the variational viewpoint is what makes the whole structure visible.

**Inheritance: every "harmonic equals invariant" statement is the same lemma applied to different structures.** The harmonic forms on a torus are constant-coefficient. The harmonic forms on a Lie group are bi-invariant. The harmonic forms on a homogeneous space $G/H$ are $G$-invariant. Each of these is the same statement: when a symmetry group acts by isometries and orbits cover the manifold, the harmonic projection is identical to the averaging projection. The proof is the same in each case: averaging commutes with the Laplacian (which commutes with isometries), so the averaging is harmonic; and the kernel of $\Delta$ is preserved by symmetries (again by commutation), so harmonic forms are themselves $G$-invariant. The inheritance pattern: a harmonic property on $M$ is inherited from an invariance property under a symmetry group acting on $M$ by isometries. This is why so much of explicit Hodge theory reduces to algebraic computations in the Lie algebra of the symmetry group — finite-dimensional Lie algebra cohomology, in the homogeneous-space case.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: see "closed Riemannian manifold + positive curvature," compute the integral of $|\nabla h|^2$ on a harmonic form.** The Bochner technique is a single computational reflex applied to every curvature-Betti problem: take a candidate harmonic form $h$, compute the Laplacian of its pointwise squared length, expand via Weitzenböck into a (nonpositive) rough-Laplacian piece and a curvature piece, integrate over $M$. The integration kills the rough Laplacian's contribution ($\int_M \Delta|h|^2 = 0$ on a closed manifold), and what remains is the equation $0 \geq -\|\nabla h\|^2 - \int \mathrm{Curv}(h, h)$. Positivity of the curvature forces $\|\nabla h\| = 0$ and $\int\mathrm{Curv}(h,h) = 0$, so $h \equiv 0$ pointwise. Whenever you see a Riemannian manifold with a curvature-positivity hypothesis and a question about Betti numbers, this is the reflex. It is the prototype of every modern result connecting curvature to topology — Lichnerowicz for spinors, Kodaira for line bundles, the entire Bochner–Weitzenböck industry.

**Density as a strategic lever: the Hodge decomposition lifts from a smooth statement to an $L^2$ statement via completion.** The Hodge theorem as stated holds for *smooth* forms on a *closed* manifold; the orthogonal direct sum is in the pre-Hilbert space $\Omega^k(M)$ with the $L^2$ inner product. But the harder, deeper result is the $L^2$ version: on the completion $L^2\Omega^k(M)$, the orthogonal direct sum still holds, with the harmonic part being a finite-dimensional subspace and the exact / coexact parts being closed subspaces. This works because the Laplacian on a closed manifold is an elliptic operator with closed image (a deep functional-analytic fact requiring the elliptic regularity theory of Sobolev spaces), and elliptic regularity says every $L^2$-harmonic form is automatically smooth. So the smooth Hodge decomposition is dense in the $L^2$ Hodge decomposition, and the smooth statement is in fact strong enough — but only because the analytic backbone, ellipticity, lets density propagate to existence. The same propagation pattern recurs throughout PDE theory: prove existence in $L^2$ via Hilbert-space methods, prove regularity to upgrade to smooth solutions.

**Local-to-global propagation is the chapter's deepest principle.** The Hodge Laplacian $\Delta = d\delta + \delta d$ is built entirely from local data — the metric and the de Rham complex are both intrinsic and local. Yet the kernel $\mathcal{H}^k(M)$ has finite dimension equal to the global Betti number $b_k(M)$, which depends on the global topology of $M$ in a deep way. The propagation from local PDE to global invariant is what elliptic operator theory makes possible: ellipticity is a local condition on the principal symbol, but on a closed manifold it forces a global Fredholm property (closed image, finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel). The Atiyah–Singer index theorem makes this propagation explicit and quantitative: the index $\dim\ker\Delta - \dim\mathrm{coker}\,\Delta$ of any elliptic operator on a closed manifold is computable purely from local data on $M$ (specifically from the principal symbol's $K$-theory class), even though both kernel and cokernel are global. Hodge theory is the canonical first example: the index of $d + \delta$ (acting on even forms, mapping to odd forms) is the Euler characteristic $\chi(M)$, an integer computable both as $\sum(-1)^k b_k$ (global, topological) and as $\int_M e(TM)$ (Pfaffian of curvature, local geometric).
