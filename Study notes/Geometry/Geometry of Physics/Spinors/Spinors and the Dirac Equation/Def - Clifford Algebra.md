# Notation

A **quadratic vector space** $(V, Q)$ is a finite-dimensional real vector space $V$ equipped with a quadratic form $Q: V \to \mathbb{R}$ (see [[Def - Quadratic Form]]). The polarization is $B(v, w) = \tfrac{1}{2}(Q(v + w) - Q(v) - Q(w))$, the unique symmetric bilinear form with $B(v, v) = Q(v)$. The **tensor algebra** is $T(V) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} V^{\otimes k}$, with $V^{\otimes 0} = \mathbb{R}$ and multiplication by concatenation $\otimes$. The Clifford algebra is denoted $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ — or $\mathrm{Cl}(V)$ when the quadratic form is clear from context, or $\mathrm{Cl}(p, q)$ for the standard form on $\mathbb{R}^{p+q}$ with signature $(p, q)$ (i.e., $Q(\vec x) = x_1^2 + \cdots + x_p^2 - x_{p+1}^2 - \cdots - x_{p+q}^2$). The unit is $1 \in \mathbb{R} \subset \mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$.

# Axiom Motivation

The Clifford algebra is the answer to the following question: **given a vector space $V$ with a quadratic form $Q$, what is the smallest associative algebra in which "$v^2 = Q(v)$" holds for every $v \in V$?** In other words, we want to *internalize* the quadratic form $Q$ as the squaring operation in some associative algebra extending $V$.

To see why such an algebra should exist and what its structure must be, start with the desiderata: an associative algebra $A$ over $\mathbb{R}$ with a linear map $\iota: V \to A$ satisfying $\iota(v)^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1_A$ for all $v \in V$. The "smallest" such algebra is constructed by taking the **tensor algebra** $T(V)$ (which is the free associative algebra on $V$) and quotienting by the relations we want to impose: $v \otimes v - Q(v) \cdot 1$ for each $v$. This produces $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q) = T(V) / \langle v \otimes v - Q(v) : v \in V\rangle$. The resulting algebra is the **free associative algebra in which $v^2 = Q(v)$**.

Why are these particular relations the right ones? Examine each axiom:

- *The squaring relation $v^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1$* is the *defining* relation; without it we have just the tensor algebra. With it we have an algebra where the quadratic form is encoded operationally as a product.

- *Why not require $vw + wv = 2B(v, w)$ directly* (the "polarised form" of the relation)? This is *equivalent* — applying $v^2 = Q(v)$ to $v + w$ gives $(v + w)^2 = Q(v + w) = Q(v) + 2B(v, w) + Q(w)$, and expanding $(v+w)^2 = v^2 + vw + wv + w^2 = Q(v) + vw + wv + Q(w)$, we deduce $vw + wv = 2B(v, w)$. So the unpolarised and polarised relations cut out the same ideal in $T(V)$.

- *Why associative?* The point is that we want to be able to compose multiplications: $(vw)u = v(wu)$. Without associativity, the algebra would be far less rigid (octonions, exceptional Jordan algebras, etc.).

- *Why an algebra over $\mathbb{R}$ rather than over some field $k$?* The construction works for any field of characteristic not equal to $2$ (the constraint comes from the polarization formula). Over $\mathbb{C}$ all non-degenerate quadratic forms of a given dimension are isomorphic, so $\mathrm{Cl}(n, \mathbb{C})$ has no signature dependence; over $\mathbb{R}$ the signature $(p, q)$ matters genuinely.

- *Why require $Q$ to be a quadratic form rather than just any function?* The polarization formula needs $B$ to be *bilinear*, which forces $Q$ to be a degree-$2$ homogeneous polynomial — exactly a quadratic form.

What if we strengthened the requirement and asked for *commutativity*? The defining relation $vw + wv = 2B(v, w)$ would then say $2vw = 2B(v, w)$, so $vw = B(v, w) \in \mathbb{R}$ — and the algebra generated by $V$ would be exactly $V \oplus \mathbb{R}$, a $4$-dimensional thing with vanishing products of $V$-elements: not very interesting. Non-commutativity is what makes Clifford algebras rich; the *amount* of non-commutativity is precisely measured by the antisymmetric part of $vw$, which is $\tfrac{1}{2}(vw - wv) \in \Lambda^2 V$ — this is the wedge product, and the Chevalley isomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q) \cong \Lambda^\bullet V$ (as graded vector spaces) makes this precise.

The motivation that drives the construction in practice: we want an algebra inside which we can take *square roots* of differential operators (Dirac's program) or *square roots* of quadratic forms (Hamilton's question). The Clifford relation $v^2 = Q(v)$ is exactly the algebraic statement "v is a square root of Q(v)", and the existence of an associative algebra where this holds is what makes Dirac's $\not\partial$ possible.

# The Definition

Let $(V, Q)$ be a finite-dimensional real quadratic vector space. The **Clifford algebra** $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ is the associative unital $\mathbb{R}$-algebra given by the quotient
$$\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q) := T(V) \,/\, \langle v \otimes v - Q(v) \cdot 1 : v \in V\rangle,$$
where $T(V) = \bigoplus_{k \geq 0} V^{\otimes k}$ is the tensor algebra and the [[Def - Ideal|ideal]] in the denominator is two-sided. The natural map $\iota: V \to \mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ sends $v$ to the class of $v$ in the quotient; it is injective.

The defining relation can be expressed in two equivalent forms:
- **Unpolarised:** $\iota(v)^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1$ for all $v \in V$.
- **Polarised:** $\iota(v) \iota(w) + \iota(w) \iota(v) = 2B(v, w) \cdot 1$ for all $v, w \in V$.

The two forms are equivalent, as the polarised form follows from the unpolarised by applying it to $v + w$ and expanding.

As a vector space, $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ has dimension $2^n$ where $n = \dim V$: if $\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}$ is a basis of $V$, then
$$\{e_{i_1} e_{i_2} \cdots e_{i_k} : 0 \leq k \leq n, \; 1 \leq i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k \leq n\}$$
is a basis of $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ (with the empty product $k = 0$ being the unit $1$). The Clifford algebra carries a natural **$\mathbb{Z}/2$-grading** $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q) = \mathrm{Cl}^0(V, Q) \oplus \mathrm{Cl}^1(V, Q)$ into "even" and "odd" elements (those expressible as products of even/odd numbers of vectors from $V$).

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The Clifford algebra is characterised by a **universal property** (see [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property]]): $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ is the *initial* associative $\mathbb{R}$-algebra equipped with a linear map $\iota: V \to \mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ satisfying $\iota(v)^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1$. Equivalently, for any associative $\mathbb{R}$-algebra $A$ and any linear map $\varphi: V \to A$ with $\varphi(v)^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1_A$, there is a unique algebra homomorphism $\tilde\varphi: \mathrm{Cl}(V, Q) \to A$ extending $\varphi$ in the sense $\tilde\varphi \circ \iota = \varphi$.

Categorically: $\mathrm{Cl}: (\mathbf{Vec}_{\mathbb{R}})_{\text{quadratic}} \to \mathbf{Alg}_{\mathbb{R}}$ is the left adjoint of the forgetful functor that sends an associative algebra $A$ to the pair $(A, x \mapsto x^2)$ — which is *quadratic* on the linear subspace generated by any element. The Clifford algebra is the free object in the category of associative algebras with a designated quadratic-form-realising subspace.

There is also a **$\mathbb{Z}$-grading** when $Q = 0$ (giving the exterior algebra), and only a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-grading in general. The Chevalley isomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q) \cong \Lambda^\bullet V$ is *as graded vector spaces* (using the symmetrisation map), not as algebras — the Clifford product differs from the exterior product by terms involving $Q$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The Clifford algebra construction unifies several apparently disparate algebraic objects:

1. **$\mathrm{Cl}(0, 0) = \mathbb{R}$** (the trivial case).
2. **$\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}, -x^2) = \mathbb{C}$** — let $e_1$ be a basis of $\mathbb{R}$ with $Q(e_1) = -1$; then $e_1^2 = -1$, so $\mathbb{R}[e_1] = \mathbb{R} \oplus \mathbb{R} e_1 = \mathbb{R} \oplus \mathbb{R} i = \mathbb{C}$.
3. **$\mathrm{Cl}(0, 2) = \mathbb{H}$** — the quaternions, as shown in [[Def - Quaternions]].
4. **$\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) = M_2(\mathbb{C})$** — the Pauli algebra, generated by the Pauli matrices.
5. **$\mathrm{Cl}(1, 3) = M_2(\mathbb{H})$** (or $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 1) = M_4(\mathbb{R})$) — the Dirac algebras.

The full classification (see [[Thm - Classification of Clifford Algebras over R]]) shows that $\mathrm{Cl}(p, q)$ is always a matrix algebra over $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, or $\mathbb{H}$ (or a direct sum of two such), with **8-fold periodicity**: $\mathrm{Cl}(p+8, q) \cong \mathrm{Cl}(p, q) \otimes M_{16}(\mathbb{R})$. This Bott periodicity is the algebraic core of real K-theory.

**True name:** The Clifford algebra is the *universal associative algebra in which a given quadratic form is realised as the squaring operation*. Equivalently, it is the *unique-up-to-iso associative algebra with a linear subspace $V \hookrightarrow A$ satisfying $v^2 = Q(v) \cdot 1$ that is initial with this property*. The universal property is operationally the most useful: it tells you that to construct a homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q) \to A$ for any algebra $A$, you only need to specify a linear map $V \to A$ that satisfies the Clifford relation, and uniqueness/existence are automatic. This is why the Pauli matrices "automatically" generate $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ (their span in $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ satisfies the Clifford relation, so the universal property gives a homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) \to M_2(\mathbb{C})$, which one checks is an isomorphism by dimension count).

Connections to other constructions:

- **The exterior algebra** $\Lambda^\bullet V$ is the Clifford algebra with $Q = 0$: the relation becomes $v \otimes v = 0$, equivalently $v \wedge v = 0$, the defining property of the exterior algebra.
- **The symmetric algebra** $\mathrm{Sym}^\bullet V = \mathbb{R}[V]$ (polynomials on $V^*$) is what you get by quotienting $T(V)$ by the *antisymmetric* relations $v \otimes w - w \otimes v$, not the Clifford relations. Symmetric algebras are commutative; Clifford algebras are not (except in dimension $\leq 1$).
- **Universal enveloping algebras** $U(\mathfrak{g})$ of Lie algebras are constructed from the tensor algebra by quotienting by $X \otimes Y - Y \otimes X - [X, Y]$. They are deformations of the symmetric algebra in the same way that Clifford algebras are deformations of the exterior algebra (Koszul duality).

# Examples / Corollaries

**Example 1: $\mathrm{Cl}(0, 0) = \mathbb{R}$.** No generators, the algebra is just the scalars.

**Example 2: $\mathrm{Cl}(1, 0)$ vs $\mathrm{Cl}(0, 1)$.** With one generator $e$:
- $\mathrm{Cl}(1, 0)$: $Q(e) = +1$, so $e^2 = 1$. The algebra is $\mathbb{R}[e]/(e^2 - 1) \cong \mathbb{R} \oplus \mathbb{R}$ (two copies of $\mathbb{R}$, with idempotents $\tfrac{1}{2}(1 \pm e)$).
- $\mathrm{Cl}(0, 1)$: $Q(e) = -1$, so $e^2 = -1$. The algebra is $\mathbb{R}[e]/(e^2 + 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$ (since $e$ plays the role of $i$).

So signature changes the *isomorphism class* of the Clifford algebra, even in dimension $1$: $\mathrm{Cl}(1, 0) \cong \mathbb{R} \oplus \mathbb{R}$ but $\mathrm{Cl}(0, 1) \cong \mathbb{C}$.

**Example 3: $\mathrm{Cl}(2, 0) = M_2(\mathbb{R})$.** Take generators $e_1, e_2$ with $e_j^2 = 1$ and $e_1 e_2 = -e_2 e_1$. Identify $e_1 = \sigma_1$ (the first Pauli matrix, swap matrix) and $e_2 = \sigma_3$ (diagonal); these are *real* matrices in $M_2(\mathbb{R})$, satisfy $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_3^2 = I$ and $\sigma_1\sigma_3 = -\sigma_3\sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. So $\mathrm{Cl}(2, 0)$ embeds in $M_2(\mathbb{R})$; dimension count ($\dim\mathrm{Cl}(2, 0) = 2^2 = 4 = \dim M_2(\mathbb{R})$) shows the embedding is an isomorphism.

**Example 4: $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) = M_2(\mathbb{C})$.** The Pauli matrices $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$ satisfy $\sigma_j^2 = I$ and $\sigma_j\sigma_k = -\sigma_k\sigma_j$ for $j \neq k$, so they generate a Clifford algebra. The full algebra has basis $\{I, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3, \sigma_1\sigma_2, \sigma_2\sigma_3, \sigma_3\sigma_1, \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = iI\}$ of dimension $8$, matching $\dim_{\mathbb{R}} M_2(\mathbb{C}) = 8$. See [[Ex - Pauli Matrices Generate Cl(R^3)]].

**Example 5: The "volume element"** $\omega = e_1 e_2 \cdots e_n$ is a distinguished element of $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ (depending on the choice of basis, up to sign). Its square is $\omega^2 = (-1)^{n(n-1)/2} \det(g) \cdot 1$, where $g$ is the matrix of $Q$ in the chosen basis. In $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$, $\omega = \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = iI$ (so $\omega^2 = -I$); in $\mathrm{Cl}(1, 3)$, $\omega = \gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3$ and $\omega^2 = -I$ as well, justifying the definition of the chirality matrix $\gamma^5 = i\omega$ with $(\gamma^5)^2 = I$.

**Non-example: a commutative "Clifford algebra".** If we try to impose both Clifford relations and commutativity, we get $vw = wv$, so $vw + wv = 2vw = 2B(v, w)$, i.e., $vw = B(v, w) \in \mathbb{R}$. The resulting algebra is $\mathbb{R} \oplus V$ (with $V$-products in $\mathbb{R}$ via $B$), of dimension $n + 1$ rather than $2^n$. This is not a Clifford algebra in the standard sense — the non-commutativity is essential for the rich structure of $2^n$-dimensional algebras.

**Non-example: dropping associativity.** Without associativity, one gets the **Cayley–Dickson algebras** (octonions $\mathbb{O}$, sedenions $\mathbb{S}$, ...), which are not Clifford algebras. They have related but different structure.

**Calibration check.** A reader should verify: (i) for $\mathrm{Cl}(2, 0)$, compute the product $e_1 e_2 \cdot e_1 = -e_1^2 e_2 = -e_2$, and confirm this matches matrix multiplication of $\sigma_1\sigma_3 \cdot \sigma_1$ in $M_2(\mathbb{R})$; (ii) check that the $2^n$ formula gives the right dimension for $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2) = 4$, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3) = 8$, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4) = 16$; (iii) verify that $\mathrm{Cl}(V, 0) \cong \Lambda^\bullet V$ by noting that the quotient $T(V)/(v \otimes v)$ in dimension $n$ is the standard construction of the exterior algebra.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Spin Group and Spinor Modules *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> Inside the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ sit the **pin group** $\mathrm{Pin}(V, Q) = \langle v \in V : Q(v) = \pm 1\rangle$ and the **spin group** $\mathrm{Spin}(V, Q) = \mathrm{Pin}(V, Q) \cap \mathrm{Cl}^0(V, Q)$ (see [[Def - Pin and Spin Groups]]). The fundamental representations of $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ — irreducible $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$-modules — are the **spinor modules**, and they restrict to representations of $\mathrm{Spin}(V, Q)$ that do not descend to $SO(V, Q)$. The whole apparatus of spinors thus emerges from the Clifford algebra by extracting subgroups and their natural representations; this is what makes Clifford algebras the algebraic foundation of all spinor physics.

> [!tip] Atiyah-Bott-Shapiro Construction *(from K-Theory)*
> The **Atiyah–Bott–Shapiro construction** uses Clifford modules to build the periodic K-theory spectra. Specifically, there is a natural map $M(\mathrm{Cl}(n, 0))/M(\mathrm{Cl}(n+1, 0)) \to KO^{-n}(\mathrm{pt})$ from the abelian group of $\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded $\mathrm{Cl}(n, 0)$-modules (modulo those that extend to $\mathrm{Cl}(n+1, 0)$-modules) into the real K-theory of a point. This map is an isomorphism for all $n$, and the **8-fold periodicity** of Clifford algebras translates *directly* into the **Bott periodicity** of real K-theory: $KO^{-n}(\mathrm{pt}) = KO^{-n-8}(\mathrm{pt})$. This is one of the deepest places in mathematics where pure algebra (Clifford modules) reproduces a topological invariant (homotopy groups of the orthogonal group).

> [!tip] Geometric Algebra
> **Geometric algebra** is a name for the Clifford algebra of $\mathbb{R}^n$ used as a *practical computational tool* in physics and engineering, popularized by David Hestenes. The idea is to treat $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$ as the "natural" algebra of 3-dimensional Euclidean space: vectors are grade-1 elements, areas (oriented planes) are grade-2 bivectors, oriented volumes are grade-3 trivectors, and a "general multivector" can mix all grades. The Clifford product unifies the dot product and the cross product: $\vec a \vec b = \vec a \cdot \vec b + \vec a \wedge \vec b$ (where the wedge produces a bivector, the Hodge-dual of the cross product in 3D). Rotations are realized as conjugation by **rotors** (unit even elements), and the formalism extends naturally to higher dimensions and pseudo-Riemannian signatures.
