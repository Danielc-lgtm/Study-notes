# Notation

For a quadratic vector space $(V, Q)$ we write $O(V, Q)$ for the orthogonal group $\{T \in GL(V) : Q(Tv) = Q(v)\}$ and $SO(V, Q)$ for the special orthogonal subgroup $\{T : \det T = 1\}$. The Clifford algebra is $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ (see [[Def - Clifford Algebra]]), with its $\mathbb{Z}/2$-grading $\mathrm{Cl} = \mathrm{Cl}^0 \oplus \mathrm{Cl}^1$ and **parity automorphism** $\alpha: \mathrm{Cl} \to \mathrm{Cl}$ defined by $\alpha(x) = x$ for $x \in \mathrm{Cl}^0$ and $\alpha(x) = -x$ for $x \in \mathrm{Cl}^1$. The **transpose** (also called the **Clifford anti-automorphism**) is the unique algebra anti-homomorphism $t: \mathrm{Cl} \to \mathrm{Cl}$ that is the identity on $V$ — equivalently, $(v_1 v_2 \cdots v_k)^t = v_k \cdots v_2 v_1$. The **Clifford norm** is $N(x) = x^t \alpha(x) \in \mathrm{Cl}$; for $v \in V$, $N(v) = v^t \alpha(v) = -v \cdot v = -Q(v)$.

This is a compound page: it defines two interlocking notions — the pin group $\mathrm{Pin}(V, Q)$ and the spin group $\mathrm{Spin}(V, Q)$ — because they are introduced together and neither is fully usable without the other (the spin group is the index-2 even subgroup of the pin group; the pin group is the full preimage of $O$ under the twisted-adjoint action).

# Axiom Motivation

The pin and spin groups answer the following question: **inside the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$, where do the orthogonal transformations of $V$ live?** Once we have the Clifford algebra realising the quadratic form as squaring, we expect to find the symmetry group $O(V, Q)$ realised somehow inside $\mathrm{Cl}$ — perhaps as automorphisms, perhaps as a subgroup. The pin and spin groups are this realisation, sitting inside $\mathrm{Cl}^\times$ as the subgroup whose adjoint-like action on $V$ produces orthogonal transformations.

The desiderata are:

1. We want a subgroup $\widetilde G \subseteq \mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)^\times$ together with a homomorphism $\pi: \widetilde G \to O(V, Q)$ such that the action of $\widetilde G$ on $V$ via $\pi$ matches the inner action $V \mapsto V$ by some kind of conjugation-by-element-of-$\widetilde G$.

2. We want $\pi$ to be *surjective* (every orthogonal transformation should be hit) and to have a small *kernel* (ideally $\{\pm 1\}$, so $\widetilde G$ is a double cover of $O(V, Q)$ — this is what makes the spin group provide spinor representations).

3. The covering map $\pi: \widetilde G \to O(V, Q)$ should be **continuous** (so $\widetilde G$ is a topological group covering $O$ as a topological group), and in fact **smooth** so that $\widetilde G$ is a Lie group covering $O$.

The first thing to try is the **adjoint action**: for $a \in \mathrm{Cl}^\times$, let $\mathrm{Ad}_a(v) = ava^{-1}$. The trouble is that $ava^{-1}$ may not lie in $V$ (which is only a subspace, not a subalgebra). The escape route is the **twisted adjoint action** $\mathrm{Ad}^*_a(v) = \alpha(a) v a^{-1}$ — using the parity automorphism $\alpha$. With this twist, for $a$ a unit vector $v_0$, $\mathrm{Ad}^*_{v_0}(v) = -v_0 v v_0^{-1} = -v_0 v v_0 / Q(v_0)$ (since $v_0^{-1} = v_0/Q(v_0)$), and a direct computation shows this is the **reflection of $v$ in the hyperplane orthogonal to $v_0$**. So unit vectors produce reflections via the twisted adjoint action.

By the **Cartan–Dieudonné theorem**, every orthogonal transformation is a product of at most $n = \dim V$ reflections, so the subgroup of $\mathrm{Cl}^\times$ generated by unit vectors maps onto $O(V, Q)$. This subgroup is the **pin group**: $\mathrm{Pin}(V, Q) = \langle v \in V : Q(v) = \pm 1\rangle$, with the twisted adjoint action giving the homomorphism $\mathrm{Pin}(V, Q) \to O(V, Q)$.

Why insist on the *twisted* adjoint? Without the twist, $\mathrm{Ad}_{v_0}(v) = v_0 v v_0^{-1}$ would *not* give a reflection: it would give the *negative* of the reflection (i.e., reflection composed with $-\mathrm{id}_V$), which is in general not in $O(V, Q)$ except in even dimensions. The twist by $\alpha$ corrects exactly for this sign, and is what makes the construction work in all dimensions.

What about $\mathrm{Spin}$? Per the same Cartan–Dieudonné count, even-number-of-reflections gives an *orientation-preserving* orthogonal transformation, i.e., an element of $SO(V, Q)$. So restricting to even elements $\mathrm{Pin}(V, Q) \cap \mathrm{Cl}^0(V, Q) = \mathrm{Spin}(V, Q)$ gives the double cover of $SO(V, Q)$ — and this is the more frequently used object, because spinor physics usually works with oriented frames.

Why is the kernel always $\{\pm 1\}$? An element $a \in \mathrm{Pin}$ with $\mathrm{Ad}^*_a = \mathrm{id}_V$ commutes with all vectors $v \in V$ up to sign $\alpha(a) v = va$. Analyzing this constraint via the Clifford basis shows that $a$ must be a scalar, and the unit-norm condition $N(a) = 1$ forces $a = \pm 1$. This is the algebraic content of "the cover is exactly $2$-fold".

# The Definition

Let $(V, Q)$ be a finite-dimensional real quadratic vector space, $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ its [[Def - Clifford Algebra|Clifford algebra]], $\alpha$ the parity automorphism, $t$ the transpose anti-automorphism, and $N(x) = x^t \alpha(x)$ the Clifford norm.

The **pin group** is
$$\mathrm{Pin}(V, Q) := \{a_1 a_2 \cdots a_k \in \mathrm{Cl}(V, Q) : k \geq 0, \; a_j \in V, \; Q(a_j) = \pm 1\},$$
the multiplicative subgroup of $\mathrm{Cl}^\times$ generated by unit vectors in $V$.

The **spin group** is the even-parity subgroup
$$\mathrm{Spin}(V, Q) := \mathrm{Pin}(V, Q) \cap \mathrm{Cl}^0(V, Q) = \{a_1 a_2 \cdots a_{2k} \in \mathrm{Cl}(V, Q) : k \geq 0, \; a_j \in V, \; Q(a_j) = \pm 1\},$$
the subgroup of $\mathrm{Pin}$ consisting of products of an *even* number of unit vectors.

The **twisted adjoint action** $\mathrm{Ad}^*: \mathrm{Pin}(V, Q) \to GL(V)$ is defined by $\mathrm{Ad}^*_a(v) := \alpha(a) v a^{-1}$. For a unit vector $a = v_0$ this acts as the reflection in $v_0^\perp$. The image of $\mathrm{Ad}^*$ is $O(V, Q)$, and restricted to $\mathrm{Spin}$, $\mathrm{Ad}^*$ takes values in $SO(V, Q)$.

The fundamental result is the **double-cover property** (see [[Thm - Spin(n) is the Double Cover of SO(n)]]):
$$1 \to \{\pm 1\} \to \mathrm{Pin}(V, Q) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Ad}^*} O(V, Q) \to 1,$$
$$1 \to \{\pm 1\} \to \mathrm{Spin}(V, Q) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{Ad}^*} SO(V, Q) \to 1.$$
Both sequences are short exact, and the kernel is $\{\pm 1\}$ in both cases.

For the standard positive-definite form on $\mathbb{R}^n$, we write $\mathrm{Pin}(n) = \mathrm{Pin}(\mathbb{R}^n, |\cdot|^2)$ and $\mathrm{Spin}(n) = \mathrm{Spin}(\mathbb{R}^n, |\cdot|^2)$. For Minkowski signature $(p, q)$, we write $\mathrm{Pin}(p, q)$ and $\mathrm{Spin}(p, q)$; sometimes the variants $\mathrm{Pin}^\pm$ are distinguished according to which sign convention $v^2 = \pm Q(v)$ is used (these are genuinely different groups for non-Euclidean signatures).

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The pin and spin groups are the natural Lie group "lifts" of the orthogonal groups. Specifically:

- $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ for $n \geq 3$ is the **universal cover** of $SO(n)$: simply connected, $2:1$ over $SO(n)$, the unique connected double cover.
- $\mathrm{Spin}(1) = \mathbb{Z}/2$, $\mathrm{Spin}(2) = U(1) = SO(2)$ (the cover is the identity in dimension 2, since $SO(2) = U(1)$ is already a connected abelian group with $\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}$, and the "double cover" is just $U(1) \to U(1)$, $z \mapsto z^2$).
- $\mathrm{Pin}(n)$ is *not* simply connected in general — it has multiple components (the kernel of the determinant map $\mathrm{Pin}(n) \to O(n)/SO(n) = \mathbb{Z}/2$).
- For Minkowski signature $(1, n)$, $\mathrm{Spin}(1, n)$ has multiple connected components corresponding to time-orientation and orientation choices; the identity component is $\mathrm{Spin}^+(1, n)$, which is the relevant double cover of the proper-orthochronous Lorentz group.

The categorical perspective: $\mathrm{Spin}: \mathbf{Vec}^Q \to \mathbf{LieGrp}^{\text{2-cover}}$ is the functor that assigns to each quadratic vector space its spin double cover, packaging the construction as a "spinification" functor.

The structural picture in low dimensions:

| $n$ | $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ | Isomorphism |
|-----|-------------------|-------------|
| 1 | $\mathbb{Z}/2$ | trivial cover |
| 2 | $U(1)$ | $U(1) \xrightarrow{z \mapsto z^2} U(1) = SO(2)$ |
| 3 | $SU(2) = S^3$ | $\mathrm{Spin}(3) \cong SU(2)$ |
| 4 | $SU(2) \times SU(2)$ | accidental isomorphism: $\mathrm{Spin}(4) \cong SU(2)_L \times SU(2)_R$ |
| 5 | $Sp(2) = \mathrm{Sp}(1, 1)$ | $\mathrm{Spin}(5) \cong Sp(2)$, the compact symplectic group |
| 6 | $SU(4)$ | $\mathrm{Spin}(6) \cong SU(4)$ |
| $\geq 7$ | no other simple identifications | the "spin group proper" begins |

These low-dimensional **accidental isomorphisms** are a recurring theme in spinor theory and are responsible for many of the special properties of low-dimensional gauge theories.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name:** The spin group is the *unique connected double cover of $SO(n)$* (for $n \geq 3$), or equivalently the *universal cover of $SO(n)$*. The pin group similarly is the natural double cover of $O(n)$, extending the spin group to include orientation-reversing transformations. The Clifford-algebraic definition is operationally most useful (gives explicit constructions), but the topological characterisation is what determines the spin group up to isomorphism.

The spin/pin groups generalize to **principal bundles** in geometry: a *spin structure* on a Riemannian manifold $M$ is a lift of the $SO(n)$-frame bundle along $\mathrm{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$. The obstruction is the second Stiefel–Whitney class $w_2(M) \in H^2(M; \mathbb{Z}/2)$, and when this class vanishes a spin structure exists; the set of spin structures is a torsor over $H^1(M; \mathbb{Z}/2)$. See [[Def - Spin Structure on a Manifold]].

In physics, $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ acts on **spinor modules** (irreducible representations of $\mathrm{Cl}(n, 0)$), and the spinor modules cannot be realized as $SO(n)$-representations — they are intrinsically spinorial. The terms "Dirac spinor" (general module), "Weyl spinor" (half-spinors in even dimensions, eigenvectors of the chirality operator), "Majorana spinor" (real-structure spinor) all describe specific types of $\mathrm{Spin}$-modules with extra structure.

Connections to neighbouring constructions:

- **$\mathrm{Spin}^c$ group:** $\mathrm{Spin}^c(n) = \mathrm{Spin}(n) \times_{\mathbb{Z}/2} U(1)$, the "spin group with a $U(1)$ twist". Useful because $\mathrm{Spin}^c$ structures exist on all almost-complex manifolds (a strictly weaker condition than spin). The Seiberg–Witten equations use $\mathrm{Spin}^c$ rather than $\mathrm{Spin}$.
- **$\mathrm{Pin}^\pm(n)$:** in Minkowski signature, there are two inequivalent versions of the pin group depending on whether $v^2 = +Q(v)$ or $v^2 = -Q(v)$ for unit vectors. The distinction matters for non-orientable manifolds. The Klein bottle admits a $\mathrm{Pin}^-$ but not a $\mathrm{Pin}^+$ structure.
- **String group $\mathrm{String}(n)$:** a higher analogue, the 3-connected cover of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$. Required for "string structures" on manifolds, which arise in heterotic string theory and the geometry of loop spaces.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Example 1: $\mathrm{Spin}(3) = SU(2)$.** Take $V = \mathbb{R}^3$ with the Euclidean form. The Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) = M_2(\mathbb{C})$ is generated by the Pauli matrices. The spin group consists of even-degree elements with norm $1$: explicitly $a = a_0 I + i a_1 \sigma_1 + i a_2 \sigma_2 + i a_3 \sigma_3$ (or equivalently using $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = i\sigma_3$, etc.) with $|a_0|^2 + |a_1|^2 + |a_2|^2 + |a_3|^2 = 1$. These are exactly $SU(2)$ matrices. The twisted adjoint action of $\mathrm{Spin}(3)$ on $V \cong \mathbb{R}^3$ is the $SU(2) \to SO(3)$ cover. See [[Thm - SU(2) is the Double Cover of SO(3)]].

**Example 2: $\mathrm{Spin}(4) = SU(2) \times SU(2)$.** In dimension $4$, the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(4, 0)$ decomposes as $M_2(\mathbb{H})$, and the even part $\mathrm{Cl}^0(4, 0)$ is $\mathbb{H} \oplus \mathbb{H}$ (two copies of the quaternions). The spin group inside is $(SU(2))_L \times (SU(2))_R$, with the two $SU(2)$ factors corresponding to **left-handed** and **right-handed** spinor representations. The cover is $\mathrm{Spin}(4) \to SO(4)$, and $SO(4) \cong (SU(2) \times SU(2))/\mathbb{Z}/2$ (diagonal $\mathbb{Z}/2$). This is the unique dimension in which the spin group is *not simple* — a fact with significant consequences for $4$-dimensional geometry (e.g., self-dual vs anti-self-dual 2-forms).

**Example 3: $\mathrm{Spin}(1, 3) = SL(2, \mathbb{C})$.** The double cover of the proper-orthochronous Lorentz group, central to spinors in special relativity. The connected component $\mathrm{Spin}^+(1, 3) = SL(2, \mathbb{C})$ realizes the cover; the full $\mathrm{Spin}(1, 3)$ has additional components related to time-reversal.

**Example 4: Reflection action.** Take a unit vector $v_0 \in V$ with $Q(v_0) = 1$. The twisted adjoint $\mathrm{Ad}^*_{v_0}(v) = \alpha(v_0) v v_0^{-1} = -v_0 v v_0$ (using $v_0^{-1} = v_0/Q(v_0) = v_0$ for unit vector). Expand using $vv_0 + v_0 v = 2B(v, v_0)$: $v_0 v v_0 = v_0(2B(v, v_0) - v_0 v) = 2B(v, v_0) v_0 - v_0^2 v = 2B(v, v_0)v_0 - v$. So $\mathrm{Ad}^*_{v_0}(v) = -v_0 v v_0 = v - 2B(v, v_0)v_0$, the reflection formula. This is the calculation that justifies the twisted-adjoint definition.

**Non-example: arbitrary even elements are not in $\mathrm{Spin}$.** For instance, $a = 1 + e_1 e_2 \in \mathrm{Cl}^0(2, 0)$ has norm $N(a) = a^t \alpha(a) = (1 + e_2 e_1)(1 + e_1 e_2) = 1 + e_1 e_2 + e_2 e_1 + e_2 e_1 e_1 e_2 = 1 - 1 + e_2(1)e_2 = e_2^2 = 1$ — wait, with $Q(e_2) = 1$ this gives $a^t\alpha(a) = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2$, so $a \notin \mathrm{Spin}(2, 0)$ as it does not have unit Clifford norm. To be in $\mathrm{Spin}$, an even element must additionally have $N(a) = 1$.

**Calibration check.** A reader should verify: (i) the reflection formula $\mathrm{Ad}^*_{v_0}(v) = v - 2B(v, v_0)v_0$ via the explicit Clifford calculation; (ii) check that $\mathrm{Spin}(2) = U(1)$ by identifying $\mathrm{Cl}^0(2, 0) = \mathbb{C}$ and the spin condition $|z|^2 = 1$; (iii) verify that the kernel of $\mathrm{Ad}^*: \mathrm{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$ is exactly $\{\pm 1\}$ by checking that $a = -1$ acts trivially on $V$ via the twisted adjoint ($\alpha(-1) v (-1)^{-1} = (-1)(v)(-1)^{-1} = v$).

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Spin Bordism and Cobordism Theory
> The **spin cobordism** ring $\Omega_*^{\mathrm{Spin}}$ classifies closed spin manifolds modulo spin-bordism (two manifolds are equivalent if they together form the boundary of a spin manifold). This is a graded ring under disjoint union and Cartesian product. The structure is computed by **Anderson–Brown–Peterson (1966)**: rationally $\Omega_*^{\mathrm{Spin}} \otimes \mathbb{Q} = \mathbb{Q}[x_4, x_8, x_{12}, \ldots]$, generated by classes in degrees divisible by 4 (which are intersection numbers of half-Pontryagin classes). The $\mathbb{Z}/2$-torsion is described by Stiefel–Whitney numbers. The cobordism ring serves as the receptacle for the **$\hat A$-genus**: a graded ring homomorphism $\hat A: \Omega_*^{\mathrm{Spin}} \to \mathbb{Q}$ that, via the Atiyah–Singer index theorem, equals the index of the Dirac operator.

> [!tip] Spin Manifolds and Positive Scalar Curvature
> The **Gromov–Lawson–Rosenberg conjecture** asserts that a closed spin manifold $M^n$ admits a positive-scalar-curvature metric iff a certain spin-cobordism-theoretic invariant vanishes. The "only if" direction is the **Lichnerowicz vanishing theorem**: if $R > 0$, then $\ker \not D = 0$, so the index $\hat A(M) = \mathrm{ind}\,\not D^+ = 0$ — providing a topological obstruction to positive scalar curvature. The "if" direction was proven by Stolze in special cases. This is a deep instance where the spin structure controls Riemannian-geometric possibilities.

> [!tip] String Theory and Higher Spin
> In string theory, the worldsheet of a string is a 2-dimensional spin manifold; the spinors on this worldsheet are **worldsheet fermions** that combine with bosonic coordinates to give the superstring. The requirement that the worldsheet admit a spin structure restricts to genus, and the *two* spin structures on a torus correspond to **Ramond and Neveu–Schwarz** boundary conditions for the fermions — a basic dichotomy in superstring theory. At the next level, **string structures** (a lift of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ to its 3-connected cover $\mathrm{String}(n)$) are needed for anomaly cancellation in heterotic string theory.
