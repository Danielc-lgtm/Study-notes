# Notation Registry

Throughout this topic $M$ and $N$ are smooth manifolds (Hausdorff, second countable, possibly with boundary). Singular homology is defined topologically, so the smoothness assumption can be relaxed to "topological space" wherever the discussion is purely about chains and homology; it is only the de Rham comparison that needs smoothness. When the coefficient group $G$ is unspecified we work with $G = \mathbb{Z}$ by default, and switch to $G = \mathbb{R}$ when comparing with de Rham cohomology.

- $\Delta^p \subset \mathbb{R}^{p+1}$ — the **standard $p$-simplex**, the convex hull $\{\sum t_i e_i : t_i \geq 0,\ \sum t_i = 1\}$ of the $p+1$ standard basis vectors $e_0, \dots, e_p$. Frankel uses the affine realisation $(P_0, \dots, P_p)$ in $\mathbb{R}^p$ where $P_0 = 0$ and $P_k = e_k$; the two realisations are affinely equivalent, and we use $\Delta^p$ for either when the choice does not matter.
- $\sigma : \Delta^p \to M$ — a **singular $p$-simplex**: a continuous map (smooth, when comparing with forms). The image $\sigma(\Delta^p) \subseteq M$ can be anything from a point to a $p$-dimensional patch.
- $C_p(M; G)$ — the **singular $p$-chain group**: the free $G$-module on all singular $p$-simplices in $M$, i.e. finite formal sums $\sum g_i \sigma_i$ with $g_i \in G$.
- $f_k : \Delta^{p-1} \to \Delta^p$ — the $k$-th **face map**, the unique affine map sending $P_j \mapsto P_j$ for $j < k$ and $P_j \mapsto P_{j+1}$ for $j \geq k$. Its image is the face opposite vertex $P_k$.
- $\partial : C_p(M; G) \to C_{p-1}(M; G)$ — the **boundary operator**, $\partial \sigma = \sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k (\sigma \circ f_k)$, extended $G$-linearly to chains.
- $Z_p(M; G) = \ker(\partial : C_p \to C_{p-1})$ — the group of **$p$-cycles**.
- $B_p(M; G) = \mathrm{im}(\partial : C_{p+1} \to C_p)$ — the group of **$p$-boundaries**. By $\partial^2 = 0$, $B_p \subseteq Z_p$.
- $H_p(M; G) = Z_p(M; G) / B_p(M; G)$ — the **$p$-th singular homology group** of $M$ with coefficients in $G$.
- $\tilde H_0(M; G)$ — **reduced** $0$-th homology: the kernel of the augmentation $\varepsilon : H_0(M; G) \to G$, $\varepsilon(\sum g_i p_i) = \sum g_i$. For connected $M$, $\tilde H_0 = 0$ and $H_0 = G$.
- $b_p(M) = \dim_\mathbb{R} H_p(M; \mathbb{R})$ — the **$p$-th Betti number** of $M$, the dimension of homology over the reals.
- $\chi(M) = \sum_p (-1)^p b_p(M)$ — the **Euler characteristic**, an alternating sum well-defined when the Betti numbers are finite (e.g. $M$ compact).
- $H^p(M; G) = \mathrm{Hom}(H_p(M; \mathbb{Z}), G)$ — the **$p$-th singular cohomology group** with coefficients in $G$. Over a field this is the linear dual of homology.
- $\Omega^k(M)$, $d : \Omega^k \to \Omega^{k+1}$, $H^k_{dR}(M)$ — smooth $k$-forms, the exterior derivative, and the de Rham cohomology from `Differential Geometry X`.
- $\langle [\omega], [c] \rangle = \int_c \omega$ — the **de Rham pairing** between a closed form $\omega$ and a smooth $p$-cycle $c$, which descends to $H^p_{dR}(M) \times H_p(M; \mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$.
- $f_* : H_p(M; G) \to H_p(N; G)$ — the homomorphism induced by a continuous map $f : M \to N$.

> [!warning] Convention: singular versus simplicial
> Singular homology uses *all* continuous maps $\Delta^p \to M$ — an enormous, uncountable family. Simplicial homology uses only a fixed finite triangulation. The two agree on triangulable spaces (Frankel 13.2c, our [[Thm - Singular and Simplicial Homology Agree on Triangulable Spaces]]), but the singular theory is the conceptually clean one because it is defined for arbitrary topological spaces without choosing a triangulation. Throughout this topic, "homology" means *singular* homology unless qualified.

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: **the obstructions to integrating a closed differential form globally are the same obstructions, counted differently, as the holes in the underlying space.** This is the de Rham theorem, and the rest of the chapter is the construction needed to even state it.

In `Differential Geometry X` we built de Rham cohomology — closed forms modulo exact forms — and showed it is a smooth invariant of the manifold. We computed $H^*_{dR}(\mathbb{R}^n) = \mathbb{R}$ in degree zero and zero elsewhere; we computed $H^1_{dR}(S^1) = \mathbb{R}$; and we observed via Mayer–Vietoris that the answer depends only on homotopy type. But we did not say *what topological invariants the de Rham groups equal*. The slogan "dimension counts the holes" is suggestive, but until we have an independent definition of "hole" the slogan is empty.

Singular homology is that independent definition. To each topological space $M$ — not just smooth manifolds — and each integer $p \geq 0$ it assigns an abelian group $H_p(M; G)$ that captures "the $p$-dimensional holes in $M$." The construction is combinatorial: take all continuous maps $\sigma : \Delta^p \to M$ from the standard simplex into $M$, form the free abelian group $C_p$ of formal $\mathbb{Z}$-linear combinations of such maps, equip it with a boundary operator $\partial : C_p \to C_{p-1}$ satisfying $\partial^2 = 0$, and take the quotient
$$
H_p(M; \mathbb{Z}) \;=\; \frac{\ker \partial}{\mathrm{im}\,\partial} \;=\; \frac{\text{cycles}}{\text{boundaries}}.
$$
A cycle is a chain with no boundary — a closed surface, intuitively; a boundary is a chain that arises as the boundary of something one dimension higher. Two cycles are homologous if they differ by a boundary, and a non-trivial homology class is a cycle that does not bound — a "hole" the cycle is wrapped around. The Betti numbers $b_p = \dim_\mathbb{R} H_p(M; \mathbb{R})$ are the dimensions of these groups: $b_0$ counts components, $b_1$ counts independent loops, $b_2$ counts independent voids, and so on.

The structural backbone of the chapter is the diagram

$$
\cdots \xrightarrow{\partial} C_{p+1}(M; G) \xrightarrow{\partial} C_p(M; G) \xrightarrow{\partial} C_{p-1}(M; G) \xrightarrow{\partial} \cdots \xrightarrow{\partial} C_0(M; G) \xrightarrow{\partial} 0
$$

with $\partial^2 = 0$. Singular homology is the failure of this sequence to be exact: $H_p = \ker\partial / \mathrm{im}\,\partial$ at each spot. Singular *cohomology* dualises everything, replacing $C_p$ by $C^p = \mathrm{Hom}(C_p, G)$ and reversing arrows. The de Rham complex is the smooth-manifold analogue with $\Omega^k$ in place of $C^k$, and the de Rham theorem says these two complexes compute the same cohomology when $G = \mathbb{R}$.

Why is this remarkable? Because the two constructions use completely different technologies. Singular cohomology is built from continuous maps, formal sums, and combinatorial boundary operators; de Rham cohomology is built from smooth forms, the exterior derivative, and integration. There is no a priori reason they should agree. The bridge between them is integration: a closed form $\omega$ defines a functional on cycles by $c \mapsto \int_c \omega$, and Stokes's theorem makes this functional well-defined on homology classes. The de Rham theorem says this map is an isomorphism, identifying $H^p_{dR}(M) \cong H^p(M; \mathbb{R})$ on every smooth manifold.

The consequences thread through every subsequent topic. The **Betti numbers** of a manifold — the central topological invariants — are computable by smooth-form techniques (integrating closed forms over cycles, counting independent cohomology classes via Mayer–Vietoris on forms). The **Euler characteristic** is the alternating sum of these dimensions and, by the Gauss–Bonnet–Chern theorem, equals an integral of a curvature form. The **periods** of a closed form on independent cycles determine its de Rham class completely. And the entire architecture of **characteristic classes** — Chern classes, Pontryagin classes, the Euler class — lives in this de Rham/singular cohomology, computable both topologically (cocycles on cells) and smoothly (curvature integrals).

What the reader should bring: comfort with smooth manifolds, smooth maps, and differential forms ([[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases]] through [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]]); the de Rham complex and its homotopy-invariant cohomology ([[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius]]); [[Thm - Stokes' Theorem on Manifolds|Stokes's theorem]] for integration of forms over chains and over manifolds with boundary; quotient groups and homomorphisms ([[Def - Quotient Group]], [[Def - Homomorphism]], [[Def - Kernel and Image]]); abelian groups and free abelian groups ([[Def - Abelian Group]], and the linear-algebra analogue of free modules from [[Def - Module]]). The Mayer–Vietoris technology from `Differential Geometry X` reappears here on the singular side and powers most of the computations.

# Concept Map

## §1.1 Singular Simplices and Chains

- **[[Def - The Standard p-Simplex]]**
	- The **standard $p$-simplex** $\Delta^p$ is the convex hull of $p+1$ affinely independent points, the universal "$p$-dimensional triangle". Frankel realises it as $(P_0, P_1, \dots, P_p) \subset \mathbb{R}^p$ with $P_0 = 0$ and $P_k$ the $k$-th unit vector; an alternative realisation places it in $\mathbb{R}^{p+1}$ as $\{(t_0, \dots, t_p) : t_i \geq 0, \sum t_i = 1\}$, the **barycentric coordinates**. The standard $0$-simplex is a point, $\Delta^1$ is an interval, $\Delta^2$ a triangle, $\Delta^3$ a tetrahedron. The faces $\Delta^{p-1}_{(k)} = (P_0, \dots, \widehat{P_k}, \dots, P_p)$ — vertex $P_k$ omitted — sit inside $\Delta^p$ as copies of the standard $(p-1)$-simplex via the face maps $f_k$.

- **[[Def - Singular Simplex]]**
	- A **singular $p$-simplex** in a space $M$ is a continuous (or smooth, when relevant) map $\sigma : \Delta^p \to M$. The map can do anything continuous: the image may be a single point (a constant simplex), an immersed disc, an embedded face of a triangulation, or a wildly tangled set. The word "singular" warns against picturing simplices as embedded triangles — they need not be injective, need not have full rank, and need carry no smooth structure. Every continuous map from a point to $M$ is a singular $0$-simplex, every continuous path is a singular $1$-simplex, every continuous map from a triangle is a singular $2$-simplex.

- **[[Def - Singular Chain]]**
	- A **singular $p$-chain** with coefficients in an abelian group $G$ is a finite formal sum $c = g_1 \sigma_1 + \cdots + g_r \sigma_r$ of singular $p$-simplices with coefficients in $G$. Equivalently, a function from singular simplices to $G$ that is zero except on finitely many simplices. The set of all $p$-chains is the **singular chain group** $C_p(M; G)$, an abelian group under termwise addition — in fact the free $G$-module on the set of singular $p$-simplices. Common coefficient choices: $G = \mathbb{Z}$ (integer chains, the universal case), $G = \mathbb{R}$ (real chains, for de Rham comparison), $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (mod-2 chains, where signs vanish — useful for non-orientable spaces).

- **[[Def - The Boundary Operator]]**
	- The **boundary** of a singular $p$-simplex $\sigma : \Delta^p \to M$ is the singular $(p-1)$-chain $\partial \sigma = \sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k (\sigma \circ f_k)$, the alternating sum of its faces. Extended $G$-linearly to chains, this gives a homomorphism $\partial : C_p(M; G) \to C_{p-1}(M; G)$. The alternating signs are exactly what makes $\partial^2 = 0$ work: the $k$-th face of the $j$-th face equals (up to a sign) the $(j-1)$-th face of the $k$-th face when $j < k$, and the signs cancel in pairs (see [[Thm - d-Squared-is-Zero for Singular Boundaries]]). Concrete examples: $\partial[\text{interval}] = (\text{endpoint}) - (\text{startpoint})$; $\partial[\text{triangle}] = (\text{three edges with alternating signs})$.

- **[[Thm - d-Squared-is-Zero for Singular Boundaries]]**
	- The composition $\partial \circ \partial : C_p \to C_{p-2}$ is identically zero. Geometrically: the boundary of a region has no boundary. Algebraically: the proof is a combinatorial cancellation in pairs of face-of-face terms, made possible by the alternating signs in $\partial$. This is the single algebraic fact that makes singular homology a well-defined theory — without $\partial^2 = 0$, the quotient $\ker \partial / \mathrm{im}\,\partial$ would not make sense because $\mathrm{im}\,\partial$ would not lie in $\ker \partial$.

- **[[Def - Chain Map and Chain Homotopy]]**
	- A **chain map** $f_\bullet : C_\bullet \to C'_\bullet$ between two chain complexes is a sequence of homomorphisms commuting with the boundary: $\partial' \circ f_p = f_{p-1} \circ \partial$. Chain maps induce homomorphisms on homology. A **chain homotopy** between two chain maps $f, g : C_\bullet \to C'_\bullet$ is a sequence of maps $h_p : C_p \to C'_{p+1}$ satisfying $g - f = \partial' h + h \partial$. Two chain-homotopic chain maps induce the *same* homomorphism on homology, because on cycles the right-hand side becomes a boundary. This is the algebraic core of homotopy invariance.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Chain Complex *(from Homological Algebra)*
> A **chain complex** in any abelian category is a sequence $\cdots \to C_{p+1} \to C_p \to C_{p-1} \to \cdots$ of objects and morphisms with $\partial^2 = 0$. Homology of a chain complex is the central object of **homological algebra**, which generalises every cohomology theory in mathematics: singular, de Rham, Čech, sheaf, group, Hochschild, Lie algebra, cyclic. The standard machinery — long exact sequences, the snake lemma, the five lemma, spectral sequences, derived functors — all lives at this level of abstraction.

> [!tip] Unlocked: CW Complex *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> The construction "build a space by gluing cells of increasing dimension along their boundaries" is the **CW complex** structure, the most flexible model for spaces in algebraic topology. Singular homology computed on a CW complex agrees with **cellular homology**, which uses only one chain group per dimension (one for each cell), making the computation enormously simpler than working with all singular simplices. Most explicit computations of $H_*(S^n)$, $H_*(\mathbb{CP}^n)$, etc., go through cellular homology.

## §1.2 The Singular Homology Functor

- **[[Def - Singular Homology]]**
	- The **$p$-th singular homology group** of $M$ with coefficients in $G$ is the quotient $H_p(M; G) = Z_p(M; G) / B_p(M; G)$, where $Z_p = \ker(\partial : C_p \to C_{p-1})$ are the **cycles** and $B_p = \mathrm{im}(\partial : C_{p+1} \to C_p)$ are the **boundaries**. Two cycles are **homologous** if they differ by a boundary; a homology class is an equivalence class of cycles modulo this relation. This is the central object of the topic: a $G$-module whose elements are "cycles, identified when they bound something." Singular homology is a *covariant functor* $H_p : \mathbf{Top} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ from topological spaces to abelian groups — a continuous map $f : M \to N$ induces $f_* : H_p(M; G) \to H_p(N; G)$ by post-composition $\sigma \mapsto f \circ \sigma$ on simplices.

- **[[Def - Augmentation and Reduced Homology]]**
	- The **augmentation map** $\varepsilon : C_0(M; G) \to G$ sends $\sum g_i p_i \mapsto \sum g_i$ — it forgets the points and sums the coefficients. It satisfies $\varepsilon \circ \partial = 0$ on $C_1$, so it descends to $\varepsilon : H_0(M; G) \to G$. The kernel $\tilde H_0(M; G) = \ker \varepsilon$ is the **reduced $0$-th homology**; for connected $M$ it is zero, and the augmentation is an isomorphism $H_0 \cong G$. Reduced homology is technically convenient: it makes the homology of a point vanish in *all* degrees (including zero), at the cost of one trivial bookkeeping shift.

- **Functoriality of singular homology**
	- A continuous map $f : M \to N$ induces a chain map $f_\# : C_p(M; G) \to C_p(N; G)$ by $f_\#(\sigma) = f \circ \sigma$ extended $G$-linearly. This commutes with $\partial$ because the boundary is built from compositions with face maps, which commute with post-composition. Hence $f_\#$ descends to $f_* : H_p(M; G) \to H_p(N; G)$. Functoriality $(g \circ f)_* = g_* \circ f_*$ and $\mathrm{id}_* = \mathrm{id}$ then makes singular homology a covariant functor $\mathbf{Top} \to \mathbf{Ab}$. Homeomorphisms induce isomorphisms; the deeper [[Thm - Homotopy Invariance of Singular Homology|homotopy invariance]] says even homotopy equivalences do.

- **[[Thm - Homotopy Invariance of Singular Homology]]**
	- If $f, g : M \to N$ are continuous and **homotopic** — there is a continuous $H : M \times [0,1] \to N$ with $H|_{t=0} = f$ and $H|_{t=1} = g$ — then the induced maps $f_*, g_* : H_p(M; G) \to H_p(N; G)$ are *equal*. The proof constructs an explicit chain homotopy $h : C_p(M) \to C_{p+1}(N)$ using a simplicial decomposition of the prism $\Delta^p \times [0,1]$. Consequence: homotopy equivalent spaces have isomorphic singular homology. Contractible spaces have the homology of a point: $H_0 = G$, $H_p = 0$ for $p \geq 1$.

- **[[Thm - Mayer-Vietoris for Singular Homology]]**
	- For an open cover $M = U \cup V$, there is a long exact sequence
	$$\cdots \to H_p(U \cap V; G) \to H_p(U; G) \oplus H_p(V; G) \to H_p(M; G) \to H_{p-1}(U \cap V; G) \to \cdots$$
	connecting the homology of $M$ to the homology of $U$, $V$, and $U \cap V$. The maps are induced by inclusions; the connecting map $\partial : H_p(M) \to H_{p-1}(U \cap V)$ is constructed by taking a cycle on $M$, decomposing it as a sum of pieces in $U$ and $V$ (using barycentric subdivision so each piece is contained in one of $U$, $V$), and taking the boundary of the $U$-piece. This is the singular analogue of the de Rham Mayer–Vietoris from [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius]], and it is the inductive engine for every explicit computation of singular homology.

- **[[Ex - Singular Homology of a Point]]** (⭐)
	- Show that $H_0(\mathrm{pt}; G) = G$ and $H_p(\mathrm{pt}; G) = 0$ for $p \geq 1$. The chain complex of a point is enumerable: there is exactly one singular $p$-simplex (the constant map) in each dimension, so $C_p(\mathrm{pt}; G) = G$. The boundary maps alternate between zero and the identity, and the cohomology of the resulting complex computes directly.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Eilenberg–Steenrod Axioms *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> Eilenberg and Steenrod identified five axioms — homotopy invariance, the long exact sequence of a pair, excision, additivity, and the dimension axiom (the value on a point) — and proved that *any* functor $\mathbf{Top} \to \mathbf{Ab}$ satisfying them is uniquely determined on CW complexes. Singular homology satisfies all five, and so does any other "ordinary" homology theory (cellular, simplicial, Čech for nice spaces). Generalised cohomology theories (K-theory, cobordism) drop only the dimension axiom and form the modern landscape of stable homotopy.

## §1.3 Computations of Singular Homology

- **[[Thm - Singular Homology of the Sphere]]**
	- For $n \geq 1$, $H_0(S^n; G) = G$, $H_n(S^n; G) = G$, and $H_p(S^n; G) = 0$ for all other $p$. The proof uses Mayer–Vietoris on the cover of $S^n$ by two open balls slightly enlarged from the upper and lower hemispheres, whose intersection deformation-retracts onto $S^{n-1}$. The base case $n = 0$ (the two-point space) gives $H_0(S^0) = G \oplus G$, and induction on $n$ produces the answer. The non-trivial top class $[S^n] \in H_n(S^n; G)$ is the **fundamental class** — the orientation cycle, when $G = \mathbb{Z}$.

- **Singular homology of the torus**
	- For the $n$-torus $T^n = (S^1)^n$, $H_k(T^n; G) = G^{\binom{n}{k}}$. The proof uses the Künneth formula (or iterated Mayer–Vietoris on the standard cube-with-identifications) and is most transparent through the equivalent computation of $H^*_{dR}(T^n)$ via wedges of the angular forms $d\theta^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge d\theta^{i_k}$. The Betti numbers are $b_k(T^n) = \binom{n}{k}$ and the Euler characteristic is $\chi(T^n) = \sum_k (-1)^k \binom{n}{k} = 0$ for $n \geq 1$.

- **Singular homology of real and complex projective space**
	- $H_k(\mathbb{RP}^n; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$ for $k = 0$ and $k = n$ (if $n$ odd), $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ for $k$ odd with $1 \leq k < n$, and zero otherwise; the answer depends sharply on parity because $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is non-orientable for even $n$. With *real* coefficients the $\mathbb{Z}/2$ torsion vanishes and the answer simplifies to $H_k(\mathbb{RP}^n; \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$ for $k = 0$ (always) and $k = n$ (odd $n$), zero otherwise — so $\mathbb{RP}^n$ has the same Betti numbers as a point for even $n$ and as $S^n$ for odd $n$. By contrast $H_k(\mathbb{CP}^n; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$ for $k = 0, 2, 4, \dots, 2n$ and zero otherwise — Betti numbers $b_{2k}(\mathbb{CP}^n) = 1$, all odd Betti numbers vanish. The torsion in real projective homology is the prototypical example of why integer coefficients see more than real coefficients.

- **[[Def - Betti Numbers]]**
	- The **$p$-th Betti number** of $M$ is $b_p(M) = \dim_\mathbb{R} H_p(M; \mathbb{R})$, the rank of the $p$-th real singular homology. Equivalently — by the universal coefficient theorem — $b_p = \mathrm{rank}_\mathbb{Z} H_p(M; \mathbb{Z})$, the integer rank of the integer homology. The Betti numbers are topological invariants: $b_0$ counts connected components, $b_1$ counts independent loops, $b_2$ counts independent voids. By the de Rham theorem they equal the dimensions of de Rham cohomology, $b_p = \dim_\mathbb{R} H^p_{dR}(M)$, so they are also the maximal number of independent closed $p$-forms modulo exact ones.

- **[[Def - Euler Characteristic]]**
	- The **Euler characteristic** of $M$ is $\chi(M) = \sum_p (-1)^p b_p(M)$, the alternating sum of the Betti numbers. It is well-defined whenever the Betti numbers are finite (e.g. $M$ compact). For a triangulated surface it equals $V - E + F$ (vertices minus edges plus faces), the classical formula; this is no coincidence — the simplicial chain complex of a triangulation has alternating-sum Euler characteristic equal to that of its homology, by linear algebra. For a closed orientable surface of genus $g$, $\chi = 2 - 2g$; for $S^n$, $\chi = 1 + (-1)^n$; for $T^n$, $\chi = 0$ for $n \geq 1$. The Euler characteristic is the central topological invariant unifying Gauss–Bonnet, Poincaré–Hopf, and Lefschetz fixed-point theory.

- **[[Thm - Euler Characteristic via Alternating Betti Numbers]]**
	- For any chain complex of finite-dimensional vector spaces over a field, the Euler characteristic — defined as $\sum (-1)^p \dim C_p$ — equals the alternating sum of the dimensions of the homology, $\sum (-1)^p \dim H_p$. This is a piece of linear algebra: the exact sequences $0 \to Z_p \to C_p \to B_{p-1} \to 0$ and $0 \to B_p \to Z_p \to H_p \to 0$ chain together and the dimensions cancel telescopically. The consequence: $\chi(M)$ can be computed from *any* chain-complex resolution of $M$ — simplicial chains of a triangulation, cellular chains of a CW structure, de Rham forms — and always returns the same integer. This is why $V - E + F = 2$ for any triangulation of the sphere.

- **[[Ex - Computing H_n of S^n via Mayer-Vietoris]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute $H_p(S^n; \mathbb{Z})$ by induction on $n$ using the cover by two open hemispheres-with-a-collar. The base case $S^0$ is two points; the inductive step uses that the intersection of the two hemispheres deformation-retracts onto the equator $S^{n-1}$. The long exact sequence then shifts the homology up by one dimension.

- **[[Ex - Computing the Homology of the Torus]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute $H_*(T^2; \mathbb{Z})$ using the standard rectangle-with-identifications triangulation. Identify the basis cycles $A$ and $B$ explicitly; verify that $T^2$ is orientable by computing $\partial[T^2] = A + B - A - B = 0$.

- **[[Ex - Singular Homology of RP^2]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Compute $H_*(\mathbb{RP}^2; G)$ for $G = \mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ from the disk-with-antipodal-identification model. Discover that $H_1(\mathbb{RP}^2; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}/2$ (torsion) while $H_1(\mathbb{RP}^2; \mathbb{R}) = 0$. The example showcases how the coefficient group sees genuinely different homological information.

- **[[Ex - Euler Characteristic of a Closed Orientable Surface is 2 - 2g]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute the Betti numbers of $\Sigma_g$ and verify $\chi(\Sigma_g) = 2 - 2g$ in two ways: from the standard $4g$-gon polygon-with-identifications triangulation, and from the alternating Betti numbers. Match against the Euler-characteristic formula $V - E + F$ for the polygon triangulation.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Poincaré Duality *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> On a compact oriented $n$-manifold without boundary, **Poincaré duality** gives an isomorphism $H_k(M; \mathbb{Z}) \cong H^{n-k}(M; \mathbb{Z})$ (or with any coefficients) realised by cap product with the fundamental class $[M] \in H_n(M; \mathbb{Z})$. The Betti-number version is $b_k(M) = b_{n-k}(M)$, the "palindrome" structure visible in the Betti polynomials of $S^n$, $T^n$, $\mathbb{CP}^n$. Poincaré duality is the topological avatar of Hodge duality $\star : \Omega^k \to \Omega^{n-k}$ on a Riemannian manifold (see [[Hodge Theory I — Harmonic Forms and the Hodge Decomposition]]).

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Hurewicz Theorem *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> For a simply connected space, the **Hurewicz theorem** identifies the first non-zero homotopy group $\pi_n(X)$ with the first non-zero homology group $H_n(X; \mathbb{Z})$. Homology and homotopy agree in the first non-trivial dimension; above that they diverge sharply — homology is "abelianised homotopy," and the higher homotopy groups $\pi_k(S^n)$ for $k > n$ encode genuinely non-abelian information not visible to homology. See [[Algebraic Topology III — Higher Homotopy and Chern Forms]].

## §1.4 Singular Cohomology and the de Rham Theorem

- **[[Def - Singular Cohomology]]**
	- The **$p$-th singular cohomology group** of $M$ with coefficients in $G$ is $H^p(M; G) = H_p(\mathrm{Hom}(C_\bullet(M; \mathbb{Z}), G))$ — the cohomology of the chain complex obtained by applying $\mathrm{Hom}(-, G)$ termwise to the singular chain complex. Equivalently and more usefully: over a field $G = K$, $H^p(M; K) = \mathrm{Hom}_K(H_p(M; K), K)$, the $K$-linear dual of singular homology. Singular cohomology is a *contravariant* functor — a continuous $f : M \to N$ induces $f^* : H^p(N; G) \to H^p(M; G)$ by pulling back cocycles. Its operational virtue over homology is the **cup product**, an associative bilinear map $H^p \otimes H^q \to H^{p+q}$ making $H^*(M; G)$ into a graded-commutative ring — homology has nothing analogous.

- **Universal Coefficient Theorem** *(forward statement)*
	- For a topological space $M$ and a coefficient group $G$, singular cohomology is computable from integer homology via the **universal coefficient theorem**: there is a (non-canonically split) short exact sequence
	$$
	0 \to \mathrm{Ext}^1(H_{p-1}(M; \mathbb{Z}), G) \to H^p(M; G) \to \mathrm{Hom}(H_p(M; \mathbb{Z}), G) \to 0.
	$$
	When $G = \mathbb{R}$ (or any field of characteristic zero), the $\mathrm{Ext}$ term vanishes because $\mathbb{R}$ is divisible, so $H^p(M; \mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{Hom}(H_p(M; \mathbb{Z}), \mathbb{R})$ — torsion in integer homology is *invisible* to real cohomology. This is the precise statement that "real homology and real cohomology lose torsion information." Used as a black box throughout the rest of this topic; the proof belongs to homological algebra and is omitted here.

- **The de Rham pairing**
	- For a closed $p$-form $\omega \in \Omega^p(M)$ and a smooth singular $p$-chain $c = \sum a_i \sigma_i$ with $\sigma_i$ smooth, the **integral** is $\int_c \omega = \sum a_i \int_{\sigma_i} \omega = \sum a_i \int_{\Delta^p} \sigma_i^* \omega$. By Stokes's theorem this pairing satisfies $\int_{\partial c} \omega = \int_c d\omega$, so for closed $\omega$ and cycles $c$ it descends to a well-defined pairing $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle : H^p_{dR}(M) \times H_p(M; \mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$. The associated map $H^p_{dR}(M) \to \mathrm{Hom}(H_p(M; \mathbb{R}), \mathbb{R}) = H^p(M; \mathbb{R})$ is the **de Rham homomorphism**, whose iso-ness is the content of the theorem.

- **[[Thm - The de Rham Theorem (Full Proof)]]**
	- For every smooth manifold $M$ and every $p \geq 0$, the de Rham homomorphism $\mathcal{I} : H^p_{dR}(M) \to H^p(M; \mathbb{R})$, $\mathcal{I}[\omega][c] = \int_c \omega$, is a $\mathbb{R}$-linear isomorphism. Two paradigmatically different cohomology theories — one from smooth forms and calculus, one from continuous simplices and combinatorics — agree on smooth manifolds. The proof reduces by Mayer–Vietoris on a good cover to the contractible case, where both sides equal $\mathbb{R}$ in degree zero and zero elsewhere (Poincaré lemma on the de Rham side, contractibility on the singular side); the inductive step uses naturality of the de Rham homomorphism with respect to the two Mayer–Vietoris sequences, and the five lemma. This is the *full proof* version of [[Thm - The de Rham Theorem (Statement)]] from `Differential Geometry X`.

- **[[Thm - Singular and Simplicial Homology Agree on Triangulable Spaces]]**
	- For a space $M$ admitting a finite simplicial triangulation $K$, the singular homology $H_p(M; G)$ equals the simplicial homology $H_p^{\mathrm{simp}}(K; G)$ computed from the finite-dimensional simplicial chain complex of $K$. The proof uses **barycentric subdivision** as a chain homotopy between the singular chain complex and a subcomplex consisting of simplices small enough to lie inside the open stars of $K$. Consequence: singular homology of compact manifolds is finitely generated and computable from a triangulation. This is the bridge between the conceptually clean singular theory and the explicitly computable simplicial theory; without it, every actual computation would be intractable.

- **Cup product and Poincaré duality** *(forward preview)*
	- The cup product $\smile : H^p(M; G) \otimes H^q(M; G) \to H^{p+q}(M; G)$ is defined on cochains by $(\alpha \smile \beta)(\sigma) = \alpha(\sigma|_{[v_0, \dots, v_p]}) \cdot \beta(\sigma|_{[v_p, \dots, v_{p+q}]})$ for a singular $(p+q)$-simplex $\sigma$, and descends to a graded-commutative ring structure on $H^*(M; G)$. Under the de Rham isomorphism, the cup product corresponds to the wedge product of differential forms — $[\omega] \smile [\eta] = [\omega \wedge \eta]$. **Poincaré duality** for a compact oriented $n$-manifold then says the cap product with the fundamental class $[M] \in H_n(M; \mathbb{Z})$ gives an isomorphism $H^k(M) \cong H_{n-k}(M)$, the algebraic shadow of the Hodge star $\star : \Omega^k \to \Omega^{n-k}$. Both topics are taken up in [[Hodge Theory I — Harmonic Forms and the Hodge Decomposition]] and beyond.

- **[[Ex - The Pairing Integral over a Simplex of a Form is a Chain-Homotopy Invariant]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show directly from Stokes's theorem that $\int_c \omega$ depends only on the homology class $[c]$ and the de Rham class $[\omega]$. The two computations are dual: if $\omega' = \omega + d\eta$ then $\int_c \omega' - \int_c \omega = \int_c d\eta = \int_{\partial c} \eta = 0$ since $\partial c = 0$; if $c' = c + \partial b$ then $\int_{c'} \omega - \int_c \omega = \int_{\partial b} \omega = \int_b d\omega = 0$ since $d\omega = 0$. This is the well-definedness step in the construction of the de Rham homomorphism.

- **[[Ex - The de Rham Pairing for H^1 of S^1]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For $M = S^1$, $H_1(S^1; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$ is generated by the loop $\gamma$ going once around, and $H^1_{dR}(S^1) = \mathbb{R}$ is generated by $[d\theta]$. Verify the de Rham pairing: $\int_\gamma d\theta = 2\pi$. Conclude that $[d\theta]/(2\pi)$ is the dual cohomology class to $[\gamma]$ in the singular cohomology. This is the minimal non-trivial instance of the de Rham isomorphism.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Spectral Sequences *(from Algebraic Topology and Homological Algebra)*
> The proof of the de Rham theorem by Mayer–Vietoris induction generalises: any time one cohomology theory can be reduced to another by repeated application of a five-term exact sequence on a filtration or a cover, one is computing a **spectral sequence**. The Čech-to-derived-functor spectral sequence, the Serre spectral sequence of a fibration, the Hodge-to-de-Rham spectral sequence in complex geometry are all instances. Every modern cohomology computation in algebraic geometry, complex geometry, and algebraic topology runs through some spectral sequence.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Steenrod Operations *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> The cup product gives $H^*(M; \mathbb{Z}/p)$ the structure of a graded-commutative ring; the **Steenrod operations** $\mathrm{Sq}^i : H^p(M; \mathbb{Z}/2) \to H^{p+i}(M; \mathbb{Z}/2)$ and their odd-prime analogues give it the structure of a module over the **Steenrod algebra**. These operations are the finer-grained cohomological invariants that distinguish spaces with the same Betti numbers but different homotopy type — they were used to prove Hopf-invariant-one results, the non-parallelisability of $S^{n}$ for $n \neq 1, 3, 7$, and many other deep results.

> [!tip] Unlocked: K-Theory *(from Algebraic Topology and Operator Algebras)*
> Replacing chains by vector bundles and the Euler characteristic by an alternating sum of bundle classes gives **topological K-theory** $K^0(M)$, the abelian group of formal differences of vector bundles on $M$. It is a generalised cohomology theory satisfying every Eilenberg–Steenrod axiom *except* dimension, and computes the same answer for $\mathbb{C}P^\infty$ as ordinary cohomology by accident, but distinguishes spaces ordinary cohomology cannot. Atiyah–Singer index theory then identifies the index of an elliptic operator as a K-theory pairing.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Étale Cohomology *(from Algebraic Geometry)*
> For varieties over fields of positive characteristic — where smooth forms and integration are not available — Grothendieck constructed **étale cohomology** as a substitute for singular cohomology that satisfies Poincaré duality, Lefschetz fixed-point counting, and the Weil conjectures. The de Rham theorem inspired the comparison theorems of $p$-adic Hodge theory, which relate étale cohomology to de Rham cohomology of lifts to characteristic zero — the central machinery of modern arithmetic geometry.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Intersection Homology *(from Algebraic Topology and Algebraic Geometry)*
> For singular spaces — algebraic varieties with singularities, stratified spaces — ordinary singular homology fails Poincaré duality. **Intersection homology** (Goresky–MacPherson) repairs this by restricting allowed simplices to meet strata transversally with controlled dimensions; the resulting groups recover Poincaré duality and form the cornerstone of the geometric Langlands program and modern Hodge theory of singular varieties.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

Exercises in this chapter cluster around five recurring goals. The most common is **computing $H_p(M; G)$ for a specific space** — a sphere, torus, projective space, surface, Klein bottle — typically by Mayer–Vietoris induction starting from contractible pieces, or by triangulating the space and reading off the simplicial homology. A second target is to **identify a singular cycle as non-trivial** (or as a boundary), which on a non-contractible space reduces to computing the pairing $\int_z \omega$ against a closed form $\omega$ representing a known cohomology class: if the integral is non-zero, the cycle is non-trivial. A third is to **compute the Betti numbers and Euler characteristic** of a manifold, often as a numerical check on a more elaborate homology computation — $b_0 = \#$components and $\chi(M) = V - E + F$ for a triangulation are the fast sanity tests. A fourth is to **verify the de Rham pairing on a concrete pair $(\omega, c)$** — integrating a specific form over a specific cycle to recover a number that identifies a cohomology class. A fifth is to **decide whether two spaces are homotopy equivalent (or distinguish them)** by computing and comparing their homology — non-isomorphic homology groups force non-equivalent spaces, though the converse fails. These five — compute $H_*$, certify (non-)triviality, compute Euler characteristic, evaluate periods, distinguish spaces — are the recurring questions, and they recur because each pins down a different aspect of "how holey is $M$, and what closed forms detect those holes."

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses in these problems are equally stereotyped. **A specific topological space whose structure is presentable** — given as a CW complex, a polygon with identifications, a regular subset of Euclidean space, or a Lie group quotient — and the presentation itself is what enables the computation. **An open cover $M = U \cup V$ with tractable pieces** — usually $U$ and $V$ are contractible (so their homology is $G$ in degree zero, zero elsewhere) and $U \cap V$ has known homology, enabling Mayer–Vietoris to compute $H_*(M)$. **A continuous (or smooth) map between spaces** — induces a chain map and a homology map, so a known map $f : M \to N$ can transfer cycles between spaces, sometimes converting a hard computation in $M$ to an easy one in $N$ via the functoriality $(f \circ g)_* = f_* g_*$. **A homotopy or homotopy equivalence** — by [[Thm - Homotopy Invariance of Singular Homology|homotopy invariance]], deforming $M$ onto a homotopy-equivalent simpler space preserves homology, so the Möbius strip is replaced by $S^1$, $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$ by $S^{n-1}$, a manifold by its $1$-skeleton or core. **A closed form on the space** — combined with a cycle, gives a number $\int_c \omega$ via the de Rham pairing; this is how a "smooth" calculation produces a topological invariant. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a cover routes to Mayer–Vietoris to a long exact sequence to $H_*(M)$; a closed form routes to integration to a number to a cohomology class; a homotopy equivalence routes to homology-preserving identification. The [[Algebraic Topology I — Singular Homology and the de Rham Theorem#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves that almost every problem in this chapter is assembled from. Read them as a checklist: when stuck, scan and try each one.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Push a $p$-chain forward along a continuous map.** Given $f : M \to N$ continuous and $c \in C_p(M; G)$, the chain $f_\#(c) = \sum a_i (f \circ \sigma_i)$ is a $p$-chain in $N$. Since $f_\#$ commutes with $\partial$, it carries cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries, hence descends to $f_* : H_p(M) \to H_p(N)$. *Trigger:* you want to compare homology classes of $M$ and $N$, or transfer a cycle between spaces. *Pattern:* push the cycle, take its class downstairs, compare with known generators.

2. **Subdivide a chain into pieces that lie in a single cover element.** Given an open cover $\{U, V\}$ of $M$ and a singular simplex $\sigma : \Delta^p \to M$, **barycentric subdivision** breaks $\sigma$ into smaller simplices each of which (after enough iterations, by a Lebesgue-number argument) lies inside $U$ or inside $V$. The result is chain-homotopic to the original $\sigma$, so homology is unchanged. *Trigger:* you want to set up Mayer–Vietoris but your cycle stretches across both cover elements. *Pattern:* subdivide, separate the pieces by which cover element they live in, take the boundary of the $U$-piece to read off the Mayer–Vietoris connecting map.

3. **Detect a non-trivial homology class by integrating a closed form against it.** If $c$ is a smooth $p$-cycle and $\omega$ is a closed $p$-form with $\int_c \omega \neq 0$, then $[c] \neq 0$ in $H_p(M; \mathbb{R})$ — and dually $[\omega] \neq 0$ in $H^p_{dR}(M)$. *Trigger:* you suspect a cycle is non-trivial; you suspect a closed form is non-exact. *Pattern:* by [[Thm - Stokes' Theorem on Manifolds|Stokes]], $\int_c \omega = \int_{c'} \omega$ for homologous cycles, so a non-zero integral certifies non-triviality on both sides simultaneously.

4. **Deform a cycle through a homotopy and conclude it is homologous to the deformed cycle.** If a $p$-cycle $z$ is continuously deformed to $z'$ through a one-parameter family of cycles $z_t$, the "deformation chain" swept out by $z_t$ is a $(p+1)$-chain $C$ with $\partial C = z' - z$, so $[z'] = [z]$ in $H_p$. *Trigger:* a cycle is hard to evaluate but admits an obvious deformation onto something simpler. *Pattern:* deform a cycle off a pole, deform onto a coordinate axis, contract onto a sub-manifold of lower dimension — the homology class never changes.

5. **Apply Mayer–Vietoris to compute homology from a two-piece cover.** Write $M = U \cup V$ with $U$, $V$, $U \cap V$ each cohomologically tractable; the long exact sequence then computes $H_*(M)$ from the simpler pieces. *Trigger:* the space has an obvious two-piece decomposition (two open hemispheres, two patches of a torus, an open set and its complement-thickened). *Pattern:* set up the sequence; identify which terms vanish (often because $U$ or $V$ is contractible); the surviving short exact sequence determines $H_p(M)$ up to extension.

6. **Use homotopy invariance to replace $M$ with a simpler space.** If $M \simeq N$ (homotopy equivalent), then $H_*(M; G) \cong H_*(N; G)$ for any coefficient group $G$. *Trigger:* the manifold of interest has obvious deformation retracts. *Pattern:* $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\} \simeq S^{n-1}$ by radial projection; Möbius strip $\simeq S^1$ by the core circle; a tubular neighborhood of a submanifold $\simeq$ the submanifold. Always look for the simplest representative of the homotopy type *before* setting up any computation.

7. **Read $H_0$ as a count of path components.** $H_0(M; G) = G^{\#\text{components}}$ — one copy of $G$ for each path component. *Trigger:* you want to check connectedness of $M$, or you want a fast sanity check on a homology computation. *Pattern:* if your computation produces $H_0$ that does not match the expected component count, the rest is wrong.

8. **Read the top homology as the orientation count.** For a closed connected $n$-manifold $M$, $H_n(M; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$ if $M$ is orientable (generated by the fundamental class $[M]$) and $0$ if $M$ is non-orientable. *Trigger:* you want to test orientability; you want to identify the top homology generator. *Pattern:* triangulate $M$, sum the simplices with consistent orientation signs, check whether $\partial[M] = 0$ (orientable, $[M]$ is a non-trivial top cycle) or $\partial[M] \neq 0$ (non-orientable).

9. **Switch coefficient groups to see different information.** Singular homology with $\mathbb{Z}$ coefficients sees torsion; with $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{Q}$ it does not (universal coefficient theorem); with $\mathbb{Z}/p$ it sees $p$-torsion specifically and removes free factors mod $p$. *Trigger:* a calculation in one coefficient ring looks degenerate or you want extra information. *Pattern:* compute $\mathbb{Z}$-homology first (sees everything), then switch to $\mathbb{R}$ to extract the Betti numbers cleanly, and to $\mathbb{Z}/2$ for non-orientable spaces where orientation signs are problematic.

10. **Use the Euler characteristic as a numerical invariant.** $\chi(M) = \sum_p (-1)^p \dim H_p(M; \mathbb{R})$ is computable from any chain complex resolution (singular, simplicial, cellular, de Rham). *Trigger:* you want a fast sanity check or a single-number distinguisher between spaces. *Pattern:* compute $\chi$ from a triangulation ($V - E + F$ for surfaces); compute it from cellular or CW structure; compute it from forms via de Rham; cross-check the results.

11. **Use a CW or cellular structure to reduce the computation to one chain group per dimension.** A CW complex has cellular chain groups $C_p^{\mathrm{CW}} = \mathbb{Z}^{\#p\text{-cells}}$, finite-dimensional in each degree. The cellular chain complex computes singular homology via the cellular boundary formula (incidence numbers between cells). *Trigger:* the space has a known CW decomposition with few cells. *Pattern:* read off the cellular chains, compute the cellular boundary, take homology — vastly faster than working with singular chains directly. This is how $H_*(\mathbb{CP}^n)$, $H_*(\mathbb{RP}^n)$, $H_*(\text{Grassmannian})$ are computed in practice.

12. **Apply naturality of the long exact sequence.** A map $f : (M, A) \to (N, B)$ of pairs induces a map of long exact sequences (of pairs, of Mayer–Vietoris, of the connecting maps), with all squares commuting. *Trigger:* you have a homology fact on one space and want to transfer it via a specific map. *Pattern:* draw the commutative diagram of two long exact sequences with $f_*$ vertical maps; use the **five lemma** to conclude that if four maps are isomorphisms, the fifth is too. This is how Mayer–Vietoris is propagated across an inductive proof — and it is the central tool in the proof of the de Rham theorem.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Confusing a singular simplex with an embedded simplex
> It is tempting to picture a singular $2$-simplex as an embedded triangle in $M$. But the definition is just "continuous map $\Delta^2 \to M$" — the image can be a single point (constant map), a curve, a wildly self-intersecting set, or any continuous image. A constant simplex $\sigma : \Delta^p \to \{q\} \subset M$ at a point $q$ has the alternating-sum boundary $\partial \sigma = \sum (-1)^k \sigma|_{\text{face}_k} = (\sum (-1)^k) \sigma_{p-1} = 0$ for $p$ even and $\sigma_{p-1}$ for $p$ odd — so constant simplices are not all zero, and the chain complex of a point is non-trivial in every dimension before taking homology. The operation becomes legal exactly when you remember that singular simplices are *maps*, not subsets, and that two maps with the same image are different simplices.

> [!warning] 2. Assuming integer homology determines real homology (forgetting torsion)
> Real and integer homology can differ in two ways. First, free parts agree: the rank of $H_p(M; \mathbb{Z})$ equals $\dim_\mathbb{R} H_p(M; \mathbb{R})$. Second, **torsion** in integer homology is invisible to real coefficients. For $\mathbb{RP}^2$, $H_1(\mathbb{RP}^2; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}/2$ (torsion) but $H_1(\mathbb{RP}^2; \mathbb{R}) = 0$ (the torsion dies after tensoring with $\mathbb{R}$). So an integer-homology computation gives strictly more information than the real one, but the real-homology answer cannot reconstruct torsion. The operation becomes legal when restricted to free integer homology — the part that survives the change of coefficient.

> [!warning] 3. Believing the converse of "non-isomorphic homology implies non-equivalent spaces"
> Homology is a homotopy invariant, so $H_*(M) \neq H_*(N)$ forces $M \not\simeq N$. The converse fails: there exist spaces with isomorphic homology in all degrees that are *not* homotopy equivalent — the Eilenberg–MacLane space $K(\mathbb{Z}, 2)$ and $\mathbb{CP}^\infty$ have the same homology but different homotopy groups; the lens spaces $L(7, 1)$ and $L(7, 2)$ have the same homology but are not homotopy equivalent (distinguished by linking numbers, equivalently by the cohomology *ring* structure). Homology alone does not classify spaces; it is a powerful invariant but not a complete one. The cup product, higher homotopy groups, and characteristic classes are needed to refine the classification.

> [!warning] 4. Concluding a cycle is a boundary just because its integrals against finitely many closed forms vanish
> The de Rham pairing $\int_c \omega$ is a perfect pairing on $H_p(M; \mathbb{R}) \times H^p_{dR}(M)$, but only when you have access to *all* closed forms. Vanishing of $\int_c \omega$ for a particular $\omega$ only tells you $[c]$ is in the kernel of *that* linear functional, not that $[c] = 0$. The cycle $A \in H_1(T^2; \mathbb{R})$ has $\int_A dB = 0$ but $\int_A dA = 2\pi \neq 0$, so $[A] \neq 0$ — vanishing against the "wrong" form proves nothing. The operation becomes legal when you have integrated against a *complete basis* of $H^p_{dR}(M)$ and they all vanish.

> [!warning] 5. Mistaking the dimension of the singular cohomology over a field for the rank of integer cohomology
> Over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{Q}$, singular cohomology dimensions equal Betti numbers and the universal coefficient theorem is transparent. Over $\mathbb{Z}/p$, cohomology dimensions can be *larger* than the Betti numbers because torsion of order $p$ in integer homology contributes to mod-$p$ cohomology in both adjacent degrees (via the Tor and Ext sequences). For $\mathbb{RP}^2$: $b_0 = 1$, $b_1 = 0$, $b_2 = 0$, but $\dim_{\mathbb{Z}/2} H^*(\mathbb{RP}^2; \mathbb{Z}/2) = (1, 1, 1)$ — three dimensions in mod-$2$ cohomology where the Betti numbers see only one. The operation "compute Betti numbers from mod-$p$ cohomology" becomes legal only when $p$ does not divide any torsion in integer homology.

> [!warning] 6. Forgetting that the de Rham theorem requires smoothness
> Singular cohomology $H^p(M; \mathbb{R})$ is defined for any topological space; de Rham cohomology $H^p_{dR}(M)$ is defined only for smooth manifolds. The de Rham theorem says the two are isomorphic *on smooth manifolds*, so the question "what is $H^*_{dR}$ of a topological space?" is ill-posed. For non-smooth spaces (CW complexes, algebraic varieties with singularities) one needs a substitute — Čech cohomology, sheaf cohomology, intersection homology. The operation "use forms to compute cohomology" becomes legal exactly when a smooth structure is available.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this chapter divide into three camps, and the routine for each is well-defined.

If the problem **asks for $H_*(M; G)$ for a specific space**, the routine is: first identify the homotopy type. Is $M$ contractible? Then $H_0 = G$ and $H_p = 0$ for $p \geq 1$, and you are done. Is $M$ homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres, a torus, a sphere, a known space? Then [[Thm - Homotopy Invariance of Singular Homology|homotopy invariance]] gives the homology for free. Failing that, choose a presentation: either a CW structure (cellular homology is fast — one chain group per cell, boundary maps from incidence numbers), or a Mayer–Vietoris cover (two open sets $U \cup V = M$, each as simple as possible, intersection tractable), or a triangulation (simplicial homology agrees with singular by [[Thm - Singular and Simplicial Homology Agree on Triangulable Spaces]]). Set up the long exact sequence, fill in the unknowns by exactness, and read off $H_p$. When the answer is non-trivial in some degree, identify an explicit generator — a cycle whose homology class spans the group — both for sanity-checking and for later use.

If the problem **asks whether a specific cycle is null-homologous** (or whether two cycles are homologous), the routine is shorter. Either (a) construct an explicit $(p+1)$-chain whose boundary is the cycle (or their difference), in which case you have proved the homology relation; or (b) find a closed $p$-form $\omega$ with $\int_c \omega \neq 0$ (respectively $\int_c \omega \neq \int_{c'} \omega$), in which case you have proved non-triviality via the de Rham pairing. Strategy (b) is often easier when one has access to the full de Rham apparatus — a closed form is easier to write down than a chain. The two strategies are dual: (a) directly exhibits a boundary, (b) certifies non-triviality through a non-zero pairing against a dual cohomology class.

If the problem **asks for the de Rham pairing in a specific case** — what is $\int_c \omega$ for given $c$ and $\omega$ — the computation is just direct integration, but the *meaning* of the answer is what is interesting. The number obtained is a topological invariant: by [[Thm - The de Rham Theorem (Full Proof)|de Rham]], it depends only on $[c] \in H_p(M; \mathbb{R})$ and $[\omega] \in H^p_{dR}(M)$, not on the chosen representatives. So if you can identify $[c]$ and $[\omega]$ as known generators of the respective groups, the integer (or rational, or real) you computed identifies one as the dual cohomology class of the other up to scaling. This is how the periods of a closed form are read off.

If the problem **asks for Betti numbers or Euler characteristic**, you have a choice of routes: compute via singular/cellular/simplicial homology and take dimensions; or compute via the de Rham complex (count independent closed forms modulo exact); or use a triangulation and count $V - E + F$ (for surfaces) or $\sum (-1)^k \#k\text{-cells}$ (for CW complexes); or, when the manifold is a Lie group quotient or a homogeneous space, use the Künneth formula and the homology of the constituent groups. Cross-check by computing two ways: a discrepancy reveals an arithmetic error or a misidentified generator.

A meta-strategy threads through everything: **the singular chain complex is enormous (uncountable in every dimension), but homology is small (often finitely generated). The art is to replace the chain complex by something computable while preserving the homology**. Triangulations replace singular by simplicial; CW structures replace simplicial by cellular; the de Rham theorem replaces singular cohomology by de Rham cohomology, which has the small finite-dimensional spaces $\Omega^k$ at each degree. Every concrete computation in algebraic topology is, in the end, a choice of "small replacement" for the singular complex.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Homotopy Invariance of Singular Homology|Homotopy invariance]]**: continuous maps that are homotopic induce equal maps on homology, hence homotopy equivalent spaces have isomorphic homology. **Typical use:** before computing $H_*(M)$, deform-retract $M$ onto a simpler homotopy-equivalent space ($\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\} \to S^{n-1}$, Möbius strip $\to S^1$, tubular neighborhood $\to$ core). This is the most-used single property of singular homology — it converts hard computations to trivial ones and shows that homology is a topological invariant of a more fundamental kind (the homotopy type, not the homeomorphism type). The disguised use: a "smooth" structure on $M$ does not matter for homology; only the underlying homotopy type does.

- **[[Thm - Mayer-Vietoris for Singular Homology|Mayer–Vietoris]]**: for any open cover $M = U \cup V$, a long exact sequence connects $H_*(U \cap V)$, $H_*(U) \oplus H_*(V)$, and $H_*(M)$. **Typical use:** induction on the number of pieces in a "good cover" — start with two open sets and chain. Every explicit computation of $H_*(S^n)$, $H_*(T^n)$, $H_*(\mathbb{CP}^n)$ runs through iterated Mayer–Vietoris. The disguised use: it shows that local information completely determines global homology, with the gluing data encoded in the connecting maps. Mayer–Vietoris is also the inductive engine in the proof of the [[Thm - The de Rham Theorem (Full Proof)|de Rham theorem]].

- **[[Thm - The de Rham Theorem (Full Proof)|The de Rham Theorem]]**: $H^p_{dR}(M) \cong H^p(M; \mathbb{R})$ via integration. **Typical use:** convert questions about smooth forms into topological questions and vice versa. Every Betti number, Euler characteristic, characteristic class, and curvature integral on a smooth manifold is at once a smooth invariant (computable from forms) and a topological invariant (intrinsic to the homotopy type). The disguised use: the answer to "what does this integral compute" in any geometric problem is "a singular cohomology pairing" — and topology then explains why the answer is invariant under deformations.

- **[[Def - Singular Homology|Singular homology as a functor \mathbf{Top} → \mathbf{Ab}]]**: continuous maps induce homology homomorphisms, and this assignment respects composition. **Typical use:** transport homological information between spaces via known maps. A retraction $r : M \to A$ with inclusion $i : A \hookrightarrow M$ satisfying $r \circ i = \mathrm{id}_A$ produces $r_* \circ i_* = \mathrm{id}$, so $i_* : H_*(A) \to H_*(M)$ is injective — the homology of any retract is a summand. The disguised use: most "homology of $M$ equals homology of $N$" arguments come from constructing two maps inducing inverse homology isomorphisms.

- **[[Thm - Singular Homology of the Sphere|Singular homology of the sphere]]**: $H_p(S^n; G) = G$ for $p = 0, n$ and zero otherwise. **Typical use:** the "atomic" building block from which most explicit computations are built — via Mayer–Vietoris (intersections deformation-retract onto lower-dimensional spheres), wedges of spheres (homology is direct sum), and suspension (raises dimension by one). The disguised use: any space whose homology is concentrated in two degrees is "sphere-like," and the comparison with $S^n$ reveals what dimension it represents.

- **The fundamental class $[M] \in H_n(M; \mathbb{Z})$**: for a closed connected orientable $n$-manifold, the top homology is $\mathbb{Z}$ with a canonical generator (up to sign — fixing the sign is choosing an orientation). **Typical use:** the fundamental class is what Poincaré duality caps against to convert cohomology to homology in complementary degree, and what one integrates a top-degree form against to get a single number. It is the universal "orientation cycle" of a manifold, and its existence/non-existence is the orientation/non-orientation dichotomy at the homological level.

# Bridges

1. **Differential geometry — de Rham cohomology equals singular cohomology with real coefficients.** This is the [[Thm - The de Rham Theorem (Full Proof)|de Rham theorem]], the central bridge of the chapter. On the de Rham side, cohomology is built from smooth differential forms with the exterior derivative; on the singular side, it is built from continuous singular cochains with the simplicial coboundary. The integration map $\omega \mapsto \int (\cdot)\, \omega$ converts a smooth form into a functional on singular cycles, descending to an isomorphism $H^p_{dR}(M) \to H^p(M; \mathbb{R})$. The consequence is that every topological invariant (Betti number, Euler characteristic, characteristic class) is computable both topologically and smoothly, and the answers must match. This is the bridge under which the entire program of [[Hodge Theory I — Harmonic Forms and the Hodge Decomposition]], [[Gauge Theory VII — The Hodge Star, Electrodynamics, and Yang–Mills Theory]], and characteristic-class theory operates.

2. **Group theory — homology groups are abelian groups, and homology with coefficients is a tensor product.** The singular homology $H_p(M; G)$ is, as the name says, an abelian group — built as a quotient of free abelian groups (the chains $C_p(M; G)$) by another abelian group (the boundaries $B_p$). When $G = \mathbb{Z}$, this is a quotient of [[Def - Free Module|free ℤ-modules]] (free abelian groups, viewed as $\mathbb{Z}$-modules) from `Modules I`; the resulting integer homology can have free parts (the Betti numbers) and torsion parts (cyclic groups). The universal coefficient theorem then computes $H_p(M; G)$ from $H_p(M; \mathbb{Z})$ via tensor and torsion-product functors, generalising the construction to arbitrary coefficient groups. The bridge to group theory is precise: every homological-algebra concept used in this chapter — short exact sequences, the snake lemma, the five lemma — is a statement about abelian groups, applied to the specific chain complexes arising from topology.

3. **Algebraic geometry — singular cohomology of complex varieties decomposes by Hodge type.** For a compact Kähler manifold $X$ (e.g. a smooth complex projective variety), the singular cohomology $H^k(X; \mathbb{C})$ decomposes as $\bigoplus_{p+q=k} H^{p,q}(X)$, where $H^{p,q}$ is represented by closed forms of bidegree $(p, q)$ — this is the **Hodge decomposition**, a refinement of the de Rham theorem available only in the Kähler setting. The Hodge numbers $h^{p,q} = \dim_\mathbb{C} H^{p,q}$ satisfy Hodge symmetry $h^{p,q} = h^{q,p}$ and Serre duality $h^{p,q} = h^{n-p,n-q}$ (with $n$ the complex dimension), and they refine the Betti numbers via $b_k = \sum_{p+q=k} h^{p,q}$. The de Rham theorem makes this refinement possible — without identifying smooth cohomology with topological cohomology, the Hodge decomposition would not be a *topological* invariant.

4. **Algebraic topology — homology and homotopy diverge above the first non-trivial degree, but agree in it (Hurewicz).** The fundamental group $\pi_1(M)$ measures loops up to homotopy; the first homology $H_1(M; \mathbb{Z})$ measures loops up to homology, which is the same thing as homotopy *plus* abelianisation. The **Hurewicz theorem** makes this precise: $H_1(M; \mathbb{Z}) = \pi_1(M)^{\mathrm{ab}}$, the abelianisation of the fundamental group. In higher degrees, for simply connected $M$, the first non-zero homotopy group $\pi_n(M)$ equals $H_n(M; \mathbb{Z})$, but above $n$ the two diverge — homology is "abelianised homotopy" and loses the non-abelian information of the higher homotopy groups. See [[Algebraic Topology II — Fundamental Group and Covering Spaces]] for the abelianisation story, and [[Algebraic Topology III — Higher Homotopy and Chern Forms]] for the higher comparison.

5. **Riemannian geometry — Euler characteristic equals an integral of Gaussian curvature.** For a closed Riemannian surface, the [[Riemannian Geometry IV — Classical Geometry of Surfaces in R^3|Gauss–Bonnet]] theorem says $\int_M K\, dA = 2\pi \chi(M)$, where $K$ is the Gaussian curvature and $dA$ the area form. The Euler characteristic — a topological invariant of $M$ — equals an integral that depends only on the metric. By the de Rham theorem and the identification of $\chi$ as an alternating sum of cohomology dimensions, this is a smooth-form expression of a singular-homology quantity. The generalisation to higher dimensions is the **Chern–Gauss–Bonnet** theorem: $\chi(M) = \int_M \mathrm{Pf}(R/2\pi)$ for $M$ a closed oriented even-dimensional manifold and $\mathrm{Pf}$ the Pfaffian of the curvature, computed via a specific Chern–Weil construction (see [[Gauge Theory VI — Chern–Weil Theory, Characteristic Classes, and the Chern–Simons Functional]]).

6. **Mathematical physics — winding numbers, instanton numbers, and topological charges as singular homology classes.** In gauge theory and condensed matter physics, "topological charges" — the winding number of a complex phase around a defect, the instanton number of a gauge field on $\mathbb{R}^4$, the Chern numbers of a band-structure bundle on the Brillouin torus — are all integer-valued invariants computed by integrating a curvature-related form over a cycle. By the de Rham theorem, each is the value of a de Rham cohomology class on a homology class — an integer because the cohomology class is the image of an integer cohomology class under the inclusion $H^*(M; \mathbb{Z}) \to H^*(M; \mathbb{R})$. This is why these "physical" integers are robust under continuous deformations: deforming the field within its homotopy class leaves the cohomology class unchanged, hence leaves the integral fixed.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: singular homology counts cycles up to bounding, and the de Rham theorem says smooth forms are exactly the right tool to detect them.** The two halves of this chapter look like different subjects — combinatorial topology of formal simplex sums on one side, smooth differential calculus and integration on the other. But they are dual constructions of the same answer. A non-trivial homology class is a cycle wrapped around a topological hole; a non-exact closed form is a form whose periods detect that hole. Integration is the bilinear pairing that converts each side into the linear functional viewpoint of the other. So "how many independent holes does $M$ have in dimension $p$" has two equivalent answers — the rank of $H_p$ and the dimension of $H^p_{dR}$ — and the de Rham theorem is the explicit pairing certifying they agree. This is the model for every later "two cohomology theories agree" theorem in mathematics, from the comparison theorems between étale and de Rham cohomology to the AdS/CFT correspondence's identification of bulk and boundary cohomologies.

**The true name of singular homology is "the abelianised, dimension-stratified homotopy."** The textbook definition of $H_p$ as $\ker\partial / \mathrm{im}\,\partial$ is the right thing to compute but the wrong thing to think. The operational viewpoint is that $H_p(M; \mathbb{Z})$ measures "$p$-dimensional cycles up to bounding," which after abelianisation becomes the linearised, dimension-stratified analogue of the higher homotopy groups $\pi_p(M)$. The Hurewicz theorem makes this precise in the first non-trivial degree, where $H_n = \pi_n^{\mathrm{ab}}$. Above that, $H_p$ continues to detect "$p$-dimensional sphere-like things in $M$ up to filling," but in the simpler, abelian world where formal sums are allowed. The price of abelianness is the loss of information beyond Eilenberg–MacLane spaces; the benefit is computability — $H_*(M; \mathbb{Z})$ is finitely computable for any reasonable $M$, while $\pi_*(M)$ is famously not (e.g. $\pi_k(S^2)$ is unknown in general).

**A trigger-reaction pattern for detecting non-triviality: see a closed form and a cycle, compute the integral. If non-zero, both are non-trivial.** This is the single most-used computational consequence of the de Rham theorem. To prove $[c] \neq 0$ in $H_p(M; \mathbb{R})$, exhibit a closed form $\omega$ with $\int_c \omega \neq 0$ — then by [[Thm - Stokes' Theorem on Manifolds|Stokes]], $c$ cannot be a boundary (because integrals of closed forms over boundaries vanish), and $\omega$ cannot be exact (because integrals of exact forms over cycles vanish). One computation certifies *both* sides simultaneously. The classical example is $S^1$ with $c$ the loop and $\omega = d\theta/(2\pi)$: the integral is $1$, certifying both that $S^1$ is not simply connected (the loop does not bound) and that $d\theta$ is not exact on $S^1$ (there is no global angle function). Every "topological invariant computed by an integral" — winding numbers, Chern numbers, instanton charges — is a pairing of a closed form against a cycle in disguise.

**Inheritance — Betti numbers come from the underlying topology, not the smooth structure.** The Betti numbers $b_p(M) = \dim H_p(M; \mathbb{R})$ are defined from the topological space alone — no smooth structure required. The de Rham theorem makes them computable by smooth means, but the answer does not depend on the smooth structure: a $7$-sphere with the standard smooth structure and a $7$-sphere with an exotic smooth structure (a Milnor sphere) have the same Betti numbers because they have the same homotopy type. Smooth structure adds *more* invariants (the differential structure can be distinguished by, e.g., Donaldson invariants on $4$-manifolds), but it cannot change the homology. The inheritance principle: every quantity in this chapter — Betti numbers, Euler characteristic, fundamental class, cohomology pairings — is inherited from the homotopy type, and smooth structure is just a convenient way to compute them.

**Local-to-global propagation through Mayer–Vietoris and the long exact sequence.** Singular homology of a space is determined by its local homology (on contractible pieces, it is trivial; on a "small enough" open set it is just $\mathbb{Z}$ in degree zero), plus the gluing combinatorics of how the pieces fit together. Mayer–Vietoris is the precise accounting: the connecting map $\delta : H_p(M) \to H_{p-1}(U \cap V)$ records the "mismatch" between local descriptions on $U$ and $V$, and that mismatch is a homology class one dimension lower. The chapter teaches that all of the global topology of $M$ is encoded in local-to-global propagation: cover $M$ by contractibles, record the intersections and triple-intersections, and the resulting combinatorial data (a Čech complex) computes the cohomology. This is the prototype of every modern cohomology theory — sheaf cohomology, étale cohomology, motivic cohomology are all variations on the same theme.

**The "purpose" of coefficient groups: $\mathbb{Z}$ to see everything, $\mathbb{R}$ to extract dimensions, $\mathbb{Z}/p$ to spot $p$-torsion.** Singular homology with different coefficient groups sees different information about a space. $\mathbb{Z}$-coefficients are universal — they encode both the free Betti-number information and the torsion. $\mathbb{R}$-coefficients (or any field of characteristic zero) kill all torsion and reveal only the Betti numbers, vastly simplifying the structure to vector-space-dimension counting. $\mathbb{Z}/p$-coefficients (with $p$ a prime) selectively reveal $p$-torsion and free parts mod $p$ — the standard tool for detecting non-orientable phenomena ($p = 2$), and for Steenrod operations more generally. The universal coefficient theorem mediates between these: $H_*(M; G)$ for any $G$ is computable from $H_*(M; \mathbb{Z})$ via tensor and torsion products. So the "right" coefficient group for a given problem is the one that exposes the information you care about without burying it in irrelevant detail.

**Density as a strategic lever: the singular chain complex is overwhelming, but homology cares only about a "small" sub-complex of representative cycles.** $C_p(M; G)$ is uncountable in every dimension (all continuous maps $\Delta^p \to M$), but $H_p(M; G)$ is finitely generated for compact $M$. The bridge is **density**: any cycle can be approximated arbitrarily well by a *simplicial* cycle (a cycle supported on a triangulation), by a *smooth* cycle (a cycle made of smooth simplices), by a *cellular* cycle (a cycle made of CW cells), and the homology class of the approximation equals the homology class of the original. So the gigantic singular chain complex collapses, on passing to homology, to whatever small finite-dimensional complex one prefers. This density argument is what makes the abstract definition of singular homology compatible with the concrete computations of triangulations, CW structures, and the de Rham complex.
