# Notation

$M$ is a connected manifold (smooth or topological), assumed without boundary unless stated. An **orientation** at a point $x \in M$ is a choice of one of the two components of $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}$ via a local chart, equivalently a generator of $H_n(M, M \setminus \{x\}; \mathbb{Z}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. The **orientable double cover** is $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}} \to M$ with projection $\pi$. See [[Algebraic Topology II — Fundamental Group and Covering Spaces]] for the full registry.

---

# Axiom Motivation

A manifold $M$ is **orientable** when one can choose an orientation at every point in a way that varies continuously. Some manifolds — like the sphere, the torus, $\mathbb{R}^n$ — admit such a global choice. Others — like the Möbius strip, the Klein bottle, $\mathbb{RP}^2$ — do not. The non-orientable manifolds have *local* orientations everywhere (every point has a small neighbourhood that is orientable), but the local choices cannot be globalised because there exist loops in $M$ along which the orientation flips.

The orientable double cover formalises the following: even though $M$ may not have a global orientation, we can build a *new* space $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ whose points are *pairs* (point of $M$, choice of orientation at that point). This double-counts each point — there are two orientations at each $x$ — so $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is a 2-sheeted cover of $M$. The deck group $\mathbb{Z}/2$ swaps the two orientations.

The crucial question: is $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ connected? It is connected if and only if $M$ is non-orientable. Here is why: if $M$ is orientable, then a global orientation gives a section $M \to \widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ that splits the cover into two disjoint copies of $M$ (one for each orientation). If $M$ is non-orientable, no global section exists, and the two "sheets" of orientations get glued together — they have to, because some loop in $M$ flips orientation, so following the lift of that loop in $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ takes you from one sheet to the other, forcing connectedness.

So the orientable double cover has a clean dichotomy:
- **$M$ orientable:** $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}} \cong M \sqcup M$, two disjoint copies. The cover is trivial.
- **$M$ non-orientable:** $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is connected, and is itself an orientable manifold. The cover is non-trivial.

In either case, **$\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is orientable** — this is the construction's central feature. The orientation on $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is defined tautologically: at the point $(x, \text{orientation})$, the orientation *is* the chosen orientation. So we have manufactured an orientable space at the cost of doubling, and any object on $M$ that needs an orientation to be defined (like a volume form) can be defined on $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ instead.

Why is this useful? It lets you do "oriented things" on non-orientable manifolds by working on the double cover. Integration of $n$-forms on a non-orientable manifold is problematic; on $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$, it works fine, and the result on $M$ can be recovered as half the result on $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ (for $\mathbb{Z}/2$-equivariant integrands) or via **pseudoforms** (twisted forms — see [[Def - Pseudoform (Twisted Form)]]).

Construction. The total space $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is the set of pairs $(x, o_x)$ where $x \in M$ and $o_x$ is one of the two orientations at $x$. The projection is $\pi(x, o_x) = x$. The topology on $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is generated by *coherent neighbourhoods*: for an orientable chart $U \subseteq M$, the preimage $\pi^{-1}(U)$ consists of two copies of $U$, one for each consistent orientation across $U$. Loops in $M$ that preserve orientation lift to loops in $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$; loops that reverse orientation lift to paths joining the two preimages of the basepoint. This is exactly the algebraic data: $\pi_1(\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}) \leq \pi_1(M)$ is the subgroup of orientation-preserving loops, and the quotient $\pi_1(M)/\pi_1(\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}) = \mathbb{Z}/2$ is the orientation-character.

Equivalent characterisation via group cohomology. Orientations of $M$ are classified by an element of $H^1(M; \mathbb{Z}/2)$ — the **first Stiefel-Whitney class** $w_1(M)$. The cover $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is the cover corresponding to the kernel of the homomorphism $\pi_1(M) \to \mathbb{Z}/2$ given by $[\gamma] \mapsto w_1(\gamma)$ (orientation behavior along $\gamma$). So $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is the cover associated to the "orientation character" of $M$.

# The Definition

Let $M$ be a connected [[Def - Smooth Manifold|manifold]] (smooth or topological) without boundary.

The **orientable double cover** of $M$ is the [[Def - Covering Space|covering space]] $\pi : \widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}} \to M$ defined by
$$
\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}} = \{(x, o_x) : x \in M, \; o_x \text{ an orientation at } x\}
$$
with $\pi(x, o_x) = x$. The topology on $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is generated by basic open sets: for each oriented chart $(U, \phi)$ of $M$ with $\phi : U \to \mathbb{R}^n$ orientation-preserving, the set $\{(x, [\phi]_x) : x \in U\}$ (where $[\phi]_x$ is the orientation induced by $\phi$ at $x$) is open in $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$, and the complementary subset (using the opposite orientation) is also open.

**Properties.**

1. (**Two-to-one cover**) $\pi$ is a 2-sheeted covering map. The fibre over each $x$ is the two orientations of $M$ at $x$.

2. (**Always orientable**) $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is always orientable, even when $M$ is not — there is a tautological orientation $(x, o_x) \mapsto o_x$.

3. (**Dichotomy**) $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is connected if and only if $M$ is non-orientable. If $M$ is orientable, then $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}} \cong M \sqcup M$.

4. (**Deck group**) The deck group of $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}} \to M$ is $\mathbb{Z}/2$, with the non-trivial deck transformation being the **orientation-swap** $\sigma : (x, o_x) \mapsto (x, -o_x)$. This transformation is **orientation-reversing** on $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$.

5. (**Subgroup correspondence**) When $M$ is non-orientable, $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ corresponds (under the Galois correspondence) to the kernel of the **orientation homomorphism** $w_1 : \pi_1(M) \to \mathbb{Z}/2$. This is a subgroup of index 2, hence automatically normal — so $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}} \to M$ is a [[Def - Regular (Galois) Covering|regular cover]].

6. (**Manifold structure inherited**) $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ inherits a smooth structure from $M$ if $M$ is smooth, and the covering map $\pi$ is smooth.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The orientable double cover is the *pullback* of the orientation double cover of $BO(n)$ (the classifying space of $O(n)$) along the classifying map $M \to BO(n)$ of the tangent bundle. More elementarily: the orientation bundle of $M$ is a real line bundle $\mathrm{or}(M) \to M$ with structure group $\mathbb{Z}/2 = \{\pm 1\}$; the orientable double cover is the unit sphere (i.e., $\{\pm 1\}$-points) of this line bundle.

In bordism / cobordism theory, the orientable double cover provides the natural "orientation-fixing" trick for any computation: to compute on a non-orientable $M$, go up to $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ and use $\mathbb{Z}/2$-equivariance to descend.

---

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The orientable double cover is **the special case of "double cover associated to a $\mathbb{Z}/2$ symmetry"**. Generalising, any homomorphism $\pi_1(M) \to G$ to a finite group $G$ produces a $|G|$-fold cover of $M$ (the cover associated to the kernel). For $G = \mathbb{Z}/2$ and the homomorphism = orientation character, we get the orientable double cover. For $G = \mathbb{Z}/2$ and homomorphism = "spin obstruction $w_2$", we get the **spin double cover** (for spin manifolds). For more general $G$, we get other characteristic covers.

In bundle theory, the orientable double cover is the **frame-bundle reduction**: it is the quotient of the frame bundle $\mathrm{Fr}(M) / SO(n)$ — the space of oriented frames modulo orientation-preserving changes of frame. This reduces structure group from $O(n)$ to $SO(n)$ at the cost of doubling the base.

**True name:** the orientable double cover is *the universal cover for orientation* — the smallest space on which orientations exist coherently. Any object on $M$ that fails to be defined because of orientation issues lifts to a well-defined object on $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ — top forms become globally defined, integration becomes well-defined, and the obstruction to descending back to $M$ is precisely $\mathbb{Z}/2$-equivariance.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: orientable double cover of the Möbius strip is the cylinder $S^1 \times [-1, 1]$.** With deck transformation $(z, t) \mapsto (-z, -t)$ — equivalently, "half-rotate around the central circle and flip the radial coordinate." See [[Ex - The Orientable Double Cover of the Möbius Strip]].

**Is an instance: orientable double cover of the Klein bottle is the torus $T^2$.** The torus has area twice that of the Klein bottle. The deck transformation is an orientation-reversing involution; in the standard fundamental polygon picture, it identifies the two horizontal halves of the torus rectangle.

**Is an instance: orientable double cover of $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is $S^n$ for $n$ even.** For $n$ even, $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is non-orientable, and $\widetilde{\mathbb{RP}^n}^{\mathrm{or}} = S^n$. For $n$ odd, $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is orientable, so $\widetilde{\mathbb{RP}^n}^{\mathrm{or}} = \mathbb{RP}^n \sqcup \mathbb{RP}^n$ (trivial double cover). The antipodal map $S^n \to S^n$ is orientation-preserving iff $n$ is odd, which is exactly the criterion.

**Is an instance: orientable double cover of any non-orientable surface $N_g$ (connected sum of $g$ projective planes) is the orientable surface $\Sigma_{g-1}$ (genus $g-1$).** Specifically:
- $N_1 = \mathbb{RP}^2$, double cover is $S^2 = \Sigma_0$.
- $N_2 =$ Klein bottle, double cover is $T^2 = \Sigma_1$.
- $N_3$, double cover is $\Sigma_2$.
- In general, $\widetilde{N_g}^{\mathrm{or}} = \Sigma_{g-1}$.

**Is an instance: the trivial double cover of any orientable manifold.** For $M$ orientable, $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}} \cong M \sqcup M$ — two disjoint copies, with deck action swapping them.

**Is NOT an instance: the universal cover is not the same as the orientable double cover.** For $\mathbb{RP}^2$, both happen to be $S^2$, but this is a coincidence ($\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) = \mathbb{Z}/2$). For the Klein bottle, the universal cover is $\mathbb{R}^2$ (with deck group the Klein-bottle group, which is $\mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z}$); the orientable double cover is the torus $T^2$ (with deck group $\mathbb{Z}/2$). These are very different covers — universal cover has infinitely many sheets, orientable cover has 2.

**Corollary (an orientation-reversing loop in $M$ lifts to a path swapping the two fibres):** if $\gamma$ is a loop in $M$ along which orientation reverses, its lift to $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ starts at $(x_0, o_0)$ and ends at $(x_0, -o_0)$ — a path between the two distinct points of the fibre. This is *how* one shows the double cover is connected (for non-orientable $M$): just exhibit such a loop.

**Corollary (orientation classes ↔ kernel):** the orientation-preserving subgroup $H = \pi_1(\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}) \leq \pi_1(M)$ is the kernel of $w_1 : \pi_1(M) \to \mathbb{Z}/2$, the first Stiefel-Whitney class as a character on $\pi_1$. This is index 2 (when $M$ non-orientable) or all of $\pi_1$ (when $M$ orientable).

**Corollary (integration on non-orientable manifolds):** a top form $\omega$ on $M$ pulls back to a top form $\pi^*\omega$ on $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$. The orientation-swap $\sigma$ pulls back: $\sigma^*\pi^*\omega = -\pi^*\omega$ (sign flip from the orientation reversal). So integration over $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ would give zero. The cure is **pseudoforms** (twisted forms): an object on $M$ whose pullback to $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is $\sigma$-invariant rather than $\sigma$-antiinvariant. See [[Def - Pseudoform (Twisted Form)]].

**Calibration check.** If you can (a) construct the orientable double cover of the Möbius strip explicitly, (b) explain why the orientable double cover is connected iff $M$ is non-orientable, and (c) state the relation between the orientable double cover and the first Stiefel-Whitney class, you have understood the definition. Bonus: explain why the orientation-swap deck transformation is orientation-reversing on $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ (it is, even though $\widetilde M^{\mathrm{or}}$ is orientable).

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Stiefel-Whitney Classes *(from Characteristic Classes)*
> The orientation character $w_1 : \pi_1(M) \to \mathbb{Z}/2$ is the simplest **Stiefel-Whitney class** of the tangent bundle. Higher Stiefel-Whitney classes $w_i \in H^i(M; \mathbb{Z}/2)$ measure higher-order obstructions to special structures: $w_2 = 0$ is the obstruction to a **spin structure**, $w_3$ to a **spin^c structure**. The pattern — characteristic class as obstruction-cocycle, vanishing iff a structure exists — generalises to Chern classes in the complex case. See [[Algebraic Topology III — Higher Homotopy and Chern Forms]].

> [!tip] Spin Manifolds and Spinors *(from Spin Geometry)*
> The orientable double cover is the prototype: it is the *frame-bundle double cover* corresponding to the homomorphism $O(n) \to \mathbb{Z}/2$ via $\det$. The **spin double cover** is the analogue at the next level: $\mathrm{Spin}(n) \to \mathrm{SO}(n)$, the universal cover of the special orthogonal group, with deck group $\mathbb{Z}/2$. A spin structure on $M$ is a lift of the frame bundle through this $\mathrm{Spin}(n) \to \mathrm{SO}(n)$ cover. See [[Spinors and the Dirac Equation]].

> [!tip] Pseudoforms and Twisted Cohomology *(from Differential Geometry)*
> Objects on a non-orientable manifold $M$ that "should be top forms" become **pseudoforms** or **densities** — sections of the line bundle $|\Lambda^n T^*M|$ rather than $\Lambda^n T^*M$. They integrate without needing an orientation. The corresponding cohomology is twisted: instead of $H^*(M)$, one uses $H^*(M; \mathrm{or})$ with coefficients in the orientation sheaf. See [[Def - Pseudoform (Twisted Form)]] and [[Ex - Integration of a Pseudoform on the Möbius Strip]].
