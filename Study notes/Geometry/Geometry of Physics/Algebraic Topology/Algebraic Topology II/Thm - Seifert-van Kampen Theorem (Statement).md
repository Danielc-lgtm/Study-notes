# Notation

$X$ is a topological space. $U, V \subseteq X$ are open subsets with $X = U \cup V$ and $U \cap V$ non-empty. $x_0 \in U \cap V$ is the base point. $\pi_1(U, x_0), \pi_1(V, x_0), \pi_1(U \cap V, x_0)$ are the fundamental groups of the three open pieces, all based at $x_0$. The inclusions $i_U : U \cap V \hookrightarrow U$, $i_V : U \cap V \hookrightarrow V$, $j_U : U \hookrightarrow X$, $j_V : V \hookrightarrow X$ induce homomorphisms on $\pi_1$. The free product is $\ast$, the amalgamated free product is $\ast_K$. See [[Algebraic Topology II — Fundamental Group and Covering Spaces]] for the full registry.

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Seifert-van Kampen).** Let $X$ be a topological space, $U, V$ open subsets with $X = U \cup V$, and assume $U, V, U \cap V$ are non-empty and **path-connected**. Let $x_0 \in U \cap V$ be a base point.
>
> Then the fundamental group of $X$ at $x_0$ is the **amalgamated free product**:
> $$
> \pi_1(X, x_0) \;\cong\; \pi_1(U, x_0) \;\ast_{\pi_1(U \cap V, x_0)}\; \pi_1(V, x_0).
> $$
> That is, $\pi_1(X)$ is generated by loops in $U$ and loops in $V$, with relations identifying loops in $U \cap V$ when viewed as loops in $U$ and as loops in $V$.
>
> More explicitly, the natural map
> $$
> \pi_1(U) \ast \pi_1(V) \twoheadrightarrow \pi_1(X)
> $$
> (induced by $j_U, j_V$) is surjective, with kernel the normal closure of
> $$
> \{(i_U)_*[\gamma] \cdot ((i_V)_*[\gamma])^{-1} : [\gamma] \in \pi_1(U \cap V, x_0)\}.
> $$

In categorical terms: $\pi_1(X)$ is the **pushout** of the diagram $\pi_1(U) \xleftarrow{(i_U)_*} \pi_1(U \cap V) \xrightarrow{(i_V)_*} \pi_1(V)$ in the category of groups.

# Motivation

How do you compute the fundamental group of a complicated space? In practice, by decomposing it into simpler pieces. Seifert-van Kampen is the precise statement that this decomposition works on the level of $\pi_1$: if you understand $\pi_1$ of each piece and how the pieces overlap, you understand $\pi_1$ of the union.

The picture: loops in $X = U \cup V$ can be subdivided into pieces lying entirely in $U$ or entirely in $V$, by general-position arguments. Each piece is a path that begins and ends on $U \cap V$. So a loop in $X$ is essentially a *word* alternating between loops in $U$ and loops in $V$. The free product $\pi_1(U) \ast \pi_1(V)$ captures all such alternating words; the amalgamation by $\pi_1(U \cap V)$ encodes the relations forced by the overlap (a loop in $U \cap V$ is the same loop, regardless of whether we think of it as a $\pi_1(U)$- or $\pi_1(V)$-loop).

This is *the* computational tool for $\pi_1$. Almost every $\pi_1$ computation in a topology course goes through Seifert-van Kampen. The fundamental group of the figure-eight ($\pi_1 = F_2$ since each circle contributes a free generator), of a wedge of spheres, of a surface of genus $g$ (write it as a polygon with edges identified, decompose), of a **CW complex** (one cell at a time) — all are Seifert-van Kampen.

The theorem also has a *negative* content: when $U \cap V$ is not path-connected, the theorem in this form *fails*. The general (groupoid) version handles this, but for the standard textbook setting one always assumes $U \cap V$ path-connected.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The hypothesis "$X = U \cup V$ with appropriate path-connectedness" can be recognised in many decomposition scenarios.

The first source is **a CW complex built cell-by-cell**. Each $n$-cell is attached to the previous skeleton along a map $S^{n-1} \to (\text{previous skeleton})$. Applying Seifert-van Kampen with $U = $ slight thickening of the new cell, $V = $ slight thickening of the previous skeleton, and $U \cap V = $ a thin collar (homotopy equivalent to $S^{n-1}$), one gets a recursive formula for $\pi_1$ of the new skeleton in terms of $\pi_1$ of the old skeleton and the attaching map. For $n = 2$, this gives presentations; for $n \geq 3$, $\pi_1$ is unchanged.

The second source is **a wedge $X \vee Y$ of two well-pointed spaces**. Take small neighbourhoods of each space; $U \cap V$ is contractible (a small open star around the wedge point). The amalgamation collapses, and $\pi_1(X \vee Y) = \pi_1(X) \ast \pi_1(Y)$ — the free product, no relations. This is why $\pi_1$ of the figure-eight is $F_2$.

The third source is **a quotient of a polygon with edge identifications**. The standard fundamental polygons for surfaces, polyhedra, and **CW complexes** all yield Seifert-van Kampen computations: $U$ = open neighbourhood of the polygon's interior (contractible), $V$ = open neighbourhood of the edge-identification skeleton (a wedge of circles), $U \cap V$ = the annular boundary region (homotopy equivalent to a circle).

The fourth source is **a 1-cell attachment**. Adding a 1-cell to a path-connected space gives a wedge with $S^1$, so $\pi_1$ gets a new free generator. This is how Cayley graphs and Cayley complexes are built.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion gives a presentation of $\pi_1(X)$ as an amalgamated free product; combined with algebraic tools, this becomes specific answers.

The first combination is **with the algebraic theory of free products and amalgams**: once $\pi_1(X) = G_1 \ast_K G_2$ is in hand, normal-form theorems (Britton's lemma, the canonical-form theorem for amalgamated products) tell you when two words represent the same element. So the theorem produces a presentation; algebra solves the resulting word problem (at least in principle).

The second combination is **with abelianization to get $H_1$**: $H_1(X; \mathbb{Z}) = \pi_1(X)^{\mathrm{ab}}$, and abelianization commutes with pushouts in $\mathbf{Ab}$. So
$$H_1(X; \mathbb{Z}) = H_1(U) \oplus_{H_1(U \cap V)} H_1(V),$$
the cokernel of $H_1(U \cap V) \to H_1(U) \oplus H_1(V)$. This is the Mayer-Vietoris sequence in degree 1, derived from Seifert-van Kampen by abelianisation.

The third combination is **with the classification of surfaces**: every closed orientable surface $\Sigma_g$ has a standard fundamental-polygon presentation. Seifert-van Kampen gives $\pi_1(\Sigma_g) = \langle a_1, b_1, \dots, a_g, b_g \mid \prod [a_i, b_i] \rangle$. Abelianising: $H_1(\Sigma_g) = \mathbb{Z}^{2g}$ (the $g$-th Betti number is $2g$ for orientable, $g - 1$ for non-orientable).

The fourth combination is **with covering-space classification**: the Galois correspondence (see [[Thm - Galois Correspondence for Covering Spaces]]) classifies covers of $X$ by subgroups of $\pi_1(X)$. So Seifert-van Kampen + Galois correspondence = full classification of covers of $X$ from a CW decomposition of $X$.

# Why Is It True

The intuition is the **subdivision-and-decomposition** picture: every loop in $X = U \cup V$ can be subdivided into a concatenation of paths each entirely in $U$ or entirely in $V$, with subdivision points in $U \cap V$. The subdivisions can be deformed into loops based at $x_0$ (using paths in $U \cap V$ to connect subdivision points back to $x_0$). So every loop becomes a word in $\pi_1(U) \ast \pi_1(V)$, and the only relations come from loops in $U \cap V$ that can be viewed both ways.

**The bolded one-liner: a loop in $U \cup V$ is, up to homotopy, an alternating word of loops in $U$ and loops in $V$; the only relations are the loops in $U \cap V$ being identified across the two presentations.**

In more detail:

1. **Surjectivity of the natural map $\pi_1(U) \ast \pi_1(V) \to \pi_1(X)$.** Given a loop $\gamma$ in $X$, use the Lebesgue number lemma to subdivide $\gamma$ into pieces $\gamma_1, \dots, \gamma_n$ each lying entirely in $U$ or $V$, with subdivision points in $U \cap V$. For each subdivision point $\gamma(t_i) \in U \cap V$, choose a path $\sigma_i$ in $U \cap V$ from $x_0$ to $\gamma(t_i)$. Then each piece $\gamma_i$, between $\gamma(t_{i-1})$ and $\gamma(t_i)$, becomes the loop $\sigma_{i-1} \cdot \gamma_i \cdot \sigma_i^{-1}$ — entirely in $U$ or $V$, based at $x_0$. The original loop $\gamma$ is then homotopic to the concatenation of these loops, which is a word in $\pi_1(U) \ast \pi_1(V)$.

2. **Identification of the kernel.** The kernel of $\pi_1(U) \ast \pi_1(V) \to \pi_1(X)$ consists of words that, when read as loops in $X$, are null-homotopic. The non-trivial elements of the kernel are *exactly* the elements identifying $\pi_1(U \cap V)$-loops as viewed in $U$ vs. as viewed in $V$ — a loop in $U \cap V$ gives an element of $\pi_1(U)$ (via $i_U$) and an element of $\pi_1(V)$ (via $i_V$), and these must agree in $\pi_1(X)$. So the kernel is the normal closure of $\{(i_U)_*[\gamma] \cdot ((i_V)_*[\gamma])^{-1}\}$.

3. **Hardest step.** The "no other relations" claim requires the *2D version* of homotopy lifting: a null-homotopy of a loop in $X$ is a continuous map $D^2 \to X$, which (by Lebesgue subdivision in 2D) decomposes into pieces lying in $U$ or $V$, with boundary pieces in $U \cap V$. Each piece becomes a null-homotopy in $U$ or $V$, and the gluing pattern is encoded by elements of $\pi_1(U \cap V)$. The result: every homotopy in $X$ is built from homotopies in $U$ and $V$ glued by overlaps in $U \cap V$, so no extra relations beyond the overlap identifications can appear.

This is a deep fact, but the picture is geometric: subdivision in 1D for surjectivity, subdivision in 2D for the kernel determination.

---

# What Makes This Hard

The theorem has two faces. The **statement** is intuitive once one has internalised the picture (loops decompose into pieces; relations come from overlaps), but the **proof** is non-trivial: the 2D subdivision argument for the kernel description is technical, and getting the precise definition of "amalgamated free product" right requires careful tracking of the inclusion homomorphisms $(i_U)_*$ and $(i_V)_*$. The classical sources (Massey, Hatcher) develop the proof carefully; we omit it in this statement-focused note.

Two common errors. **First**, applying the theorem when $U \cap V$ is *not* path-connected — the statement above gives the wrong answer in that case, and you need the groupoid version. The classical workaround is to refine the cover until $U \cap V$ becomes path-connected (e.g., by adding more open sets or using small enough neighbourhoods). **Second**, forgetting to compute the inclusion homomorphisms $(i_U)_*, (i_V)_*$ — they are part of the amalgamation data, and the answer depends on them. For wedges, where $U \cap V$ is contractible, both inclusions are trivial and the amalgamation collapses to a free product; for general gluings, the inclusions carry essential information.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the statement and key applications.**

The proof is technical and beyond the scope of this scaffold. We instead focus on **deploying the theorem in standard applications**.

**High-level strategy:** Decompose $X = U \cup V$; identify $\pi_1(U), \pi_1(V), \pi_1(U \cap V)$; compute the inclusion homomorphisms $(i_U)_*, (i_V)_*$; assemble the amalgamated free product.

**Standard computations (sub-templates):**

1. **Wedge $X \vee Y$.** Take $U$ = thickening of $X$, $V$ = thickening of $Y$, $U \cap V$ = thickening of the wedge point (contractible). Then $\pi_1(U \cap V) = 0$, amalgamation collapses, and $\pi_1(X \vee Y) = \pi_1(X) \ast \pi_1(Y)$.
   - Example: $\pi_1(S^1 \vee S^1) = \mathbb{Z} \ast \mathbb{Z} = F_2$. The figure-eight has $\pi_1$ the free group on two generators.

2. **Polygon with edge identifications (closed orientable surface $\Sigma_g$).** $U$ = open polygon (contractible), $V$ = open neighbourhood of the identified-edges 1-skeleton (a wedge of $2g$ circles), $U \cap V$ = annular boundary region (homotopy equivalent to $S^1$).
   - $\pi_1(U) = 0$, $\pi_1(V) = F_{2g}$, $\pi_1(U \cap V) = \mathbb{Z}$, with $(i_V)_*$ sending the generator to the **boundary word** $\prod [a_i, b_i]$.
   - Result: $\pi_1(\Sigma_g) = F_{2g} / \langle\langle \prod[a_i, b_i] \rangle\rangle = \langle a_1, b_1, \dots, a_g, b_g \mid \prod [a_i, b_i] \rangle$.

3. **CW complex via attaching 2-cells.** If $X$ is the $1$-skeleton with a 2-cell attached along a loop $\alpha$, then $\pi_1(X) = \pi_1(\text{1-skeleton}) / \langle\langle [\alpha] \rangle\rangle$. (2-cells impose relations; higher-dimensional cells leave $\pi_1$ unchanged.)
   - Example: $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) = \pi_1(S^1) / \langle\langle a^2 \rangle\rangle = \mathbb{Z}/2$ (the $S^1$ boundary of the 2-cell is traversed twice in the attaching map).

4. **Sphere $S^n$ for $n \geq 2$.** $U$ = open upper hemisphere minus a tiny disc at the south pole, $V$ = open lower hemisphere minus a tiny disc at the north pole. Both are contractible. $U \cap V$ is an open annulus (homotopy equivalent to $S^{n-1}$).
   - For $n \geq 2$: $\pi_1(U \cap V) = \pi_1(S^{n-1}) = 0$, so the amalgamation collapses to $\pi_1(U) \ast \pi_1(V) = 0$. Hence $\pi_1(S^n) = 0$ for $n \geq 2$.
   - For $n = 1$: $\pi_1(U \cap V) = \pi_1(\text{two arcs}) = $ not path-connected! Theorem in this form does not directly apply, hence $\pi_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ requires a separate argument (the lift-to-$\mathbb{R}$ proof). See [[Thm - Pi_1 of S^1 is Z]].

# Lemma Decomposition

(We do not prove Seifert-van Kampen here; the proof is technical and is best read in Hatcher, *Algebraic Topology*, §1.2. We instead state the key algebraic input and provide an example calculation.)

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Universal property of the amalgamated free product
> **Statement:** Given groups $G_1, G_2$ and homomorphisms $\phi_1 : K \to G_1$, $\phi_2 : K \to G_2$ from a third group $K$, the **amalgamated free product** $G_1 \ast_K G_2$ is the group satisfying: for any group $H$ and pair of homomorphisms $\psi_1 : G_1 \to H$, $\psi_2 : G_2 \to H$ with $\psi_1 \circ \phi_1 = \psi_2 \circ \phi_2$, there exists a unique $\psi : G_1 \ast_K G_2 \to H$ with $\psi|_{G_i} = \psi_i$.
>
> **Hint:** Construct as the quotient $G_1 \ast G_2 / \langle\langle \phi_1(k) \phi_2(k)^{-1} : k \in K \rangle\rangle$.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the algebraic structure that $\pi_1(X)$ is identified with.
>
> > [!note]- Construction
> > Take the free product $G_1 \ast G_2$ (see [[Def - Free Group and Free Product]]) and quotient by the normal subgroup generated by elements of the form $\phi_1(k) \phi_2(k)^{-1}$ for $k \in K$. The universal property follows: any compatible $(\psi_1, \psi_2)$ on the factors extends uniquely to the free product, and the compatibility condition $\psi_1 \phi_1 = \psi_2 \phi_2$ ensures the generators of the quotient subgroup are killed, descending to a homomorphism on the amalgamation.

> [!note]- Example: $\pi_1$ of the figure-eight
> **Statement:** $\pi_1(S^1 \vee S^1) = F_2 = \mathbb{Z} \ast \mathbb{Z}$.
>
> **Hint:** Take two open arcs in the figure-eight, each homotopy equivalent to one of the circles; the intersection is a small neighbourhood of the wedge point, contractible.
>
> **Why needed:** Canonical example.
>
> > [!note]- Computation
> > Let $X = S^1 \vee S^1$ be the figure-eight, with wedge point $x_0$. Let $U$ = the figure-eight minus a small interior point of the second circle (this contains all of the first circle and most of the second); $V$ = the figure-eight minus a small interior point of the first circle. Each deformation-retracts to a single circle, so $\pi_1(U) = \pi_1(V) = \mathbb{Z}$. Let $U \cap V$ = the figure-eight minus interior points on both circles, which deformation-retracts to the wedge point alone, hence contractible. So $\pi_1(U \cap V) = 0$.
> >
> > By Seifert-van Kampen: $\pi_1(X) = \pi_1(U) \ast_{\pi_1(U \cap V)} \pi_1(V) = \mathbb{Z} \ast_{0} \mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z} \ast \mathbb{Z} = F_2$.

> [!note]- Example: $\pi_1$ of $\mathbb{RP}^2$
> **Statement:** $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) = \mathbb{Z}/2$.
>
> **Hint:** $\mathbb{RP}^2$ = a 2-cell attached to a circle via the "double cover" map $z \mapsto z^2$. So $\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z} / \langle\langle z^2 \rangle\rangle = \mathbb{Z}/2$.
>
> **Why needed:** Standard example, used in [[Ex - Pi_1 of RP^n is Z over 2 for n at least 2]].
>
> > [!note]- Computation
> > $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is the quotient of $D^2$ identifying antipodal points on $S^1 = \partial D^2$. Equivalently, it is the 1-skeleton $S^1$ with a single 2-cell attached via the map $f : S^1 \to S^1$, $f(z) = z^2$. Apply the 2-cell-attachment version of Seifert-van Kampen.
> >
> > Let $V$ = open neighbourhood of the attached 2-cell (the open 2-cell, which is just $D^2 \setminus \partial D^2$, contractible); $U$ = open neighbourhood of the 1-skeleton $S^1$ (homotopy equivalent to $S^1$). Then $U \cap V$ is an annulus (homotopy equivalent to $S^1$). $\pi_1(U) = \mathbb{Z} = \langle a \rangle$; $\pi_1(V) = 0$; $\pi_1(U \cap V) = \mathbb{Z}$. The inclusion $(i_V)_* : \pi_1(U \cap V) \to \pi_1(V) = 0$ is trivial. The inclusion $(i_U)_* : \pi_1(U \cap V) \to \pi_1(U)$ sends the generator of $\pi_1(U \cap V)$ to the boundary loop of the 2-cell, which is $a^2$ (the attaching map is $z \mapsto z^2$).
> >
> > Seifert-van Kampen: $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2) = \pi_1(U) \ast_{\pi_1(U \cap V)} \pi_1(V) = \mathbb{Z} \ast_\mathbb{Z} 0 = \mathbb{Z} / \langle\langle a^2 \rangle\rangle = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof (referenced)
> The proof is technical, requiring 2-dimensional Lebesgue subdivisions and careful homotopy bookkeeping. The standard reference is **Hatcher, *Algebraic Topology*, §1.2, Theorem 1.20**; see also Massey, *A Basic Course in Algebraic Topology*, Chapter 4.
>
> The key technical input beyond what we have proved is **2-dimensional homotopy subdivision**: a null-homotopy of a loop in $X = U \cup V$ (a continuous map $D^2 \to X$ extending the loop) can be subdivided so that each small piece lies in $U$ or in $V$, with boundary pieces in $U \cap V$. This is a 2-dimensional version of the Lebesgue argument used in path lifting.
>
> $\qquad\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Knot theory: Wirtinger presentation of a knot group.** For any knot $K \subset S^3$, the fundamental group $\pi_1(S^3 \setminus K)$ has a presentation derived from a knot diagram by Seifert-van Kampen: one generator $x_i$ for each arc in the diagram (the segments between under-crossings), with one relation per crossing of the form $x_k = x_i x_j x_i^{-1}$ (over-arc conjugates under-arc to over-arc). The decomposition of $S^3 \setminus K$ into "above" and "below" each crossing, plus tubular neighbourhoods, is a Seifert-van Kampen computation. This is the standard tool in knot theory.

**Combinatorial group theory: HNN extensions and group splittings.** The Seifert-van Kampen theorem for a space with a self-gluing (a quotient $X / \sim$ identifying two subspaces $A \subset X$) gives an **HNN extension** of $\pi_1(X)$ by the identification homomorphism on $\pi_1(A)$. This is the algebraic analogue of attaching a handle to a manifold. **Bass-Serre theory** then describes all groups arising as iterated free products with amalgamation and HNN extensions as fundamental groups of graphs of groups.

**Geometric group theory: surface group presentations.** Closed orientable surfaces of genus $g$ have $\pi_1 = \langle a_1, b_1, \dots, a_g, b_g \mid \prod[a_i, b_i] \rangle$ by Seifert-van Kampen on the polygon-with-identifications fundamental domain. These **surface groups** are central in geometric group theory: their geometry (Cayley graph quasi-isometry types, Gromov hyperbolicity for $g \geq 2$) is the prototype for the geometric study of groups.

**Algebraic topology: $\pi_1$ of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ and $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ via Seifert-van Kampen on $S^3$ minus poles.** Direct application: $S^3 = \mathrm{SU}(2)$ minus two points is path-connected, and $\pi_1(S^3) = 0$ follows by Seifert-van Kampen (chop into upper and lower hemispheres). Then for $\mathrm{SO}(3) = S^3 / \{\pm 1\}$ (the 2-to-1 quotient), Galois correspondence gives $\pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2$. See [[Ex - SU(2) is the Universal Cover of SO(3)]].

# Bridges

- **[[Def - Free Group and Free Product]]** — the amalgamated free product is the universal construction in [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2|group theory]] that the Seifert-van Kampen output lands in. Without the algebraic machinery of free products, the theorem could not even be stated. The free product is the topological-coproduct in $\mathbf{Grp}$, and the amalgamated version is the pushout.

- **Mayer-Vietoris sequence in singular homology** — abelianising Seifert-van Kampen gives the degree-1 piece of the Mayer-Vietoris sequence:
$$H_1(U \cap V) \to H_1(U) \oplus H_1(V) \to H_1(X) \to H_0(U \cap V) \to H_0(U) \oplus H_0(V) \to H_0(X).$$
The same decomposition principle that gives $\pi_1$ from open covers gives $H_*$ for any open cover. See [[Algebraic Topology I — Singular Homology and the de Rham Theorem]].

- **The pushout in topological spaces** — Seifert-van Kampen says $\pi_1$ preserves the pushout: the pushout of $U \leftarrow U \cap V \rightarrow V$ in $\mathbf{Top}$ is $X = U \cup V$, and $\pi_1$ sends this to a pushout in $\mathbf{Grp}$. This is the categorical content: $\pi_1$ commutes with appropriate colimits (open-cover pushouts). The general fact "homotopy functors commute with appropriate homotopy colimits" generalises this throughout algebraic topology.

- **Bass-Serre theory and graphs of groups** — generalising Seifert-van Kampen to a *graph* of open sets gives the fundamental group as the **fundamental group of a graph of groups**. The decomposition is now into vertex spaces and edge spaces, with amalgamations encoded by the graph structure. This is the topological backbone of [[Def - Free Group and Free Product|Bass-Serre theory]].

- **The CW recursion for $\pi_1$** — applying Seifert-van Kampen to the inclusion of skeleta of a CW complex: $\pi_1$ is unchanged by adding cells of dimension $\geq 3$, presented by generators from 1-cells (modulo a maximal tree) and relations from 2-cells. So for any CW complex, $\pi_1 = \langle$ free generators from non-tree 1-cells $\mid$ attaching boundaries of 2-cells $\rangle$. This is the **edge-path group** presentation, and is the practical computational engine.

- **Étale fundamental group of a scheme** — Seifert-van Kampen in algebraic geometry takes the form of a Mayer-Vietoris/decomposition principle for étale fundamental groups: $\pi_1^{\mathrm{ét}}(X) = \pi_1^{\mathrm{ét}}(U) \ast_{\pi_1^{\mathrm{ét}}(U \cap V)} \pi_1^{\mathrm{ét}}(V)$ in the profinite category, for $X$ a sufficiently nice scheme with appropriate Zariski cover.
