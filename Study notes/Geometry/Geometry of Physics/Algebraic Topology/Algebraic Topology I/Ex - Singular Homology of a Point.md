# Problem Statement

Let $M = \{*\}$ be a topological space consisting of a single point. Compute the singular homology $H_p(\{*\}; G)$ for every $p \geq 0$ and every abelian group $G$.

**Recall:**

A [[Def - Singular Simplex|singular p-simplex]] in a space $M$ is a continuous map $\sigma : \Delta^p \to M$ from the standard $p$-simplex to $M$. The singular chain group $C_p(M; G)$ is the free $G$-module on the set of all such maps.

The [[Def - The Boundary Operator|boundary operator]] $\partial : C_p(M; G) \to C_{p-1}(M; G)$ is defined on a singular simplex $\sigma$ by
$$
\partial \sigma = \sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k (\sigma \circ f_k),
$$
where $f_k : \Delta^{p-1} \to \Delta^p$ is the $k$-th face map of the standard simplex. The [[Def - Singular Homology|singular homology]] is $H_p(M; G) = \ker \partial / \mathrm{im}\, \partial$.

---

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** This is a direct-computation exercise: given a specific space (a single point), compute its homology from the definition. The class is "compute $H_*(M)$ for a fixed simple space, by working out the chain complex explicitly and taking kernels and cokernels." It is the base case of every Mayer–Vietoris induction, the building block of "homology of a point."

**Assumption pattern:** The space $M$ is a single point. The set of singular $p$-simplices in $M$ is therefore tiny — there is exactly one continuous map $\Delta^p \to \{*\}$ for each $p$, namely the constant map. So $C_p(\{*\}; G) = G \cdot \sigma_p$ is one copy of $G$, generated by the unique constant $p$-simplex $\sigma_p$. The chain complex is one-dimensional in each degree, and the boundary maps are determined by the face structure on a single simplex.

**Theorem routing:** Direct computation from [[Def - The Boundary Operator|the boundary operator definition]]. The face $\sigma_p \circ f_k$ is again a constant map (composing a constant map with anything is constant), so $\sigma_p \circ f_k = \sigma_{p-1}$ for every $k$. The boundary becomes $\partial \sigma_p = (\sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k) \sigma_{p-1}$, and the alternating sum $\sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k$ equals $1$ for even $p$ and $0$ for odd $p$. This determines all the boundary maps, and the homology follows.

**Key decision point:** The non-obvious step is noticing that the "trivial" constant simplices do *not* immediately give zero in the chain complex — they form a non-trivial chain group $G$ in each degree, and the alternating-sum boundary structure is what determines whether they contribute to homology. The pattern $\partial = 1, 0, 1, 0, \dots$ (alternating between identity and zero) is what makes the homology vanish in positive degrees: each non-zero $\partial$ has its source and target each isomorphic to $G$, and the map is an isomorphism, so the kernel is zero.

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Direct computation from the boundary formula** (analogous to operation 3 from the topic page — compute boundaries explicitly). For each $p$, compute $\partial \sigma_p$ as the alternating sum of faces. Since each face is the same constant simplex, the answer is a multiple of $\sigma_{p-1}$, with the multiplier being the alternating sum of $\pm 1$.

2. **Read $H_0$ as the count of path components** (operation 7 from the topic page). A single point has one path component, so $H_0(\{*\}; G) = G$.

3. **Identify the chain group structure** (analogous to operation 11 — use the cellular structure of the space). Here the "cellular structure" is the trivial one with a single cell in dimension zero — corresponding to the single point and its unique constant singular simplex.

# Solution

The proof breaks into three steps. Step 1 identifies the chain group $C_p(\{*\}; G) = G$ for each $p$, generated by the unique constant simplex. Step 2 computes the boundary maps as $\partial = 1$ when going from even to odd degree and $\partial = 0$ when going from odd to even. Step 3 takes kernels and quotients to find $H_0 = G$ and $H_p = 0$ for $p \geq 1$.

**Step 1: Identify the chain group $C_p(\{*\}; G) = G$.**

For every $p \geq 0$, there is exactly one continuous map $\Delta^p \to \{*\}$ — the constant map sending every point of $\Delta^p$ to the unique point of $\{*\}$. Call this map $\sigma_p : \Delta^p \to \{*\}$. So the set of singular $p$-simplices in $\{*\}$ is the single-element set $\{\sigma_p\}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The space $\{*\}$ has only one point. A map into $\{*\}$ assigns to every input the same value (the unique point), so it is the constant map. Continuity is automatic for constant maps. So $\mathrm{Maps}(\Delta^p, \{*\}) = \{\sigma_p\}$, a set with one element.
>
> The singular chain group $C_p(\{*\}; G)$ is the free $G$-module on this set: $C_p(\{*\}; G) = G \cdot \sigma_p \cong G$. Every $p$-chain is of the form $g \cdot \sigma_p$ for some $g \in G$.

**Step 2: Compute the boundary $\partial \sigma_p$ in terms of $\sigma_{p-1}$.**

The boundary $\partial \sigma_p = \sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k (\sigma_p \circ f_k)$. Each face $\sigma_p \circ f_k$ is a continuous map $\Delta^{p-1} \to \{*\}$ — again the unique constant map, namely $\sigma_{p-1}$. So $\partial \sigma_p = (\sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k) \sigma_{p-1}$, with multiplier
$$
\sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } p \text{ is even}, \\ 0 & \text{if } p \text{ is odd}. \end{cases}
$$

> [!note]- Derivation
> The face map $f_k : \Delta^{p-1} \to \Delta^p$ is a continuous map between standard simplices. Composing with the constant map $\sigma_p : \Delta^p \to \{*\}$ gives the constant map $\Delta^{p-1} \to \{*\}$, which is $\sigma_{p-1}$. (Composition of a constant map with anything is the same constant map.) So $\sigma_p \circ f_k = \sigma_{p-1}$ for every $k = 0, 1, \dots, p$.
>
> Therefore $\partial \sigma_p = \sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k \sigma_{p-1} = (\sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k) \sigma_{p-1}$.
>
> Compute the multiplier:
> - If $p = 0$: there are no faces (the empty sum), so $\partial \sigma_0 = 0$. (Or equivalently, $C_{-1} = 0$, so $\partial : C_0 \to C_{-1}$ is the zero map.)
> - If $p \geq 1$ is even ($p = 2, 4, 6, \dots$): $\sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k = 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots + 1 = 1$ (an even number of terms with alternating signs, ending with $+1$). So $\partial \sigma_p = \sigma_{p-1}$.
> - If $p \geq 1$ is odd ($p = 1, 3, 5, \dots$): $\sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k = 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots - 1 = 0$. So $\partial \sigma_p = 0$.

**Step 3: Take kernels and quotients.**

The chain complex of $\{*\}$ over $G$ is
$$
\cdots \xrightarrow{0} G \xrightarrow{1} G \xrightarrow{0} G \xrightarrow{1} G \xrightarrow{0} G \xrightarrow{1} G \xrightarrow{0} 0,
$$
reading from right to left (the degree decreases by $1$ at each arrow), with $\partial = 1$ (identity) in even degrees and $\partial = 0$ in odd degrees, ending at $C_{-1} = 0$.

Compute $H_p = \ker \partial / \mathrm{im}\, \partial$ at each spot:

- **$p = 0$:** $\partial : C_0 \to C_{-1} = 0$ is the zero map (vacuously). $\ker = C_0 = G$. $\mathrm{im}(\partial : C_1 \to C_0) = \mathrm{im}(0) = 0$. So $H_0 = G/0 = G$.
- **$p$ odd, $p \geq 1$:** $\partial : C_p \to C_{p-1}$ is the zero map. $\ker = C_p = G$. $\mathrm{im}(\partial : C_{p+1} \to C_p) = \mathrm{im}(\mathrm{id}) = C_p = G$ (since $p + 1$ is even). So $H_p = G/G = 0$.
- **$p$ even, $p \geq 2$:** $\partial : C_p \to C_{p-1}$ is the identity. $\ker = 0$. So $H_p = 0/\mathrm{anything} = 0$.

Hence
$$
H_p(\{*\}; G) = \begin{cases} G & p = 0, \\ 0 & p \geq 1. \end{cases}
$$

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Degree zero:* $\partial : C_0 \to C_{-1} = 0$ is the zero map (the target is the zero group). So $Z_0 = \ker \partial = C_0 = G$. The boundary into $C_0$ comes from $\partial : C_1 \to C_0$. By Step 2, $\partial \sigma_1 = 0$ (since $p = 1$ is odd). So $B_0 = 0$. Hence $H_0 = Z_0 / B_0 = G / 0 = G$.
>
> *Degree $p = 1$:* $\partial : C_1 \to C_0$ sends $\sigma_1 \mapsto 0$ (odd $p$). So $Z_1 = C_1 = G$. $\partial : C_2 \to C_1$ sends $\sigma_2 \mapsto \sigma_1$ (even $p = 2$). So $B_1 = \langle \sigma_1 \rangle = G$. Hence $H_1 = Z_1 / B_1 = G / G = 0$.
>
> *Degree $p = 2$:* $\partial : C_2 \to C_1$ sends $\sigma_2 \mapsto \sigma_1$ (the identity on the underlying $G$). So $Z_2 = \ker = 0$. Hence $H_2 = 0$.
>
> *Higher degrees:* the pattern repeats. For even $p \geq 2$, $\partial$ is the identity, so $Z_p = 0$ and $H_p = 0$. For odd $p \geq 1$, $Z_p = G$ but $B_p = \mathrm{im}(\partial : C_{p+1} \to C_p) = G$ (since $p+1$ is even and $\partial = \mathrm{id}$), so $H_p = G/G = 0$.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Problem.** Compute $H_p(\{*\}; G)$ for every $p \geq 0$ and every abelian group $G$.
>
> *Solution.*
>
> Let $M = \{*\}$, a one-point space. For each $p \geq 0$, there is exactly one continuous map $\Delta^p \to \{*\}$ — the constant map $\sigma_p$. Hence $C_p(\{*\}; G) = G \cdot \sigma_p \cong G$ for every $p$.
>
> Compute the boundary $\partial \sigma_p = \sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k (\sigma_p \circ f_k)$. Each face $\sigma_p \circ f_k$ is the constant map $\Delta^{p-1} \to \{*\}$, namely $\sigma_{p-1}$. So $\partial \sigma_p = (\sum_{k=0}^p (-1)^k) \sigma_{p-1}$. The alternating sum equals $1$ when $p$ is even and $0$ when $p$ is odd (for $p \geq 1$; for $p = 0$ the boundary is zero by convention).
>
> The chain complex is therefore:
> $$
> \cdots \xrightarrow{1} G \xrightarrow{0} G \xrightarrow{1} G \xrightarrow{0} G \to 0,
> $$
> with $C_{2k}$ at the even-degree slots and $C_{2k+1}$ at the odd-degree slots, all identified with $G$.
>
> Compute homology:
> - $H_0 = \ker(\partial : C_0 \to 0) / \mathrm{im}(\partial : C_1 \to C_0) = G / 0 = G$ (since $\partial : C_1 \to C_0$ is the zero map, as $1$ is odd).
> - For $p \geq 1$ odd: $H_p = \ker(\partial : C_p \to C_{p-1}) / \mathrm{im}(\partial : C_{p+1} \to C_p) = G / G = 0$ (kernel is all of $G$ since the map is zero; image is all of $G$ since the map from $C_{p+1}$ — with $p+1$ even — is the identity).
> - For $p \geq 2$ even: $H_p = \ker / \mathrm{im} = 0 / \cdot = 0$ (kernel is zero since the map is the identity).
>
> Hence $H_p(\{*\}; G) = G$ for $p = 0$ and $H_p(\{*\}; G) = 0$ for $p \geq 1$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Constant simplices are not zero in the chain complex — but their alternating-sum boundaries cancel correctly.** A common beginner intuition is that "trivial" things like constant maps should give zero in the chain complex. They do not: the constant simplices form a non-trivial chain group $G$ in each degree. What makes the homology trivial in positive degrees is not vanishing of chains but the *alternating sign cancellation*: $\sum (-1)^k = 1$ for even $p$ and $0$ for odd $p$, and this alternating pattern is exactly what makes the boundary maps alternate between the identity and zero. The resulting chain complex is acyclic except in degree zero. This is the prototype of "non-trivial chain groups with vanishing homology" — and it generalises to the contractibility of any space.

**The homology of a point is the base case of homotopy invariance.** Once we know $H_*(\{*\}) = G$ in degree zero and zero elsewhere, [[Thm - Homotopy Invariance of Singular Homology|homotopy invariance]] gives the homology of every contractible space for free: $\mathbb{R}^n$, the open ball, any star-shaped region, any cone over a space — all have $H_0 = G$ and $H_p = 0$ for $p \geq 1$. This base case is the first input to every later inductive computation; without it, the recursion for $H_*(S^n)$, $H_*(T^n)$, $H_*(\mathbb{CP}^n)$ would have nothing to start from. The computation here is therefore not just a special case — it is the foundation.

**Reduced homology eliminates the degree-zero noise.** The non-zero $H_0(\{*\}) = G$ is sometimes inconvenient: it means a point has "non-trivial homology" in some sense, which doesn't match the intuition "a point is trivial." [[Def - Augmentation and Reduced Homology|Reduced homology]] $\tilde H_0(\{*\}) = 0$ fixes this, with $\tilde H_p = 0$ in all degrees. This is one of the main motivations for introducing reduced homology: it makes the homology of a point be "zero everywhere," cleaner for stating wedge-sum and suspension formulas. The transition between the two conventions is a single one-step shift in degree zero, no deeper.

**The chain complex of a point is the universal model.** Every space has the same set of "constant simplices" — one per dimension — and they form a sub-chain complex isomorphic to the chain complex of a point. This sub-complex is canonical and computes $H_*(\{*\}; G)$. For a non-trivial space, the homology of the full chain complex is larger (it has non-constant simplices contributing in higher degrees), but the constant-simplex sub-complex is always a sub-chain-complex whose homology is the homology of a point. This is why $H_0(M; G) \supseteq G$ always (one $G$ per connected component, with the constant simplex at any point in each component contributing one $G$ to $H_0$).
