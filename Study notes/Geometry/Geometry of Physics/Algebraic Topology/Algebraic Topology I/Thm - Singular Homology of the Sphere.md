# Notation

$S^n$ is the $n$-sphere ($n \geq 0$). $G$ is an abelian coefficient group; we use $G = \mathbb{Z}$ unless stated otherwise.

The two open hemispheres of $S^n$ are
$$
U_+ = S^n \setminus \{(-e_{n+1})\}, \qquad U_- = S^n \setminus \{e_{n+1}\}
$$
(slightly thickened to be open). Each is contractible (homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n$ via stereographic projection from the missing pole). Their intersection $U_+ \cap U_-$ is the sphere minus two antipodal points, which deformation-retracts onto the equator $S^{n-1}$.

The **fundamental class** of $S^n$ (when $G = \mathbb{Z}$, $n \geq 1$) is the generator $[S^n] \in H_n(S^n; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$; it is the singular cycle obtained by triangulating $S^n$ and summing the simplices with consistent orientation signs.

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Homology of the Sphere).** For every $n \geq 1$ and every abelian group $G$,
> $$
> H_p(S^n; G) = \begin{cases} G & \text{if } p = 0 \text{ or } p = n, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}
> $$
>
> For $n = 0$ (the two-point space), $H_0(S^0; G) = G \oplus G$ and $H_p(S^0; G) = 0$ for $p \geq 1$.

> **Corollary (Betti numbers and Euler characteristic of $S^n$).** For $n \geq 1$, $b_0(S^n) = 1$, $b_n(S^n) = 1$, all others zero. The Euler characteristic is $\chi(S^n) = 1 + (-1)^n = 2$ for $n$ even, $0$ for $n$ odd.

> **Corollary (Fundamental class).** For $n \geq 1$, $H_n(S^n; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$ is generated by the **fundamental class** $[S^n]$ — the orientation cycle of $S^n$. The generator is unique up to sign (the two orientations of $S^n$).

The proof is by induction on $n$ using Mayer–Vietoris on the cover by two contractible hemispheres.

---

# Motivation

The sphere is the universal "$n$-dimensional hole" — the simplest closed $n$-manifold, the boundary of the $(n+1)$-ball, the simplest non-contractible space in dimension $n$. Its homology should reflect this universality: it should have one non-trivial class in degree $n$ (the orientation cycle) and nothing else above degree zero.

This computation is the **base case** for nearly every explicit homology calculation in algebraic topology. The homology of more complicated spaces is built up from $S^n$ via Mayer–Vietoris, products (Künneth formula), and CW cell-attachment. So knowing $H_*(S^n)$ explicitly is the foundation of the field.

Specifically: $H_*(T^n) = H_*(S^1)^{\otimes n}$ by Künneth applied to the product $T^n = (S^1)^n$; $H_*(\mathbb{CP}^n)$ by iterated Mayer–Vietoris using the cover by an affine chart and a hyperplane (homotopy equivalent to $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$); $H_*(\mathbb{RP}^n)$ similarly. Every explicit homology calculation starts from $H_*(S^k)$ for various $k$ and assembles via the standard tools.

The Mayer–Vietoris proof is itself instructive: it shows the power of "cover by two contractibles whose intersection has known homology." Both $U_\pm$ are contractible (hence have trivial reduced homology); $U_+ \cap U_-$ deformation-retracts onto $S^{n-1}$ (homology one dimension lower). The connecting map $\delta : H_p(S^n) \to H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$ then provides the inductive step: $H_p(S^n) \cong H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$ for $p \geq 2$ (since the flanking terms in the Mayer–Vietoris sequence vanish). Starting from $H_*(S^0)$ as the base case, this recursion completely determines $H_*(S^n)$.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition $A$ is: *we have the singular homology functor and Mayer–Vietoris.*

The first disguised source is **any space homotopy equivalent to $S^n$.** Property $B$: $X \simeq S^n$. By homotopy invariance, $H_*(X) = H_*(S^n)$. *Example application:* $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\} \simeq S^{n-1}$ (radial deformation retract), so $H_*(\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}) = H_*(S^{n-1})$. The complement of a point in any contractible space has the homology of a sphere.

The second disguised source is **a manifold whose CW or simplicial structure has only two cells (one in dimension zero, one in dimension $n$).** Property $B$: a CW complex with cell-structure $\{e^0\} \cup \{e^n\}$. The cellular chain complex is $\mathbb{Z}$ in degrees $0$ and $n$, zero elsewhere, with all boundary maps zero. The cellular homology is $\mathbb{Z}$ in degrees $0$ and $n$, matching the singular homology of $S^n$. *Example application:* any space with such a minimal CW structure has the homology of $S^n$.

The third disguised source is **the suspension of $S^{n-1}$.** Property $B$: $X = \Sigma S^{n-1}$, the reduced suspension. The bridge: $\Sigma S^{n-1} \cong S^n$ by direct geometric construction (two cones over $S^{n-1}$ glued along $S^{n-1}$). By the **suspension isomorphism** in reduced homology, $\tilde H_p(\Sigma S^{n-1}) = \tilde H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$, recovering $\tilde H_p(S^n) = \tilde H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$ — the same recursion as Mayer–Vietoris but obtained via suspension.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion $C$: *$H_p(S^n; G) = G$ for $p = 0, n$; zero otherwise.*

Combine $C$ with **the de Rham theorem.** The de Rham cohomology of $S^n$ is also $\mathbb{R}$ in degrees $0$ and $n$, zero otherwise — by the de Rham theorem, $H^p_{dR}(S^n) = H^p(S^n; \mathbb{R})$, which has the same dimension as $H_p(S^n; \mathbb{R})$ over a field. The further result $E$: the de Rham cohomology of $S^n$ is computable by smooth-form methods (the volume form generates $H^n_{dR}$), and the dimension matches the topological count.

Combine $C$ with **Poincaré duality.** For a closed oriented $n$-manifold, $H^k = H_{n-k}$. For $S^n$: $H^0(S^n) = H_n(S^n) = G$ and $H^n(S^n) = H_0(S^n) = G$, matching the Poincaré-duality symmetry $b_k = b_{n-k}$. The further result $E$: the Poincaré-duality isomorphism is realised by cap product with the fundamental class $[S^n]$.

Combine $C$ with **the Hopf invariant**. For a map $f : S^{2n-1} \to S^n$, the **Hopf invariant** $H(f) \in \mathbb{Z}$ is defined via the cup-product structure on the mapping cone, using the generators of $H^n(S^n)$ and $H^{2n-1}(S^{2n-1})$. Adams proved $H(f) = 1$ is achievable only for $n = 1, 2, 4$ (with the Hopf maps for $S^1, S^3, S^7$). The further result $E$: the existence of normed division algebras ($\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}, \mathbb{H}, \mathbb{O}$) is forced by the cohomology of spheres.

# Why Is It True

**The single sentence: covering $S^n$ by two open contractible hemispheres whose intersection is homotopy-equivalent to $S^{n-1}$, the Mayer–Vietoris sequence shifts homology up by one dimension at each induction step, starting from $S^0$ (two points) and propagating to all higher dimensions.**

The intuition is dimensional. A sphere $S^n$ has a single "hole" of dimension $n$ (the interior, which is *not* part of the sphere — the sphere is the boundary of the ball). To detect this hole homologically, we need a cycle of dimension $n$ that wraps around it, and the fundamental class $[S^n]$ is precisely such a cycle: the triangulated $n$-sphere, summed with consistent orientations, is a cycle that does not bound (because the only $(n+1)$-chain it could bound is the interior ball, which is not a chain in $S^n$).

For other dimensions $0 < p < n$, the sphere should have no homology: a $p$-dimensional cycle in $S^n$ can always be "filled" because the sphere has no $p$-dimensional "holes" for $p < n$. This is the geometric content of $H_p(S^n) = 0$ for $0 < p < n$.

The Mayer–Vietoris proof captures this dimensional shift formally. The two hemispheres $U_+$ and $U_-$ are contractible, so their homology is trivial in positive degrees and $G$ in degree zero. Their intersection $U_+ \cap U_-$ has the homology of $S^{n-1}$ (the equator). The Mayer–Vietoris sequence then reads, in degrees $p \geq 2$:
$$
\underbrace{H_p(U_+) \oplus H_p(U_-)}_{= 0} \to H_p(S^n) \xrightarrow{\delta} H_{p-1}(U_+ \cap U_-) \to \underbrace{H_{p-1}(U_+) \oplus H_{p-1}(U_-)}_{= 0}
$$
which gives $H_p(S^n) \cong H_{p-1}(U_+ \cap U_-) = H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$.

So $H_p(S^n) = H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$ for $p \geq 2$. Starting with $H_*(S^0)$ (the two-point space, with $H_0 = \mathbb{Z}^2$ and zero elsewhere), this recursion gives:
- $H_p(S^1) = H_{p-1}(S^0)$ for $p \geq 2$, so $H_p(S^1) = 0$ for $p \geq 2$. Direct computation of $H_1(S^1)$ via the same sequence gives $H_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$. And $H_0(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ (connected).
- $H_p(S^2) = H_{p-1}(S^1)$ for $p \geq 2$, so $H_2(S^2) = H_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$, $H_p(S^2) = 0$ for $p \geq 3$. $H_1(S^2) = 0$ and $H_0(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$.
- Continuing the induction, $H_n(S^n) = \mathbb{Z}$ and $H_p(S^n) = 0$ for $0 < p < n$ and $p > n$.

The fundamental class $[S^n]$ — the generator of $H_n(S^n; \mathbb{Z})$ — is the orientation cycle of $S^n$. With a triangulation of $S^n$, one assigns $\pm 1$ to each top-dimensional simplex so that adjacent simplices induce opposite orientations on their common face; the resulting chain is closed, hence a cycle, and represents the generator. The existence of a consistent orientation is the orientability of $S^n$.

# What Makes This Hard

The conceptual obstacle is **the base case computation** $H_*(S^0) = G \oplus G$. Many readers expect $H_0$ to be $G$ regardless, but for $S^0$ (the two-point space) it is $G^2$ because there are two path components. This must be handled separately from the inductive case $n \geq 1$.

The technical step that requires care is the **Mayer–Vietoris induction starting point** for $H_1(S^1)$. The naive recursion $H_1(S^1) = H_0(S^0)$ gives $\mathbb{Z}^2$, but the correct answer is $\mathbb{Z}$. The issue is that for $p = 1$, the Mayer–Vietoris sequence has non-trivial flanking $H_0$ terms (since $H_0(U_+) = H_0(U_-) = G$ and $H_0(U_+ \cap U_-) = G^2$), and these terms must be tracked through the exact sequence: $H_1(S^1)$ fits in $0 \to H_1(S^1) \to H_0(S^0) \to H_0(U_+) \oplus H_0(U_-) \to H_0(S^1) \to 0$, which gives $H_1(S^1) = \mathbb{Z}^2 / \mathrm{im}((i_{U_*}, i_{V_*})) = \mathbb{Z}$ once one tracks the kernel/image correctly. Reduced homology cleans this up: the reduced Mayer–Vietoris sequence works without the $H_0$ correction.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** Cover $S^n$ by two open hemispheres $U_+, U_-$, each contractible. Their intersection $U_+ \cap U_-$ deformation-retracts onto the equator $S^{n-1}$. Apply Mayer–Vietoris and use contractibility of $U_\pm$ to shift the homology by one dimension. Inductively start from $H_*(S^0)$.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Base case $H_*(S^0)$.** $S^0 = \{N, S\}$ is two points. $H_0(S^0; G) = G \oplus G$, $H_p(S^0; G) = 0$ for $p \geq 1$.
   - *Hint:* The chain complex has $C_0 = G$ at $N$ plus $G$ at $S$, all other $C_p$ generated by constant maps with alternating-sum boundaries; the homology in positive degrees is zero, and in degree zero it is $G^2$.
   - *Why needed:* Base case of the induction.

2. **Construct the cover of $S^n$.** Let $U_+ = S^n \setminus \{-e_{n+1}\}$ (sphere minus south pole) and $U_- = S^n \setminus \{e_{n+1}\}$ (sphere minus north pole). Each is open and contractible (homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n$ via stereographic projection from the missing pole). $U_+ \cup U_- = S^n$. $U_+ \cap U_- \simeq S^{n-1}$ via radial deformation retraction onto the equator.
   - *Hint:* The stereographic projection from a pole sends $S^n$ minus that pole homeomorphically to $\mathbb{R}^n$.
   - *Why needed:* Sets up the Mayer–Vietoris cover.

3. **Apply Mayer–Vietoris and use contractibility.** The Mayer–Vietoris sequence is
   $$
   \cdots \to H_p(U_+ \cap U_-) \to H_p(U_+) \oplus H_p(U_-) \to H_p(S^n) \to H_{p-1}(U_+ \cap U_-) \to \cdots
   $$
   Since $U_\pm$ are contractible, $H_p(U_\pm) = 0$ for $p \geq 1$ and $H_0(U_\pm) = G$. Substituting and using $H_*(U_+ \cap U_-) = H_*(S^{n-1})$ gives the inductive recursion.
   - *Hint:* Track the sequence carefully, separating the special case $p = 0$ from $p \geq 1$.
   - *Why needed:* This is the inductive step.

4. **Extract the recursion $H_p(S^n) = H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$ for $p \geq 2$.** In degree $p \geq 2$, the flanking terms in Mayer–Vietoris vanish, leaving $H_p(S^n) \cong H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$.
   - *Hint:* Both $H_p(U_+) \oplus H_p(U_-) = 0$ (positive degree, contractible) and $H_{p-1}(U_+) \oplus H_{p-1}(U_-) = 0$ (positive degree, contractible).
   - *Why needed:* This is the clean recursion that propagates the homology up dimensions.

5. **Handle the special case $p = 1$, $n = 1$.** The sequence becomes $0 \to H_1(S^1) \to H_0(S^0) \to H_0(U_+) \oplus H_0(U_-) \to H_0(S^1) \to 0$, i.e. $0 \to H_1(S^1) \to G^2 \to G^2 \to G \to 0$. The map $G^2 \to G^2$ has rank $1$ (image is the diagonal), so $H_1(S^1) = G$.
   - *Hint:* Compute the map $(i_{U_*}, i_{V_*})$ on $H_0(S^0)$ — it sends a generator at each pole to the same generator in $H_0(U_+) = H_0(U_-) = G$. Hence the image is the diagonal of $G \oplus G$.
   - *Why needed:* The base case for the inductive recursion (the first time the recursion gives a non-trivial answer in degree $1$).

6. **Iterate to get $H_n(S^n) = G$.** Starting from $H_1(S^1) = G$, apply the recursion $H_n(S^n) = H_{n-1}(S^{n-1})$ to conclude $H_n(S^n) = G$ for every $n \geq 1$.
   - *Hint:* Induction.
   - *Why needed:* Completes the proof.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: The Hemispheres are Contractible
> **Statement:** $U_+ = S^n \setminus \{-e_{n+1}\}$ and $U_- = S^n \setminus \{e_{n+1}\}$ are both contractible.
>
> **Hint:** Stereographic projection from the missing pole.
>
> **Why needed:** Eliminates $H_*(U_\pm)$ from the Mayer–Vietoris sequence in positive degrees.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > The **stereographic projection** $\varphi_+ : U_- = S^n \setminus \{e_{n+1}\} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ sends a point $x \in S^n$ (other than the north pole $e_{n+1}$) to the unique point $\varphi_+(x) \in \mathbb{R}^n \times \{0\} \subset \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ where the line through $e_{n+1}$ and $x$ intersects the hyperplane $z = 0$. Explicitly, $\varphi_+(x_1, \dots, x_n, z) = (x_1, \dots, x_n)/(1 - z)$. This is a homeomorphism. Similarly $\varphi_- : U_+ = S^n \setminus \{-e_{n+1}\} \to \mathbb{R}^n$.
> >
> > Since $\mathbb{R}^n$ is contractible (the straight-line homotopy $H((y, t)) = (1-t)y$ from $\mathrm{id}$ to the constant map to $0$ is continuous), so are $U_\pm$. Hence $H_*(U_\pm; G) = G$ in degree zero, zero in positive degrees.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: $U_+ \cap U_-$ Deformation-Retracts onto the Equator
> **Statement:** $U_+ \cap U_- = S^n \setminus \{\pm e_{n+1}\}$ (sphere minus two antipodal poles) deformation-retracts onto the equator $\{x \in S^n : x_{n+1} = 0\} \cong S^{n-1}$.
>
> **Hint:** Push every point along its meridian back to the equator.
>
> **Why needed:** Identifies the intersection's homology as $H_*(S^{n-1})$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Define $H : (S^n \setminus \{\pm e_{n+1}\}) \times [0, 1] \to S^n \setminus \{\pm e_{n+1}\}$ by $H(x, t)$ = the point obtained by sliding $x$ along its meridian (the geodesic from $-e_{n+1}$ to $e_{n+1}$ passing through $x$) toward the equator, with parameter $t$. Explicitly, in coordinates $(x_1, \dots, x_n, z)$, set
> > $$
> > H((x_1, \dots, x_n, z), t) = \frac{(x_1, \dots, x_n, (1-t)z)}{\|\text{(numerator)}\|}.
> > $$
> > At $t = 0$ this is the identity; at $t = 1$ this maps to $(x_1/\|x\|, \dots, x_n/\|x\|, 0)$, a point on the equator (with $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ the horizontal coordinates, $\|x\| > 0$ since we removed the poles). This is continuous and gives a strong deformation retract onto the equator, which is homeomorphic to $S^{n-1}$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Mayer–Vietoris Gives the Recursion
> **Statement:** For $n \geq 1$ and $p \geq 2$, $H_p(S^n; G) \cong H_{p-1}(S^{n-1}; G)$.
>
> **Hint:** Apply Mayer–Vietoris with the cover $\{U_+, U_-\}$ and use Lemmas 1 and 2 to identify the terms.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the inductive recursion that propagates the answer to all dimensions.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > The Mayer–Vietoris sequence reads
> > $$
> > \cdots \to H_p(U_+ \cap U_-) \to H_p(U_+) \oplus H_p(U_-) \to H_p(S^n) \xrightarrow{\delta} H_{p-1}(U_+ \cap U_-) \to H_{p-1}(U_+) \oplus H_{p-1}(U_-) \to \cdots
> > $$
> > By Lemma 1, $H_p(U_\pm) = 0$ for $p \geq 1$. Hence for $p \geq 2$, the term $H_p(U_+) \oplus H_p(U_-) = 0$ and the term $H_{p-1}(U_+) \oplus H_{p-1}(U_-) = 0$ (since $p - 1 \geq 1$). The sequence simplifies to
> > $$
> > 0 \to H_p(S^n) \xrightarrow{\delta} H_{p-1}(U_+ \cap U_-) \to 0,
> > $$
> > showing $\delta$ is an isomorphism. By Lemma 2, $H_{p-1}(U_+ \cap U_-) = H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$. Hence $H_p(S^n) \cong H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$ for $p \geq 2$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> **Theorem.** For $n \geq 1$, $H_p(S^n; G) = G$ if $p = 0$ or $p = n$, and $H_p(S^n; G) = 0$ otherwise. For $n = 0$, $H_0(S^0; G) = G \oplus G$ and $H_p(S^0; G) = 0$ for $p \geq 1$.
>
> *Proof.* By induction on $n$.
>
> **Base case ($n = 0$).** $S^0 = \{N, S\}$ has two path components, so $H_0(S^0; G) = G \oplus G$ by the connected-components rule. For $p \geq 1$, the singular chain complex on each component is the chain complex of a point, which has trivial homology in positive degrees; so $H_p(S^0; G) = 0$ for $p \geq 1$.
>
> **Base case ($n = 1$).** $H_0(S^1; G) = G$ (connected). For $p \geq 2$, by Lemma 3, $H_p(S^1; G) \cong H_{p-1}(S^0; G)$, which is zero for $p - 1 \geq 1$, hence zero for $p \geq 2$. For $p = 1$, the Mayer–Vietoris sequence reads
> $$
> 0 \to H_1(U_+ \cap U_-) \to H_1(U_+) \oplus H_1(U_-) \to H_1(S^1) \to H_0(U_+ \cap U_-) \to H_0(U_+) \oplus H_0(U_-) \to H_0(S^1) \to 0.
> $$
> Substituting: $H_1(U_\pm) = 0$, $H_1(U_+ \cap U_-) = H_1(S^0) = 0$ (positive degree of a discrete space), $H_0(U_+ \cap U_-) = H_0(S^0) = G \oplus G$, $H_0(U_\pm) = G$, $H_0(S^1) = G$. The sequence becomes
> $$
> 0 \to 0 \to 0 \to H_1(S^1) \to G \oplus G \xrightarrow{\alpha} G \oplus G \xrightarrow{\beta} G \to 0,
> $$
> where $\alpha$ is induced by $(i_{U_+}, i_{U_-})$ and $\beta$ by $j_{U_+} - j_{U_-}$. By the connectedness of $U_\pm$, the maps $H_0(U_+ \cap U_-) \to H_0(U_\pm)$ send each connected component of $U_+ \cap U_-$ to the unique connected component of $U_\pm$ — so $\alpha(g_N, g_S) = (g_N + g_S, g_N + g_S)$ (both points become the same component of $U_\pm$, contributing their sum). The image of $\alpha$ is the diagonal $\{(g, g) : g \in G\} \cong G$, and $\ker \alpha = \{(g_N, g_S) : g_N + g_S = 0\} \cong G$ (parametrised by $g_N$).
> 
> By exactness at $G \oplus G$ (the first one), $H_1(S^1) \cong \ker \alpha \cong G$.
>
> **Inductive step.** Assume the theorem for $S^{n-1}$. By Lemma 3, $H_p(S^n; G) \cong H_{p-1}(S^{n-1}; G)$ for $p \geq 2$. By induction, $H_{p-1}(S^{n-1}) = G$ if $p - 1 = n - 1$ (i.e. $p = n$) and zero otherwise (for $p - 1 \geq 1$, i.e. $p \geq 2$). So $H_n(S^n) = G$ and $H_p(S^n) = 0$ for $2 \leq p \neq n$.
> 
> For $p = 1$: by the same Mayer–Vietoris sequence as in the $n = 1$ base case, but with $U_+ \cap U_- \simeq S^{n-1}$ (now with $n \geq 2$), we have $H_1(S^{n-1}) = 0$ (by induction, since $n - 1 \geq 1 \neq 1$ only when $n \geq 3$... need care). Direct from the sequence: for $n \geq 2$, $H_1(S^{n-1}) = 0$ (assuming the inductive hypothesis), $H_0(S^{n-1}) = G$, and the sequence becomes $0 \to 0 \to 0 \to H_1(S^n) \to G \to G \oplus G \to G \to 0$, where the map $G \to G \oplus G$ is the diagonal. The diagonal is injective, so $H_1(S^n) = \ker(\text{diagonal}) = 0$.
> 
> For $p = 0$: $H_0(S^n) = G$ since $S^n$ is path-connected for $n \geq 1$.
>
> This completes the induction. $\qquad\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Homology of the punctured Euclidean space.** $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\} \simeq S^{n-1}$ by radial deformation retraction, so $H_*(\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{0\}) = H_*(S^{n-1})$. This is the standard "remove a point and get a sphere" homotopy that converts an unbounded computation into a known sphere computation.

**De Rham version on $S^n$.** $H^p_{dR}(S^n) = \mathbb{R}$ for $p = 0, n$ and zero otherwise — by the de Rham theorem applied to the singular homology answer. The generator of $H^n_{dR}(S^n)$ is the volume form $\omega_S$ (the standard rotation-invariant top-degree form on the sphere), and $\int_{S^n} \omega_S$ equals the volume of the sphere — a non-zero number, confirming that $\omega_S$ is non-exact.

**Mapping spheres and degree.** A continuous map $f : S^n \to S^n$ induces $f_* : H_n(S^n; \mathbb{Z}) \to H_n(S^n; \mathbb{Z})$, multiplication by an integer $\deg f$ — the **degree** of $f$. Two maps are homotopic iff they have the same degree. So $H_n(S^n) = \mathbb{Z}$ is the home of the topological degree, and the classification of self-maps of the sphere up to homotopy is a single integer.

**No retraction from $D^n$ to $S^{n-1}$.** A retraction $r : D^n \to S^{n-1}$ with $r|_{S^{n-1}} = \mathrm{id}$ would give $r_* \circ i_* = \mathrm{id}$ on $H_{n-1}(S^{n-1}) = \mathbb{Z}$. But $H_{n-1}(D^n) = 0$ (the disk is contractible), so $i_* = 0$, forcing $r_* \circ i_* = 0 \neq \mathrm{id}$. Contradiction. This is the standard one-line proof of Brouwer's fixed point theorem, relying on $H_{n-1}(S^{n-1}) = \mathbb{Z}$.

**Suspension and reduced homology.** The (reduced) suspension $\Sigma X$ satisfies $\tilde H_p(\Sigma X) = \tilde H_{p-1}(X)$. Since $\Sigma S^{n-1} \cong S^n$, this gives $\tilde H_p(S^n) = \tilde H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$, recovering our recursion via the suspension functor — another route to the same answer.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Mayer-Vietoris for Singular Homology|Mayer–Vietoris]]** — the engine of the proof. The cover by two contractible hemispheres is the simplest non-trivial Mayer–Vietoris setup, and it produces the recursion $H_p(S^n) = H_{p-1}(S^{n-1})$ for $p \geq 2$. Every iterated-sphere computation runs through this same Mayer–Vietoris argument.

- **[[Thm - Homotopy Invariance of Singular Homology|Homotopy invariance]]** — used to identify $U_+ \cap U_- \simeq S^{n-1}$ (the intersection deformation-retracts onto the equator). Without homotopy invariance, the homology of $U_+ \cap U_-$ would need to be computed directly, which is awkward.

- **The de Rham theorem** — over $\mathbb{R}$, $H^p_{dR}(S^n) = H^p(S^n; \mathbb{R})$, so the smooth-form computation (via the volume form on $S^n$) and the singular-homology computation must give the same answer. This is the simplest non-trivial consistency check on the de Rham theorem.

- **Suspension and stable homotopy** — the relation $\tilde H_p(\Sigma X) = \tilde H_{p-1}(X)$ converts $H_*(S^n)$ into the simplest "stable" invariant: a single $\mathbb{Z}$ in the top dimension, surviving all suspensions. The suspension spectrum of $S^0$ generates the **sphere spectrum**, the foundational object of stable homotopy theory.

- **Hopf fibration** — the Hopf map $S^3 \to S^2$ has degree zero on every $H_p$ for $p < 2$, but in higher dimensions ($p = 2$) and via the cohomology ring structure, the Hopf map is non-trivial in $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$. The "richness" of homotopy versus homology is starkest in the sphere case — see [[Algebraic Topology III — Higher Homotopy and Chern Forms]].

> [!tip] Brouwer Fixed Point Theorem *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> Every continuous self-map $f : D^n \to D^n$ has a fixed point. Proof: if not, $f$ gives a retraction $r : D^n \to S^{n-1}$ which contradicts $H_{n-1}(D^n) = 0 \neq H_{n-1}(S^{n-1}) = \mathbb{Z}$ — uses precisely the homology of the sphere we have computed.

> [!tip] Topological Degree of Maps $S^n \to S^n$ *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> The action of $f_* : H_n(S^n; \mathbb{Z}) \to H_n(S^n; \mathbb{Z})$ is multiplication by an integer $\deg f$, classifying continuous self-maps of the sphere up to homotopy. Many topological theorems (the fundamental theorem of algebra, the Lefschetz fixed-point formula, winding-number identities) are statements about degrees of maps between spheres.

> [!tip] **Cohomology Ring of the Sphere** *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> $H^*(S^n; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2)$ where $x \in H^n(S^n; \mathbb{Z})$ is the generator dual to the fundamental class. The cup product is trivial above degree $n$ (since $x^2 \in H^{2n} = 0$). This makes spheres "homologically minimal" in the sense that they have the simplest possible cup product structure.

> [!tip] **Suspension Spectrum and Stable Homotopy** *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> The infinite sequence $S^0, S^1, S^2, \dots$ with suspension maps forms the **sphere spectrum** $\mathbb{S}$, the generator of the stable homotopy category. Stable homotopy groups $\pi_k^s$ of the sphere are the "ultimate" invariants of stable homotopy theory, and computing them (in low dimensions) is one of the central problems of algebraic topology.

> [!tip] **Hopf Invariant** *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> For a map $f : S^{2n-1} \to S^n$, the **Hopf invariant** $H(f) \in \mathbb{Z}$ uses the cup-product structure on the mapping cone $C_f$. Adams's theorem says $H(f) = 1$ is achievable only for $n = 1, 2, 4$ (with the maps $S^1, S^3, S^7$ → respective spheres), corresponding to the four real normed division algebras $\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}, \mathbb{H}, \mathbb{O}$.
