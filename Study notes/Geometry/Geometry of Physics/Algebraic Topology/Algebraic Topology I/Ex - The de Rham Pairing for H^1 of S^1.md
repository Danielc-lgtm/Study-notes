# Problem Statement

Let $M = S^1 \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ be the unit circle. By [[Thm - Singular Homology of the Sphere]] (or direct Mayer–Vietoris), $H_1(S^1; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$, generated by the loop $\gamma : [0, 1] \to S^1$, $\gamma(t) = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$. By [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius|de Rham cohomology]], $H^1_{dR}(S^1) = \mathbb{R}$, generated by the cohomology class $[d\theta]$ of the angular form $d\theta = (-y\, dx + x\, dy)/(x^2 + y^2)$ restricted to $S^1$ (where $\theta$ is the angle coordinate).

Compute the de Rham pairing $\int_\gamma d\theta$ explicitly. Verify that the result is $2\pi$, and conclude that $[d\theta]/(2\pi)$ is the unique cohomology class in $H^1(S^1; \mathbb{R})$ that takes the value $1$ on the homology generator $[\gamma]$ — i.e., $[d\theta]/(2\pi)$ is the dual basis element to $[\gamma]$.

**Recall:**

The circle $S^1 = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + y^2 = 1\}$. The angular form $d\theta$ is the closed (but not exact) $1$-form $(-y\, dx + x\, dy)/(x^2 + y^2)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$; restricted to $S^1$, it is the standard form satisfying $\int_{S^1} d\theta = 2\pi$.

The loop $\gamma : [0, 1] \to S^1$ goes once around the circle counterclockwise. As a singular $1$-simplex it is the parameterised curve; as an element of $H_1(S^1; \mathbb{Z})$ it generates the homology.

By the [[Thm - The de Rham Theorem (Full Proof)|de Rham theorem]], $H^1_{dR}(S^1) \cong H^1(S^1; \mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{Hom}(H_1(S^1; \mathbb{R}), \mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$. The isomorphism sends $[\omega] \in H^1_{dR}(S^1)$ to the functional $[c] \mapsto \int_c \omega$ in $H^1(S^1; \mathbb{R})$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** This is a direct-computation exercise: evaluate the de Rham pairing on a specific cohomology-homology pair. The class is "compute $\int_c \omega$ for given $c$ and $\omega$, and interpret the result as a dual-basis correspondence." It is the minimal non-trivial instance of the de Rham theorem, and the prototype for every later period computation.

**Assumption pattern:** $S^1$ is the simplest space with non-trivial first cohomology ($\mathbb{R}$). Its first homology is generated by the loop $\gamma$ — a single explicit map. Its first de Rham cohomology is generated by $[d\theta]$ — a single explicit form. The pairing $\int_\gamma d\theta$ is a one-line computation reducing to a definite integral over $[0, 1]$.

**Theorem routing:** Compute $\int_\gamma d\theta = \int_{[0, 1]} \gamma^* d\theta$ by pulling back the form via $\gamma$. The pullback $\gamma^* d\theta = d(\theta \circ \gamma) = d(2\pi t)$ on $[0, 1]$, giving $\int_{[0, 1]} 2\pi\, dt = 2\pi$. By [[Thm - The de Rham Theorem (Full Proof)|de Rham]], this pairing tells us that the cohomology class $[d\theta] \in H^1_{dR}(S^1)$ corresponds to the singular cohomology class that evaluates to $2\pi$ on the homology generator. Dividing by $2\pi$ gives the dual basis: $[d\theta]/(2\pi)$ evaluates to $1$ on $[\gamma]$.

**Key decision point:** The non-obvious step is recognising that $\gamma^* d\theta$ is well-defined and computable even though $\theta$ is not a globally defined function on $S^1$. The form $d\theta$ is globally defined as a $1$-form (even though $\theta$ is multi-valued on $S^1$), and the pullback $\gamma^* d\theta$ on $[0, 1]$ is well-defined because $[0, 1]$ is contractible and $\theta \circ \gamma : [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ is a well-defined lift (single-valued) of the multi-valued $\theta$.

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Detect a non-trivial cohomology class by integrating against a homology generator** (operation 3 from the topic page). $\int_\gamma d\theta = 2\pi \neq 0$ certifies $[d\theta] \neq 0$ in $H^1_{dR}(S^1)$, and equivalently $[\gamma] \neq 0$ in $H_1(S^1; \mathbb{R})$.

2. **Use the de Rham theorem to identify dual basis elements** (analogous to operation 11). $H^1_{dR}(S^1) \cong H^1(S^1; \mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{Hom}(H_1, \mathbb{R})$ via the integration pairing. The pairing identifies the dual basis element of $[\gamma]$ as the cohomology class of $d\theta/(2\pi)$.

3. **Pullback under a smooth map** (operation 1). $\gamma^* d\theta$ on $[0, 1]$ is a smooth form computable from the local formula for $d\theta$ and the parameterisation of $\gamma$.

# Solution

The computation has three steps. Step 1 sets up the integral $\int_\gamma d\theta$ as a definite integral over $[0, 1]$. Step 2 computes the pullback $\gamma^* d\theta$. Step 3 evaluates the integral and interprets the result.

**Step 1: Set up the integral.**

By the definition of the integral of a form over a singular simplex,
$$
\int_\gamma d\theta \;=\; \int_{[0, 1]} \gamma^* d\theta,
$$
where $\gamma^* d\theta$ is the pullback of the form $d\theta$ via the smooth map $\gamma : [0, 1] \to S^1 \subset \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The integral of a $1$-form over a singular $1$-simplex $\sigma : \Delta^1 \to M$ is defined as $\int_\sigma \omega = \int_{\Delta^1} \sigma^* \omega$. For $\sigma = \gamma : [0, 1] \cong \Delta^1 \to S^1$, this gives $\int_\gamma d\theta = \int_{[0, 1]} \gamma^* d\theta$. The pullback $\gamma^* d\theta$ is a smooth $1$-form on $[0, 1]$, which can be integrated as an ordinary definite integral.

**Step 2: Compute $\gamma^* d\theta$.**

The form on $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ is
$$
d\theta = \frac{-y\, dx + x\, dy}{x^2 + y^2}.
$$
On $S^1$, $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, so $d\theta = -y\, dx + x\, dy$ when restricted (the denominator is $1$).

Compute $\gamma^* dx$ and $\gamma^* dy$ at the point $\gamma(t) = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$:
$$
\gamma^* dx = d(\cos 2\pi t) = -2\pi \sin 2\pi t\, dt,
$$
$$
\gamma^* dy = d(\sin 2\pi t) = 2\pi \cos 2\pi t\, dt.
$$

Substituting $x = \cos 2\pi t$, $y = \sin 2\pi t$:
$$
\gamma^* d\theta = -\sin 2\pi t \cdot (-2\pi \sin 2\pi t)\, dt + \cos 2\pi t \cdot (2\pi \cos 2\pi t)\, dt = 2\pi \sin^2 2\pi t\, dt + 2\pi \cos^2 2\pi t\, dt = 2\pi (\sin^2 + \cos^2) 2\pi t\, dt = 2\pi\, dt.
$$

> [!note]- Derivation
> The pullback of a $1$-form under a smooth map sends a form to a form on the source. For $\gamma : [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}^2$, $\gamma^* (f\, dx + g\, dy) = (f \circ \gamma)\, \gamma^* dx + (g \circ \gamma)\, \gamma^* dy = (f \circ \gamma)\, \frac{d(x \circ \gamma)}{dt}\, dt + (g \circ \gamma)\, \frac{d(y \circ \gamma)}{dt}\, dt$.
>
> Substituting $x \circ \gamma = \cos 2\pi t$ and $y \circ \gamma = \sin 2\pi t$:
> - $\gamma^* dx = d(\cos 2\pi t)/dt\, dt = -2\pi \sin 2\pi t\, dt$
> - $\gamma^* dy = d(\sin 2\pi t)/dt\, dt = 2\pi \cos 2\pi t\, dt$
>
> For $d\theta = -y\, dx + x\, dy$ on $S^1$:
> $$
> \gamma^* d\theta = -(\sin 2\pi t)(-2\pi \sin 2\pi t\, dt) + (\cos 2\pi t)(2\pi \cos 2\pi t\, dt)
> $$
> $$
> = 2\pi \sin^2(2\pi t)\, dt + 2\pi \cos^2(2\pi t)\, dt = 2\pi (\sin^2 + \cos^2)(2\pi t)\, dt = 2\pi\, dt.
> $$
> So $\gamma^* d\theta$ is the constant $1$-form $2\pi\, dt$ on $[0, 1]$.
>
> *Alternative derivation:* on $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$, $d\theta$ is the differential of the (multi-valued) angle function. For the loop $\gamma$, the lift $\theta \circ \gamma$ is well-defined and single-valued on $[0, 1]$: $\theta \circ \gamma(t) = 2\pi t$. So $\gamma^* d\theta = d(2\pi t) = 2\pi\, dt$. This shorter derivation captures the geometric meaning: the form $d\theta$ pulls back to the differential of the angle traversed.

**Step 3: Evaluate the integral and interpret.**

$$
\int_\gamma d\theta = \int_{[0, 1]} 2\pi\, dt = 2\pi [t]_0^1 = 2\pi.
$$

By the de Rham theorem, the cohomology class $[d\theta] \in H^1_{dR}(S^1) = \mathbb{R}$ corresponds (via the integration pairing) to the singular cohomology class $\mathcal{I}[d\theta] \in H^1(S^1; \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$ given by $\mathcal{I}[d\theta][\gamma] = 2\pi$. Equivalently, $[d\theta]/(2\pi)$ is the unique cohomology class that takes the value $1$ on the homology generator $[\gamma]$ — i.e., it is the dual basis element to $[\gamma]$ under the natural pairing between $H^1(S^1; \mathbb{R})$ and $H_1(S^1; \mathbb{R})$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> $\int_{[0, 1]} 2\pi\, dt = 2\pi \int_0^1 dt = 2\pi \cdot 1 = 2\pi$. So $\int_\gamma d\theta = 2\pi$.
>
> Interpretation via de Rham: $H^1(S^1; \mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{Hom}(H_1(S^1; \mathbb{R}), \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$ (since $H_1(S^1; \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$). A basis is determined by a single non-zero element; the standard choice is the functional sending $[\gamma]$ to $1$. The de Rham theorem says this corresponds to a class in $H^1_{dR}(S^1) = \mathbb{R}$ — and our computation identifies it as $[d\theta]/(2\pi)$.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Theorem.** $\int_\gamma d\theta = 2\pi$, where $\gamma : [0, 1] \to S^1$, $\gamma(t) = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$, and $d\theta$ is the angular $1$-form on $S^1$. Consequently $[d\theta]/(2\pi) \in H^1_{dR}(S^1)$ is the dual basis element of $[\gamma] \in H_1(S^1; \mathbb{R})$ under the de Rham pairing.
>
> *Proof.*
>
> Compute the pullback $\gamma^* d\theta$ on $[0, 1]$. On $S^1$, $d\theta = -y\, dx + x\, dy$. The pullback under $\gamma(t) = (\cos 2\pi t, \sin 2\pi t)$:
> $$
> \gamma^* d\theta = -(\sin 2\pi t)\, d(\cos 2\pi t) + (\cos 2\pi t)\, d(\sin 2\pi t)
> $$
> $$
> = -(\sin 2\pi t)(-2\pi \sin 2\pi t)\, dt + (\cos 2\pi t)(2\pi \cos 2\pi t)\, dt = 2\pi (\sin^2 + \cos^2)(2\pi t)\, dt = 2\pi\, dt.
> $$
>
> Integrate:
> $$
> \int_\gamma d\theta = \int_{[0, 1]} 2\pi\, dt = 2\pi.
> $$
>
> By the [[Thm - The de Rham Theorem (Full Proof)|de Rham theorem]], $H^1_{dR}(S^1) \cong H^1(S^1; \mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{Hom}(H_1(S^1; \mathbb{R}), \mathbb{R})$ via the pairing $[\omega] \mapsto ([c] \mapsto \int_c \omega)$. The cohomology class $[d\theta]/(2\pi)$ thus corresponds to the functional sending $[\gamma]$ to $1$ — the dual basis element. $\qquad\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**This is the minimal non-trivial instance of the de Rham theorem.** $S^1$ has $H^1_{dR} = \mathbb{R}$ and $H_1(S^1; \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$, the simplest non-trivial first cohomology and homology. The de Rham pairing $\int_\gamma d\theta = 2\pi$ is a one-line calculation that directly verifies the isomorphism $\mathcal{I}$ in this case. Every subsequent de Rham pairing computation — periods on the torus, magnetic fluxes through closed surfaces, instanton numbers on $S^4$ — extends this prototype to higher dimensions and more complex spaces. The pattern "compute pullback, integrate over the parameter domain, get a topological invariant" is universal.

**The factor $2\pi$ is the source of normalisation conventions throughout physics.** $\int_{S^1} d\theta = 2\pi$ rather than $1$ is the reason quantum mechanics has $\hbar = h/(2\pi)$, the reason magnetic flux quanta are $\Phi_0 = h/q$ (not $h/(2\pi q)$), the reason the Dirac monopole quantisation involves $e \cdot g = n\hbar/2$, and the reason characteristic class formulae often have $2\pi$ or $1/(2\pi)$ factors. The geometric origin: $d\theta$ on $S^1$ integrates to the circumference $2\pi$, and any "natural" normalisation must reckon with this. The form $d\theta/(2\pi)$ has integral $1$ on $S^1$, and it is the "natural" choice in many contexts — but the form $d\theta$ is the more directly geometric one.

**The duality between cohomology and homology is realised concretely.** $H^1_{dR}(S^1) = \mathbb{R}$ is the linear dual of $H_1(S^1; \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$, via the integration pairing. The basis $\{[d\theta]\}$ of cohomology pairs with the basis $\{[\gamma]\}$ of homology via $\int_\gamma d\theta = 2\pi$. The dual basis (i.e. the basis paired to $1$, not $2\pi$) is $\{[d\theta]/(2\pi)\}$. This is the prototype of cohomology-as-dual-of-homology: every higher-dimensional example follows the same pattern, with $\dim H^p = \dim H_p$ over $\mathbb{R}$, and the de Rham pairing providing the explicit isomorphism between them.

**The angular form $d\theta$ is the "winding number" generator.** For a loop $\gamma : S^1 \to S^1 \subset \mathbb{C}^*$ in the punctured complex plane (or any space whose fundamental group has a $\mathbb{Z}$ factor), the integral $\int_\gamma d\theta/(2\pi)$ computes the **winding number** of $\gamma$ around the origin — an integer counting how many times the loop wraps around. The de Rham pairing is the algebraic incarnation of this winding number. Every "topological charge" in physics — magnetic monopole charge, vortex charge in superfluids, instanton number in gauge theories — is at heart a de Rham pairing of some closed form against a cycle, valued in integers (when the form represents an integer cohomology class).

**This computation is the foundation of computational topology.** Every higher de Rham computation reduces (via Mayer–Vietoris induction on a good cover) to computations like this one on contractible pieces and their intersections — which are spheres of lower dimension. The base case $\int_\gamma d\theta = 2\pi$ on $S^1$ is therefore the starting point of every iterated cohomology calculation: the cohomology of $S^n$ from Mayer–Vietoris reduces to the cohomology of $S^{n-1}$, ultimately to $S^1$, where the computation is this one. Similarly for $T^n$, $\mathbb{RP}^n$, $\mathbb{CP}^n$, and so on — all bottom out in the angular form on $S^1$.
