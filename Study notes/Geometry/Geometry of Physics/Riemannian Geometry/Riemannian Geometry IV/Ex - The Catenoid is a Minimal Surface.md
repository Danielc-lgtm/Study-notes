# Problem Statement

The **catenoid** is the surface of revolution obtained by rotating the **catenary** curve about the $z$-axis. Parametrise it as
$$
\mathbf{x}(u, v) = (a\cosh(v/a)\cos u,\, a\cosh(v/a)\sin u,\, v),
$$
with $u \in [0, 2\pi)$, $v \in \mathbb{R}$, and $a > 0$ a constant.

Show that the catenoid is a **minimal surface**: its mean curvature is $H \equiv 0$ everywhere. Moreover, compute the principal curvatures $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$ explicitly and verify they sum to zero, with the Gauss curvature $K = -1/(a^2\cosh^4(v/a)) < 0$.

**Recall:**

![[Def - Minimal Surface#The Definition]]

The mean curvature in classical notation is
$$
H = \frac{Eg_\mathrm{II} + Ge - 2Ff}{EG - F^2},
$$
with $E, F, G$ the components of the [[Def - First Fundamental Form|first fundamental form]] and $e, f, g_\mathrm{II}$ the components of the [[Def - Second Fundamental Form|second fundamental form]].

For a surface of revolution with rotational symmetry around the $z$-axis, the cross terms $F$ (in $g$) and $f$ (in $b$) vanish, simplifying the formula to $H = (Eg_\mathrm{II} + Ge)/(EG)$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** Direct verification that an explicit parametrised surface is minimal. The mechanic is: compute the first and second fundamental forms via the standard six-step routine, then evaluate $H = (Eg_\mathrm{II} + Ge - 2Ff)/(EG - F^2)$ and check it vanishes. For surfaces of revolution, the rotational symmetry forces $F = f = 0$, so the formula simplifies to $H = e/E + g_\mathrm{II}/G = \kappa_1 + \kappa_2$.

**Assumption pattern:** The catenoid is a surface of revolution generated by the catenary $r(v) = a\cosh(v/a)$. The catenary has a special property: it is the only meridian curve (up to scaling and shifting) whose surface of revolution is minimal. This is **Euler's theorem on minimal surfaces of revolution** (1744). The defining ODE for the catenary $r(v)$ is $r r'' = 1 + (r')^2$, which we will not directly use, but it encodes the minimality.

**Theorem routing:** Compute the metric and second fundamental form of the catenoid in $(u, v)$ coordinates; assemble $H$ using the formula. The principal curvatures will be the diagonal entries of $\mathrm{diag}(\kappa_1, \kappa_2) = \mathrm{diag}(e/E, g_\mathrm{II}/G)$ (in the orthonormal frame aligned with the coordinate directions, scaled by the metric); their sum will be $H$, and the result will be $0$.

**Key decision point:** Recognising that the meridian and azimuthal coordinate directions are principal directions for any surface of revolution. The rotational symmetry around the $z$-axis means that the azimuthal direction is an eigendirection of the shape operator (the symmetry permutes meridians, fixing $\partial/\partial\varphi$ as a principal direction). The meridian direction, being orthogonal, is the other principal direction. So $\kappa_1 = \kappa_v$ (meridian curvature) and $\kappa_2 = \kappa_u$ (azimuthal curvature) can be computed directly without diagonalising a matrix.

# Legal Operations Used

2. **Operation 9 from the topic page (identify principal directions via the eigenvector equation):** The rotational symmetry shortcuts the eigenvalue problem: meridian and azimuthal directions are principal, with eigenvalues directly computable from the diagonal entries of the shape operator matrix.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> The catenoid's meridian curve is the catenary $r(v) = a\cosh(v/a)$, parametrised by $v$ (which is *not* arc length — the arc length differential along the meridian is $ds = \sqrt{(dr/dv)^2 + 1}\, dv = \cosh(v/a)\, dv$). Compute $E$ and $G$ — they will involve $\cosh^2(v/a)$, and have the special property $E = G = \cosh^2(v/a)$ (the catenoid is **conformally flat** in $(u, v)$ coordinates).

> [!note]- Hint 2
> The unit normal $N$ to the catenoid points radially outward in the cross-section plane, but tilted inward along the $z$-axis as $v$ varies. Specifically, $N = (\cos u\cdot\mathrm{sech}(v/a), \sin u\cdot\mathrm{sech}(v/a), -\tanh(v/a))$, with $|N| = 1$. Verify this by computing the cross product $\mathbf{x}_u \times \mathbf{x}_v$ and normalising.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> The second fundamental form components are $e = -a$, $f = 0$, $g_\mathrm{II} = a$. The signs differ ($e < 0$, $g_\mathrm{II} > 0$), which immediately tells you the principal curvatures have opposite signs — necessary for $H = \kappa_1 + \kappa_2 = 0$. Combine to verify $H = 0$ and $K = -1/(a\cosh^2(v/a))^2$.

# Solution

The proof breaks into three steps. Step 1 computes the first fundamental form. Step 2 finds the unit normal and the second fundamental form. Step 3 evaluates $H$ and $K$.

**Step 1: First fundamental form is $\mathrm{I} = \cosh^2(v/a)(du^2 + dv^2)$ — conformally flat.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Compute tangent vectors:
> $$
> \mathbf{x}_u = (-a\cosh(v/a)\sin u, a\cosh(v/a)\cos u, 0),
> $$
> $$
> \mathbf{x}_v = (\sinh(v/a)\cos u, \sinh(v/a)\sin u, 1).
> $$
> Inner products:
> $$
> E = \langle\mathbf{x}_u, \mathbf{x}_u\rangle = a^2\cosh^2(v/a)(\sin^2 u + \cos^2 u) = a^2\cosh^2(v/a),
> $$
> $$
> G = \langle\mathbf{x}_v, \mathbf{x}_v\rangle = \sinh^2(v/a) + 1 = \cosh^2(v/a),
> $$
> $$
> F = \langle\mathbf{x}_u, \mathbf{x}_v\rangle = -a\cosh(v/a)\sin u\cdot\sinh(v/a)\cos u + a\cosh(v/a)\cos u\cdot\sinh(v/a)\sin u + 0 = 0.
> $$
> So $\mathrm{I} = a^2\cosh^2(v/a)\, du^2 + \cosh^2(v/a)\, dv^2$.
>
> Note: $E/a^2 = G$, so up to a constant rescaling of $u$ (let $\tilde u = au$), $\mathrm{I} = \cosh^2(v/a)(d\tilde u^2 + dv^2)$ — **conformally flat**, with conformal factor $\cosh^2(v/a)$. This is a special feature of the catenoid; for a generic surface of revolution this conformal-flatness need not hold.
>
> So $\det g_{\alpha\beta} = EG - F^2 = a^2\cosh^4(v/a)$.

**Step 2: Second fundamental form $\mathrm{II} = -a\, du^2 + a\, dv^2$ (no, let me recompute — this isn't quite right).**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Compute the unit normal. The cross product:
> $$
> \mathbf{x}_u \times \mathbf{x}_v = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} & \mathbf{j} & \mathbf{k} \\ -a\cosh(v/a)\sin u & a\cosh(v/a)\cos u & 0 \\ \sinh(v/a)\cos u & \sinh(v/a)\sin u & 1\end{vmatrix}.
> $$
> Expanding:
> - $\mathbf{i}$-component: $a\cosh(v/a)\cos u\cdot 1 - 0\cdot\sinh(v/a)\sin u = a\cosh(v/a)\cos u$.
> - $\mathbf{j}$-component: $-((-a\cosh(v/a)\sin u)\cdot 1 - 0\cdot\sinh(v/a)\cos u) = a\cosh(v/a)\sin u$.
> - $\mathbf{k}$-component: $-a\cosh(v/a)\sin u\cdot\sinh(v/a)\sin u - a\cosh(v/a)\cos u\cdot\sinh(v/a)\cos u = -a\cosh(v/a)\sinh(v/a)$.
>
> So $\mathbf{x}_u \times \mathbf{x}_v = a\cosh(v/a)(\cos u, \sin u, -\sinh(v/a))$, with magnitude $a\cosh(v/a)\sqrt{1 + \sinh^2(v/a)} = a\cosh^2(v/a)$.
>
> Unit normal (outward, in the sense of "away from the $z$-axis on the outer cross-section, tilted to satisfy the orientation"):
> $$
> N = \frac{\mathbf{x}_u \times \mathbf{x}_v}{|\mathbf{x}_u \times \mathbf{x}_v|} = \frac{(\cos u, \sin u, -\sinh(v/a))}{\cosh(v/a)} = (\mathrm{sech}(v/a)\cos u, \mathrm{sech}(v/a)\sin u, -\tanh(v/a)).
> $$
> Verify $|N|^2 = \mathrm{sech}^2(v/a)(\cos^2 u + \sin^2 u) + \tanh^2(v/a) = \mathrm{sech}^2 + \tanh^2 = 1$. Good.
>
> Compute second derivatives:
> $$
> \mathbf{x}_{uu} = (-a\cosh(v/a)\cos u, -a\cosh(v/a)\sin u, 0),
> $$
> $$
> \mathbf{x}_{uv} = (-\sinh(v/a)\sin u, \sinh(v/a)\cos u, 0),
> $$
> $$
> \mathbf{x}_{vv} = ((1/a)\cosh(v/a)\cos u, (1/a)\cosh(v/a)\sin u, 0).
> $$
> Dot with $N$:
> $$
> e = \langle\mathbf{x}_{uu}, N\rangle = -a\cosh(v/a)\cos u\cdot\mathrm{sech}(v/a)\cos u - a\cosh(v/a)\sin u\cdot\mathrm{sech}(v/a)\sin u + 0\cdot(-\tanh(v/a))
> $$
> $$
> = -a(\cos^2 u + \sin^2 u) = -a.
> $$
> Similarly,
> $$
> f = \langle\mathbf{x}_{uv}, N\rangle = -\sinh(v/a)\sin u\cdot\mathrm{sech}(v/a)\cos u + \sinh(v/a)\cos u\cdot\mathrm{sech}(v/a)\sin u + 0 = 0,
> $$
> $$
> g_\mathrm{II} = \langle\mathbf{x}_{vv}, N\rangle = (1/a)\cosh(v/a)\cos u\cdot\mathrm{sech}(v/a)\cos u + (1/a)\cosh(v/a)\sin u\cdot\mathrm{sech}(v/a)\sin u + 0
> $$
> $$
> = (1/a)(\cos^2 u + \sin^2 u) = 1/a.
> $$
>
> So $\mathrm{II} = -a\, du^2 + (1/a)\, dv^2$.

**Step 3: Verify $H = 0$ and $K = -1/(a^2\cosh^4(v/a))$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> The principal curvatures (since the surface of revolution has principal directions aligned with $u, v$ coordinates by symmetry) are:
> $$
> \kappa_1 = e/E = (-a)/(a^2\cosh^2(v/a)) = -1/(a\cosh^2(v/a)),
> $$
> $$
> \kappa_2 = g_\mathrm{II}/G = (1/a)/\cosh^2(v/a) = 1/(a\cosh^2(v/a)).
> $$
> Sum: $\kappa_1 + \kappa_2 = -1/(a\cosh^2(v/a)) + 1/(a\cosh^2(v/a)) = 0$. So $H = 0$, the catenoid is minimal.
>
> Product: $K = \kappa_1\kappa_2 = -1/(a^2\cosh^4(v/a)) < 0$. The catenoid is hyperbolic (saddle-shaped) everywhere, with curvature decaying away from the throat ($v = 0$).

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Parametrise the catenoid as $\mathbf{x}(u, v) = (a\cosh(v/a)\cos u, a\cosh(v/a)\sin u, v)$ with $u \in [0, 2\pi)$, $v \in \mathbb{R}$.
>
> Tangent vectors $\mathbf{x}_u = (-a\cosh(v/a)\sin u, a\cosh(v/a)\cos u, 0)$, $\mathbf{x}_v = (\sinh(v/a)\cos u, \sinh(v/a)\sin u, 1)$. First fundamental form: $E = a^2\cosh^2(v/a)$, $F = 0$, $G = \cosh^2(v/a)$, with $\det g = a^2\cosh^4(v/a)$.
>
> Unit normal $N = (\mathrm{sech}(v/a)\cos u, \mathrm{sech}(v/a)\sin u, -\tanh(v/a))$, with $|N| = 1$.
>
> Second derivatives: $\mathbf{x}_{uu} = -a\cosh(v/a)(\cos u, \sin u, 0)$, $\mathbf{x}_{uv} = \sinh(v/a)(-\sin u, \cos u, 0)$, $\mathbf{x}_{vv} = (1/a)\cosh(v/a)(\cos u, \sin u, 0)$.
>
> Dotting with $N$: $e = -a$, $f = 0$, $g_\mathrm{II} = 1/a$. So $\mathrm{II} = -a\, du^2 + (1/a)\, dv^2$.
>
> Principal curvatures (from the diagonal of $b^\alpha_{\;\beta} = g^{\alpha\gamma}b_{\gamma\beta}$ — which is diagonal here):
> $$
> \kappa_1 = \frac{e}{E} = -\frac{1}{a\cosh^2(v/a)}, \qquad \kappa_2 = \frac{g_\mathrm{II}}{G} = \frac{1}{a\cosh^2(v/a)}.
> $$
> Mean curvature: $H = \kappa_1 + \kappa_2 = 0$. The catenoid is minimal. $\square$
>
> Gauss curvature: $K = \kappa_1\kappa_2 = -1/(a^2\cosh^4(v/a)) < 0$. The catenoid is hyperbolic everywhere, with curvature concentrating at the "throat" $v = 0$ (where $K = -1/a^2$) and decaying exponentially as $|v| \to \infty$.

> [!warning] Illegal but tempting: "Surfaces of revolution with $K = $ constant negative are minimal"
> This is false. The catenoid has variable $K = -1/(a^2\cosh^4(v/a))$ but $H = 0$ — minimal. The **pseudosphere** (see [[Ex - Gauss Curvature of the Pseudosphere is -1]]) has constant $K = -1/a^2$ but $H \neq 0$ — *not* minimal. The properties "constant negative curvature" and "minimal" are independent on a surface of revolution; the catenoid is the unique minimal surface of revolution (up to scaling), while constant-negative-curvature surfaces of revolution form a different one-parameter family.

> [!tip] Frame-invariance check: hairy-ball-style independence of the choice of normal
> Reversing the normal $N \to -N$ flips the sign of every $b_{\alpha\beta}$, hence of $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$, and so flips the sign of $H$. But $K = \kappa_1\kappa_2$ is invariant: $(-\kappa_1)(-\kappa_2) = \kappa_1\kappa_2$. So the conclusion "$H = 0$" is the *same* for either choice of normal, and the catenoid's minimality is intrinsic to the surface (orientation-independent). The conclusion $K = -1/(a^2\cosh^4(v/a))$ is also independent of the normal choice.

# Key Takeaways

**The catenoid is the unique nonplanar minimal surface of revolution.** Euler's 1744 theorem: any minimal surface of revolution in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is either a plane or a catenoid (scaled and translated). The proof: the minimal surface equation for a surface of revolution with meridian $r(v)$ reduces to the ODE $rr'' = 1 + (r')^2$ (the "catenary equation"), whose solutions are $r(v) = c_1\cosh((v - c_2)/c_1)$ for constants $c_1, c_2$, plus the degenerate case $r = \text{const}$ (cylinder, *not* minimal — has $H = 1/c_1 \neq 0$). Setting the constants gives exactly the catenoid family. This rigidity result is a typical Euler-style classification theorem.

**The trick "rotational symmetry $\Rightarrow$ principal directions aligned with coordinates" saves enormous computation.** For a generic surface, computing $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$ requires solving the $2\times 2$ eigenvalue problem $\det(b - \kappa g) = 0$ at each point. For a surface of revolution, the rotational symmetry means $\partial/\partial\varphi$ is automatically an eigendirection (the rotation fixes it), hence $\partial/\partial v$ (the meridian direction) is the other principal direction by orthogonality. So $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$ are just the diagonal entries $e/E, g_\mathrm{II}/G$. The same shortcut applies to any surface with a $1$-parameter isometry group: the Killing vector field is a principal direction. **Trigger:** "rotationally symmetric surface" → "principal directions are meridian and azimuth, no eigenvalue problem needed".

**The catenoid bounds the **Plateau region** for two coaxial circular rings.** A soap film spanning two coaxial circular wire rings of radius $R$ at distance $L$ apart forms a catenoidal film when $L < L_*\approx 1.325\cdot R$, and a "Goldschmidt solution" (two flat discs joined by a thin neck) for $L > L_*$. The critical distance $L_*$ corresponds to the catenoid becoming unstable (its second variation of area has a negative direction). At $L = L_*$, both solutions have equal area, and physically the soap film "jumps" from catenoid to Goldschmidt as $L$ increases. This is the prototype example of a **bifurcation** in minimal-surface theory.

**The Weierstrass–Enneper representation makes the catenoid's holomorphic structure visible.** Every minimal surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$ can be parametrised by a holomorphic function $g$ (the Gauss map) and a holomorphic $1$-form $\eta$ on a Riemann surface. For the catenoid, $g(z) = e^z$ and $\eta = -e^{-z}dz$ on $\mathbb{C}$. The corresponding immersion is $\mathbf{x}(z) = \mathrm{Re}\int(1 - g^2, i(1 + g^2), 2g)\eta = (\cosh(\mathrm{Re}z)\cos(\mathrm{Im}z), \cosh(\mathrm{Re}z)\sin(\mathrm{Im}z), \mathrm{Re}z)$ — the catenoid. The **conjugate minimal surface** (with the same $g, \eta$ but $\mathbf{x} = \mathrm{Im}\int$ instead of $\mathrm{Re}\int$) is the **helicoid** $\mathbf{x}(z) = (\sinh(\mathrm{Re}z)\sin(\mathrm{Im}z), -\sinh(\mathrm{Re}z)\cos(\mathrm{Im}z), \mathrm{Im}z)$. The Bonnet deformation $\mathrm{Re}(e^{i\theta}\int\cdots)$ for $\theta \in [0, \pi/2]$ interpolates continuously between catenoid ($\theta = 0$) and helicoid ($\theta = \pi/2$), with all intermediate surfaces also minimal and locally isometric to both. See [[Ex - The Helicoid is Minimal]].

**Companion exercises:** Compare with [[Ex - Gauss Curvature of the Pseudosphere is -1]] (constant negative curvature, not minimal) and [[Ex - The Helicoid is Minimal]] (Bonnet-conjugate minimal surface, the "twisted catenoid"). The catenoid and helicoid together are the two simplest nonplanar minimal surfaces and the prototypes of the **Weierstrass–Enneper** family of minimal surfaces.
