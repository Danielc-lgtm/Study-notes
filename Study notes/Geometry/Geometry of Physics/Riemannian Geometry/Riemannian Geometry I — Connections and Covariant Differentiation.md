# Notation Registry

A standing convention for this topic: **a connection $\nabla$ on a smooth vector bundle $E \to M$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear operation $(X, s) \mapsto \nabla_X s$ that is $C^\infty(M)$-linear in $X$ and a Leibniz derivation in $s$.** When $E = TM$ this is an **affine connection**; when $(M, g)$ is Riemannian and the connection is additionally torsion-free and metric-compatible, it is the **Levi-Civita connection**, the unique connection delivered by [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Statement)]] in the [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds|DG XII]] preview and built in full here. Indices run from $1$ to $n = \dim M$ and we use the Einstein summation convention throughout. Where formulas care about it, Greek letters $\alpha, \beta, a, b$ range over frame indices and Latin letters $i, j, k, l$ over coordinate indices; for orthonormal frames we always use Greek.

- $M, N$ — smooth manifolds; $\dim M = n$
- $E \to M$, $\pi : E \to M$ — a smooth vector bundle over $M$
- $\Gamma(E)$ — the $C^\infty(M)$-module of smooth sections of $E$
- $TM, T^*M$ — the tangent and cotangent bundles; $\mathfrak{X}(M) = \Gamma(TM)$
- $X, Y, Z$ — smooth vector fields; $s, t, u$ — sections of a general $E$
- $\nabla$ — a connection on $E$, with $\nabla_X s \in \Gamma(E)$ for $X \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$, $s \in \Gamma(E)$
- $\nabla s$ — the **covariant differential** of $s$, an $E$-valued 1-form: $\nabla s \in \Gamma(T^*M \otimes E)$, with $(\nabla s)(X) = \nabla_X s$
- $\partial_i = \partial/\partial x^i$ — coordinate vector fields in a chart $(x^1, \ldots, x^n)$
- $e = (e_1, \ldots, e_n)$ — a local **frame** of vector fields (in general not a coordinate frame; $[e_a, e_b] = c^c_{ab}\, e_c$ in general)
- $\sigma = (\sigma^1, \ldots, \sigma^n)^T$ — the dual coframe, $\sigma^a(e_b) = \delta^a_b$
- $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ — the **Christoffel symbols** of the Levi-Civita connection in a coordinate frame: $\nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j = \Gamma^k_{ij}\,\partial_k$
- $\omega^a{}_b$ or $\Gamma^a{}_b$ — the **connection 1-forms** in a general frame: $\nabla e_b = e_a \otimes \omega^a{}_b$, or equivalently $\omega^a{}_b = \omega^a_{cb}\,\sigma^c$ with $\nabla_{e_c}e_b = \omega^a_{cb}\,e_a$. We use $\omega$ in the structure-equation context and $\Gamma$ when emphasising the Christoffel parallel
- $\Omega^a{}_b$ or $\theta^a{}_b$ — the **curvature 2-forms** in the same frame: $\Omega^a{}_b = d\omega^a{}_b + \omega^a{}_c \wedge \omega^c{}_b$, the entries of $\Omega = d\omega + \omega \wedge \omega$
- $\tau^a$ — the **torsion 2-forms** in a general frame: $\tau^a = \tfrac{1}{2}T^a_{bc}\,\sigma^b \wedge \sigma^c$
- $T(X, Y) = \nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X - [X, Y]$ — the **torsion tensor**, an $E$-valued 2-form when $E = TM$
- $R(X, Y)Z = \nabla_X \nabla_Y Z - \nabla_Y \nabla_X Z - \nabla_{[X, Y]} Z$ — the **Riemann curvature tensor** (developed in detail in [[Riemannian Geometry III — Riemann Curvature and Topology]])
- $P_\gamma^{t_1 \leftarrow t_0} : E_{\gamma(t_0)} \to E_{\gamma(t_1)}$ — **parallel transport** along the curve $\gamma$ from time $t_0$ to time $t_1$
- $g_{ij}, g^{ij}$ — components of the metric and its inverse; $g^{ij}g_{jk} = \delta^i_k$
- $\Gamma' = g^{-1}\Gamma g + g^{-1}dg$ — the **gauge transformation law** for the matrix of connection 1-forms under a change of frame $e' = e g$

The full notation registry for the underlying manifold-and-metric machinery lives on [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds]]; everything new in this topic is added above.

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: a connection is the data needed to take the derivative of a vector field on a manifold. That is the whole of it, but the reasons are deep, because a smooth manifold by itself has no canonical way to differentiate a vector field — the obvious attempt is meaningless, and curing the meaninglessness *is* the geometry.

To see why the obvious attempt is meaningless, recall what differentiation does in $\mathbb{R}^n$. To compute $\partial_i v^j$ at a point $p$, one compares the value $v^j(p + \varepsilon e_i)$ at a nearby point to the value $v^j(p)$, takes the difference, and divides by $\varepsilon$. On $\mathbb{R}^n$ this comparison is licit because $v^j(p + \varepsilon e_i)$ and $v^j(p)$ both live in the same standard copy of $\mathbb{R}^n$ — the components are numbers, and numbers can always be subtracted. On a curved manifold this is no longer true. A vector field $V$ assigns to each point $p$ a tangent vector $V(p) \in T_pM$, and $T_pM$ and $T_qM$ are *different* vector spaces for $p \neq q$. The values $V(p)$ and $V(q)$ live in unrelated copies of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and there is no canonical way to subtract them. The naive coordinate derivative $\partial_j V^i$ depends on the chart and does *not* transform as a tensor — it transforms with extra inhomogeneous pieces involving second derivatives of the change-of-chart map.

A **connection** is the additional piece of data needed to make this comparison possible. It promises, for every curve $\gamma$ and every tangent vector $v \in T_{\gamma(0)}M$, a way to transport $v$ along $\gamma$ to a tangent vector at every other point of the curve — **parallel transport**. Once parallel transport is in hand, differentiation becomes well-defined: to compute $\nabla_X V$ at $p$, walk an infinitesimal distance along $X$, parallel-transport the value of $V$ there back to $p$, and take the limiting ratio. The result $\nabla_X V$ is a tangent vector at $p$ — a coordinate-free, chart-independent object.

There is more than one connection on a generic manifold; in fact there is an infinite affine space of them. To pin down a canonical one some extra information is needed, and the canonical move on a *Riemannian* manifold is to demand that parallel transport preserve the metric — that is, lengths and angles. This is the **metric-compatibility** condition. Combined with the requirement of **torsion-freeness** (a symmetry condition: $\nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X = [X, Y]$), it selects a *unique* connection, the **Levi-Civita connection** of the metric, by the [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Statement)|fundamental theorem of Riemannian geometry]]. The statement of this theorem was the closing forward bridge of [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds]]; **this chapter delivers the connection it promised**. Everything subsequent in Riemannian geometry — [[Def - Geodesic|geodesics]], the exponential map, the Riemann curvature tensor, the Gauss–Bonnet theorem, gauge theory — refers to *the* Levi-Civita connection of *the* Riemannian (or Lorentzian) metric, and that uniqueness is what makes the subject canonical.

$$
\text{smooth manifold } M \;\xrightarrow{\text{pick }\nabla}\; \text{affine connection} \;\xrightarrow{\text{add metric }g}\; \text{Levi-Civita connection } \nabla^g \;\xrightarrow{\text{curvature}}\; \text{Riemannian geometry}
$$

The plan of this chapter follows the arc above. Section §1.1 introduces the abstract definition of an [[Def - Affine Connection on a Vector Bundle|affine connection on a vector bundle]] — the operation $(X, s) \mapsto \nabla_X s$, $C^\infty$-linear in $X$ and a derivation in $s$ — and specialises to the tangent bundle. The [[Def - Christoffel Symbols|Christoffel symbols]] $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ are the components of the connection in a coordinate frame, $\nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j = \Gamma^k_{ij}\,\partial_k$. We define the [[Def - Covariant Derivative along a Curve|covariant derivative along a curve]] and [[Def - Parallel Transport|parallel transport]] — the resolution of the cross-tangent-space comparison problem. The [[Def - Torsion Tensor|torsion tensor]] $T(X, Y) = \nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X - [X, Y]$ measures the failure of the connection to be symmetric. Section §1.2 specialises to the Riemannian setting: the [[Def - Metric-Compatible Connection|metric-compatibility condition]] $X g(Y, Z) = g(\nabla_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla_X Z)$ is shown to be equivalent to "parallel transport is a linear [[Def - Isometry|isometry]]" ([[Thm - Parallel Transport is an Isometry for Metric-Compatible Connections]]), and the [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Full Proof)|fundamental theorem]] is proved in full via the [[Thm - Koszul Formula|Koszul formula]]. Section §1.3 reformulates everything in Cartan's moving-frame language: the [[Def - Connection 1-Forms (Cartan)|connection 1-forms]] $\omega^a{}_b$ and [[Def - Curvature 2-Forms (Cartan)|curvature 2-forms]] $\Omega^a{}_b$, and Cartan's [[Thm - Cartan's First Structural Equation|first]] and [[Thm - Cartan's Second Structural Equation|second]] **structural equations**, which encode torsion and curvature in the language of differential forms. Section §1.4 examines parallel transport globally, the [[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Connection 1-Forms|gauge-transformation law]] $\Gamma' = g^{-1}\Gamma g + g^{-1}dg$ that records how connection 1-forms transform under change of frame, the [[Def - Induced Connection on Tensor Bundles|induced connection on tensor bundles]], and the connection between vector-bundle connections and the more general **principal-bundle connections** that govern Yang-Mills theory (full development in [[Gauge Theory V — Parallel Transport, Holonomy, Gauge Transformations, and Flat Connections]]).

The reader is assumed fluent with smooth manifolds ([[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases|DG I]]), vector fields and the Lie bracket ([[Differential Geometry V — Vector Fields, Flows, and the Lie Bracket|DG V]]), vector bundles, local frames, and sections ([[Differential Geometry VI — Vector Bundles and the Cotangent Bundle|DG VI]]), tensor fields ([[Differential Geometry VII — Tensors and Tensor Fields|DG VII]]), differential forms and the exterior derivative ([[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms|DG VIII]]), and Riemannian and semi-Riemannian metrics ([[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds|DG XII]]). The linear-algebra backbone is [[Def - Inner Product Space|inner product spaces]] with [[Def - Orthonormal Basis|orthonormal bases]], and the [[Def - Linear Map|linear-map]] formalism for parallel transport. The reader who has worked through [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds|DG XII]] holds the topic-prerequisite line.

# Concept Map

## §1.1 Affine Connections on a Vector Bundle

- **[[Def - Affine Connection on a Vector Bundle]]**
	- An **affine connection** on a smooth vector bundle $E \to M$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear operation $\nabla : \mathfrak{X}(M) \times \Gamma(E) \to \Gamma(E)$, $(X, s) \mapsto \nabla_X s$, satisfying $\nabla_{fX}s = f\nabla_X s$ (tensorial / $C^\infty$-linear in $X$) and the **Leibniz rule** $\nabla_X(fs) = X(f)\,s + f\,\nabla_X s$ (derivation in $s$). The first axiom is what makes $\nabla_X s$ depend only on the value of $X$ at the basepoint; the Leibniz rule is the irreducible "derivative-like" content. When $E = TM$ this is an affine connection on $M$, and the difference of any two connections on $E$ is a tensor field, so connections form an affine space modelled on $\Gamma(T^*M \otimes \mathrm{End}\,E)$.

- **[[Def - Christoffel Symbols]]**
	- In a local coordinate chart $(x^i)$ on $(M, \nabla)$ with coordinate frame $\partial_i$, the **Christoffel symbols** $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ are the $n^3$ smooth functions defined by $\nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j = \Gamma^k_{ij}\,\partial_k$. They encode the connection in the chart: $(\nabla_X Y)^k = X^i \partial_i Y^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\, X^i Y^j$ for any vector fields $X, Y$. They are *not* the components of a tensor; under change of chart they transform inhomogeneously with a second-derivative correction $\Gamma'{}^k_{ij} = \frac{\partial x'^k}{\partial x^p}\frac{\partial x^q}{\partial x'^i}\frac{\partial x^r}{\partial x'^j}\Gamma^p_{qr} + \frac{\partial x'^k}{\partial x^p}\frac{\partial^2 x^p}{\partial x'^i \partial x'^j}$, the inhomogeneous piece being precisely what is needed for $\nabla_X Y$ to be a genuine vector field.

- **[[Def - Covariant Derivative along a Curve]]**
	- For a smooth curve $\gamma : I \to M$ and a smooth section $V$ of $E$ defined along $\gamma$ (a map $V : I \to E$ with $\pi \circ V = \gamma$), the **covariant derivative along $\gamma$**, written $\nabla_t V$ or $DV/dt$, is the unique $\mathbb{R}$-linear operation extending $\nabla$ that satisfies the Leibniz rule $\nabla_t(fV) = f'\,V + f\,\nabla_t V$ for smooth $f : I \to \mathbb{R}$ and reduces to $\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\tilde V$ when $V$ is the restriction of a globally defined section $\tilde V$. In coordinates, $(\nabla_t V)^k = \dot V^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\,\dot\gamma^i V^j$. The existence and uniqueness of $\nabla_t$ is mechanical from the connection axioms; the value at $t$ depends only on the values of $V$ near $t$ along the curve, not on any extension.

- **[[Def - Parallel Transport]]**
	- A section $V$ along $\gamma$ is **parallel** if $\nabla_t V = 0$; the **parallel transport** operator $P_\gamma^{t_1 \leftarrow t_0} : E_{\gamma(t_0)} \to E_{\gamma(t_1)}$ sends $v$ to the value at $t_1$ of the unique parallel section with $V(t_0) = v$. Existence and uniqueness follow from the linear ODE $\dot V^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\,\dot\gamma^i V^j = 0$ together with the standard ODE existence theorem; the operator is a **linear isomorphism** $E_{\gamma(t_0)} \to E_{\gamma(t_1)}$. Parallel transport is in general path-dependent: along two different curves from $p$ to $q$, the resulting maps $E_p \to E_q$ usually differ, and **curvature** is the measure of this path-dependence.

- **[[Def - Torsion Tensor]]**
	- For a connection $\nabla$ on $TM$, the **torsion tensor** is $T(X, Y) := \nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X - [X, Y]$, a vector-field-valued antisymmetric quantity that — despite the appearance of derivatives — is $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear in $X$ and $Y$ (the derivative terms cancel via the Leibniz rule and the $[X,Y]$ correction), so $T$ is a $(1,2)$-tensor field. In a coordinate frame, $T^k_{ij} = \Gamma^k_{ij} - \Gamma^k_{ji}$, so torsion-freeness is the statement that the Christoffel symbols are symmetric in their lower indices. The Levi-Civita connection is torsion-free by definition; for general connections (e.g., the spin connection on a spin manifold, the Weitzenböck connection on a parallelizable manifold) torsion can be nonzero and carries geometric content.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Connections on Principal Bundles *(from Gauge Theory)*
> Replace "vector bundle" by **principal $G$-bundle**, and replace the section $s$ by an equivariant horizontal lift. The result is a connection in the sense of [[Gauge Theory V — Parallel Transport, Holonomy, Gauge Transformations, and Flat Connections|Gauge Theory V]], the framework in which **Yang-Mills theory** lives — the gauge field is a connection on a principal bundle, and its curvature 2-form is the field strength $F = dA + A \wedge A$. The Levi-Civita connection on $TM$ is the special case where $G = \mathrm{GL}(n)$ acting on $\mathbb{R}^n$, with the orthonormal-frame reduction giving $G = O(n)$; the metric-compatibility and torsion-freeness conditions then pick out exactly one connection in the affine space of principal connections. The **Bianchi identity** $dF + A \wedge F - F \wedge A = 0$ for principal connections is the generalisation of the symmetries of the Riemann tensor.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Geodesic Equation *(from Riemannian Geometry)*
> A **geodesic** is a curve whose velocity is parallel-transported along itself: $\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma = 0$. In coordinates this is the famous geodesic equation $\ddot\gamma^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\dot\gamma^i\dot\gamma^j = 0$. Given the ODE existence theorem, given any $p \in M$ and any $v \in T_pM$, there is a unique geodesic with $\gamma(0) = p$, $\dot\gamma(0) = v$, defined on some maximal interval. The full development — exponential map, normal coordinates, geodesic completeness, Hopf-Rinow, variational characterisation — is the content of [[Riemannian Geometry II — Geodesics, the Exponential Map, and Variational Principles|Riemannian Geometry II]].

- **[[Ex - The Tangent Bundle of a Lie Group has a Canonical Flat Connection]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For any Lie group $G$, declare the left-invariant vector fields to be parallel: $\nabla_X Y = 0$ for $X, Y$ left-invariant. Verify that this is a connection on $TG$, and compute its torsion tensor to be $T(X, Y) = -[X, Y]$ (the Lie-algebra bracket); deduce $\nabla$ has zero curvature but nonzero torsion on every non-abelian Lie group.

## §1.2 The Levi-Civita Connection

- **[[Def - Metric-Compatible Connection]]**
	- A connection $\nabla$ on $TM$ is **metric-compatible** with the metric $g$ (equivalently, **$g$-compatible**, or just "compatible") if $\nabla g = 0$, where $g$ is regarded as a section of $T^*M \otimes T^*M$ and $\nabla$ is the induced connection on this tensor bundle. Equivalently and most usefully: $X\,g(Y, Z) = g(\nabla_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla_X Z)$ for all $X, Y, Z \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ — a Leibniz rule for $g$ — which by [[Thm - Parallel Transport is an Isometry for Metric-Compatible Connections]] is the same as "parallel transport along any curve is a linear isometry of tangent spaces". On a Riemannian manifold this preserves lengths and angles; on a Lorentzian manifold it preserves the causal classification of vectors (timelike stays timelike, null stays null).

- **[[Def - Levi-Civita Connection]]**
	- The **Levi-Civita connection** of a Riemannian (or semi-Riemannian) manifold $(M, g)$ is the unique connection $\nabla$ on $TM$ that is both **torsion-free** ($\nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X = [X, Y]$) and **metric-compatible** ($Xg(Y, Z) = g(\nabla_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla_X Z)$). Existence and uniqueness are the content of the [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Full Proof)|fundamental theorem of Riemannian geometry]], proved here in full. In a coordinate frame, its Christoffel symbols are $\Gamma^k_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{kl}(\partial_i g_{jl} + \partial_j g_{il} - \partial_l g_{ij})$.

- **[[Thm - Koszul Formula]]**
	- For the Levi-Civita connection $\nabla$ of $(M, g)$, the inner product $g(\nabla_X Y, Z)$ is determined by the metric and Lie brackets alone via $2g(\nabla_X Y, Z) = X g(Y, Z) + Y g(X, Z) - Z g(X, Y) + g([X, Y], Z) - g([X, Z], Y) - g([Y, Z], X)$. This formula is the proof of uniqueness (any two torsion-free metric-compatible connections satisfy it, so they agree by non-degeneracy of $g$) and of existence (defining $\nabla_X Y$ by this formula and the sharp map produces a connection that one verifies is torsion-free and metric-compatible). Computationally, it is the master formula from which the coordinate Christoffel formula is read off.

- **[[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Full Proof)]]**
	- On every Riemannian (or semi-Riemannian) manifold $(M, g)$ there is a **unique** connection $\nabla$ on $TM$ that is torsion-free and metric-compatible — the Levi-Civita connection. Uniqueness follows by symmetrising the metric-compatibility identity over the three slots and using torsion-freeness to eliminate cross terms, obtaining the [[Thm - Koszul Formula]] which, by non-degeneracy of $g$, determines $\nabla_X Y$ from $g$. Existence is by reading the connection off the Koszul formula and verifying the axioms; in coordinates this delivers $\Gamma^k_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{kl}(\partial_i g_{jl} + \partial_j g_{il} - \partial_l g_{ij})$ — the **Christoffel formula**.

- **[[Thm - Parallel Transport is an Isometry for Metric-Compatible Connections]]**
	- A connection $\nabla$ on $TM$ is metric-compatible if and only if for every smooth curve $\gamma$, the parallel transport operator $P_\gamma^{t_1 \leftarrow t_0} : T_{\gamma(t_0)}M \to T_{\gamma(t_1)}M$ is a linear isometry — that is, $g_{\gamma(t_1)}(P_\gamma v, P_\gamma w) = g_{\gamma(t_0)}(v, w)$ for all $v, w$. This is the operational content of metric-compatibility: lengths and angles are conserved along parallel transport, which is what makes "constant velocity" meaningful for a particle moving on a curved manifold.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The [[Def - Riemann Curvature Tensor|Riemann Curvature Tensor]] *(from Riemannian Geometry)*
> Once the Levi-Civita connection is in hand, the **Riemann curvature tensor** $R(X, Y)Z := \nabla_X \nabla_Y Z - \nabla_Y \nabla_X Z - \nabla_{[X, Y]}Z$ measures the failure of $\nabla$ to commute; equivalently it is the obstruction to parallel transport being path-independent. Its symmetries (antisymmetry in first two slots, antisymmetry in last two, pair symmetry, first **Bianchi identity**) and contractions (sectional curvature, Ricci, scalar) are the content of [[Riemannian Geometry III — Riemann Curvature and Topology|Riemannian Geometry III]]; the curvature-to-topology theorems (Bonnet-Myers, Cartan-Hadamard, Synge, Gauss-Bonnet) sit on top of this.

- **[[Ex - Christoffel Symbols of the Round Metric on the Sphere]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute the nonzero Christoffel symbols of the round metric $g = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2$ on $S^2$ in spherical coordinates and verify they are consistent with great-circle geodesics.

- **[[Ex - Christoffel Symbols of the Hyperbolic Plane]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute the Christoffel symbols of the upper-half-plane hyperbolic metric $g = (dx^2 + dy^2)/y^2$ and verify the geodesics are vertical lines and circles centred on the $x$-axis.

- **[[Ex - The Levi-Civita Connection of Polar Coordinates]]** (⭐)
	- Compute the Christoffel symbols of the flat Euclidean metric $g = dr^2 + r^2 d\theta^2$ in polar coordinates on $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$, verify that the curvature 2-forms vanish, and contrast with the trivial connection in Cartesian coordinates.

## §1.3 Cartan's Structural Equations

- **[[Def - Connection 1-Forms (Cartan)]]**
	- Given a connection $\nabla$ on $TM$ and a local frame $e = (e_1, \ldots, e_n)$ with dual coframe $(\sigma^a)$, the **connection 1-forms** $\omega^a{}_b$ are the unique 1-forms determined by $\nabla e_b = e_a \otimes \omega^a{}_b$, equivalently $\nabla_X e_b = \omega^a{}_b(X)\, e_a$. The data of $\nabla$ in the frame $e$ is exactly the matrix-valued 1-form $\omega = (\omega^a{}_b)$. When the frame is **orthonormal** for a metric $g$, the connection 1-forms of the Levi-Civita connection are *antisymmetric*: $\omega^a{}_b + \omega^b{}_a = 0$ (after lowering indices with the standard form $\delta_{ab}$ or $\eta_{ab}$) — this is metric-compatibility in the frame language. When the frame is a coordinate frame $e_i = \partial_i$, the components of $\omega^k{}_j$ are the Christoffel symbols: $\omega^k{}_j = \Gamma^k_{ij}\,dx^i$.

- **[[Def - Curvature 2-Forms (Cartan)]]**
	- The **curvature 2-forms** of a connection in a frame $e$ are the entries of the matrix-valued 2-form $\Omega = d\omega + \omega \wedge \omega$, with components $\Omega^a{}_b = d\omega^a{}_b + \omega^a{}_c \wedge \omega^c{}_b$. Equivalently $\nabla\nabla e_b = e_a \otimes \Omega^a{}_b$, so $\Omega$ measures the failure of $\nabla$ to be a "second-order differential" — the obstruction to commuting two covariant derivatives. The components $\Omega^a{}_b = \tfrac{1}{2}R^a{}_{bcd}\,\sigma^c \wedge \sigma^d$ are exactly the components of the **Riemann curvature tensor**: the second structural equation is the most efficient computation of curvature in practice. Unlike the connection forms, the curvature forms *do* transform as a matrix of a linear transformation under change of frame: $\Omega' = g^{-1}\Omega g$, so they descend to a tensor.

- **[[Thm - Cartan's First Structural Equation]]**
	- For a connection on $TM$ with connection 1-forms $\omega^a{}_b$ in a frame $e$ and dual coframe $\sigma^a$, the exterior derivative of $\sigma^a$ is given by $d\sigma^a + \omega^a{}_b \wedge \sigma^b = \tau^a$, where $\tau^a = \tfrac{1}{2}T^a_{bc}\,\sigma^b \wedge \sigma^c$ are the torsion 2-forms. When the connection is torsion-free this becomes $d\sigma^a = -\omega^a{}_b \wedge \sigma^b$ — the elegant identity that, given a coframe, lets one compute the connection 1-forms purely from $d\sigma^a$ by demanding antisymmetry in an orthonormal frame.

- **[[Thm - Cartan's Second Structural Equation]]**
	- For a connection on any vector bundle with connection 1-forms $\omega^a{}_b$ in a frame, the curvature 2-forms are $\Omega^a{}_b = d\omega^a{}_b + \omega^a{}_c \wedge \omega^c{}_b$, equivalently $\Omega = d\omega + \omega \wedge \omega$. In an orthonormal frame for a Riemannian metric the structural equations become the fastest practical method for computing the Riemann curvature tensor: write down the coframe, compute $d\sigma^a$, read off $\omega^a{}_b$ from antisymmetry and the first structural equation, take $d\omega$ and $\omega \wedge \omega$, and read off $R^a{}_{bcd}$ from $\Omega^a{}_b$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - The Yang-Mills Field Strength|The Yang-Mills Field Strength]] *(from Gauge Theory)*
> Cartan's second structural equation $\Omega = d\omega + \omega \wedge \omega$ is the geometric prototype of the **Yang-Mills field strength** $F = dA + A \wedge A$, where $A$ is a Lie-algebra-valued 1-form on a principal $G$-bundle (the "gauge potential") and $F$ is its curvature (the "gauge field strength"). Maxwell's electromagnetism is the abelian case $G = U(1)$, where $A \wedge A = 0$ and $F = dA$. The **Bianchi identity** $d\Omega + \omega \wedge \Omega - \Omega \wedge \omega = 0$ is then the homogeneous Maxwell equation $dF = 0$, and the Yang-Mills equation $d\star F + \omega \wedge \star F - \star F \wedge \omega = 0$ is the inhomogeneous one. The full theory is the content of **Yang-Mills theory** and is developed in [[Gauge Theory VII — The Hodge Star, Electrodynamics, and Yang–Mills Theory|Gauge Theory VII]].

- **[[Ex - Cartan Structural Equations on S^2]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Using the orthonormal coframe $\sigma^1 = d\theta$, $\sigma^2 = \sin\theta\,d\varphi$ on $S^2$, derive the connection 1-form $\omega^1{}_2 = -\cos\theta\,d\varphi$ from Cartan's first structural equation, then compute the single nonzero curvature 2-form $\Omega^1{}_2 = \sin\theta\,d\theta \wedge d\varphi$ from the second structural equation, and read off the Gaussian curvature $K = 1$.

- **[[Ex - Computing Curvature 2-Forms in an Orthonormal Frame]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Set up an orthonormal frame for the Schwarzschild metric $g = -(1 - 2M/r)dt^2 + (1 - 2M/r)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2)$, compute the connection 1-forms from Cartan's first structural equation, and use the second structural equation to extract the curvature 2-forms — the route used in essentially every general relativity textbook to compute curvature efficiently.

## §1.4 Parallel Transport, Tensor Bundles, and Gauge Transformations

- **[[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Connection 1-Forms]]**
	- Under a change of frame $e' = e\, g$ (with $g : U \to \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ a smooth matrix-valued function on the overlap), the matrix $\Gamma$ of connection 1-forms transforms inhomogeneously: $\Gamma' = g^{-1} \Gamma g + g^{-1}\,dg$. The first term is the matrix-conjugation that a tensor would undergo; the second term is the inhomogeneous "$g^{-1}dg$" correction, and its presence is exactly why $\Gamma$ is not a tensor. The curvature 2-forms, by contrast, transform homogeneously: $\Omega' = g^{-1}\Omega\,g$, which is why they descend to a tensor (the Riemann tensor). In physics this is the **gauge transformation** of the connection viewed as a gauge potential, and $g$ is the gauge group element.

- **[[Def - Induced Connection on Tensor Bundles]]**
	- A connection $\nabla$ on $TM$ uniquely extends to a connection on every tensor bundle $T^{(r,s)}M$, by demanding $\nabla(X \otimes Y) = (\nabla X) \otimes Y + X \otimes (\nabla Y)$ (Leibniz over tensor product), $\nabla f = df$ for functions, and $\nabla$ commuting with contractions. For a 1-form $\alpha$ this gives $(\nabla_X \alpha)(Y) = X(\alpha(Y)) - \alpha(\nabla_X Y)$; for a $(0,2)$-tensor $T$ this gives $(\nabla_X T)(Y, Z) = X(T(Y, Z)) - T(\nabla_X Y, Z) - T(Y, \nabla_X Z)$. With this convention, **metric-compatibility** reads $\nabla g = 0$, which is the cleanest formulation. The Riemann tensor, the Ricci tensor, and the scalar curvature are all sections of tensor bundles and can be covariantly differentiated using this induced connection — the **second Bianchi identity** $\nabla_e R_{abcd} + \nabla_c R_{abde} + \nabla_d R_{abec} = 0$ is one of the central identities of Riemannian geometry.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Holonomy and the Ambrose-Singer Theorem *(from Differential Geometry / Riemannian Geometry)*
> The collection of parallel-transport-around-loops at a point $p$ — the maps $P_\gamma : T_pM \to T_pM$ for $\gamma$ a loop at $p$ — forms a subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}(T_pM)$ called the **holonomy group** $\mathrm{Hol}(p)$. For a Riemannian connection it is a subgroup of the orthogonal group $O(T_pM, g_p)$. The **Ambrose-Singer theorem** identifies the Lie algebra of $\mathrm{Hol}(p)$ as the span of all curvature-tensor values $R(X, Y) : T_qM \to T_qM$ where $q$ ranges over points reachable from $p$ by parallel transport and $X, Y$ over tangent vectors at $q$. This converts "global path-dependence" into "infinitesimal curvature" and is the gateway to **Berger's classification** of Riemannian holonomy [[Def - Group|groups]] (the holonomy is generically the full $O(n)$, but special structures like Kähler, Calabi-Yau, hyperkähler, $G_2$, $\mathrm{Spin}(7)$ correspond to reduced holonomy).

- **[[Ex - Parallel Transport around a Geodesic Triangle on the Sphere]]** (⭐⭐)
	- On the round 2-sphere, parallel-transport a tangent vector around the geodesic triangle with vertices at the north pole, $(\pi/2, 0)$, and $(\pi/2, \pi/2)$, and verify the transported vector returns rotated by exactly $\pi/2$ — the **holonomy angle** of the loop. Verify by a second method using the Gauss-Bonnet formula: the holonomy is $\int_\Sigma K\,dA =$ area of the triangle = $\pi/2$ on the unit sphere.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises and theorems of this topic chase a small recurring set of goals. The most common is to **compute the Christoffel symbols** of a given metric — usually in spherical, cylindrical, polar, hyperbolic, or warped-product coordinates — using either the explicit Christoffel formula $\Gamma^k_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{kl}(\partial_i g_{jl} + \partial_j g_{il} - \partial_l g_{ij})$ or Cartan's first structural equation in an orthonormal frame; the symbols then feed into the geodesic equation, the parallel-transport ODE, and the curvature formula. A second target is to **identify the Levi-Civita connection of a given manifold-with-metric** — for which the route is invariably "compute the metric, then read off Christoffel symbols or set up an orthonormal coframe and use the structural equations". A third is to **verify torsion-freeness or metric-compatibility** of a candidate connection; either is checked by direct manipulation of the defining identity, with the most common payoff being uniqueness via the Koszul formula. A fourth target is to **compute curvature**, either via the coordinate formula $R^l{}_{ijk} = \partial_i\Gamma^l_{jk} - \partial_j\Gamma^l_{ik} + \Gamma^l_{im}\Gamma^m_{jk} - \Gamma^l_{jm}\Gamma^m_{ik}$ or via Cartan's second structural equation $\Omega = d\omega + \omega \wedge \omega$ in an orthonormal frame — the latter is faster by an order of magnitude in practice. A fifth is **parallel-transport computations**: solve the parallel-transport ODE $\dot V^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\dot\gamma^i V^j = 0$ along a specific curve, often with the payoff being a holonomy calculation around a closed loop. Across all five the unifying observation is that *the Levi-Civita connection is uniquely determined by the metric* and the work of the chapter is in the bookkeeping of extracting it efficiently.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses fall into a few stereotyped patterns. **A Riemannian or semi-Riemannian metric is given**, either as an abstract $g$ or in coordinates as a symmetric positive-definite matrix function $g_{ij}(x)$; this is the most fundamental source, because by the [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Full Proof)|fundamental theorem]] it immediately delivers a unique connection. **An embedding into Euclidean space is given**, allowing the Levi-Civita connection to be computed as the tangential projection of the flat connection of $\mathbb{R}^N$ — this is the original Levi-Civita construction (1917) and is how Frankel introduces the subject. **A local frame is given**, which converts an abstract connection into a concrete matrix-valued 1-form $\Gamma$; an orthonormal frame is the most powerful choice because it converts metric-compatibility into the cleanest algebraic condition (antisymmetry of $\Gamma$). **A specific coordinate system is given**, allowing the Christoffel formula to be applied mechanically; for highly symmetric metrics (spherical, cylindrical, conformal) only a few $\Gamma$'s are nonzero. **A symmetry group acting by isometries is given**, allowing one to argue that the Levi-Civita connection is also invariant under the symmetries — this is the bi-invariant-metric machinery on Lie groups, where the Levi-Civita connection takes the elegant form $\nabla_X Y = \tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y]$ on left-invariant fields. The recurring move is to route a source to a target via the Koszul formula or its coordinate consequence the Christoffel formula: a metric routes through Koszul to the connection, the connection routes through the geodesic equation to geodesics, and through Cartan's second structural equation to curvature. The [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in the topic routes through. When stuck, scan and try each one. Everything is self-contained: a reader with no Riemannian-geometry background should be able to follow each operation from the description alone.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Compute Christoffel symbols from the metric.** Given the metric components $g_{ij}(x)$ in a chart, the [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita]] Christoffel symbols are $\Gamma^k_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{kl}(\partial_i g_{jl} + \partial_j g_{il} - \partial_l g_{ij})$. This is the workhorse formula — the entire concrete content of the connection is in these $n^2(n+1)/2$ functions (symmetric in $i, j$). *Trigger:* you have a metric in coordinates and want any connection-dependent quantity (geodesic equation, parallel transport, curvature). *Pattern:* compute the inverse metric $g^{kl}$ first, then take the three partial derivatives, then assemble.

2. **Set up an orthonormal coframe and use Cartan's first structural equation.** Choose a coframe $(\sigma^a)$ with $g = \delta_{ab}\sigma^a \otimes \sigma^b$ (or $\eta_{ab}$ for Lorentzian), compute $d\sigma^a$, then [[Thm - Cartan's First Structural Equation|read off]] the connection 1-forms $\omega^a{}_b$ by demanding $d\sigma^a + \omega^a{}_b \wedge \sigma^b = 0$ (torsion-free) together with antisymmetry $\omega^a{}_b + \omega^b{}_a = 0$ (metric-compatibility). *Trigger:* a metric with enough symmetry that an orthonormal frame is natural; especially common for diagonal metrics, warped products, and the Schwarzschild geometry. *Pattern:* this is often dramatically faster than the coordinate Christoffel formula, particularly for computing curvature.

3. **Apply Cartan's second structural equation to compute curvature.** Once the connection 1-forms $\omega^a{}_b$ are in hand, the curvature 2-forms are $\Omega^a{}_b = d\omega^a{}_b + \omega^a{}_c \wedge \omega^c{}_b$ ([[Thm - Cartan's Second Structural Equation]]). Reading $\Omega^a{}_b = \tfrac{1}{2}R^a{}_{bcd}\sigma^c \wedge \sigma^d$ then gives the components of the Riemann tensor in the frame. *Trigger:* you have $\omega$ and want $R$. *Pattern:* the $\omega \wedge \omega$ term is often the bottleneck — for orthonormal frames where many $\omega^a{}_b$ vanish, this term is short.

4. **Solve the parallel transport ODE along a curve.** Given a curve $\gamma(t)$ with $\dot\gamma^i$, parallel transport satisfies the linear ODE $\dot V^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}(\gamma(t))\,\dot\gamma^i V^j = 0$ with $V(0) = v$. *Trigger:* you want to compare tangent vectors at two points on a curve, or compute holonomy around a loop. *Pattern:* for symmetric backgrounds the ODE often decouples or has constant coefficients; for special curves (geodesics) the velocity itself $\dot\gamma$ is parallel.

5. **Use the Koszul formula to verify metric-compatibility + torsion-freeness.** The Koszul formula $2g(\nabla_X Y, Z) = X g(Y, Z) + Y g(X, Z) - Z g(X, Y) + g([X, Y], Z) - g([X, Z], Y) - g([Y, Z], X)$ ([[Thm - Koszul Formula]]) gives the Levi-Civita connection directly from $g$ and Lie brackets. *Trigger:* you want a coordinate-free expression for $\nabla_X Y$, or you want to verify uniqueness, or the manifold is a Lie group (Lie brackets are known explicitly).

6. **Extend the connection to tensor bundles by Leibniz.** Given $\nabla$ on $TM$, the [[Def - Induced Connection on Tensor Bundles|induced connection]] on any $T^{(r,s)}M$ is determined by the rules $\nabla f = df$, $\nabla$ acts on $TM$ as given, $\nabla$ commutes with contractions, and $\nabla$ is a Leibniz derivation over tensor products. *Trigger:* you need to differentiate a covector field, a tensor, or a differential form. *Pattern:* for a 1-form, $(\nabla_X \alpha)(Y) = X(\alpha(Y)) - \alpha(\nabla_X Y)$ — the "consequence of $\nabla$ commuting with the contraction $\langle \alpha, Y\rangle$".

7. **Change frame and transform the connection via the gauge law.** Under $e' = eg$, the connection matrix transforms as $\Gamma' = g^{-1}\Gamma g + g^{-1}dg$ ([[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Connection 1-Forms]]); the curvature matrix transforms homogeneously $\Omega' = g^{-1}\Omega g$. *Trigger:* you have $\Gamma$ in one frame (e.g., coordinate) and want it in another (e.g., orthonormal), or vice versa. *Pattern:* if $\Gamma = 0$ in one frame the connection is *flat* (in that frame); but $\Gamma' = g^{-1}dg$ in another frame will be nonzero — flatness is not the same as $\Gamma = 0$.

8. **Identify the connection on a submanifold as the tangential projection.** For an embedded submanifold $S \subseteq M$ with induced metric, the Levi-Civita connection of $S$ is the tangential projection of the Levi-Civita connection of $M$: $\nabla^S_X Y = (\nabla^M_X Y)^\top$. *Trigger:* the manifold is given as a submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^N$ or of another Riemannian manifold. *Pattern:* compute $\nabla^M$ first (often it is the flat connection of $\mathbb{R}^N$), then take the tangential projection at each point.

9. **Use metric-compatibility to differentiate inner products freely.** For metric-compatible $\nabla$, $X\,g(Y, Z) = g(\nabla_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla_X Z)$ — the inner product satisfies a Leibniz rule. *Trigger:* a problem involves the derivative of a length or inner product along a curve. *Pattern:* this is how "geodesics have constant speed" is proved: $\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma = 0$ implies $\dot\gamma\,g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) = 2g(\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) = 0$, so $|\dot\gamma|^2$ is constant.

10. **Use torsion-freeness to convert antisymmetric covariant derivatives into Lie brackets.** $\nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X = [X, Y]$ ([[Def - Torsion Tensor|torsion-free condition]]). *Trigger:* you have a $\nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X$ in a formula and want to replace it with something computable from the smooth structure alone. *Pattern:* this is essential in deriving the second Bianchi identity, in working with Killing fields, and in the proof of the Koszul formula itself.

11. **Recognise a connection on a vector bundle as more general than a connection on $TM$.** The same abstract definition ([[Def - Affine Connection on a Vector Bundle]]) covers connections on the tangent bundle, the cotangent bundle, tensor bundles, the spinor bundle, complex line bundles, principal bundles (with modifications), and any associated bundle. *Trigger:* the geometric object you want to differentiate lives in a bundle other than $TM$ — for instance, a spinor field, an electromagnetic potential viewed as a $U(1)$-connection, or a stress tensor on a deformable body. *Pattern:* the Christoffel symbols are replaced by other "connection coefficients" with the same role; on a $U(1)$-bundle, $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ is replaced by the electromagnetic vector potential $A_\mu$.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating Christoffel symbols as tensor components
> The Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ have three indices and look like a $(1,2)$-tensor, and it is tempting to manipulate them as such — to raise and lower indices freely, to compute "tensor contractions" of them, to take their absolute value pointwise. They are **not** a tensor. Under change of coordinates they transform with an inhomogeneous second-derivative term: $\Gamma'{}^k_{ij} = \frac{\partial x'^k}{\partial x^p}\frac{\partial x^q}{\partial x'^i}\frac{\partial x^r}{\partial x'^j}\Gamma^p_{qr} + \frac{\partial x'^k}{\partial x^p}\frac{\partial^2 x^p}{\partial x'^i \partial x'^j}$, and the inhomogeneous piece destroys tensorial behaviour. Concretely: on $\mathbb{R}^2$ in Cartesian coordinates all $\Gamma^k_{ij} = 0$, but in polar coordinates $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r$ and $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = 1/r$ — a tensor that is zero in one coordinate system cannot become nonzero in another. The operation becomes legal exactly when the inhomogeneous piece vanishes, which happens for *linear* changes of coordinates (constant Jacobian).

> [!warning] 2. Assuming "the partial derivative is the covariant derivative" in any chart
> In a coordinate chart, it is tempting to identify $\partial_i V^j$ with $(\nabla_i V)^j$. This is wrong in general: $(\nabla_i V)^j = \partial_i V^j + \Gamma^j_{ik} V^k$, and the Christoffel correction is essential. The naive identification is correct *only* at a point in **normal coordinates** (where all $\Gamma^k_{ij}(p) = 0$ at the basepoint $p$), and even there it fails for second derivatives. The operation is legal in Cartesian coordinates on $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the Euclidean metric — and only then.

> [!warning] 3. Confusing the Lie derivative with the covariant derivative
> Both $\mathcal{L}_X Y$ and $\nabla_X Y$ produce vector fields, both are derivations, and both reduce to the partial derivative in some special cases. They are not the same: $\mathcal{L}_X Y = [X, Y]$ depends only on the smooth structure (no connection needed), is *not* $C^\infty(M)$-linear in $X$ (it depends on derivatives of $X$ too: $\mathcal{L}_{fX}Y = f\mathcal{L}_X Y - Y(f)X$), and is always antisymmetric in $X, Y$. The covariant derivative $\nabla_X Y$ requires a connection, *is* $C^\infty(M)$-linear in $X$, and is not antisymmetric in $X, Y$ — its antisymmetric part is the torsion plus $[X, Y]$. The trigger to remember the distinction: if your formula has no metric in sight, you probably want the Lie derivative; if you have a metric and care about "constant velocity along a curve", you want the covariant derivative.

> [!warning] 4. Forgetting that parallel transport depends on the path
> It is tempting to think of parallel transport as a way to canonically compare tangent vectors at $p$ and $q$ — to write "the parallel transport of $v$ from $p$ to $q$" as if it were a well-defined map $T_pM \to T_qM$. This is wrong in general: parallel transport depends on the *path*, and along two different curves from $p$ to $q$ the resulting maps usually differ. The difference around a small loop is exactly what is measured by the **curvature tensor**: the loop $\gamma$ bounding a parallelogram spanned by $X, Y$ at $p$ gives $P_\gamma v - v \approx -R(X, Y)v$ to leading order. The operation "parallel-transport from $p$ to $q$" is well-defined only when the connection is **flat** (curvature zero) on a simply-connected region, and even then path-independence requires simple connectivity.

> [!warning] 5. Assuming every connection is metric-compatible
> If the manifold is Riemannian, it is tempting to assume the only connection in play is the Levi-Civita one — and consequently that metric-compatibility, isometric parallel transport, and the Koszul formula all hold by default. They hold for *that* connection, not for all connections. On any Riemannian manifold there are infinitely many connections, of which exactly one is the Levi-Civita connection. The **Weitzenböck connection** on a Lie group (parallelise by left-invariant fields) has zero curvature but nonzero torsion and is not metric-compatible with a generic left-invariant metric. The fix is to be explicit: when working with a non-Levi-Civita connection, always state which it is and which properties (compatibility, torsion-freeness, flatness, ...) you assume.

> [!warning] 6. Confusing "torsion-free" with "flat"
> Torsion measures $\nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X - [X, Y]$; curvature measures $\nabla_X \nabla_Y - \nabla_Y \nabla_X - \nabla_{[X,Y]}$. These are entirely independent quantities — a connection can have any combination of {zero, nonzero} torsion and {zero, nonzero} curvature. The flat connection on $\mathbb{R}^n$ has both zero; the Levi-Civita connection on $S^2$ has zero torsion and nonzero curvature; the Weitzenböck connection on $S^3$ (via the trivialisation by [[Def - Quaternions|quaternions]]) has zero curvature and nonzero torsion; the Cartan-Schouten connections on a general Lie group can have both nonzero. Confusing torsion-freeness with flatness is the most common conceptual error in a first course; the trigger to disentangle is "does the failure of $\nabla$ to commute matter, or does the failure of $\nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X$ to equal $[X, Y]$?"

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic split into two main routes — **compute the connection from a given metric** and **use the connection to compute something else (geodesic, parallel transport, curvature)** — and almost all of them route through the [[Def - Christoffel Symbols|Christoffel symbols]] in some form.

If the problem **asks for the Christoffel symbols of a given metric**, the route is mechanical and almost always goes one of two ways. The straightforward way is to apply the Christoffel formula $\Gamma^k_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{kl}(\partial_i g_{jl} + \partial_j g_{il} - \partial_l g_{ij})$ directly: compute the inverse metric $g^{kl}$, take the partial derivatives, sum. This is fine for small [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]] and simple metrics. The much faster way, when the metric is diagonal with enough symmetry, is to set up an orthonormal coframe and use Cartan's first structural equation: write $g = \delta_{ab}\sigma^a \otimes \sigma^b$ for orthonormal $\sigma^a$, compute $d\sigma^a$, then solve $d\sigma^a + \omega^a{}_b \wedge \sigma^b = 0$ for $\omega^a{}_b$ subject to antisymmetry $\omega^a{}_b + \omega^b{}_a = 0$. This skips the inverse-metric computation and the index gymnastics. For the round sphere, this single move replaces a tedious 3-page coordinate calculation with three lines; see [[Ex - Cartan Structural Equations on S^2]].

If the problem **asks for geodesics**, the route runs through the geodesic equation $\ddot\gamma^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\dot\gamma^i\dot\gamma^j = 0$, which is a second-order ODE system in the coordinates of $\gamma$. The standard approach: compute the Christoffel symbols, write down the geodesic equation, then either solve it directly (in highly symmetric cases) or use conservation laws to reduce the order. **Killing fields** give conserved quantities along geodesics ($g(\dot\gamma, K)$ is constant for any Killing field $K$), and the metric itself gives $g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma) =$ constant via metric-compatibility. For spherically symmetric metrics, conservation of energy and angular momentum reduce the geodesic problem to a one-dimensional effective-potential problem — the route used for computing perihelion precession in the Schwarzschild geometry and for the radial geodesics of de Sitter space.

If the problem **asks for parallel transport along a specific curve**, the route is solving the linear first-order ODE $\dot V^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}(\gamma(t))\,\dot\gamma^i V^j = 0$ with the given initial condition. This is always solvable in principle (linear ODEs always exist on the maximal interval), but is often a nasty calculation in practice. For geodesics the velocity itself is parallel, so the only nontrivial computation is for the *orthogonal* directions. For closed loops the result is a **holonomy** transformation, and the most efficient route is often to use the Gauss-Bonnet relation between holonomy and integrated curvature: on a 2-dimensional Riemannian manifold, the holonomy angle around a loop bounding a region $D$ is $\int_D K\,dA + \int_{\partial D}\kappa_g\,ds$ for $K$ the Gaussian curvature and $\kappa_g$ the geodesic curvature of the boundary. See [[Ex - Parallel Transport around a Geodesic Triangle on the Sphere]].

If the problem **asks for the Riemann curvature tensor**, *always* try Cartan's second structural equation first: it is faster than the coordinate formula $R^l{}_{ijk} = \partial_i\Gamma^l_{jk} - \partial_j\Gamma^l_{ik} + \Gamma^l_{im}\Gamma^m_{jk} - \Gamma^l_{jm}\Gamma^m_{ik}$ by a substantial factor, and it scales much better with dimension. The standard pattern is: (i) write down the metric and a natural orthonormal coframe $\sigma^a$; (ii) compute $d\sigma^a$ and use Cartan's first equation to read off $\omega^a{}_b$ from the antisymmetry condition; (iii) compute $d\omega^a{}_b$ and $\omega^a{}_c \wedge \omega^c{}_b$; (iv) sum to get $\Omega^a{}_b$; (v) read off $R^a{}_{bcd}$ from $\Omega^a{}_b = \tfrac{1}{2}R^a{}_{bcd}\sigma^c \wedge \sigma^d$. This is the route in every general relativity textbook for computing the Schwarzschild, Kerr, FRW, and de Sitter curvatures.

If the problem **involves a Lie group and bi-invariant metric**, the entire problem usually collapses via the formula $\nabla_X Y = \tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y]$ for left-invariant fields. This is a special structural shortcut: it says the connection is "half the Lie bracket" on left-invariant fields, geodesics through the identity are one-parameter [[Def - Subgroup|subgroups]], and the Riemannian exponential map coincides with the Lie-group exponential of [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|DG XI]]. See [[Ex - The Tangent Bundle of a Lie Group has a Canonical Flat Connection]].

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle: **the metric determines the connection uniquely (Levi-Civita), the connection determines parallel transport, parallel transport determines geodesics and curvature, and curvature controls the global geometry**. Every question in this chapter is some version of "given $g$, extract $\nabla$, and from $\nabla$ extract the geometric quantity you need". The hard work is in step one (Christoffel symbols or connection 1-forms), and the rest is bookkeeping.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Full Proof)|The Fundamental Theorem]]**: every Riemannian (or semi-Riemannian) manifold has a unique torsion-free metric-compatible connection. **Typical use:** this is the licence to say "the Levi-Civita connection" — without it, every connection-dependent quantity (geodesic, parallel transport, curvature) would depend on an arbitrary choice. The uniqueness is what makes the entire subject canonical. The proof technique — symmetrise three permutations of metric-compatibility and use torsion-freeness to eliminate cross terms — is reusable for any "two conditions overdetermine an object just enough" argument; the *first Bianchi identity* in [[Riemannian Geometry III — Riemann Curvature and Topology|Riemannian Geometry III]] is proved by exactly the same symmetrisation move.

- **[[Thm - Koszul Formula|The Koszul Formula]]**: $2g(\nabla_X Y, Z) = X g(Y, Z) + Y g(X, Z) - Z g(X, Y) + g([X, Y], Z) - g([X, Z], Y) - g([Y, Z], X)$. **Typical use:** this is the master formula for computing $\nabla_X Y$ when a manifold-intrinsic (not coordinate-based) description is needed — particularly on Lie groups where Lie brackets are known explicitly. It also delivers a coordinate-free proof that the Levi-Civita connection on a bi-invariant Riemannian Lie group is $\nabla_X Y = \tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y]$, and it is the structural input to the proof that geodesics through the identity of a bi-invariant Lie group are one-parameter subgroups.

- **[[Thm - Cartan's Second Structural Equation|Cartan's structural equations]]**: $d\sigma^a + \omega^a{}_b \wedge \sigma^b = 0$ (torsion-free), $\Omega^a{}_b = d\omega^a{}_b + \omega^a{}_c \wedge \omega^c{}_b$. **Typical use:** the most efficient practical method for computing the Levi-Civita connection and the Riemann curvature tensor of a metric, by a wide margin. Every concrete curvature calculation in differential geometry, general relativity, and gauge theory routes through these equations in an orthonormal frame. The pattern generalises: in [[Gauge Theory — Series Map|gauge theory]] the same equations with $G$-valued connection 1-forms encode the **Yang-Mills field strength** $F = dA + A \wedge A$ and the corresponding Bianchi identity $dF + [A, F] = 0$.

- **[[Def - Parallel Transport|Parallel transport as a linear isomorphism]]**: along any curve $\gamma$ from $p$ to $q$, the connection defines a linear isomorphism $P_\gamma : T_pM \to T_qM$, and for metric-compatible connections it is a linear isometry. **Typical use:** this is the formal mechanism for comparing tangent vectors at different points — without it, "the velocity vector is constant along the curve" has no meaning. The path-dependence of $P_\gamma$ is the geometric content of curvature: when $\nabla$ is flat and the region is simply connected, $P_\gamma$ depends only on the endpoints (this is essentially Frobenius integrability for the horizontal distribution).

- **[[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Connection 1-Forms|The gauge-transformation law]]** $\Gamma' = g^{-1}\Gamma g + g^{-1}dg$. **Typical use:** the inhomogeneous transformation rule explains why $\Gamma$ is not a tensor and why "zero connection in one frame" does not mean "zero connection in another frame". In physics it is the bridge between the abstract geometric definition of a connection and the working definition of a **gauge potential** $A_\mu$ — a gauge potential is just a connection 1-form on a $U(1)$ (electromagnetism) or non-abelian (Yang-Mills) principal bundle, and a **gauge transformation** is just a change of trivialisation. The pattern is the same in differential geometry and in particle physics: the connection 1-forms transform with a $g^{-1}dg$ correction, the curvature 2-forms transform homogeneously $\Omega' = g^{-1}\Omega g$.

# Bridges

1. **Multivariate analysis — the covariant derivative of a vector field is the directional derivative corrected by a Christoffel term.** On $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the Euclidean metric and Cartesian coordinates, the Christoffel symbols are all zero, so the covariant derivative is just the ordinary directional derivative: $\nabla_X Y$ is the vector whose components are $X^i \partial_i Y^k$. This is what makes the Euclidean calculus of [[Multivariate Analysis I — Differentiation in Several Variables|multivariate analysis]] work: implicitly, every "directional derivative" in vector calculus is a covariant derivative for the flat connection. Once one moves to a non-Cartesian coordinate system (polar, spherical, cylindrical) the Christoffel symbols become nonzero and one must add the correction — this is the origin of the unfamiliar terms in the gradient, divergence, and Laplacian in non-Cartesian coordinates. The lesson: every formula in vector calculus that one learns "in coordinates" is the coordinate expression of a connection-theoretic identity, with the Christoffel symbols absorbed into the formula.

2. **Gauge theory — vector-bundle connections generalise to principal-bundle connections, and electromagnetism is the abelian case.** A vector-bundle connection $\nabla$ on $E \to M$ specifies how to differentiate sections of $E$; equivalently it specifies a "horizontal distribution" in the total space of $E$. In [[Gauge Theory — Series Map|gauge theory]], the same idea is applied to principal $G$-bundles $P \to M$ for a Lie group $G$: a connection is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form $A$ on $P$ that is $G$-equivariant and reduces to the Maurer-Cartan form on fibres. When $G = \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ and $P$ is the frame bundle of $M$, this principal connection is equivalent to a vector-bundle connection on $TM$. When $G = U(1)$ the connection becomes the **electromagnetic vector potential** $A_\mu$, and the curvature 2-form is the **electromagnetic field-strength tensor** $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$. When $G = SU(N)$ the connection is the **Yang-Mills gauge field** of the strong and electroweak interactions. The Levi-Civita connection on a Riemannian manifold is the $G = O(n)$ case for the orthonormal-frame bundle, with metric-compatibility being the reduction from $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ to $O(n)$ and torsion-freeness being the additional constraint that picks out one connection. The conceptual takeaway: differential geometry and gauge theory are two faces of the same connection theory, and the **Bianchi identity** $\nabla F = 0$ (in either formulation) is the prototype of the homogeneous Maxwell equation $dF = 0$.

3. **Algebraic topology — the Chern-Weil theory of characteristic classes is the global integration of curvature.** A connection $\nabla$ on a complex vector bundle $E \to M$ has a curvature 2-form $\Omega$ taking values in $\mathrm{End}(E)$. The Chern-Weil theorem says that the trace $\mathrm{tr}\,\Omega$, the Pfaffian, and other invariant polynomials of $\Omega$ are closed differential forms, and their de Rham cohomology classes are *independent of the choice of connection* — they are topological invariants of $E$ called **characteristic classes** (Chern classes for complex bundles, Pontryagin classes for real bundles, Euler class for oriented bundles). This is how curvature integrates into topology: the integrals $\int_M c_k(E)$ are integers, the **Chern numbers**, and they count things like the topological charge of an instanton. The **Gauss-Bonnet theorem** on a closed surface — $\int_M K\,dA = 2\pi\chi(M)$ — is the simplest Chern-Weil identity, with $K$ the Gaussian curvature (the trace of the curvature 2-form of the Levi-Civita connection in an orthonormal frame) and $\chi$ the Euler characteristic. The full development belongs to [[Algebraic Topology III — Higher Homotopy and Chern Forms]] and to **Chern-Weil theory** proper.

4. **Riemannian geometry of geodesics — the connection delivers the geodesic equation, which is the variational characterisation of "shortest distance".** A **geodesic** is a curve whose velocity is parallel-transported along itself: $\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma = 0$. By the equivalent variational characterisation, this is the same as being a critical point of the energy functional $E(\gamma) = \tfrac{1}{2}\int g(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)\,dt$. The bridge from "connection" to "shortest distance" is that the Levi-Civita connection's geodesics are precisely the curves that locally minimise length (up to reparametrisation) — this is the **first variation formula**. The full development — the exponential map, normal coordinates, completeness, Hopf-Rinow, Jacobi fields, the second variation, the index form — is the content of [[Riemannian Geometry II — Geodesics, the Exponential Map, and Variational Principles]]. The conceptual takeaway: the connection is the data that turns "parallel transport" into a well-defined operation, and geodesics are the curves along which "parallel transport of the velocity" is automatic.

5. **General relativity — the Levi-Civita connection of a Lorentzian metric is the gravitational field.** In Einstein's theory of gravitation, spacetime is a 4-dimensional Lorentzian manifold $(M, g)$. The Levi-Civita connection of $g$ is the "gravitational connection": its geodesics are the worldlines of free-falling test particles (timelike for massive, null for light), and its Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}$ are the components of the **gravitational field** in the sense that the geodesic equation $\ddot x^\lambda + \Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu \dot x^\nu = 0$ is Newton's second law for a particle in a gravitational field. The curvature tensor of the Levi-Civita connection is what enters the **Einstein field equations** $R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac{1}{2}R\,g_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$: the Ricci tensor $R_{\mu\nu}$ is a contraction of the Riemann tensor of $\nabla$, and this equation says the curvature of $\nabla$ is sourced by the energy-momentum of matter. The full development belongs to [[General Relativity I — Einstein's Equations and Schwarzschild]]; what this chapter establishes is the connection that makes that theory rigorous.

6. **Classical differential geometry of surfaces — the Levi-Civita connection on a surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the tangential projection of the flat connection.** For an embedded surface $S^2 \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^3$ with the induced metric, the Levi-Civita connection of $S$ is computed by taking the ordinary $\mathbb{R}^3$-derivative of a tangent vector field along the surface, then projecting orthogonally onto $T_p S$. This is **Levi-Civita's original construction** (1917) and it makes the abstract definition concrete: the covariant derivative is "the directional derivative minus its normal part". The normal part is what carries the **second fundamental form** $II(X, Y) = (\nabla^{\mathbb{R}^3}_X Y)^\perp$, and the **Gauss equation** then relates the curvature of the surface to its second fundamental form. The full theory — first fundamental form, second fundamental form, principal curvatures, Gauss and mean curvatures, Theorema Egregium, Gauss-Bonnet for surfaces — is the content of [[Riemannian Geometry IV — Classical Geometry of Surfaces in R^3]]. The conceptual takeaway: the intrinsic Levi-Civita connection of a submanifold is the orthogonal projection of the ambient connection.

7. **Information geometry — the Fisher information metric on a statistical manifold has a canonical pair of connections.** A parametric family of probability distributions $\{p(x; \theta)\}$ forms a statistical manifold whose Riemannian metric is the [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds#Bridges|Fisher information metric]] $g_{ij}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\partial_i \log p \cdot \partial_j \log p]$. The Levi-Civita connection of this metric is one of an infinite family of "$\alpha$-connections" indexed by $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$; the two distinguished members are the **e-connection** ($\alpha = +1$, exponential families are flat) and the **m-connection** ($\alpha = -1$, mixture families are flat), and they are *dual* in the sense that parallel transport by one preserves the metric pairing with parallel transport by the other. This **dually flat** structure is the substrate of Amari's information geometry, and the asymmetry of the two connections (each is flat but not metric-compatible; the symmetric Levi-Civita is metric-compatible but generally not flat) is the geometric content of the asymmetry between "natural parameters" and "expectation parameters" in exponential families.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a connection is the data needed to compare tangent vectors at different points.** A smooth manifold $M$ provides tangent spaces $T_pM$ at each point, but no canonical way to relate $T_pM$ to $T_qM$. A connection $\nabla$ is exactly the additional structure that provides this: through parallel transport along curves. Every operation in connection theory is in some way about this comparison problem. Covariant differentiation compares the value of a vector field at neighbouring points (after parallel-transporting the second back to the first). The covariant derivative along a curve compares values at successive points along the curve. Curvature measures the *path-dependence* of the comparison — what happens when you transport around a loop and compare with where you started. Holonomy is the cumulative effect of curvature integrated over loops. Once one sees the chapter as the systematic resolution of "how do we compare tangent vectors at different points", every concept fits into place: the connection provides the comparison; metric-compatibility makes it an isometry; torsion-freeness makes it symmetric; curvature measures its path-dependence; geodesics are the curves where comparison is automatic.

**The true name of the Levi-Civita connection is "the unique connection for which parallel transport is an isometry and infinitesimal parallelograms close".** The formal definition — "torsion-free, metric-compatible" — is the right thing to verify but the wrong thing to think. The two conditions have clean geometric meanings: metric-compatibility says parallel transport preserves lengths and angles (so "constant speed along a curve" makes sense); torsion-freeness says that if you parallel-transport an infinitesimal vector $X$ along $Y$ and an infinitesimal $Y$ along $X$, the resulting two displaced points coincide (the parallelogram closes). The remarkable structural fact is that these two innocuous-looking conditions, together with the metric, pin down *exactly one* connection. There are torsion-free connections that are not metric-compatible, and metric-compatible connections that are not torsion-free; the conjunction is what selects the canonical choice. A smaller condition would be underdetermined, a larger one inconsistent. This is the algebraic miracle at the heart of Riemannian geometry, and it is what makes the subject canonical and unambiguous.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: "want curvature on a Riemannian manifold" → "set up an orthonormal coframe and apply Cartan's structural equations".** The most-used computational technique in all of differential geometry and general relativity: instead of computing Christoffel symbols and then $R^l{}_{ijk}$ from the coordinate formula, set up an orthonormal coframe $\sigma^a$, compute $d\sigma^a$, read off the connection 1-forms $\omega^a{}_b$ from $d\sigma^a + \omega^a{}_b \wedge \sigma^b = 0$ together with antisymmetry, then compute $\Omega^a{}_b = d\omega^a{}_b + \omega^a{}_c \wedge \omega^c{}_b$. This route is dramatically faster than the coordinate calculation for any nontrivial metric — see [[Ex - Cartan Structural Equations on S^2]] for a three-line computation that would take a page in coordinates, and [[Ex - Computing Curvature 2-Forms in an Orthonormal Frame]] for the standard Schwarzschild calculation that every general-relativity student does this way. The trigger is "I want curvature"; the reaction is "set up an orthonormal frame". Once internalised, this becomes muscle memory.

**Inheritance — where does the connection come from?** When confronted with a manifold-with-connection, ask: where did the connection come from? The answer is almost always one of a small list. (i) It is the Levi-Civita connection of a Riemannian or Lorentzian metric; this is the most common case, and the metric determines the connection by the fundamental theorem. (ii) It is the tangential projection of the flat connection on an ambient Euclidean space; this is how the Levi-Civita connection of a submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^N$ is computed concretely, and it is the original Levi-Civita construction. (iii) It is a "trivial" connection on a parallelizable manifold (a Lie group), with all left-invariant fields parallel; this is the **Weitzenböck connection**, flat but with torsion. (iv) It is a $G$-equivariant connection on an associated bundle, coming from a principal-bundle connection in gauge theory; the Christoffel symbols are replaced by the gauge potential. (v) It is a connection induced from another by extension to a tensor bundle, complexification, or restriction to a subbundle. In every case, recognising the origin gives you the entire structure for free — its symmetries, its compatibility properties, its curvature behaviour. The reusable lesson: never accept "we have a connection" without asking where it comes from.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: "see $\Gamma' = g^{-1}\Gamma g + g^{-1}dg$" → "think gauge transformation, think non-tensorial connection".** The inhomogeneous transformation law is the diagnostic for "$\Gamma$ is a connection, not a tensor". Whenever you see a $g^{-1}dg$-type term in a transformation law, you are looking at the connection-theoretic gauge transformation. The same law governs the electromagnetic vector potential under a gauge transformation $\psi \to e^{i\chi}\psi$ ($A_\mu \to A_\mu + \partial_\mu \chi$), the Yang-Mills gauge potential ($A \to g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$), and the Christoffel symbols under change of coordinates. The reaction: do not try to manipulate $\Gamma$ as a tensor; instead, work with quantities that *are* tensors (curvature $\Omega$, torsion $T$, covariant differences $\nabla_X Y$) — or fix a frame and work in coordinates without ever transforming.

**A unifying frame for the entire chapter: the geometry is in the curvature, not in the connection.** The connection itself is non-tensorial gauge-dependent data — different choices of frame give different $\Gamma$'s. The geometry — the genuinely coordinate-invariant, intrinsic structure — lives in the curvature: $\Omega$ transforms homogeneously, $R(X, Y)Z$ is a genuine tensor, and the global geometry of $(M, g)$ is encoded in the symmetries and integrals of curvature (sectional, Ricci, scalar; Gauss-Bonnet, Chern numbers, Atiyah-Singer index). The connection is the bookkeeping; curvature is the content. This is the same conceptual move as in gauge theory: the gauge potential $A$ is gauge-dependent and unphysical (only field configurations modulo gauge are physical), while the field strength $F = dA + A \wedge A$ is gauge-covariant and is what couples to physical observables. The conceptual takeaway: in any connection-theoretic problem, identify the gauge-invariant content (curvature, holonomy, Chern numbers) and work with that — not with the gauge-dependent connection data.
