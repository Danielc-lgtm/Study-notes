# Notation

$(M, g)$ a Riemannian manifold with cotangent bundle $T^*M$ and projection $\pi : T^*M \to M$. Local coordinates $(q^1, \ldots, q^n)$ on $M$ induce coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$ on $T^*M$, where a covector $\alpha \in T^*_qM$ is written $\alpha = p_i\, dq^i$. The canonical 1-form is $\theta = p_i\, dq^i$ (well-defined globally), and the canonical symplectic form is $\omega = -d\theta = dq^i \wedge dp_i$. The Riemannian metric $g$ gives the inverse metric $g^{ij}$ on cotangent vectors via $g^{ij} g_{jk} = \delta^i_k$. Vector field $X_H$ for a Hamiltonian $H : T^*M \to \mathbb{R}$ is defined by $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$. Full registry on [[Riemannian Geometry II — Geodesics, the Exponential Map, and Variational Principles]].

# Axiom Motivation

The motivating question is: **what is the cleanest Hamiltonian formulation of geodesic flow, and what is gained by passing from $TM$ to $T^*M$?** Geodesic flow on $TM$ — the flow of the geodesic vector field generated by the geodesic ODE — is already a perfectly good dynamical system. The Hamiltonian formulation on $T^*M$ adds something new: the *symplectic* structure, with all its computational and conceptual benefits.

The natural Lagrangian on $TM$ is $L(q, \dot q) = \tfrac12 g_{ij}(q) \dot q^i \dot q^j$ — the kinetic energy of a unit-mass particle. Its Euler–Lagrange equations are the geodesic equation (see [[Thm - Hamilton's Principle Gives the Geodesic Equation]]). To Hamilton-ise it, perform the **Legendre transform**: set
$$p_i := \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i} = g_{ij}(q)\, \dot q^j.$$
This is the **momentum** conjugate to the position $q^i$. The map $\dot q \mapsto p$ is a linear isomorphism $T_qM \to T_q^*M$ for each $q$ (it is the lowering-of-indices map of the metric, $\flat$), so it is a global [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]] $TM \to T^*M$.

The Hamiltonian is obtained from the Lagrangian by the Legendre dual:
$$H(q, p) = p_i \dot q^i - L(q, \dot q) = g_{ij}\dot q^i \dot q^j - \tfrac12 g_{ij}\dot q^i \dot q^j = \tfrac12 g_{ij}\dot q^i \dot q^j = \tfrac12 g^{ij}p_i p_j,$$
where the last equality uses $\dot q^j = g^{ji}p_i$. So **the Hamiltonian is half the squared metric-norm of the momentum**:
$$H(q, p) = \frac{1}{2} g^{ij}(q)\, p_i\, p_j = \frac{1}{2}|p|^2_{g^{-1}}.$$
This is the most elegant expression possible: the Hamiltonian is the kinetic energy, written using the inverse metric on cotangent vectors. The entire geodesic equation is encoded in this single function on $T^*M$.

Hamilton's equations are then
$$\dot q^i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} = g^{ij}\, p_j, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i} = -\tfrac12 \partial_i g^{jk}\, p_j\, p_k.$$
Substituting into each other, these recover the geodesic equation $\ddot q^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\dot q^i \dot q^j = 0$. So Hamiltonian flow of $H$ on $T^*M$ is geodesic flow.

**Why is the cotangent-bundle formulation worth the extra abstraction?** Three reasons.

First, **symplectic invariants**. $T^*M$ carries the canonical symplectic form $\omega = dq^i \wedge dp_i$, intrinsic and independent of coordinates. Hamiltonian flow preserves $\omega$ (Liouville's theorem). This means the flow preserves the symplectic volume $\omega^n / n!$ — a strong conservation law that has no clean expression in the $TM$ picture. It also means that flows of *any* Hamiltonian, not just $H = \tfrac12 |p|^2$, are symplectic; the framework generalises to mechanics with potentials, magnetic fields, gauge connections, and beyond.

Second, **Poisson brackets**. The bracket $\{F, G\} := \omega(X_F, X_G) = \frac{\partial F}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial q^i} - \frac{\partial F}{\partial q^i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i}$ measures the rate of change of $G$ along the flow of $F$ (and vice versa, with a sign). Conserved quantities along geodesic flow are exactly functions $F$ with $\{F, H\} = 0$. This converts conservation laws into an algebraic problem on $C^\infty(T^*M)$, which is the foundation of Noether's theorem in the Hamiltonian framework.

Third, **integrability and KAM theory**. Action–angle coordinates exist exactly when the Hamiltonian system has enough commuting conserved quantities ($n$ functions in involution on a $2n$-dimensional phase space). The existence of these coordinates is the question of *integrability*. For geodesic flow, integrability is a special property — the round sphere is integrable (rotational symmetries provide enough Killing vectors), Schwarzschild is integrable (4 conserved quantities), but a generic compact manifold is *not* integrable. The Hamiltonian framework on $T^*M$ is the natural setting for these questions.

A subtler design choice is **whether to use $H = \tfrac12 |p|^2$ or $H = |p|$**. The latter is also a Hamiltonian whose flow on the unit cotangent bundle is "the same" as geodesic flow up to reparametrisation; it is the **geodesic Hamiltonian** in the sub-Riemannian-geometry convention. The quadratic form $\tfrac12 |p|^2$ is smoother (the linear form $|p|$ has a singularity at $p = 0$) and gives a flow that respects the structure of $TM$ as a vector bundle, parametrising [[Def - Geodesic|geodesics]] naturally at constant speed. The choice $\tfrac12 |p|^2$ is standard.

The **conserved energy**. Since $H$ does not depend on $t$ explicitly, $H$ is conserved along the flow: $H(q(t), p(t)) = \mathrm{const}$. This is the **energy conservation** law, and on the level set $\{H = E\}$ the flow is the time-rescaled geodesic flow on the constant-speed geodesics with $|\dot q|^2 = 2E$ (so the level $E = \tfrac12$ corresponds to unit-speed geodesics on the unit cotangent bundle $S^*M$).

# The Definition

Let $(M, g)$ be a Riemannian manifold with cotangent bundle $T^*M$ and canonical symplectic form $\omega = dq^i \wedge dp_i$ (where $(q^i, p_i)$ are canonical local coordinates on $T^*M$). The **kinetic-energy Hamiltonian** is the smooth function
$$H : T^*M \to \mathbb{R}, \qquad H(q, p) = \frac{1}{2}\, g^{ij}(q)\, p_i\, p_j = \frac{1}{2}\, |p|^2_{g^{-1}},$$
where $g^{ij}$ is the inverse of the metric matrix $g_{ij}$.

The associated **Hamiltonian vector field** $X_H$ on $T^*M$ is the unique vector field satisfying
$$\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH,$$
which in canonical coordinates is
$$X_H = g^{ij}\, p_j\, \frac{\partial}{\partial q^i} - \tfrac{1}{2}\partial_i g^{jk}\, p_j\, p_k\, \frac{\partial}{\partial p_i}.$$

The **geodesic flow on $T^*M$** is the flow $\phi_t^H : T^*M \to T^*M$ of $X_H$. The projection $\pi \circ \phi_t^H(q_0, p_0) : \mathbb{R} \to M$ is the [[Def - Geodesic|geodesic]] $\gamma$ of $g$ with $\gamma(0) = q_0$ and $\dot\gamma(0) = g^{ij}(q_0)(p_0)_j$ (the metric-raised version of $p_0$).

**Energy conservation.** $H$ is constant along the flow: $H \circ \phi_t^H = H$ for all $t$.

**Liouville's theorem.** The flow $\phi_t^H$ preserves the symplectic form $\omega$, hence the symplectic volume $\omega^n / n!$ on $T^*M$ (and the induced volume on each energy level set $\{H = E\}$).

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name:** **half the squared norm of the cotangent vector, viewed as a Hamiltonian**. The single equation $H = \tfrac12 g^{ij}p_i p_j$ contains the entire geodesic equation on $M$, lifted via the Legendre transform to the symplectic phase space $T^*M$.

**The kinetic-energy Hamiltonian is the Lagrangian's Legendre transform.** Starting from the Lagrangian $L(q, \dot q) = \tfrac12 g_{ij}(q) \dot q^i \dot q^j$ on $TM$, Legendre transform $p_i = \partial L / \partial \dot q^i = g_{ij}\dot q^j$ produces $H = \tfrac12 g^{ij} p_i p_j$ on $T^*M$. The Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulations are completely equivalent and connected by this transform; the choice between $TM$ and $T^*M$ is the choice of which side of the Legendre transform is more convenient.

**The Hamiltonian for a particle in a potential.** When a potential $V : M \to \mathbb{R}$ is added, the Lagrangian becomes $L = T - V$ and the Hamiltonian becomes $H = T + V$ — the **total energy**. The Hamilton equations give Newton's law in the form $\ddot q^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\dot q^i \dot q^j = -g^{ki}\partial_i V$, the geodesic equation plus a "force" $-\mathrm{grad}_g V$. **Jacobi's principle** then converts back to a pure-geodesic equation: at fixed total energy $E$, the trajectories are geodesics of the conformally rescaled Jacobi metric $\tilde g = 2(E - V) g$.

**This is the simplest non-trivial Hamiltonian system.** Free motion on a Riemannian manifold — no potential, no time-dependence — is the textbook example of a Hamiltonian dynamical system. Most of the elementary symplectic-geometry constructions (Poisson brackets, action–angle coordinates, Liouville integrability) are most easily checked on this example. See [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics]] for the full apparatus.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: free particle on $\mathbb{R}^n$.** $g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$, $g^{ij} = \delta^{ij}$, $H = \tfrac12 |p|^2$. Hamilton's equations: $\dot q^i = p_i$, $\dot p_i = 0$. Solutions: $p(t) = p_0$ constant, $q(t) = q_0 + p_0 t$ — straight lines at constant velocity. The geodesic equation on Euclidean space.

**Is an instance: free particle on $S^2$ (round metric).** $g = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\, d\varphi^2$ in spherical coordinates, so $g^{\theta\theta} = 1$, $g^{\varphi\varphi} = 1/\sin^2\theta$. Hamiltonian: $H = \tfrac12 (p_\theta^2 + p_\varphi^2/\sin^2\theta)$. The angular momentum $p_\varphi$ is conserved (since $\partial H / \partial \varphi = 0$), and energy $H$ is conserved. Two conserved quantities on a $4$-dimensional phase space leave a $2$-dimensional level, which is foliated by integral curves — the system is integrable. Solutions are great circles.

**Is an instance: free particle on the hyperbolic plane.** In the half-plane model $\mathbb{H}^2 = \{(x, y) : y > 0\}$ with $g = (dx^2 + dy^2)/y^2$, the inverse metric is $g^{xx} = g^{yy} = y^2$, so $H = \tfrac12 y^2 (p_x^2 + p_y^2)$. Hamilton's equations: $\dot x = y^2 p_x$, $\dot y = y^2 p_y$, $\dot p_x = 0$ (since $H$ does not depend on $x$ — translational symmetry), $\dot p_y = -y(p_x^2 + p_y^2)$. Two conservation laws: $p_x$ and $H$. Solutions are vertical lines ($p_x = 0$) and semicircles (general $p_x$).

**Is an instance: Schwarzschild geodesics in general relativity.** The Schwarzschild metric on $\mathbb{R} \times (2M, \infty) \times S^2$ is $ds^2 = -(1 - 2M/r)\, dt^2 + (1 - 2M/r)^{-1} dr^2 + r^2 d\Omega^2$. The corresponding Hamiltonian $H = \tfrac12 g^{\mu\nu}p_\mu p_\nu$ has conserved quantities $p_t$ (energy), $p_\varphi$ (angular momentum), $g^{\mu\nu}p_\mu p_\nu$ (norm), and one more from the Killing tensor — four conservation laws on an $8$-dimensional phase space, making the system *integrable*. This integrability is what allows the perihelion precession of Mercury and the light-bending angles to be computed in closed form.

**Is an instance: the Kepler problem.** A particle in the Coulomb / gravitational potential $V(r) = -k/r$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ has Hamiltonian $H = \tfrac12 |p|^2 - k/|q|$. By Jacobi's principle, at fixed energy $E < 0$ (bound orbits), the trajectories are geodesics of the Jacobi metric $\tilde g = 2(E + k/r)\, \delta$ on the bounded region. The orbits are conic sections, and the unusual integrability (more conservation laws than would be generic, via the Runge–Lenz vector) reflects the special structure of $1/r$.

**Is NOT an instance: a Hamiltonian that is not quadratic in $p$.** A relativistic free particle has Hamiltonian $H = \sqrt{m^2 + |p|^2}$ — not quadratic in $p$. The flow is still a Hamiltonian system on $T^*M$, but the projection to $M$ is *not* geodesic flow of any Riemannian metric on $M$; it is geodesic flow of a different geometric structure (timelike geodesics of a Lorentzian metric on $\mathbb{R} \times M$ with a particular signature). The quadratic-in-$p$ structure is essential for the projection to be Riemannian geodesic flow.

**Corollary (energy conservation).** $H$ is conserved along its own flow: $\{H, H\} = 0$. *Calibration check:* trivially, the Poisson bracket of anything with itself vanishes.

**Corollary (constant speed of projected geodesic).** Since $H = \tfrac12 |p|^2_{g^{-1}}$ is constant along the flow, and on the projection to $M$ the velocity is $\dot q^i = g^{ij}p_j$ with $|\dot q|^2_g = g_{ij}\dot q^i \dot q^j = g^{ij}p_i p_j = 2H$, the projected curve has constant Riemannian speed $\sqrt{2H}$. *Calibration check:* this matches the [[Def - Geodesic|geodesic]] property that $|\dot\gamma|$ is constant.

**Corollary (Killing vectors give conserved momenta).** If $X$ is a [[Def - Vector Field on a Manifold|Killing field]] on $M$, then its "momentum" $P_X(q, p) := p_i X^i(q)$ is conserved along geodesic flow: $\{P_X, H\} = 0$. *Calibration check:* this is the Hamiltonian version of Noether's theorem — symmetries of the metric give conservation laws for geodesic flow. The conservation is most easily verified by computing $\{P_X, H\}$ in coordinates and using the Killing equation $\nabla_{(i} X_{j)} = 0$.

**Corollary (Liouville's theorem applied).** The symplectic volume $\omega^n/n!$ on $T^*M$ is invariant under geodesic flow. Restricted to a constant-energy level $\{H = E\}$, the induced volume $\omega^{n-1}/(n-1)!$ (after factoring out the energy direction) is also invariant. *Calibration check:* this is what makes geodesic flow on $T^*M$ the natural setting for ergodic theory — the existence of an invariant measure is the precondition for asking about ergodicity, mixing, and entropy.

**Calibration check.** If you can verify (a) that on Euclidean space the kinetic-energy Hamiltonian generates straight-line motion, (b) that the Hamilton equations recover the geodesic equation, and (c) that the energy $H = \tfrac12 |p|^2_{g^{-1}}$ is conserved along the flow — then you have understood the definition.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Symplectic Geometry as the Geometry of Phase Space *(from Geometric Mechanics)*
> The Hamiltonian formulation lifts geodesic flow into the world of symplectic geometry, where Liouville's theorem, Poisson brackets, action–angle coordinates, and the entire integrability theory live. The general framework — symplectic manifolds, Hamiltonian flows, moment maps, symplectic reduction — is developed in [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics]]; the kinetic-energy Hamiltonian is the simplest concrete instance.

> [!tip] Integrability of Geodesic Flow *(from Hamiltonian Mechanics)*
> Geodesic flow on $T^*M$ is **completely integrable** iff there exist $n$ independent functions in involution (Poisson-commuting), where $n = \dim M$. Round spheres, ellipsoids, Schwarzschild, and Lie groups with bi-invariant metrics are integrable. Generic metrics are not, and the geodesic flow then exhibits chaotic behaviour — the **KAM theorem** quantifies how integrability persists under small perturbations.

> [!tip] **Geodesic Flow as Hyperbolic Dynamics** *(from Dynamical Systems)*
> On a compact Riemannian manifold of negative sectional curvature, the geodesic flow is **Anosov** — a uniformly hyperbolic dynamical system. Ergodicity, mixing, exponential decay of correlations, and the entire theory of hyperbolic dynamics (Smale's program, the Sinai–Ruelle–Bowen measure, Margulis' lattice counting) apply. This is one of the principal sources of explicit ergodic Hamiltonian systems and a deep bridge between geometry and dynamics.

> [!tip] **Quantum Mechanics on a Riemannian Manifold** *(from Quantum Mechanics)*
> Replace classical $H = \tfrac12 g^{ij}p_i p_j$ by the quantum operator $-\tfrac12 \Delta_g$ (half the **Laplace–Beltrami operator**). Wavefunctions are sections of a line bundle, and the time evolution is given by the Schrödinger equation $i\partial_t \psi = -\tfrac12 \Delta_g \psi$. The spectrum of $\Delta_g$ encodes geometric data (the Weyl law for eigenvalue asymptotics; the heat-kernel expansion gives Riemannian invariants), and the semiclassical limit recovers classical geodesic flow on $T^*M$. This is the foundation of **quantum chaos** and **spectral geometry**.
