# Problem Statement

Show that the Pauli matrices $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$ generate (as a real algebra inside $M_2(\mathbb{C})$) the **Clifford algebra** $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3, |\cdot|^2) = \mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$. Specifically:

1. The linear map $\varphi: \mathbb{R}^3 \to M_2(\mathbb{C})$, $\vec e_j \mapsto \sigma_j$ satisfies the Clifford relation $\varphi(\vec x)^2 = |\vec x|^2 I$ for all $\vec x \in \mathbb{R}^3$.
2. By the universal property of Clifford algebras, $\varphi$ extends to a unique algebra homomorphism $\tilde\varphi: \mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) \to M_2(\mathbb{C})$.
3. $\tilde\varphi$ is an isomorphism — i.e., the real subalgebra of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ generated by $\{I, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3\}$ is all of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ (which has real dimension $8 = \dim_\mathbb{R}\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$).
4. Find an explicit basis of $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ given by Pauli-matrix products, and identify the element corresponding to the volume form $\omega = e_1 e_2 e_3 \in \mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** *Identifying a concrete matrix algebra as a Clifford algebra.* This is the prototypical exercise in applying the universal property: rather than constructing $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ as a tensor-algebra quotient, we *recognize* it inside $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ using the Pauli matrices as generators.

**Assumption pattern:** Given the Pauli matrices in $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, we exploit their algebraic properties: $\sigma_j^2 = I$ (anticipating the Clifford squaring $\varphi(v)^2 = Q(v)I$ with $Q(\vec e_j) = 1$); $\sigma_j\sigma_k = -\sigma_k\sigma_j$ for $j \neq k$ (the anticommutation); and the dimension count $\dim_\mathbb{R}M_2(\mathbb{C}) = 8 = 2^3 = \dim_\mathbb{R}\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ (forcing the universal-property homomorphism to be an isomorphism).

**Theorem routing:** Step 1: verify the Clifford relation via direct computation using $\sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}I + i\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$ from [[Def - The Pauli Matrices]]. Step 2: apply the universal property from [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property]] to get a homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) \to M_2(\mathbb{C})$. Step 3: dimension count to upgrade to isomorphism.

**Key decision point:** The trickiest part is *finding* the basis of $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ as Pauli products. The natural choice is $\{I, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3, \sigma_1\sigma_2, \sigma_2\sigma_3, \sigma_3\sigma_1, \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3\}$, with $8$ elements matching $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) = 2^3 = 8$. The volume form $e_1 e_2 e_3 \in \mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ corresponds to $\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3$, which by the Pauli product identity equals $iI$. The fact that the volume form is the imaginary unit $i$ is one of the most striking features of $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$.

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Operation 3 from the topic page (reduce a Clifford-algebra expression to standard form via the Clifford relation):** We use $\sigma_j^2 = I$ and $\sigma_j\sigma_k = -\sigma_k\sigma_j$ ($j \neq k$) to reduce any product of Pauli matrices to a sorted product $\sigma_{i_1}\sigma_{i_2}\cdots\sigma_{i_k}$ with $i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k$.

2. **Operation 1 from the topic page (use the Pauli product identity):** The identity $\sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}I + i\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$ is the explicit form of the Clifford relation in $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$, simultaneously expressing the algebra's commutator and anticommutator structure.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Verify the Clifford relation $\varphi(\vec x)^2 = |\vec x|^2 I$ in two ways: (a) use $\varphi(\vec x) = \vec x \cdot \vec\sigma = \sum_j x^j \sigma_j$, and expand $(\vec x \cdot \vec\sigma)^2$ using $\sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}I + i\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$; (b) use the Pauli identity $(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec a)(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec b) = (\vec a \cdot \vec b)I + i\vec\sigma\cdot(\vec a \times \vec b)$ with $\vec a = \vec b = \vec x$, which gives $(\vec x \cdot \vec\sigma)^2 = |\vec x|^2 I + i\vec\sigma\cdot(\vec x \times \vec x) = |\vec x|^2 I$ (since $\vec x \times \vec x = 0$).

> [!note]- Hint 2
> For the dimension count: $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ has dimension $2^3 = 8$ over $\mathbb{R}$ (basis is $\{e_S : S \subseteq \{1, 2, 3\}\}$ with $e_S = \prod_{j \in S} e_j$). $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ has real dimension $8$ (it has complex dimension $4$, hence real dimension $8$). So the universal-property homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) \to M_2(\mathbb{C})$ — between $8$-dimensional spaces — is an isomorphism iff it is injective, iff its image is all of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> The volume form $\omega = e_1 e_2 e_3$ in $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ maps to $\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3$ in $M_2(\mathbb{C})$. Compute: $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = i\sigma_3$ (from the Pauli identity); then $\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = i\sigma_3^2 = iI$. So $\omega \leftrightarrow iI$, the imaginary scalar! This is why $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ is *complex*, not just real — the imaginary unit emerges from the volume form.

# Solution

The proof has four steps. Step 1 verifies the Clifford relation. Step 2 invokes the universal property to get a homomorphism. Step 3 lists the $8$ products that span the image, and dimension counts upgrade to isomorphism. Step 4 identifies the volume form $\omega$ with $iI$.

**Step 1: Verify the Clifford relation.**

For all $\vec x \in \mathbb{R}^3$, $\varphi(\vec x)^2 = (\vec x \cdot \vec\sigma)^2 = |\vec x|^2 I$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Compute $(\vec x \cdot \vec\sigma)^2 = (\sum_j x^j\sigma_j)(\sum_k x^k\sigma_k) = \sum_{j, k} x^j x^k \sigma_j\sigma_k$.
>
> Use $\sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}I + i\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$:
> $$(\vec x\cdot\vec\sigma)^2 = \sum_{j, k} x^j x^k (\delta_{jk}I + i\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l) = \sum_j (x^j)^2 I + i\sum_{j, k, l} x^j x^k \epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l.$$
> The first sum is $|\vec x|^2 I$. The second sum vanishes: $\sum_{j, k} \epsilon_{jkl} x^j x^k$ is the $l$-component of $\vec x \times \vec x = 0$.
>
> So $(\vec x \cdot \vec\sigma)^2 = |\vec x|^2 I$, confirming the Clifford relation for the quadratic form $Q(\vec x) = |\vec x|^2$.

**Step 2: Apply the universal property.**

By [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|the universal property of Clifford algebras]], the linear map $\varphi: \mathbb{R}^3 \to M_2(\mathbb{C})$, $\vec e_j \mapsto \sigma_j$ extends to a unique algebra homomorphism $\tilde\varphi: \mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) \to M_2(\mathbb{C})$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The hypothesis of the universal property is exactly $\varphi(\vec x)^2 = Q(\vec x) \cdot 1_A = |\vec x|^2 I$, which we verified in Step 1. So $\varphi$ extends uniquely to $\tilde\varphi$, with $\tilde\varphi$ sending the generator $e_j \in \mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ to $\sigma_j \in M_2(\mathbb{C})$.

**Step 3: $\tilde\varphi$ is an isomorphism.**

The $8$ elements $\{I, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3, \sigma_1\sigma_2, \sigma_2\sigma_3, \sigma_3\sigma_1, \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3\}$ form an $\mathbb{R}$-basis of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, matching the dimension of $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$, so $\tilde\varphi$ is an isomorphism.

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Dimension of $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$.* As shown in [[Def - Clifford Algebra]], $\dim_\mathbb{R} \mathrm{Cl}(V) = 2^{\dim V}$. So $\dim_\mathbb{R}\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) = 2^3 = 8$.
>
> *Dimension of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$.* As a complex algebra, $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ has complex dimension $4$ (basis $\{I, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3\}$ — these are linearly independent over $\mathbb{C}$ and span $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ as a $\mathbb{C}$-vector space). As a real algebra, $\dim_\mathbb{R} M_2(\mathbb{C}) = 2 \cdot 4 = 8$.
>
> *The $8$ Pauli products span $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ over $\mathbb{R}$.* Compute the basis elements explicitly:
> - $I, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$ are $4$ matrices, linearly independent over $\mathbb{R}$ (and over $\mathbb{C}$).
> - $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = i\sigma_3$ (from $\sigma_j\sigma_k = i\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$). So $\sigma_1\sigma_2 \in \{c\sigma_3 : c \in \mathbb{C}\}$, a real $2$-dimensional subspace.
> - Similarly $\sigma_2\sigma_3 = i\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3\sigma_1 = i\sigma_2$.
> - $\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = i\sigma_3 \cdot \sigma_3 = iI$.
>
> So the $8$ elements expanded are: $\{I, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3, i\sigma_3, i\sigma_1, i\sigma_2, iI\}$, which is exactly $\{I, iI\} \cdot \{I, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3\}$ — the real-span of the Pauli matrices plus their imaginary multiples. This is a real $8$-dimensional basis of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, matching the dimension count.
>
> Since $\tilde\varphi$ is a homomorphism between $8$-dimensional $\mathbb{R}$-algebras whose image contains an $\mathbb{R}$-basis of the target, $\tilde\varphi$ is surjective; surjective + equal-dimension = isomorphism.

**Step 4: The volume form corresponds to $iI$.**

The volume form $\omega = e_1 e_2 e_3 \in \mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ maps under $\tilde\varphi$ to $\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = iI \in M_2(\mathbb{C})$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Compute $\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = (\sigma_1\sigma_2)\sigma_3 = i\sigma_3 \cdot \sigma_3 = i I$.
>
> Verify it is central: $\omega = \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3$ commutes with $\sigma_j$ for each $j$. For instance: $\omega\sigma_1 = \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3\sigma_1 = -\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_1\sigma_3 = \sigma_1\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = I \sigma_2\sigma_3 = \sigma_2\sigma_3$, and $\sigma_1\omega = \sigma_1\sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = \sigma_2\sigma_3$. So $\omega\sigma_1 = \sigma_1\omega$, confirming centrality.
>
> The fact $\omega = iI$ has structural significance: the **center of $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$** is $\mathbb{R}\{1, \omega\} = \mathbb{R}\{I, iI\} = \mathbb{C}$ — i.e., the center is *complex*. This is why $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ is naturally a complex algebra: the complex structure $i$ is encoded as the volume form. Compare with $\mathrm{Cl}(0, 1)$ where the single generator $e$ already satisfies $e^2 = -1$, giving $\mathrm{Cl}(0, 1) = \mathbb{C}$ in one dimension — here the same $i$-like behaviour arises from the higher-dimensional volume form.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Define $\varphi: \mathbb{R}^3 \to M_2(\mathbb{C})$ by $\varphi(\vec e_j) = \sigma_j$ for $j = 1, 2, 3$, extended linearly.
>
> *Clifford relation.* For $\vec x \in \mathbb{R}^3$: $\varphi(\vec x)^2 = (\vec x \cdot \vec\sigma)^2 = x^j x^k \sigma_j\sigma_k = x^j x^k(\delta_{jk}I + i\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l) = |\vec x|^2 I + i(\vec x \times \vec x) \cdot \vec\sigma = |\vec x|^2 I$ (since $\vec x \times \vec x = 0$). So $\varphi$ is Clifford-compatible.
>
> *Universal property.* By [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property]], $\varphi$ extends uniquely to an algebra homomorphism $\tilde\varphi: \mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) \to M_2(\mathbb{C})$.
>
> *Dimension count.* $\dim_\mathbb{R}\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0) = 2^3 = 8 = \dim_\mathbb{R} M_2(\mathbb{C})$.
>
> *Surjectivity.* The $8$ elements $\{I, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3, \sigma_1\sigma_2, \sigma_2\sigma_3, \sigma_3\sigma_1, \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3\}$ in the image of $\tilde\varphi$ evaluate (using $\sigma_j\sigma_k = i\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$ for $j \neq k$) to $\{I, \sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3, i\sigma_3, i\sigma_1, i\sigma_2, iI\}$, which is an $\mathbb{R}$-basis of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$. So $\tilde\varphi$ is surjective.
>
> *Isomorphism.* Equal-dimension + surjective $\implies$ isomorphism.
>
> *Volume form.* $\tilde\varphi(e_1 e_2 e_3) = \sigma_1\sigma_2\sigma_3 = (\sigma_1\sigma_2)\sigma_3 = (i\sigma_3)\sigma_3 = i I$. So the volume form is the imaginary scalar.

# Key Takeaways

**The universal property is the operational way to recognize a Clifford algebra.** This exercise illustrates the standard pattern: rather than constructing $\mathrm{Cl}(V, Q)$ from scratch via the tensor-algebra quotient, one *finds* the Clifford algebra inside a familiar matrix algebra by checking the Clifford relation on candidate generators and invoking the universal property. The same pattern identifies the **gamma matrices** as generators of $\mathrm{Cl}(1, 3) \otimes \mathbb{C} \cong M_4(\mathbb{C})$, the **quaternions** as $\mathrm{Cl}(0, 2)$, and so on. The triple of (generators satisfying Clifford relation) + (universal property) + (dimension count) is the standard recipe.

**The volume form $\omega = e_1 e_2 e_3$ being equal to $iI$ is the algebraic source of "complex" structure in $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$.** The center of $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ is $\mathbb{R}\{1, \omega\} = \mathbb{C}$ — generated as a real algebra by the volume form and the identity. This is the reason $\mathrm{Cl}(3, 0)$ is *complex* matrix algebra $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ rather than real $M_2(\mathbb{R})$: the "extra" complex structure comes from inside the Clifford algebra, not from outside. The same phenomenon appears whenever the Clifford volume form has $\omega^2 = -1$, which depends on dimension and signature in the period-$8$ pattern (see [[Thm - Classification of Clifford Algebras over R]]).

**The trigger-reaction pattern: when you see anticommuting square roots of $\pm I$, think "Clifford algebra".** Any time multiple matrices satisfying $\{A_j, A_k\} = 2\eta_{jk}I$ appear in physics or geometry, the universal property says they generate a Clifford algebra. This applies to the Pauli matrices, the gamma matrices, the Dirac matrices in any dimension, the Jordan–Wigner fermion operators in lattice models, and various index-theoretic constructions. The recipe: find the Clifford algebra, identify the spinor module as the irreducible representation, and exploit the algebraic structure.
