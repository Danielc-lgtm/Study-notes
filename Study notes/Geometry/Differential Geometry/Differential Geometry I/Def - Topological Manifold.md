# Notation

Throughout, $M$ is a topological space — we write the topology implicitly. The integer $n \geq 0$ is reserved for the dimension. We write $\mathbb{R}^n$ for $n$-dimensional Euclidean space with its standard topology (generated by open balls under the Euclidean metric). The phrase "open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$" always refers to this topology. We write $U \subseteq M$ for an open subset, and a [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphism]] $\varphi : U \to \widehat{U}$ for a bijective continuous map with continuous inverse onto its image $\widehat{U} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. The full notation registry lives on [[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases]].

> [!warning] Convention: Hausdorff and second-countable are part of the definition
> Some authors (notably some textbooks of algebraic topology) omit Hausdorff or second countability from the definition of topological manifold. We follow Lee and include both, because every later result of the subject — partitions of unity, paracompactness, embedding theorems, the existence of Riemannian metrics — requires both, and natural examples never fail either condition. When sources diverge we flag the divergence explicitly.

# Axiom Motivation

The goal is to axiomatize the everyday notion of "$n$-dimensional surface" — a space that locally looks like $\mathbb{R}^n$ — while keeping the definition free of any ambient embedding. The classical surfaces of multivariable calculus ($S^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$, the torus $T^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$) are visibly two-dimensional because they admit local parametrizations by open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$; but you would like to say the same of the projective plane $\mathbb{RP}^2$, which has no natural embedding in low-dimensional Euclidean space. So the definition must work intrinsically: write down the local data and the global topological hypotheses, and forget the ambient space.

The geometric heart is the **locally Euclidean** condition: every point of $M$ has an open neighbourhood $U$ that admits a [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphism]] $\varphi : U \to \widehat{U} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ onto an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. This is what *locally looks like $\mathbb{R}^n$* means precisely: not "is contained in" or "embeds in" but "has charts to". The choice of "open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$" rather than "an open ball" or "all of $\mathbb{R}^n$" looks fussy, but the three are equivalent (Lee Exercise 1.1): any open subset is locally homeomorphic to an open ball, and an open ball is homeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n$. The "open subset" formulation is technically the most flexible, since it does not require us to shrink the chart domain unnecessarily.

But locally Euclidean alone is not enough. Two further conditions — Hausdorff and second-countable — are imposed, and each excludes a class of pathologies that would otherwise break the theory downstream. Consider each in turn.

The **Hausdorff condition** says: any two distinct points $p, q \in M$ have disjoint open neighbourhoods. What goes wrong without it? Lee Problem 1-1 constructs the "line with two origins": take $X = \mathbb{R} \times \{0, 1\}$ with the product topology, then identify $(x, 0) \sim (x, 1)$ for every $x \neq 0$. The quotient is locally Euclidean of dimension 1 (each point has a chart) and second-countable (the quotient of a second-countable space by a sufficiently nice equivalence relation is second-countable). But the two origins $[0, 0]$ and $[0, 1]$ cannot be separated: every neighbourhood of $[0, 0]$ contains an interval around $0$ on the $\{0\}$-line, every neighbourhood of $[0, 1]$ contains an interval around $0$ on the $\{1\}$-line, and these intervals must overlap off $0$. So in this "manifold" a convergent sequence can have *two* limits — the sequence $1/n \to [0, 0]$ but also $1/n \to [0, 1]$, since the two origins are not separated. Without Hausdorff, uniqueness of limits — the most basic property of any reasonable notion of convergence — fails, and any theorem stating "the limit is unique" or "two functions agreeing on a dense set are equal" becomes nonsense. The repair is the Hausdorff axiom; Lee's example is the canonical pathology to keep in mind, the construction worth memorizing.

The **second-countable condition** says: $M$ has a countable basis for its topology. What goes wrong without it? Lee Problem 1-2: take a disjoint union of uncountably many copies of $\mathbb{R}$. This space is locally Euclidean (each copy is open and locally Euclidean) and Hausdorff (each pair of points lies in one or two copies, easily separated). But there is no countable basis: any basis must include open sets in each of the uncountably many components, hence the basis has cardinality at least the continuum. Without second countability, the proof of paracompactness fails (every open cover has a *locally finite* refinement, which requires reducing uncountable covers to countable ones), and without paracompactness, the existence of [[Def - Partition of Unity|partitions of unity]] fails ([[Thm - Paracompact Has Partitions of Unity]]) — and partitions of unity are the *single most useful tool* of the entire subject (used to construct Riemannian metrics, smooth bump functions, integrals on manifolds, sheaf-theoretic obstructions). So second countability is the gateway to all the existence theorems of [[Differential Geometry II — Smooth Maps and Partitions of Unity|DG II]]. The repair is the second-countability axiom; Lee's disjoint-union example is the pathology.

Could we *weaken* any axiom and still have a usable theory? Three substitutes have been tried: (i) paracompact + connected, in place of second countable; Lee Problem 1-5 shows this is equivalent for locally Euclidean Hausdorff spaces, so it is the same theory with different packaging. (ii) Hausdorff dropped, but in the special context of "non-Hausdorff manifolds" — these arise in algebraic geometry (the étale topology) and in degenerate quotient situations; theory is harder, and most analytic statements no longer hold. (iii) Locally Euclidean dropped — gives "topological spaces in general", which is too weak to support any differentiation. None of these alternatives is more useful in practice than the Lee definition. Could we *strengthen* the axioms? Adding compactness would exclude $\mathbb{R}^n$ itself, which we want to keep; adding metrizability would be redundant since every Hausdorff second-countable manifold is metrizable (Urysohn); adding orientability is geometric content not visible at this level. The three axioms — Hausdorff, second-countable, locally Euclidean — are the minimal compatible package.

The dimension $n$ is part of the definition: a topological manifold has a fixed dimension. We exclude spaces of mixed dimension (e.g., the disjoint union of a plane and a line) from the manifold concept. This is well-defined because *dimension is a topological invariant* ([[Thm - Invariance of Dimension]]): a nonempty $n$-manifold cannot be homeomorphic to an $m$-manifold for $n \neq m$. The proof of invariance of dimension requires algebraic topology (singular homology or de Rham cohomology in [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius|DG X]]) and is not visible from the definition alone, but the definition would be incoherent without it.

The empty set satisfies the definition for every $n$ — there are no points to verify the local condition for, the Hausdorff and second-countability conditions hold vacuously. By convention, the empty manifold is allowed (the same convention as "the empty set is a topological space") and we usually ignore it without comment.

# The Definition

Let $n \geq 0$ be a nonnegative integer. A **topological manifold of dimension $n$** (also written **topological $n$-manifold**) is a topological space $M$ such that:

1. $M$ is **Hausdorff**: for every pair of distinct points $p, q \in M$, there are disjoint open sets $U, V \subseteq M$ with $p \in U$ and $q \in V$.

2. $M$ is **second-countable**: there exists a countable basis for the topology of $M$.

3. $M$ is **locally Euclidean of dimension $n$**: every point $p \in M$ has an open neighbourhood $U \subseteq M$ that is homeomorphic to an open subset $\widehat{U} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$.

When the dimension is understood or unimportant, we say "topological manifold". The integer $n$ is uniquely determined by $M$ (when nonempty) by [[Thm - Invariance of Dimension]], so we may speak of "the dimension of $M$", written $\dim M = n$ or "$M$ is $n$-dimensional".

Equivalent formulations of axiom (3): one may demand that every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open ball in $\mathbb{R}^n$, or to all of $\mathbb{R}^n$, without changing the class of spaces defined (Lee Exercise 1.1).

The empty set $\emptyset$ satisfies all three axioms for every $n$ and is considered a topological $n$-manifold for each $n$.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The category $\mathbf{TopMan}$ of topological manifolds has topological manifolds as objects and [[Def - Continuous Map|continuous maps]] as morphisms; it sits inside the category $\mathbf{Top}$ of topological spaces as a full subcategory (every continuous map between manifolds is a morphism in $\mathbf{Top}$, and every continuous map in $\mathbf{Top}$ between manifolds is a morphism here). The inclusion functor $\mathbf{TopMan} \to \mathbf{Top}$ has no left adjoint — there is no functorial way to assign to an arbitrary topological space a "best" topological manifold — but it does have nontrivial structural properties:

- The category has finite products (since the product of topological manifolds is a topological manifold of the sum of [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]] — see [[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]] for the smooth version, and the topological version follows verbatim).
- The category has coproducts only with respect to manifolds *of the same dimension*: the disjoint union of two $n$-manifolds is an $n$-manifold, but the disjoint union of a line and a plane has mixed dimension and is therefore not a manifold in our sense.
- The category does not have equalizers in general: the equalizer of two smooth maps $f, g : M \to N$ — namely $\{p \in M : f(p) = g(p)\}$ — is in general only a topological [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] of $M$, not a manifold, since it need not be locally Euclidean (cf. the cusp-singularity examples in algebraic geometry).
- Pullbacks exist along surjective submersions (the fibre-bundle pullback), giving the construction underlying [[Differential Geometry VI — Vector Bundles and the Cotangent Bundle|vector bundles]].

A more structural viewpoint: a topological $n$-manifold is a topological space that is *locally* in the (essentially unique) topological category of open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$. This is the prototype of the **étale local model** picture that pervades modern geometry — a space is described by a model and a class of permissible local data — and recurs in algebraic geometry (schemes are locally affine), in [[Def - Homotopy|homotopy]] theory ($\infty$-topoi are locally simplicial sets), and in physics (spacetime is locally Minkowski).

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name:** A topological $n$-manifold is "a Hausdorff space that admits an atlas of charts to $\mathbb{R}^n$, with the topological hypotheses needed for partition-of-unity arguments to work." The Hausdorff axiom keeps limits unique; the second-countability axiom unlocks partitions of unity (via paracompactness); the locally Euclidean axiom is the geometric content. Whenever you need to verify "$M$ is a topological manifold", run these three checks; whenever you need to *use* the property, the operative consequence is "I have a chart $\varphi : U \to \widehat{U} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ near every point, and partitions of unity exist."

In algebraic geometry, the analogue is a **scheme of dimension $n$**: a topological space locally modelled on $\mathrm{Spec}\, A$ for a commutative ring $A$ of Krull dimension $n$. The "open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$" of differential topology become "affine schemes" of algebraic geometry; "homeomorphism" becomes "isomorphism of locally ringed spaces"; the Hausdorff condition gets replaced (or rather, generalized) by "separated", a more flexible cousin. Schemes drop both Hausdorff and second countability and replace them with sheaf-theoretic conditions tuned to algebraic settings — but the categorical pattern (a space locally modelled on a chosen model, with chosen compatibility) is exactly the one of this definition.

In low-dimensional topology, every topological surface (2-manifold) admits a smooth structure ([[Def - Smooth Manifold]]) by the classification of surfaces, and this smooth structure is unique. Every topological 3-manifold admits a smooth structure, again essentially uniquely. The smooth-vs-topological distinction is *invisible* in dimensions $\leq 3$. It first becomes visible in dimension 4 (Kervaire and Milnor; Donaldson and Freedman): some topological 4-manifolds admit no smooth structure, and $\mathbb{R}^4$ admits uncountably many smooth structures. This is one of the deepest phenomena in geometry.

In condensed-matter physics, a topological manifold is the appropriate setting for **bulk topology of a system**: the Brillouin zone of a crystal is the torus $T^n$, the parameter space of band insulators is a manifold, and the topological-invariant calculations of band theory depend on the manifold structure. The Hausdorff and second-countability axioms are physically uninteresting; the locally Euclidean structure is what carries the physics.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: $\mathbb{R}^n$ itself.** With the standard topology, $\mathbb{R}^n$ is Hausdorff (it is a metric space), second-countable (the open balls with rational centres and rational radii form a countable basis), and locally Euclidean of dimension $n$ (the single chart $\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^n}$ covers it). It is the prototype, and the existence of an atlas with a *single* chart says it is a topological manifold in the most direct way possible. Any open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is also a topological $n$-manifold (Hausdorff and second-countable inherited; locally Euclidean from the inclusion as a chart).

**Is an instance: $S^n$, the $n$-sphere.** As a subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ with the [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] topology, it is Hausdorff and second-countable. Locally Euclidean of dimension $n$ by graph coordinates: the upper and lower hemispheres in each coordinate direction give $2(n+1)$ charts homeomorphic to the open ball $\mathbb{B}^n$ via projection (Lee Example 1.4). Alternatively, two stereographic charts suffice (Lee Problem 1-7); see [[Ex - The Sphere as a Smooth Manifold via Stereographic Projection]].

**Is an instance: $\mathbb{RP}^n$, real projective $n$-space.** Constructed as the quotient $(\mathbb{R}^{n+1} \setminus \{0\})/\sim$ where $x \sim \lambda x$ for $\lambda \neq 0$ — the space of lines through the origin. Hausdorff and second-countable by the quotient construction (with care — the verification is nontrivial, Lee Exercise 1.6). Locally Euclidean by $(n+1)$ charts $U_i = \{[x^1 : \dots : x^{n+1}] : x^i \neq 0\}$ with $\varphi_i([x^1 : \dots : x^{n+1}]) = (x^1/x^i, \dots, \widehat{x^i/x^i}, \dots, x^{n+1}/x^i) \in \mathbb{R}^n$. See [[Ex - Real Projective Space is a Smooth Manifold]] for full construction.

**Is an instance: products of topological manifolds.** If $M_1, \dots, M_k$ are topological manifolds of [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]] $n_1, \dots, n_k$, then $M_1 \times \cdots \times M_k$ is a topological manifold of dimension $n_1 + \cdots + n_k$ (Lee Example 1.8). Hausdorff and second-countable are inherited (finite products preserve both). Locally Euclidean via product charts. The $n$-torus $T^n = (S^1)^n$ is the canonical example.

**Is an instance: graphs of continuous functions.** If $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is open and $f : U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ is continuous, the graph $\Gamma(f) = \{(x, f(x)) : x \in U\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n+k}$ is a topological $n$-manifold, with the single chart $\varphi : \Gamma(f) \to U$, $\varphi(x, y) = x$ (Lee Example 1.3). This is why every smooth hypersurface defined locally by $z = g(x, y)$ is automatically a manifold.

**Is NOT an instance: the line with two origins.** Take $\mathbb{R} \times \{0, 1\}$ and identify $(x, 0) \sim (x, 1)$ for $x \neq 0$ (Lee Problem 1-1). The result is locally Euclidean (each "origin" has the chart $(-\varepsilon, \varepsilon)$, and other points have ordinary $\mathbb{R}$-charts) and second-countable, but **not Hausdorff**: the two origins cannot be separated by disjoint open neighbourhoods. This is the standard counterexample for Hausdorff failure; it shows the Hausdorff axiom is not implied by the other two.

**Is NOT an instance: uncountable disjoint union of $\mathbb{R}$'s.** Take $\mathbb{R} \times S$ with $S$ an uncountable set in the discrete topology — equivalently, an uncountable disjoint union of copies of $\mathbb{R}$ (Lee Problem 1-2). The result is locally Euclidean (each copy is locally Euclidean) and Hausdorff (different copies have disjoint open neighbourhoods, same copy is a Hausdorff $\mathbb{R}$), but **not second-countable**: any basis must include open sets in each of uncountably many components. This is the standard counterexample for second-countability failure; note that this space *is* paracompact, so it is *not* a counterexample for paracompactness, only for second countability.

**Is NOT an instance: $\mathbb{Q}$ with the subspace topology from $\mathbb{R}$.** Hausdorff and second-countable, but not locally Euclidean of any dimension: every nonempty open subset of $\mathbb{Q}$ is totally disconnected, so it cannot be homeomorphic to any open ball in $\mathbb{R}^n$ (a nonempty connected space) for $n \geq 1$, and for $n = 0$ the locally Euclidean condition would force $\mathbb{Q}$ to be discrete, which it is not.

**Is NOT an instance: a "manifold with corners" or "manifold with boundary"** in the strict sense — see [[Def - Smooth Manifold with Boundary]]. The closed interval $[0, 1]$ is not a topological 1-manifold in the sense of this definition because the endpoints have no neighbourhood homeomorphic to an *open* subset of $\mathbb{R}$; they only have neighbourhoods homeomorphic to relatively open subsets of $[0, \infty)$. This is why a separate definition is needed for manifolds with boundary.

**Corollary (every open subset is a manifold).** If $M$ is a topological $n$-manifold and $U \subseteq M$ is open, then $U$ is a topological $n$-manifold (with the subspace topology). Hausdorff and second-countable inherited; locally Euclidean by restricting charts.

**Corollary (the disjoint union of finitely many topological $n$-manifolds is a topological $n$-manifold).** Each property is preserved by finite disjoint union; the dimension must be the same, since we cannot mix dimensions. *Note:* countable disjoint unions also work (preserve second countability), but uncountable ones do not.

**Calibration check.** Verify that $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ is a topological manifold of dimension $n^2$ (it is the open set $\det \neq 0$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$). Verify that the closed unit interval $[0, 1]$ is *not* a topological manifold by the definition given (the endpoints lack open neighbourhoods homeomorphic to open subsets of $\mathbb{R}$). Verify that the "topologist's sine curve" $\{(x, \sin(1/x)) : x \in (0, 1]\} \cup \{0\} \times [-1, 1] \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, while connected, is not a topological 1-manifold (the points on the $y$-axis lack locally Euclidean neighbourhoods).

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Smooth Manifold *(from this topic)*
> Adding a maximal smooth atlas to a topological manifold gives a [[Def - Smooth Manifold|smooth manifold]] — the object on which calculus is defined. The smooth structure is a refinement, not an automatic consequence: there are topological manifolds with no smooth structure (Kervaire 1960, dim 10) and topological manifolds with many inequivalent smooth structures (Milnor's 28 exotic 7-spheres, Donaldson's uncountably many exotic $\mathbb{R}^4$'s).

> [!tip] Manifold with Boundary *(from this topic, §1.4)*
> Generalizing to allow boundary points — [[Def - Smooth Manifold with Boundary|topological/smooth manifolds with boundary]] — by replacing the chart target $\mathbb{R}^n$ with either $\mathbb{R}^n$ or the closed upper half-space $\mathbb{H}^n$. This is the framework needed for Stokes's theorem and integration by parts in [[Differential Geometry IX — Orientation, Integration, and Stokes' Theorem|DG IX]].

> [!tip] Riemannian Manifold *(from Riemannian Geometry)*
> Once a smooth structure is in place, one can ask for a smoothly varying inner product on each tangent space — a **Riemannian metric**. The pair $(M, g)$ is a Riemannian manifold, and a partition-of-unity argument (which requires second countability!) shows every paracompact smooth manifold admits a Riemannian metric. Distance, angle, geodesics, volume, and curvature all come from $g$.

> [!tip] Topological Invariants and Algebraic Topology *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> The classification of topological manifolds up to homeomorphism is one of the deepest projects in mathematics — solved in dimensions $\leq 3$ (Poincaré, Thurston, Perelman), partially open in dimension 4 (Freedman, Donaldson), partially solved by surgery theory in dimensions $\geq 5$. The invariants used — **Betti numbers**, **fundamental group**, **homology and cohomology groups**, **characteristic classes** — all depend only on the topological structure, not on a smooth structure, and the discipline that studies them is **algebraic topology**.
