# Notation

$G$ denotes a Lie group with identity $e$. For each $g \in G$, the **left translation** $L_g : G \to G$ is the diffeomorphism $L_g(h) = gh$, with differential $d(L_g)_h : T_h G \to T_{gh} G$ at the point $h$. A vector field $X$ on $G$ has value $X_g = X(g) \in T_g G$ at $g$. The pushforward of $X$ by a diffeomorphism $\phi$ is $\phi_* X$. We use $X^L$ (or $\tilde X$ in some sources) for the left-invariant vector field generated by a tangent vector $X_e \in T_e G$. See [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map]] for the full notation registry.

# Axiom Motivation

A Lie [[Def - Group|group]] has a special feature absent from a general smooth manifold: **homogeneity via the [[Def - Group|group]] multiplication**. Left translation $L_g$ is a [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]] of $G$ sending $e$ to $g$, so any tangent vector $X_e \in T_e G$ at the identity can be transported to a tangent vector $d(L_g)_e(X_e) \in T_g G$ at any other point $g$. This transport is canonical — there is exactly one way to do it, dictated by the group structure. The resulting collection of tangent vectors, one at each point of $G$, is a vector field on $G$, and it is called the **left-invariant vector field** generated by $X_e$.

The motivation for naming this class of vector fields specifically is twofold.

**First, left-invariant vector fields are determined by their value at a single point.** A general vector field on $G$ requires infinitely many independent specifications — one tangent vector at every point of $G$. A left-invariant vector field is rigid: its value at any single point determines its value everywhere, because $X_g = d(L_{g h^{-1}})_h(X_h)$ — the value at $g$ is the left-translated value at any $h$. This collapses the infinite-dimensional space of vector fields $\mathfrak{X}(G)$ to a finite-dimensional [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] isomorphic to $T_e G$. The collapse is the essence of why Lie group theory is so much more tractable than general manifold theory: the algebra-respecting vector fields form a finite-dimensional space.

**Second, the Lie bracket of two left-invariant vector fields is again left-invariant.** This is a property of the Lie bracket of vector fields (it commutes with pushforward under [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphisms]], Lee Cor 8.31), and it is what makes the finite-dimensional space of left-invariant vector fields a [[Def - Lie Algebra|Lie algebra]] under the bracket. So *the left-invariant vector fields form not just a vector [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$, but a Lie subalgebra*, and this Lie subalgebra is the Lie algebra of $G$.

Why **left**-invariance and not **right**-invariance, or **bi**-invariance? The choice is conventional and breaks a symmetry, but the alternative would lead to an equally valid theory. **Right-invariant vector fields** $X$ satisfy $d(R_g)_h(X_h) = X_{hg}$ where $R_g(h) = hg$. They form an isomorphic Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$, isomorphic to $T_e G$ via the same evaluation $X \mapsto X_e$, but the bracket they inherit is the **negative** of the bracket on left-invariant vector fields. The reason is that the inversion map $\iota : G \to G$, $\iota(g) = g^{-1}$, exchanges left and right translations (via $\iota \circ L_g = R_{g^{-1}} \circ \iota$), and its differential at the identity is $-\mathrm{id}$ — so pushforward by $\iota$ takes left-invariant to right-invariant, but the differential at $e$ is multiplication by $-1$, which on the bracket gives $\iota_*[X, Y] = [\iota_* X, \iota_* Y]$ corresponding to $-[(-X), (-Y)] = -[X, Y]$. **Bi-invariant vector fields** — invariant under both $L_g$ and $R_g$ — form a much smaller subspace, the center $Z(\mathfrak{g})$ of the Lie algebra; on a non-abelian Lie group there are typically very few of them. The choice of left-invariance is Lee's (and the dominant convention); the theory works equally with right-invariance up to signs.

What goes wrong if we **drop left-invariance** and consider arbitrary smooth vector fields? Then $\mathfrak{X}(G)$ is an infinite-dimensional vector space — and an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra under the bracket, true, but the connection to the finite-dimensional structure of $G$ at the identity is lost. The point of the construction is to identify a finite-dimensional subspace of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$ canonically associated to $G$ as a Lie group; left-invariance is the unique condition that does this.

What if we **strengthen** the demand to left-invariance under a chosen [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]] $H \leq G$, not all of $G$? Then the resulting space of $H$-invariant vector fields is intermediate in [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] between $\mathfrak{g}$ and $\mathfrak{X}(G)$ — it has [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] $\dim G \cdot |G/H|$ in spirit, or really $\dim G$ at each $H$-orbit. This is a useful construction (it gives invariant vector fields on homogeneous spaces $G/H$), but it is not what we want for the Lie algebra of $G$ itself.

The summary: left-invariance is the condition that picks out a finite-dimensional Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$ canonically isomorphic to $T_e G$, with the bracket transferring to $T_e G$ via the evaluation isomorphism. It is the bridge between the infinite-dimensional world of all vector fields on $G$ and the finite-dimensional algebra that characterizes $G$ as a Lie group.

# The Definition

Let $G$ be a Lie group. A vector field $X \in \mathfrak{X}(G)$ is **left-invariant** if it is invariant under every left translation: for every $g \in G$,

$$(L_g)_* X = X, \qquad \text{equivalently}, \qquad d(L_g)_{g'}(X_{g'}) = X_{gg'} \quad \text{for all } g' \in G.$$

Spelled out: the value of $X$ at $g g'$ equals the left-translation $d(L_g)_{g'}$ of the value of $X$ at $g'$. In particular, taking $g' = e$, the value of $X$ at any point $g \in G$ is $X_g = d(L_g)_e(X_e)$ — completely determined by its value at the identity.

The set of all smooth left-invariant vector fields on $G$ is a real vector space under pointwise addition and scalar multiplication, denoted $\mathrm{Lie}(G)$ or $\mathfrak{g}$. The **evaluation map** $\varepsilon : \mathrm{Lie}(G) \to T_e G$, $X \mapsto X_e$, is a vector space isomorphism (Lee Thm 8.37): every tangent vector $v \in T_e G$ extends to a unique left-invariant vector field $v^L$ given by $v^L|_g = d(L_g)_e(v)$.

**Remark on smoothness.** It is automatic that every left-invariant vector field is smooth (Lee Cor 8.38). So we lose nothing by working with smooth left-invariant vector fields specifically: the smoothness condition is no extra constraint, because left-invariance forces it. The proof: smoothness can be checked by testing against smooth functions $f \in C^\infty(G)$, and a direct computation shows that $X^L f$ is smooth on $G$ when $X^L|_g = d(L_g)_e(v)$ for fixed $v$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A left-invariant vector field is the **rigid extension to all of $G$ of a tangent vector at the identity, using left translations**. It is the manifold-theoretic analogue of "constant" in a group-theoretic sense: just as the constant function on a manifold is determined by its value at one point, the left-invariant vector field is determined by its value at one point — but the "constancy" is with respect to the group structure, not the manifold structure.

From the [[Def - Vector Field on a Manifold|vector-field side]], a left-invariant vector field is just a vector field; from the [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group side]], it is a section of the tangent bundle that is preserved by every left translation. The two perspectives coincide in the **canonical trivialization** $TG \cong G \times \mathfrak{g}$ given by $(g, X) \mapsto d(L_g)_e(X)$. Under this trivialization, a left-invariant vector field is exactly a **constant section** $G \to G \times \mathfrak{g}$, $g \mapsto (g, X)$ for fixed $X \in \mathfrak{g}$. So Lee's slogan: **the tangent bundle of a Lie group is canonically trivial**, with the trivialization implemented by left translation.

**True name:** A left-invariant vector field is **the integral data of a one-parameter group of right multiplications** — equivalently, the generator of a right translation. The flow of the left-invariant vector field $X^L$ is precisely $\phi_t^{X^L}(g) = g \exp(tX)$, right multiplication by the one-parameter subgroup $\exp(tX)$. This is Lee Prop 20.8(h). So a left-invariant vector field is the infinitesimal generator of right translations, even though it is *left*-invariant — the asymmetry between "invariant under left" and "generates a right action" is one of the things to internalize. (Right-invariant vector fields generate left translations.)

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: a basis of $\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ given by constant vector fields.** On $(\mathbb{R}^n, +)$, left translation is $L_a(x) = a + x$, with differential the identity map $T_x \mathbb{R}^n \to T_{a + x} \mathbb{R}^n$ under the standard identification. So a vector field $V = V^i \partial/\partial x^i$ is left-invariant if and only if all the $V^i$ are *constants*. The space of left-invariant vector fields is the $n$-dimensional space of constant vector fields, canonically identified with $\mathbb{R}^n = T_0 \mathbb{R}^n$. The Lie bracket of any two constant vector fields is zero (since their coefficients are constant), so $\mathfrak{g} = \mathbb{R}^n$ is an abelian Lie algebra.

**Is an instance: the angle vector field on $S^1$.** The vector field $\partial/\partial\theta$ on the circle, defined locally in any angle chart, is left-invariant — left multiplication by $e^{i\alpha}$ corresponds to angle shift $\theta \mapsto \theta + \alpha$, whose differential is the identity in $\theta$-coordinates. So $\mathfrak{g} = \mathrm{Lie}(S^1)$ is the $1$-dimensional space spanned by $\partial/\partial\theta$, isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$.

**Is an instance: matrix-valued vector fields on $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$.** For $X \in M(n, \mathbb{R}) \cong T_I \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, the left-invariant vector field $X^L$ has value at $g \in \mathrm{GL}(n)$ given by $X^L|_g = gX \in T_g \mathrm{GL}(n) \cong M(n, \mathbb{R})$ — left-multiplication of the matrix $X$ by $g$. (Left translation $L_g(h) = gh$ is the linear map $h \mapsto gh$ on $M(n, \mathbb{R})$, and its differential at any point is again $h \mapsto gh$; so the left-invariant vector field with value $X$ at $I$ has value $gX$ at $g$.) This gives the matrix realization of $\mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{R})$ as left-invariant vector fields on $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$.

**Is NOT an instance: the radial vector field on $\mathbb{R}^n$.** The vector field $V(x) = x^i \partial/\partial x^i$ pointing radially outward has variable coefficient $x^i$, so it is *not* left-invariant on $(\mathbb{R}^n, +)$: $V(a + x) = (a^i + x^i) \partial/\partial x^i \neq V(x)$ — the translation $L_a$ shifts the vector field. So radial vector fields are a counterexample to left-invariance.

**Is NOT an instance: a non-constant vector field on $S^1$.** A vector field like $f(\theta) \partial/\partial\theta$ for non-constant $f$ is not left-invariant — left translation by $\alpha$ would shift the argument of $f$, changing the vector field unless $f$ is constant.

**Corollary (uniqueness from value at $e$).** A left-invariant vector field is uniquely determined by its value at $e$: if $X^L|_e = Y^L|_e$ then $X^L = Y^L$. (This is the injectivity of the evaluation map.)

**Corollary (every tangent vector extends).** For every $v \in T_e G$, the formula $v^L|_g = d(L_g)_e(v)$ defines a left-invariant vector field with $v^L|_e = v$. So the evaluation map is surjective, hence an isomorphism.

**Corollary (left-invariant vector fields form a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$).** The Lie bracket of two left-invariant vector fields is left-invariant — see [[Thm - Left-Invariant Vector Fields Form a Lie Algebra]]. The proof is that pushforward by a diffeomorphism preserves the Lie bracket, so $(L_g)_* [X^L, Y^L] = [(L_g)_* X^L, (L_g)_* Y^L] = [X^L, Y^L]$.

**Corollary (the flow is right-translation by a one-parameter [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]]).** The flow of $X^L$ is $\phi_t^{X^L}(g) = g \exp(tX)$ (Lee Prop 20.8(h)). In particular the integral curve through $e$ is $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$, which is the one-parameter subgroup generated by $X$.

**Calibration check.** If you can (i) verify that on $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ the left-invariant vector field for $X \in \mathfrak{gl}(n)$ is $g \mapsto gX$; (ii) explain why left-invariant vector fields on $G$ form an $n$-dimensional vector space, with $n = \dim G$; and (iii) compute the flow of $X^L$ as $g \mapsto g \exp(tX)$ — you have understood the definition correctly.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Lie Algebra of a Lie Group *(from this chapter)*
> The space of left-invariant vector fields, with the bracket of vector fields, is the **Lie algebra** of $G$. See [[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group]] and [[Thm - Left-Invariant Vector Fields Form a Lie Algebra]]. Equivalently $\mathfrak{g} \cong T_e G$ as vector spaces, with the bracket transported via the evaluation isomorphism.

> [!tip] Parallelizability of Lie Groups *(from Differential Geometry)*
> Every Lie group is **parallelizable**: its tangent bundle is canonically trivial $TG \cong G \times \mathfrak{g}$ via the left-invariant trivialization $(g, X) \mapsto d(L_g)_e(X)$. As a consequence, $S^1$ and $S^3$ are parallelizable, but $S^2$ (which admits no Lie group structure) is not — there is no nowhere-vanishing vector field on $S^2$ (the "hairy ball theorem").

> [!tip] Maurer–Cartan Form *(from Lie Groups, Advanced)*
> The dual notion to left-invariant vector fields is the **Maurer–Cartan form** $\theta \in \Omega^1(G, \mathfrak{g})$, a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form defined by $\theta_g(v) = d(L_{g^{-1}})_g(v)$. It assigns to each tangent vector at $g$ its left-translated value back to the identity. The Maurer–Cartan form encodes the entire Lie group structure in differential-form language, and satisfies the Maurer–Cartan equation $d\theta + \tfrac{1}{2}[\theta, \theta] = 0$.
