# Notation

$G$ is a Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$. The exponential map is $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ (sometimes $\exp_G$ when the group is ambiguous). For matrix Lie groups, $e^A$ denotes the matrix exponential $\sum_{k \geq 0} A^k/k!$, which coincides with $\exp(A)$. See [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map]] for the full notation registry.

---

# Axiom Motivation

We have, on one side, the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$ — a finite-dimensional vector space with a bracket. On the other side, the Lie group $G$ — a smooth manifold with a group structure. The two are related by linearization (Lie algebra $\to$ tangent space at identity) and we want to construct the reverse direction: a smooth map $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ that integrates infinitesimal data back to group elements.

What should $\exp(X)$ be? The simplest answer is: "follow the curve generated by $X$ for time $1$". Concretely, the [[Def - One-Parameter Subgroup|one-parameter subgroup]] $\gamma_X : \mathbb{R} \to G$ generated by $X$ has initial velocity $X$, and is the unique smooth homomorphism $\mathbb{R} \to G$ with this property. Define $\exp(X) := \gamma_X(1)$. Equivalently, $\exp$ is the integral curve of the left-invariant vector field $X^L$ on $G$ at time $1$, starting from $e$. This is Lee's definition, and it is the cleanest way to construct $\exp$ once one has the existence-and-uniqueness theorem for ODEs in hand.

Several questions arise.

**Why "exponential"?** The name comes from the matrix case. For $G = \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, the one-parameter subgroup with initial velocity $A \in \mathfrak{gl}(n)$ is $\gamma_A(t) = e^{tA}$ where $e^{tA} = \sum_{k \geq 0} (tA)^k/k!$ is the matrix exponential (Lee Prop 20.2). So $\exp(A) = e^A$, the matrix exponential. The general Lie-group exponential map is the abstraction of this — see [[Ex - The Exponential Map of GL(n,R) is the Matrix Exponential]].

**Why is $\exp$ smooth?** Because it is the time-$1$ flow of a smoothly-varying family of vector fields parameterized by $\mathfrak{g}$, and smoothness of flows in the parameter is a basic ODE fact (Lee Prop 20.8(a), proved via the "$(X^L, 0)$ on $G \times \mathfrak{g}$" trick).

**Why is $\exp$ a local diffeomorphism near $0$?** Because the differential $d \exp_0 : T_0 \mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{g} \to T_e G = \mathfrak{g}$ is the identity map (Lee Prop 20.8(e)). The argument is: for $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, the curve $\sigma(t) = tX$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ has $\sigma'(0) = X$, so $d \exp_0(X) = (\exp \circ \sigma)'(0) = \gamma_X'(0) = X$. So $d \exp_0 = \mathrm{id}$, and by the inverse function theorem, $\exp$ is a local diffeomorphism near $0 \in \mathfrak{g}$.

**Why is $\exp$ generally not surjective?** Because the image of $\exp$ is the union of one-parameter subgroups starting at $e$, and this union need not be all of $G$. For connected compact Lie groups, $\exp$ *is* surjective (a theorem using the Hopf-Rinow style argument with a bi-invariant metric). For connected nilpotent Lie groups, $\exp$ is also surjective (BCH terminates, and the inverse can be computed). But for $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ (which is connected but not compact, not nilpotent), $\exp$ misses elements like $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$ (Lee Problem 20-6). In general, every element of the identity component is a *finite product* of exponentials (Lee Prop 7.14), but not necessarily a single exponential.

**Why is $\exp$ generally not a group homomorphism?** Because $\exp(X + Y) \neq \exp(X) \exp(Y)$ in general — they agree only when $[X, Y] = 0$ or $G$ is abelian. The deviation is governed by the [[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map|Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula]]: $\log(\exp X \exp Y) = X + Y + \tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y] + \cdots$, with all higher terms involving nested brackets. So $\exp$ is a group homomorphism iff the bracket vanishes, iff $G$ is abelian on the identity component.

**Why bother with $\exp$ if it is not surjective and not a homomorphism?** Because it is the **central bridge** between the Lie algebra and the Lie group. Three reasons:

1. **Naturality** (Lee Prop 20.8(g)): for any Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$, $F \circ \exp_G = \exp_H \circ F_*$. So $\exp$ intertwines the Lie functor with itself, and many calculations of Lie group homomorphisms reduce to calculations of Lie algebra homomorphisms via this square.
2. **Generation**: the image of $\exp$ generates the identity component (Lee Prop 7.14). So the failure of $\exp$ to be surjective is mitigated by the fact that products of exponentials still cover the identity component.
3. **Local diffeomorphism near $0$** lets one transport calculations from $G$ to $\mathfrak{g}$ near the identity, where they are linear-algebraic.

The four pieces — definition via one-parameter subgroups, smoothness, $d\exp_0 = \mathrm{id}$, naturality — together make $\exp$ the right bridge between the two sides of Lie theory.

# The Definition

Let $G$ be a Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$. The **exponential map** of $G$ is the smooth map

$$\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G, \qquad \exp(X) = \gamma_X(1),$$

where $\gamma_X : \mathbb{R} \to G$ is the unique [[Def - One-Parameter Subgroup|one-parameter subgroup]] with $\gamma_X'(0) = X$ — equivalently, the integral curve of the [[Def - Left-Invariant Vector Field|left-invariant vector field]] $X^L$ starting at $e$, evaluated at time $1$.

The key properties (Lee Prop 20.8):

1. **Smoothness.** $\exp$ is a smooth map $\mathfrak{g} \to G$.
2. **One-parameter subgroup formula.** For $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $t \in \mathbb{R}$, $\exp(tX) = \gamma_X(t)$. So $\exp$ takes lines through the origin in $\mathfrak{g}$ to one-parameter subgroups of $G$.
3. **Additivity along lines.** $\exp((s + t) X) = \exp(sX) \exp(tX)$ for all $s, t \in \mathbb{R}$ and $X \in \mathfrak{g}$. (NOT $\exp(X + Y) = \exp(X) \exp(Y)$ — that fails unless $[X, Y] = 0$.)
4. **Inverse.** $\exp(-X) = (\exp X)^{-1}$.
5. **Differential at $0$.** $(d \exp)_0 : T_0 \mathfrak{g} \to T_e G$ is the identity map, under the canonical identifications $T_0 \mathfrak{g} \cong \mathfrak{g}$ and $T_e G \cong \mathfrak{g}$.
6. **Local diffeomorphism.** By (5) and the inverse function theorem, $\exp$ restricts to a diffeomorphism from some neighborhood of $0 \in \mathfrak{g}$ onto some neighborhood of $e \in G$.
7. **Naturality.** For any Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$, the diagram

	$$
	\begin{array}{ccc}
	\mathfrak{g} & \xrightarrow{F_*} & \mathfrak{h} \\
	\exp_G \downarrow & & \downarrow \exp_H \\
	G & \xrightarrow{F} & H
	\end{array}
	$$

	commutes: $\exp_H \circ F_* = F \circ \exp_G$. See [[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map]].
8. **Flow of $X^L$.** The flow of the left-invariant vector field $X^L$ is $\phi_t^{X^L}(g) = g \exp(tX)$, right-multiplication by the one-parameter subgroup.

For matrix Lie groups $G \leq \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, the exponential map is the matrix exponential: $\exp(A) = e^A = \sum_{k \geq 0} A^k/k!$ — see [[Ex - The Exponential Map of GL(n,R) is the Matrix Exponential]].

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The exponential map is the **canonical bridge from Lie algebra to Lie group**, integrating infinitesimal data $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ to a group element $\exp(X) \in G$. It is the analogue of the matrix exponential, generalized to arbitrary Lie groups, and it is the central tool linking the linear-algebraic structure of $\mathfrak{g}$ to the manifold structure of $G$.

From the [[Def - Flow of a Vector Field|flow side]], $\exp(X)$ is the time-$1$ flow of $X^L$ starting at $e$. From the [[Def - One-Parameter Subgroup|one-parameter subgroup side]], $\exp(X) = \gamma_X(1)$, where $\gamma_X$ is the unique one-parameter subgroup with initial velocity $X$. From the matrix side (for $G \leq \mathrm{GL}(n)$), $\exp(A) = e^A$, the matrix exponential.

**True name:** $\exp$ is **the inverse-function-theorem isomorphism between $\mathfrak{g}$ and $G$ near zero**. Operationally, the key fact is that $d\exp_0 = \mathrm{id}$ — the Lie algebra is the *first-order Taylor expansion* of $\exp$ at zero. So $\exp$ is the diffeomorphism that converts infinitesimal data (vectors in $\mathfrak{g}$) into group elements (points of $G$) up to higher-order corrections, with the BCH formula computing those corrections in terms of nested brackets. The slogan: **the bracket is the second-order correction to "$\exp$ is a homomorphism".**

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: $\exp$ on $(\mathbb{R}^n, +)$.** $\mathfrak{g} = \mathbb{R}^n$, one-parameter subgroups are $\gamma_v(t) = tv$, so $\exp(v) = v$. The exponential map is the **identity** on $\mathbb{R}^n$, and it is a diffeomorphism everywhere.

**Is an instance: $\exp$ on $S^1$.** $\mathfrak{g} = \mathbb{R}$, identifying $T_1 S^1 \cong \mathbb{R}$ via the angle. The one-parameter subgroup generated by $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ is $\gamma_\alpha(t) = e^{i\alpha t}$, so $\exp(\alpha) = e^{i\alpha}$. The exponential map is the standard exponential $\mathbb{R} \to S^1$, $\alpha \mapsto e^{i\alpha}$, which is **surjective** but **not injective** (kernel $2\pi \mathbb{Z}$).

**Is an instance: $\exp$ on $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$.** $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{gl}(n) = M(n, \mathbb{R})$, one-parameter subgroups are $\gamma_A(t) = e^{tA}$ (matrix exponential), so $\exp(A) = e^A$. **Not surjective**: $e^A$ has positive determinant for any $A$ (since $\det e^A = e^{\mathrm{tr} A} > 0$), so $\exp$ misses the components of $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ with negative determinant. Not injective: $e^{2\pi i k} = 1$ in $\mathrm{GL}(1, \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}^\times$ for any integer $k$.

**Is an instance: $\exp$ on $\mathrm{SO}(3)$.** $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{so}(3)$, antisymmetric $3 \times 3$ matrices, which under the "hat" map $\mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathfrak{so}(3)$, $v \mapsto \widehat v$, become $\widehat v = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -v_3 & v_2 \\ v_3 & 0 & -v_1 \\ -v_2 & v_1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. The exponential $\exp(\widehat v) = e^{\widehat v}$ is **Rodrigues' rotation formula**: $e^{\theta \widehat n} = I + \sin\theta \cdot \widehat n + (1 - \cos\theta) \cdot \widehat n^2$ for unit vector $n$. **Surjective** (every rotation is the exponential of some antisymmetric matrix, by spectral decomposition), **not injective** (since rotations by $2\pi$ in the same axis give the identity, $\exp(\theta \widehat n) = \exp((\theta + 2\pi) \widehat n)$).

**Is NOT an instance of "$\exp$ surjective": $\exp$ on $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$.** $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ is connected, but $\exp : \mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb{R}) \to \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ is **not surjective**. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ is not the exponential of any trace-zero matrix (Lee Problem 20-6). However, every element of $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ is a finite product of two exponentials (this is the general Lee Prop 7.14, applied here).

**Is NOT an instance of "$\exp$ a homomorphism": $\exp$ on $\mathrm{SU}(2)$.** Take $X = i \pi \sigma_1$ and $Y = i \pi \sigma_2$ where $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ are Pauli matrices. Then $\exp(X) \exp(Y) = (-I)(-I) = I$ (each is $-I$ since $\exp(i\pi \sigma_a) = \cos(\pi) I + i\sin(\pi)\sigma_a = -I$). On the other hand $\exp(X + Y) = \exp(i\pi(\sigma_1 + \sigma_2))$, and $|\sigma_1 + \sigma_2| = \sqrt{2}$, so $\exp(X + Y) = \cos(\pi\sqrt 2) I + i\sin(\pi\sqrt 2) (\sigma_1 + \sigma_2)/\sqrt 2 \neq I$. The deviation is the obstruction governed by $[X, Y]$ via BCH.

**Corollary ($d\exp_0 = \mathrm{id}$).** The differential of $\exp$ at $0 \in \mathfrak{g}$ is the identity. Hence $\exp$ is a local diffeomorphism near $0$.

**Corollary (additivity along a line).** $\exp(sX) \exp(tX) = \exp((s + t) X)$ for all $s, t \in \mathbb{R}$. So along any line through the origin in $\mathfrak{g}$, $\exp$ *is* a group homomorphism — this is exactly the statement that $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ is a one-parameter subgroup.

**Corollary (exp generates identity component).** The image of $\exp$ generates the connected component of $e$ in $G$ (Lee Prop 7.14): every element of $G_0$ is a finite product of elements of $\exp(\mathfrak{g})$.

**Corollary (naturality).** For $F : G \to H$ a Lie group homomorphism, $F(\exp X) = \exp(F_* X)$ — see [[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map]].

**Calibration check.** If you can (i) compute $\exp$ on $\mathfrak{so}(2)$ as the rotation matrices (using $\exp(\theta J) = \cos\theta I + \sin\theta J$ for $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$); (ii) verify that $\exp$ on $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ is the identity map; and (iii) explain why $\exp$ is not surjective on $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ but is on $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ — you have understood the definition correctly.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff Formula *(from Lie Groups, Advanced)*
> The **Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula** computes $\log(\exp X \exp Y)$ as a series in nested brackets of $X$ and $Y$:
> $$\log(\exp X \exp Y) = X + Y + \tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y] + \tfrac{1}{12}[X, [X, Y]] - \tfrac{1}{12}[Y, [X, Y]] + \cdots$$
> This is the precise statement that the bracket controls the deviation of group multiplication from vector-space addition. On the domain where $\exp$ is a diffeomorphism, BCH **completely reconstructs** the multiplication on $G$ from the bracket on $\mathfrak{g}$.

> [!tip] Lie Correspondence *(from Lie Groups, Advanced)*
> Restricting to connected, simply connected Lie groups, the functor $G \mapsto \mathfrak{g}$ is an equivalence of categories with finite-dimensional Lie algebras (Lee Thm 20.21). The exponential map is the cornerstone of this equivalence: it is how a Lie algebra is "integrated" to a Lie group, and how Lie algebra homomorphisms are lifted to Lie group homomorphisms.

> [!tip] Logarithm and the Inverse of exp *(from Lie Groups, Advanced)*
> On a neighborhood of $e$ in $G$, $\exp$ has a smooth inverse $\log : U \to V$, where $V \ni 0$ is a neighborhood in $\mathfrak{g}$. This $\log$ is the **first chart of the Lie group near the identity**: it gives a canonical identification of a neighborhood of $e$ with a neighborhood of $0$ in $\mathfrak{g}$, with the group operation pulled back to $\mathfrak{g}$ being the BCH product.

> [!tip] Geodesics on Compact Lie Groups *(from Riemannian Geometry)*
> When $G$ is a compact Lie group with a bi-invariant Riemannian metric, the **geodesics** of $G$ through $e$ are exactly the one-parameter subgroups: the Riemannian exponential map equals the Lie group exponential map. This is a deep coincidence linking algebra and geometry, and it implies in particular that compact Lie groups have $\exp$ surjective (since compact Riemannian manifolds have geodesically complete exponential maps reaching every point).
