# Notation

$G$ is a Lie group with identity $e$ and Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$. A one-parameter subgroup is written $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to G$. The initial velocity is $\gamma'(0) \in T_e G \cong \mathfrak{g}$. We write $\gamma_X$ for the one-parameter subgroup with $\gamma_X'(0) = X \in \mathfrak{g}$. See [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map]] for the full notation registry.

---

# Axiom Motivation

The question we want to answer is: given a tangent vector $X \in T_e G$, can we extend it to a curve in $G$ that respects the group structure? The answer is the **one-parameter subgroup** generated by $X$ — a smooth homomorphism $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to G$ from the additive Lie group $\mathbb{R}$, whose initial velocity at $0$ is $X$.

Why **homomorphism** from $\mathbb{R}$? The additive group $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ is the simplest $1$-dimensional Lie group; a homomorphism from $\mathbb{R}$ into $G$ is the natural notion of a "$1$-dimensional subgroup of $G$ traced out by a curve". The homomorphism condition $\gamma(s + t) = \gamma(s) \gamma(t)$ is what makes the curve compatible with the multiplication on both sides — without it, $\gamma$ is just a curve in $G$, with no algebraic content. With it, $\gamma$ is a Lie group homomorphism, and as such it is determined by its derivative at $0$ (since Lie group homomorphisms are determined by their differentials at the identity — see [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]]).

Why **smooth**? Continuous one-parameter subgroups (continuous homomorphisms $\mathbb{R} \to G$) are automatically smooth, by Lee Problem 20-11. So the smoothness condition is no extra restriction — it is forced by continuity plus the homomorphism property. We include "smooth" in the definition for explicitness, but it could be dropped without loss.

The motivating fact, which sits at the heart of the chapter, is the **bijection between one-parameter subgroups and Lie algebra elements**: $\{\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to G \text{ smooth homomorphism}\} \leftrightarrow \mathfrak{g}$, with the bijection sending $\gamma \mapsto \gamma'(0)$. This is a corollary of the existence-and-uniqueness theorem for integral curves applied to left-invariant vector fields: each $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ has a corresponding left-invariant vector field $X^L$ on $G$, the integral curve of $X^L$ starting at $e$ is a smooth curve $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to G$ defined for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$ (left-invariant vector fields are complete, Lee Cor 9.18), and this curve turns out to be a group homomorphism. The argument: for any fixed $s$, both $t \mapsto \gamma(s + t)$ and $t \mapsto \gamma(s) \gamma(t)$ are integral curves of $X^L$ starting at $\gamma(s)$ — the second uses the fact that left translation by $\gamma(s)$ takes integral curves of $X^L$ to integral curves of $X^L$ (because $X^L$ is left-invariant) — and by uniqueness they agree, giving $\gamma(s + t) = \gamma(s) \gamma(t)$.

What goes wrong if we **weaken** the condition by allowing $\gamma$ to be defined on only an open interval around $0$? This is the notion of a **local one-parameter subgroup**, and it is sometimes needed (for instance when the relevant vector field is not complete, in a more general setting). For Lie groups, however, left-invariant vector fields are always complete, so local one-parameter subgroups always extend to global ones. The global definition is the right one.

What if we **strengthen** by allowing $\gamma$ to be a homomorphism from a larger group, say $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{C}$? Then we get a higher-dimensional subgroup of $G$, but not "one-parameter". The terminology specifically refers to $\mathbb{R}$ as the source, which is why "**one**-parameter".

**Why $\mathbb{R}$ and not $S^1$?** A homomorphism $S^1 \to G$ is a one-parameter subgroup that is also *periodic* — its image is a (possibly degenerate) circle subgroup of $G$. These are a proper subclass of one-parameter subgroups; the difference is whether the integral curve eventually returns to $e$. For most $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ the integral curve is non-periodic, but on a compact connected Lie group every one-parameter subgroup eventually returns close to $e$ (by recurrence of geodesics on a compact manifold), even though it may not be exactly periodic. The relation between $\mathbb{R}$-homomorphisms and $S^1$-homomorphisms is that the latter factor through $\mathbb{R} \to S^1$ via the quotient by some lattice $\tau \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{R}$.

The summary: a one-parameter subgroup is the unique smooth curve in $G$ whose initial velocity is $X$ and which is multiplicatively compatible with $\mathbb{R}$ — it is exactly the integral curve of $X^L$ starting at $e$.

# The Definition

A **one-parameter subgroup** of a Lie group $G$ is a smooth Lie group homomorphism

$$\gamma : (\mathbb{R}, +) \to G,$$

i.e., a smooth map $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to G$ satisfying

1. $\gamma(0) = e$,
2. $\gamma(s + t) = \gamma(s) \gamma(t)$ for all $s, t \in \mathbb{R}$.

(Property $1$ follows from $2$ on setting $s = t = 0$.)

The **initial velocity** of $\gamma$ is the tangent vector $\gamma'(0) \in T_e G$. The assignment $\gamma \mapsto \gamma'(0)$ is a bijection

$$\{\text{one-parameter subgroups of } G\} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \mathfrak{g} = T_e G.$$

Concretely, for each $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, there is a unique one-parameter subgroup $\gamma_X$ with $\gamma_X'(0) = X$. It is the integral curve of the [[Def - Left-Invariant Vector Field|left-invariant vector field]] $X^L$ through $e$, extended over all of $\mathbb{R}$ (which is possible because left-invariant vector fields are complete). Explicitly: $\gamma_X(t) = \phi_t^{X^L}(e)$, where $\phi_t^{X^L}$ is the flow of $X^L$.

For matrix Lie groups $G \leq \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, the one-parameter subgroups are exactly the curves $\gamma(t) = e^{tA}$ for $A \in \mathfrak{g} \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{R})$ (Lee Prop 20.2 and Example 20.4), where $e^{tA} = \sum_{k \geq 0} (tA)^k/k!$ is the **matrix exponential**.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A one-parameter subgroup is the **integration of an element $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ to a curve in $G$**: it is the unique extension of the infinitesimal data "$X$ at $e$" to global data "$\gamma_X : \mathbb{R} \to G$" compatible with the group structure. In the language of flows, it is the integral curve of $X^L$ through $e$, extended to all of $\mathbb{R}$ by completeness.

**True name:** A one-parameter subgroup is **the flow line through $e$ of the left-invariant vector field $X^L$**. By Lee Prop 20.8(h), the flow of $X^L$ is right-multiplication by $\gamma_X(t)$: $\phi_t^{X^L}(g) = g \gamma_X(t)$. So the one-parameter subgroup is the integral curve through $e$, and the global flow is "translate this curve to every point of $G$ by right multiplication". This is operationally the right thing to remember: one-parameter subgroups are flow lines.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $\gamma(t) = at$.** For any $a \in \mathbb{R}$, the map $t \mapsto at$ is a one-parameter subgroup of $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ with $\gamma'(0) = a$. So one-parameter subgroups of $\mathbb{R}$ are exactly the linear maps $t \mapsto at$, in bijection with $\mathbb{R} = \mathrm{Lie}(\mathbb{R})$.

**Is an instance: $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to S^1$, $\gamma(t) = e^{i\alpha t}$.** For $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, the map $t \mapsto e^{i\alpha t}$ is a one-parameter subgroup of $S^1$ with $\gamma'(0) = i\alpha \in i\mathbb{R} = T_1 S^1 \cong \mathbb{R}$. The image is all of $S^1$ if $\alpha \neq 0$, and is dense vs periodic depending on whether $\alpha$ is rational vs irrational multiple of $\pi$... actually $\gamma$ is periodic with period $2\pi/\alpha$ for any nonzero $\alpha$, so the image is always $S^1$.

**Is an instance: $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, $\gamma(t) = e^{tA}$.** For $A \in \mathfrak{gl}(n) = M(n, \mathbb{R})$, the matrix exponential $e^{tA}$ is a one-parameter subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ with $\gamma'(0) = A$. The group property $e^{(s+t)A} = e^{sA} e^{tA}$ comes from the fact that the series satisfies the ODE $\gamma'(t) = A \gamma(t) = \gamma(t) A$ (since $A$ commutes with itself), and the uniqueness of solutions. See [[Ex - The Exponential Map of GL(n,R) is the Matrix Exponential]].

**Is an instance: $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to \mathrm{SO}(2)$, rotation.** The map $\gamma(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t \\ \sin t & \cos t \end{pmatrix}$ is a one-parameter subgroup of $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, with $\gamma'(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathfrak{so}(2)$. It is periodic with period $2\pi$.

**Is an instance: the irrational winding $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to T^2$.** For irrational $\alpha$, $\gamma(t) = (e^{2\pi i t}, e^{2\pi i \alpha t})$ is a one-parameter subgroup of $T^2$ with image dense in $T^2$. Not periodic (irrationality means the image never closes up), and image is *not* an embedded submanifold.

**Is NOT an instance: a smooth curve that does not satisfy $\gamma(s + t) = \gamma(s) \gamma(t)$.** The curve $\gamma(t) = (1 + t)$ in $\mathbb{R}^\times = \mathrm{GL}(1, \mathbb{R})$ is smooth and satisfies $\gamma(0) = 1$, but $\gamma(s) \gamma(t) = (1 + s)(1 + t) = 1 + s + t + st \neq \gamma(s + t) = 1 + s + t$. So it is a smooth curve but not a one-parameter subgroup. The correct one-parameter subgroup of $\mathbb{R}^\times$ through $1$ with initial velocity $1$ is $\gamma(t) = e^t$.

**Corollary (existence and uniqueness).** For every $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, there exists a unique one-parameter subgroup $\gamma_X : \mathbb{R} \to G$ with $\gamma_X'(0) = X$. *Proof:* it is the integral curve of $X^L$ through $e$, which exists globally (left-invariant vector fields are complete) and is unique (by uniqueness of integral curves).

**Corollary (rescaling).** $\gamma_{cX}(t) = \gamma_X(ct)$ for any $c \in \mathbb{R}$. *Proof:* the curve $t \mapsto \gamma_X(ct)$ has initial velocity $c \gamma_X'(0) = cX$, and the rescaling factor multiplies the velocity but preserves the group-homomorphism property. By uniqueness of one-parameter subgroups with prescribed initial velocity, $\gamma_X(ct) = \gamma_{cX}(t)$.

**Corollary (one-parameter subgroups generate the identity component).** The image of $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ generates the connected component of the identity (Lee Prop 7.14). For connected $G$, every element is a finite product of exponentials. This is the source of the importance of one-parameter subgroups: they are not just a few curves in $G$, they cover the identity component multiplicatively.

**Corollary (the image is either $\mathbb{R}$, $S^1$, or trivial).** The image of a non-trivial one-parameter subgroup is a connected $1$-dimensional Lie subgroup of $G$, hence isomorphic as a Lie group to either $\mathbb{R}$ or $S^1$ (Lee Problem 20-1).

**Calibration check.** If you can (i) verify that $\gamma(t) = e^{tA}$ for $A \in M(n)$ is a one-parameter subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}(n)$; (ii) check the bijection between $\mathfrak{g}$ and one-parameter subgroups via the initial-velocity map; and (iii) state and use the rescaling identity $\gamma_{cX}(t) = \gamma_X(ct)$ — you have understood the definition correctly.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The Exponential Map *(from this chapter)*
> The **exponential map** $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is defined by $\exp(X) = \gamma_X(1)$, where $\gamma_X$ is the one-parameter subgroup with initial velocity $X$. Equivalently $\gamma_X(t) = \exp(tX)$, so one-parameter subgroups are parametrized lines in the image of $\exp$. See [[Def - Exponential Map of a Lie Group]].

> [!tip] Generation of $G$ by Exponentials *(from this chapter)*
> The image of $\exp$ generates the identity component of $G$ (Lee Prop 7.14). This is the algorithmic content of "the Lie algebra reconstructs the group": every element of the identity component is a finite product of exponentials, with each factor coming from $\mathfrak{g}$ via $\exp$.

> [!tip] Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff Formula *(from Lie Groups, Advanced)*
> The product of two one-parameter subgroups is not a one-parameter subgroup in general; the deviation is governed by the BCH formula $\log(\exp X \exp Y) = X + Y + \tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y] + \cdots$. This is the precise sense in which the bracket measures the failure of $\exp$ to be a group homomorphism.

> [!tip] Quantum Mechanical Time Evolution *(from Quantum Mechanics)*
> In quantum mechanics, the time-evolution operator $U(t) = e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ is a one-parameter subgroup of the unitary group on Hilbert space, generated by the Hamiltonian $H \in i\mathfrak{u}(\mathcal{H})$. The Schrödinger equation $i\hbar \, d\psi/dt = H\psi$ is the ODE for the integral curves of the left-invariant vector field corresponding to $H$, and stationary states are eigenvectors of $H$.
