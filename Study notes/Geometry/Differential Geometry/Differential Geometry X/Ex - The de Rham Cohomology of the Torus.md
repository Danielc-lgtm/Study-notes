# Problem Statement

Show that the de Rham cohomology of the $n$-torus $T^n = (S^1)^n$ is

$$H^k_{dR}(T^n) = \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{k}} \qquad \text{for } 0 \leq k \leq n.$$

Identify an explicit basis: for each $k$-element subset $\{i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k\}$ of $\{1, \dots, n\}$, the wedge product $d\theta^{i_1} \wedge d\theta^{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge d\theta^{i_k}$ — where $\theta^j$ is the angular coordinate on the $j$-th $S^1$ factor — gives a closed form whose cohomology class is a basis element.

**Recall:**

![[Def - de Rham Cohomology#The Definition]]

![[Thm - The Mayer-Vietoris Sequence#Statement]]

The cohomology of $S^1$: $H^0_{dR}(S^1) = \mathbb{R}$ (connected), $H^1_{dR}(S^1) = \mathbb{R}$ (generated by $[d\theta]$, the closed-but-not-exact angular form). Higher: $H^k_{dR}(S^1) = 0$ for $k \geq 2$ (dimensional reasons, no $k$-forms in dimension $1$).

**Künneth formula (stated as a forward result, proved via Mayer–Vietoris):** For smooth manifolds $M, N$ of finite cohomology, $H^k_{dR}(M \times N) = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} H^p_{dR}(M) \otimes_\mathbb{R} H^q_{dR}(N)$, with the isomorphism given on representatives by $[\omega] \otimes [\eta] \mapsto [\pi_M^*\omega \wedge \pi_N^*\eta]$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** Compute de Rham cohomology of a *product manifold* — specifically, an iterated product $T^n = S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1$. The natural route is to use the **Künneth formula** to express $H^*(T^n)$ as a tensor power of $H^*(S^1)$, then count [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]].

**Assumption pattern:** $T^n$ decomposes as a product, with each factor having known cohomology ($H^0(S^1) = H^1(S^1) = \mathbb{R}$, others zero). The Künneth formula applies because each factor has finite-dimensional cohomology. The structure of $H^*(T^n)$ as a graded $\mathbb{R}$-algebra is then forced.

**Theorem routing:** [[Thm - The Mayer-Vietoris Sequence|Mayer–Vietoris]] computes $H^*(S^1) = (\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}, 0, \dots)$ (two-arc cover of $S^1$, intersection two contractible arcs). The Künneth formula (stated as a forward result, derivable from iterated Mayer–Vietoris on $T^n = T^{n-1} \times S^1$) gives $H^*(T^n) = H^*(S^1)^{\otimes n}$. Counting in each degree gives $H^k(T^n) = \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{k}}$.

**Key decision point:** The choice to use Künneth (rather than directly iterating Mayer–Vietoris on $T^n$) is what makes the computation tractable. The product structure of $T^n$ is essential; without it, computing the cohomology of an $n$-manifold would require explicit good covers and many sequential Mayer–Vietoris applications. The Künneth formula packages this into a single tensor-product computation.

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Use [[Def - Homotopy|homotopy]] invariance to replace $M$ with a [[Def - Homotopy|homotopy]]-equivalent simpler space** (operation 7 from the topic page). $T^n$ is *not* homotopy equivalent to anything simpler — it is the homotopy-theoretic skeleton of a wedge of spheres in some senses, but no contractible space matches it. So we work with $T^n$ directly. However, in computing $H^*(S^1)$ we use homotopy invariance: $S^1$ minus a point is contractible (homotopy equivalent to a point).

2. **Cut into pieces and assemble via Mayer–Vietoris** (operation 8 from the topic page). For each step in the inductive computation $H^*(T^k)$ from $H^*(T^{k-1})$, use Mayer–Vietoris on $T^k$ with cover $T^{k-1} \times U \cup T^{k-1} \times V$ for $\{U, V\}$ a two-arc cover of $S^1$.

3. **Detect non-trivial cohomology classes by integration over a cycle** (operation 3 from the topic page). To verify that $[d\theta^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge d\theta^{i_k}]$ is a non-trivial basis element, integrate over the $k$-cycle that is the corresponding sub-torus $T^k \hookrightarrow T^n$ and observe the integral is $(2\pi)^k \neq 0$.

# Solution

The proof has three steps. Step 1 computes $H^*(S^1) = (\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}, 0, \dots)$ using the two-arc cover and Mayer–Vietoris. Step 2 applies the Künneth formula inductively: $H^*(T^n) = H^*(S^1)^{\otimes n}$. Step 3 verifies the explicit basis by integration over sub-tori.

**Step 1: $H^*(S^1) = (\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R}, 0, 0, \dots)$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Cover $S^1$ by two open arcs $U = S^1 \setminus \{N\}$ (omitting the "north pole") and $V = S^1 \setminus \{S\}$ (omitting the "south pole"). Each is diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$ (an arc, contractible), so $H^*(U) = H^*(V) = (\mathbb{R}, 0, 0, \dots)$. The intersection $U \cap V$ is two disjoint open arcs, each contractible, so $H^*(U \cap V) = (\mathbb{R}^2, 0, 0, \dots)$.
>
> By Mayer–Vietoris:
> $$0 \to H^0(S^1) \to H^0(U) \oplus H^0(V) \to H^0(U \cap V) \to H^1(S^1) \to 0 \to 0 \to \cdots$$
> i.e. $0 \to H^0(S^1) \to \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2 \to H^1(S^1) \to 0$.
>
> The map $\mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is $(a, b) \mapsto (a - b, a - b)$ (restriction of $(f_U, f_V)$ to two components of $U \cap V$, taking differences); its image is the diagonal $\{(x, x)\}$, $1$-dimensional, and its kernel is the diagonal $\{(a, a)\}$, $1$-dimensional. So $\dim H^0(S^1) = 1$ (matches: $S^1$ connected) and $\dim H^1(S^1) = 2 - 1 = 1$.
>
> For $k \geq 2$, all flanking terms vanish, so $H^k(S^1) = 0$.
>
> An explicit generator of $H^1(S^1)$: the closed but not exact $1$-form $d\theta$ on $S^1$ (defined locally by $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$, which has $d\theta = (-y\,dx + x\,dy)/(x^2 + y^2)$ globally on $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$, restricted to $S^1$). The integral $\int_{S^1} d\theta = 2\pi$ confirms non-triviality.

**Step 2: $H^*(T^n) = H^*(S^1)^{\otimes n}$, equivalently $H^k(T^n) = \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{k}}$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> By the **Künneth formula** (stated as a forward result; proved inductively from Mayer–Vietoris on $M \times N$ for $M$, $N$ each admitting good covers), for smooth manifolds with finite-dimensional cohomology:
> $$H^k_{dR}(M \times N) = \bigoplus_{p + q = k} H^p_{dR}(M) \otimes_\mathbb{R} H^q_{dR}(N).$$
>
> Apply with $M = S^1$, $N = T^{n-1}$, inducting on $n$. The base case $n = 1$ is Step 1: $H^*(T^1) = H^*(S^1)$.
>
> Inductive step: assuming $H^k(T^{n-1}) = \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n-1}{k}}$, the Künneth formula gives
> $$H^k(T^n) = \bigoplus_{p+q=k} H^p(S^1) \otimes H^q(T^{n-1}) = H^0(S^1) \otimes H^k(T^{n-1}) \oplus H^1(S^1) \otimes H^{k-1}(T^{n-1})$$
> $$= \mathbb{R} \otimes \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n-1}{k}} \oplus \mathbb{R} \otimes \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n-1}{k-1}} = \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n-1}{k} + \binom{n-1}{k-1}} = \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{k}},$$
> using Pascal's identity $\binom{n-1}{k} + \binom{n-1}{k-1} = \binom{n}{k}$.

**Step 3: Explicit basis and verification by integration.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Let $\theta^j$ be the angular coordinate on the $j$-th $S^1$ factor of $T^n = S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1$. For each $k$-subset $I = \{i_1 < \cdots < i_k\} \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$, the form
> $$\omega_I := d\theta^{i_1} \wedge d\theta^{i_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge d\theta^{i_k}$$
> is a smooth $k$-form on $T^n$ (well-defined globally because each $d\theta^j$ is global on $S^1$, even though $\theta^j$ itself isn't). It is closed: $d\omega_I = \sum_l (-1)^{l-1} d\theta^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge d(d\theta^{i_l}) \wedge \cdots = 0$, since $d^2\theta^{i_l} = 0$.
>
> To verify the $\omega_I$ are linearly independent in $H^k(T^n)$: integrate each over the sub-torus $T_I \hookrightarrow T^n$ defined by holding all coordinates *not* in $I$ fixed (say at 0) and varying the coordinates in $I$. The sub-torus $T_I$ is itself a $k$-torus, and on it $\omega_I = d\theta^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge d\theta^{i_k}$ restricts to the standard volume form (up to normalization). Compute:
> $$\int_{T_I} \omega_I = \int_0^{2\pi} \cdots \int_0^{2\pi} d\theta^{i_1} \cdots d\theta^{i_k} = (2\pi)^k.$$
>
> For $J \neq I$ with $|J| = k$: $\omega_J$ contains some $d\theta^{j_l}$ for $j_l \notin I$. Restricted to $T_I$ (where $\theta^{j_l}$ is constant), $d\theta^{j_l}|_{T_I} = 0$. So $\omega_J|_{T_I} = 0$, and $\int_{T_I}\omega_J = 0$.
>
> The integration pairing $H^k(T^n) \times H_k(T^n) \to \mathbb{R}$, $([\omega], [c]) \mapsto \int_c \omega$, has the $\omega_I$ pairing diagonally with the $T_I$'s: $\langle\omega_I, T_J\rangle = (2\pi)^k \delta_{IJ}$. This is a non-degenerate pairing on the $\binom{n}{k}$-element set, so the $[\omega_I]$ are linearly independent.
>
> By dimension count (Step 2), they form a basis.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Combine Steps 1, 2, 3. Step 1 establishes $H^*(S^1)$ via Mayer–Vietoris on the two-arc cover. Step 2 uses the Künneth formula (stated, derivable via Mayer–Vietoris on $M \times N$) to inductively compute $H^*(T^n) = H^*(S^1)^{\otimes n}$, giving the dimension count $\binom{n}{k}$ in each degree. Step 3 exhibits the explicit basis $\{[\omega_I]\}_{I \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}, |I|=k}$ where $\omega_I = d\theta^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge d\theta^{i_k}$, verifying linear independence by integration over sub-tori: $\int_{T_I}\omega_J = (2\pi)^k\delta_{IJ}$.
>
> Conclusion: $H^k_{dR}(T^n) = \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{k}}$, with basis the wedges of coordinate $1$-forms. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**The Künneth formula reduces cohomology of products to tensor products of cohomologies.** This is the most powerful single tool for computing cohomology of product manifolds. Whenever a manifold has a product structure $M \times N$, the Künneth formula breaks the cohomology computation into pieces that can be done separately on each factor and then combined algebraically. The torus is the prototype application — every cohomology computation of $T^n$, $T^n \times S^k$, $S^p \times S^q$, etc. uses Künneth at some point. The trigger to recognize: any manifold that is a Cartesian product (in the smooth-manifold sense) should be tackled via Künneth before resorting to direct Mayer–Vietoris.

**Cohomology classes of wedges of $d\theta^j$ are detected by integration over sub-tori.** The integration pairing $(\omega, c) \mapsto \int_c \omega$ between de Rham cohomology and singular homology is non-degenerate (by the de Rham theorem). For the torus, the pairing is particularly transparent: $\omega_I$ pairs non-trivially with $T_I$ and trivially with $T_J$ for $J \neq I$, giving an explicit dual basis. This pattern recurs whenever a manifold has a natural family of submanifolds that "see" different cohomology classes — the torus is the simplest example, but the same principle applies to projective spaces (Schubert cycles dual to Chern classes), Grassmannians, and flag varieties. The trigger to recognize: any cohomology class on a manifold with a "preferred" homology basis can be detected by integration over a basis cycle.

**The wedge product on $H^*_{dR}$ makes the cohomology a graded-commutative [[Def - Ring|ring]].** Beyond just being a graded vector space, $H^*_{dR}(M)$ is also a [[Def - Ring|ring]] under $[\omega] \cup [\eta] := [\omega \wedge \eta]$. For the torus, this gives $H^*_{dR}(T^n) \cong \Lambda^*(\mathbb{R}^n)$ — the exterior algebra on $n$ generators (one for each $d\theta^j$). The graded-commutative structure $[\omega] \cup [\eta] = (-1)^{|\omega||\eta|}[\eta]\cup[\omega]$ encodes more than just [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]]: it tells you the multiplicative relations between cohomology classes. This is the bridge from "Betti numbers" to the full ring structure, and the **cup product** is the topological analogue of the wedge product. The trigger: when computing cohomology *with structure*, remember that $H^*$ is a ring, not just a vector space.

**The result $H^k(T^n) = \mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{k}}$ matches the Betti numbers of the topological $n$-torus.** By the [[Thm - The de Rham Theorem (Statement)|de Rham theorem]], the de Rham cohomology equals the singular cohomology, and the dimensions are the Betti numbers $b_k(T^n)$. The Poincaré polynomial of $T^n$ is $(1 + t)^n$, which expands to $\sum_k \binom{n}{k} t^k$ — matching our computation. The Euler characteristic is $\chi(T^n) = (1-1)^n = 0$ for $n \geq 1$ — consistent with the fact that $T^n$ is parallelizable (has a global frame) and hence has vanishing Euler class. This is a quintessential example of "smooth-form computation gives topological invariants." *Companion exercise:* [[Ex - Computing H^1 of S^1 via Mayer-Vietoris]] establishes the base case used in Step 1.
