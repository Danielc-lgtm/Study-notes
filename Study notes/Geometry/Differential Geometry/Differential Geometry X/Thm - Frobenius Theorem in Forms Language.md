# Notation

$M$ is a smooth $n$-manifold; $D$ is a smooth distribution of rank $k$ on $M$ — see [[Def - Distribution on a Manifold]]. A $1$-form $\theta$ **annihilates $D$** if $\theta(X) = 0$ for every section $X \in \Gamma(D)$; more generally, a $p$-form $\omega$ annihilates $D$ if $\omega(X_1, \dots, X_p) = 0$ whenever every $X_i \in \Gamma(D)$. The set of forms annihilating $D$ is the **annihilating ideal** $\mathcal{I}(D) = \bigoplus_p \mathcal{I}^p(D) \subseteq \Omega^*(M)$, a graded ideal of the form algebra. An ideal $\mathcal{I} \subseteq \Omega^*(M)$ is a **differential ideal** if $d\mathcal{I} \subseteq \mathcal{I}$.

Local **defining $1$-forms** for $D$ on an open set $U$ are $1$-forms $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}$ on $U$, pointwise linearly independent, such that $D_q = \bigcap_i \ker \omega^i_q$ for $q \in U$.

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (Frobenius Theorem in Forms Language).** Let $M$ be a smooth manifold and $D$ a smooth distribution of rank $k$ on $M$. The following are equivalent:
>
> (a) $D$ is **involutive**: $[X, Y] \in \Gamma(D)$ for all $X, Y \in \Gamma(D)$.
>
> (b) For every local defining $1$-form $\theta$ of $D$, $d\theta$ also annihilates $D$.
>
> (c) The annihilating ideal $\mathcal{I}(D)$ is a **differential ideal**: $d\mathcal{I}(D) \subseteq \mathcal{I}(D)$.
>
> (d) For local defining $1$-forms $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}$ on $U$, there exist smooth $1$-forms $\alpha^i_j$ on $U$ ($i, j = 1, \dots, n-k$) such that
> $$d\omega^i = \sum_{j=1}^{n-k} \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j \qquad \text{for each } i.$$
>
> (e) For local defining $1$-forms $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}$ on $U$,
> $$d\omega^i \wedge \omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k} = 0 \qquad \text{for each } i.$$

> **Corollary (codimension-1 case).** For a rank-$(n-1)$ distribution $D = \ker \omega$ given by a single nowhere-vanishing $1$-form $\omega$, $D$ is involutive iff $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$. When this holds and $\omega$ is nowhere vanishing, $\omega = \lambda \, df$ locally for a positive function $\lambda$ (the **integrating factor**) and a function $f$; the integral hypersurfaces are level sets of $f$.

# Motivation

The vector-field version of [[Thm - The Frobenius Theorem|Frobenius]] is the natural statement when a distribution is presented by spanning vector fields, but many distributions arise as kernels of $1$-forms — particularly when they come from physical constraints (a mechanical constraint $\omega(\dot q) = 0$) or geometric structures (a contact $1$-form, the canonical $1$-form on a cotangent bundle). For these, we need a Frobenius criterion expressed entirely in forms.

The bridge between the two formulations is Cartan's identity:
$$d\theta(X, Y) = X(\theta(Y)) - Y(\theta(X)) - \theta([X, Y]).$$
If $\theta$ annihilates $D$ ($\theta(X) = 0$ for $X \in \Gamma(D)$), then for $X, Y \in \Gamma(D)$ the first two terms vanish, leaving $d\theta(X, Y) = -\theta([X, Y])$. Involutivity says $[X, Y] \in \Gamma(D)$, so $\theta([X, Y]) = 0$, so $d\theta(X, Y) = 0$. The converse is the same argument run backwards: if $d\theta(X, Y) = 0$ for every annihilating $\theta$ and every $X, Y \in \Gamma(D)$, then $\theta([X, Y]) = 0$ for every annihilating $\theta$, so $[X, Y] \in \bigcap_\theta \ker \theta = D$. This bridge is the content of the forms-language Frobenius.

The conceptual content: *involutivity is a closure condition for $D$ under brackets; differential-ideal closure is the dual closure condition for $\mathcal{I}(D)$ under $d$.* The two are equivalent because brackets and exterior derivatives are duals to each other via Cartan's formula. So the forms-language theorem is "the same theorem read upside down."

The forms version is often *more computable* in practice. For a rank-$(n-1)$ distribution defined by a single $1$-form $\omega$, the involutivity test is the single algebraic identity $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$. This is a direct check on the coefficients of $\omega$ — no commuting frame to construct, no brackets to compute. The condition $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$ on a single $1$-form is the **integrability condition** of classical mechanics and the contact-versus-foliation dichotomy of geometric topology.

The criterion (e), $d\omega^i \wedge \omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k} = 0$, is what naturally generalizes "$\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$" to higher codimension. It says the exterior derivative of each defining form, when wedged with all the defining forms together, gives zero — the geometric meaning being that $d\omega^i$ lies in the ideal generated by the $\omega^j$.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The precondition $A$ is: *$D$ is given by annihilating $1$-forms $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}$, and we want to test involutivity.*

The first disguised source is **a velocity constraint in mechanics.** Property $B$: a physical system has a constraint $\omega(\dot q) = 0$ for some $1$-form $\omega$ on configuration space $Q$. The bridge: the constraint distribution $D = \ker \omega$ has involutivity testable by $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$, equivalently the holonomicity of the constraint. *Example application:* a skate's velocity is constrained to be along the blade, $\omega = -\sin\phi\,dx + \cos\phi\,dy = 0$ for skate orientation $\phi$; the constraint is non-holonomic iff $\omega \wedge d\omega \neq 0$ identically. Direct computation: $d\omega = -\cos\phi\,d\phi \wedge dx - \sin\phi\,d\phi \wedge dy$, so $\omega \wedge d\omega = d\phi \wedge dx \wedge dy \neq 0$.

The second disguised source is **a Pfaffian system in classical PDE.** Property $B$: a "Pfaffian system" is a system of $1$-form equations $\omega^1 = 0, \dots, \omega^r = 0$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$, asking when these define a $k$-dimensional submanifold. The bridge: this is exactly the distribution $D = \bigcap_i \ker \omega^i$, and integrability is the forms-language Frobenius condition. *Example application:* the classical theory of total differential equations $\sum P_i\,dx^i = 0$, with integrability tested by $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$.

The third disguised source is **a contact $1$-form in geometric optics or thermodynamics.** Property $B$: a single $1$-form $\alpha$ on an odd-dimensional manifold satisfying $\alpha \wedge (d\alpha)^n \neq 0$ everywhere — the maximal non-degeneracy condition. The bridge: the *negation* of the involutivity condition $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$, taken to an extreme — contact $1$-forms are *maximally non-involutive*. The forms-language Frobenius says "involutive iff $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$"; the contact condition says "$\omega$ maximally violates this." *Example:* the standard contact form $\alpha = dz - y\,dx$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ is maximally non-involutive (computed in [[Ex - A Non-Integrable Distribution on R^3 from the Standard Contact Form]]).

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion $C$: *involutivity is equivalent to differential-ideal closure of $\mathcal{I}(D)$.*

Combine $C$ with **explicit defining forms.** The condition $d\omega^i = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j$ for some $1$-forms $\alpha^i_j$ is a *positive* statement about the structure of $d\omega^i$ — it forces a specific form of the exterior derivative. The further result $E$: integrability implies a specific decomposition of $d\omega^i$ in terms of the $\omega^j$ and "structure forms" $\alpha^i_j$. This is the basis of the **Cartan structure equations** in Lie theory and Riemannian geometry (which run the same machinery with $\omega^j$ replaced by a coframe and $\alpha^i_j$ by connection $1$-forms).

Combine $C$ with **integration to a primitive function.** For a codimension-$1$ distribution $D = \ker \omega$ with $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$, Frobenius produces foliation leaves; locally, the leaves are level sets of some function $f$, and $\omega = \lambda \, df$ for an **integrating factor** $\lambda$. The further result $E$: every involutive codimension-$1$ Pfaffian system can be put in the form $df = 0$ after multiplication by an integrating factor — the classical theory of integrating factors. *Example:* in thermodynamics, $\delta Q$ is not closed (not a state function), but $\delta Q/T = dS$ is — with $T$ the absolute temperature as the integrating factor.

Combine $C$ with **higher exterior products of the constraint forms.** The differential-ideal condition $d\mathcal{I}(D) \subseteq \mathcal{I}(D)$ can be unpacked further: $d(\omega^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{i_p})$ must lie in $\mathcal{I}(D)$ for every multi-index. This recovers all the algebraic identities involving wedges of $d\omega^i$'s. The further result $E$: the entire algebra of forms vanishing on $D$ is preserved by $d$, giving a sub-complex $(\mathcal{I}(D), d) \subseteq (\Omega^*(M), d)$ whose cohomology is a foliation invariant.

# Why Is It True

**The single sentence: Cartan's identity $d\theta(X, Y) = X\theta(Y) - Y\theta(X) - \theta([X, Y])$ translates "$\theta$ annihilates $[X, Y]$" into "$d\theta$ annihilates the pair $(X, Y)$," up to derivative terms that vanish on sections of $D$.**

The argument has two halves, both running on Cartan's identity.

*Involutive $\Longrightarrow$ $d\theta$ annihilates $D$.* Let $\theta$ be a $1$-form annihilating $D$ on $U$, so $\theta(X) = 0$ for every $X \in \Gamma(D|_U)$. Take any $X, Y \in \Gamma(D|_U)$. Cartan's identity gives
$$d\theta(X, Y) = X(\theta(Y)) - Y(\theta(X)) - \theta([X, Y]).$$
The first two terms vanish: $\theta(Y) = 0$ and $\theta(X) = 0$ identically, so taking derivatives of zero gives zero. The third term: by involutivity $[X, Y] \in \Gamma(D)$, so $\theta([X, Y]) = 0$. Hence $d\theta(X, Y) = 0$, meaning $d\theta$ annihilates the pair $(X, Y)$. Since $X, Y$ were arbitrary sections of $D$, $d\theta$ annihilates $D$.

*$d\theta$ annihilates $D$ $\Longrightarrow$ involutive.* Let $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}$ be local defining forms for $D$, so $D = \bigcap_i \ker \omega^i$. Suppose each $d\omega^i$ annihilates $D$ (i.e. $d\omega^i(X, Y) = 0$ for $X, Y \in \Gamma(D)$). For $X, Y \in \Gamma(D)$, Cartan's identity gives
$$\omega^i([X, Y]) = X(\omega^i(Y)) - Y(\omega^i(X)) - d\omega^i(X, Y) = 0 - 0 - 0 = 0.$$
So $\omega^i([X, Y]) = 0$ for every $i$, meaning $[X, Y] \in \bigcap_i \ker \omega^i = D$. Hence $D$ is involutive.

The remaining equivalences (a) $\Leftrightarrow$ (c) $\Leftrightarrow$ (d) $\Leftrightarrow$ (e) are formal manipulations of the annihilating ideal:

- (c) and (b) are equivalent because $\mathcal{I}(D)$ is the *ideal* generated (as an ideal of the form algebra) by the $1$-forms annihilating $D$; the differential of any ideal element decomposes into terms each of which annihilates $D$ iff each $d\omega^i$ does (using the Leibniz rule for $d$).

- (d) is a restatement of (b): the condition $d\omega^i$ annihilates $D$ at the form level is equivalent to $d\omega^i$ lying in the *ideal* $\mathcal{I}(D)$ generated by $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}$, which by the structure of forms means $d\omega^i = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j$ for some $1$-forms $\alpha^i_j$ (Lemma 19.6 in Lee).

- (e) is a restatement of (d): if $d\omega^i = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j$, then $d\omega^i \wedge \omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k} = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j \wedge \omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k}$, and each term has a repeated $\omega^j$ factor (since $\omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k}$ already contains $\omega^j$), making the wedge zero. Conversely, if $d\omega^i \wedge \omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k} = 0$, expanding $d\omega^i$ in a coframe extending the $\omega^j$ shows that the components transverse to the $\omega$-span must vanish — hence $d\omega^i$ lies in the ideal.

The wedge condition (e) is the most computationally efficient: a single algebraic identity that one can check on the coefficients of $\omega^i$ via direct calculation.

# What Makes This Hard

The conceptual obstacle is the **duality between brackets and exterior derivatives** — that the algebraic operation $d$ on forms is the same operation as $[\cdot, \cdot]$ on vector fields, viewed dually. Once this is internalized, the entire theorem is essentially one application of Cartan's identity, but the duality itself can take time to appreciate. The non-obvious step is **recognizing that Cartan's identity transmits all the information**: the two sides of "$\theta$ annihilates $D$" (involving $\theta(X)$ values) and "$d\theta$ annihilates $D$" (involving the bracket through Cartan's identity) are bridged by exactly the three-term formula, with two terms vanishing by the annihilation hypothesis. The most common error is to try to prove the forms version by direct computation in coordinates, missing the cleaner Cartan-identity proof.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** Use Cartan's identity $d\theta(X, Y) = X\theta(Y) - Y\theta(X) - \theta([X, Y])$ to transmit between "$\theta$ annihilates" data and "$d\theta$ annihilates" data; the involutivity-equivalent term is $\theta([X, Y])$, which is forced to zero by annihilation iff $[X, Y] \in D$.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Recall Cartan's identity for $d$ of a $1$-form.** For any smooth $1$-form $\theta$ and vector fields $X, Y$ on $M$:
   $$d\theta(X, Y) = X(\theta(Y)) - Y(\theta(X)) - \theta([X, Y]).$$
   - *Hint:* This is a special case of the general formula for the exterior derivative — see [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold]].
   - *Why needed:* This is the master identity.

2. **Direction (a) $\Longrightarrow$ (b): involutive $\Longrightarrow$ $d\theta$ annihilates $D$.** Let $\theta$ annihilate $D$ and $X, Y \in \Gamma(D)$. Apply Cartan's identity; the first two terms vanish (annihilation), the third vanishes (involutivity, $[X, Y] \in \Gamma(D)$).
   - *Hint:* Just substitute zeros for the two "$\theta$ on a section of $D$" terms.
   - *Why needed:* The forward direction.

3. **Direction (b) $\Longrightarrow$ (a).** Let local defining forms $\omega^i$ satisfy "$d\omega^i$ annihilates $D$" and take $X, Y \in \Gamma(D)$. Apply Cartan's identity; the first two terms vanish (each $\omega^i$ annihilates the sections), and the $d\omega^i$ term vanishes (hypothesis), so the third term $\omega^i([X, Y])$ must vanish for every $i$. Hence $[X, Y] \in \bigcap_i \ker \omega^i = D$.
   - *Hint:* This is the reverse direction of Step 2, run on local defining forms.
   - *Why needed:* The backward direction.

4. **Equivalence (b) $\Leftrightarrow$ (c).** $\mathcal{I}(D)$ is the ideal generated by annihilating $1$-forms; by Lemma 19.6 in Lee, $\eta \in \mathcal{I}^p(D)$ iff locally $\eta = \sum_i \omega^i \wedge \beta^i$ for $(p-1)$-forms $\beta^i$. Then $d\eta = \sum_i (d\omega^i \wedge \beta^i - \omega^i \wedge d\beta^i)$. The second sum is in $\mathcal{I}(D)$ (each term has an $\omega^i$ factor); the first is in $\mathcal{I}(D)$ iff each $d\omega^i$ is.
   - *Hint:* This is a Leibniz-rule consequence of the structure of the ideal.
   - *Why needed:* It connects the differential-ideal closure to the local defining-form criterion.

5. **Equivalence (d) $\Leftrightarrow$ (b).** "$d\omega^i$ annihilates $D$" iff "$d\omega^i \in \mathcal{I}^2(D)$" iff (by the lemma) $d\omega^i = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j$ for some $1$-forms $\alpha^i_j$.
   - *Hint:* The ideal-membership lemma is the key step.
   - *Why needed:* It exhibits the structure of $d\omega^i$ in the involutive case.

6. **Equivalence (e) $\Leftrightarrow$ (d).** If $d\omega^i = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j$, then wedging with $\omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k}$ kills it because each $\omega^j$ already appears. Conversely, given $d\omega^i \wedge \omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k} = 0$, expand $d\omega^i$ in a coframe $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}, \xi^1, \dots, \xi^k$ extending the defining forms; the wedge condition forces only $\omega^j \wedge (\text{stuff})$ terms.
   - *Hint:* The wedge with $\omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k}$ is the "indicator" of being outside the ideal.
   - *Why needed:* It gives the cleanest computational criterion.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Cartan's identity
> **Statement:** For a smooth $1$-form $\theta$ and smooth vector fields $X, Y$ on $M$:
> $$d\theta(X, Y) = X(\theta(Y)) - Y(\theta(X)) - \theta([X, Y]).$$
>
> **Hint:** This is a special case of the invariant formula for the exterior derivative (Theorem 14.32 in Lee, formula 14.28); for $1$-forms it follows from the local formula $d\theta = \sum \partial_j \theta_i\,dx^j \wedge dx^i$ together with the formula for the bracket.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the master identity connecting brackets to $d$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > In local coordinates, $\theta = \sum_i \theta_i\,dx^i$ and $X = \sum_i X^i \partial_i$, $Y = \sum_i Y^i \partial_i$. Then $\theta(Y) = \sum \theta_i Y^i$, $\theta(X) = \sum \theta_i X^i$, and $X(\theta(Y)) = \sum X^j \partial_j(\theta_i Y^i) = \sum X^j(\partial_j\theta_i Y^i + \theta_i \partial_j Y^i)$.
> >
> > Similarly $Y(\theta(X)) = \sum Y^j(\partial_j\theta_i X^i + \theta_i \partial_j X^i)$.
> >
> > $\theta([X, Y]) = \sum \theta_i[X, Y]^i = \sum \theta_i(X^j \partial_j Y^i - Y^j \partial_j X^i)$.
> >
> > Substituting and collecting, the $\theta_i \partial_j X^i$ and $\theta_i \partial_j Y^i$ terms cancel between $X(\theta(Y)) - Y(\theta(X))$ and $\theta([X, Y])$, leaving
> > $$X(\theta(Y)) - Y(\theta(X)) - \theta([X, Y]) = \sum (X^j Y^i - X^i Y^j) \partial_j \theta_i = d\theta(X, Y).$$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Ideal-membership characterization
> **Statement:** Let $D$ be a smooth distribution and $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}$ local defining $1$-forms on $U$. A $p$-form $\eta$ on $U$ annihilates $D$ iff $\eta = \sum_{i=1}^{n-k} \omega^i \wedge \beta^i$ for some smooth $(p-1)$-forms $\beta^i$.
>
> **Hint:** This is Lemma 19.6 in Lee. The "$\Longleftarrow$" direction is direct: any form with $\omega^i$ in a wedge factor vanishes on sections of $D$ since $\omega^i$ vanishes on them. The "$\Longrightarrow$" direction uses a coframe extension.
>
> **Why needed:** It says "annihilates $D$" = "lies in the ideal generated by $\omega^i$" — the algebraic translation of the geometric annihilation condition.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > See Lee Lemma 19.6. *Easy direction:* If $\eta = \sum_i \omega^i \wedge \beta^i$ and $X_1, \dots, X_p$ are sections of $D$, then $\eta(X_1, \dots, X_p)$ has each term containing $\omega^i(X_l)$ for some $l$, which is zero.
> >
> > *Hard direction:* Extend $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}$ to a smooth local coframe $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^n$. Then any $p$-form $\eta$ has a unique expansion $\eta = \sum_I \eta_I \omega^{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{i_p}$ where $I = (i_1 < \cdots < i_p)$. Let $(E_1, \dots, E_n)$ be the dual frame; $D$ is locally spanned by $E_{n-k+1}, \dots, E_n$. Then $\eta(E_{j_1}, \dots, E_{j_p}) = \eta_{(j_1, \dots, j_p)}$ for $j_1 < \cdots < j_p$. The condition "$\eta$ annihilates $D$" means $\eta_{(j_1, \dots, j_p)} = 0$ whenever all $j_l \in \{n-k+1, \dots, n\}$. So in the expansion, only multi-indices $I$ with at least one $i_l \in \{1, \dots, n-k\}$ contribute — and each such term has at least one factor $\omega^{i_l}$ for $l \leq n-k$, so the term is in the ideal generated by $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> We prove (a) $\Longleftrightarrow$ (b). The remaining equivalences are derivations of (b) using Lemma 2.
>
> **(a) $\Longrightarrow$ (b):** Suppose $D$ is involutive. Let $\theta$ be a local $1$-form annihilating $D$ on $U$, and let $X, Y \in \Gamma(D|_U)$. By Cartan's identity (Lemma 1),
> $$d\theta(X, Y) = X(\theta(Y)) - Y(\theta(X)) - \theta([X, Y]).$$
> Since $\theta$ annihilates $D$, $\theta(X) = \theta(Y) = 0$, so the first two terms vanish. By involutivity, $[X, Y] \in \Gamma(D)$, so $\theta([X, Y]) = 0$. Hence $d\theta(X, Y) = 0$, i.e. $d\theta$ annihilates $D$ on pairs $(X, Y)$.
>
> **(b) $\Longrightarrow$ (a):** Let $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}$ be local defining $1$-forms for $D$ on $U$, and assume each $d\omega^i$ annihilates $D$. Let $X, Y \in \Gamma(D)$. By Cartan's identity for each $\omega^i$,
> $$d\omega^i(X, Y) = X(\omega^i(Y)) - Y(\omega^i(X)) - \omega^i([X, Y]).$$
> The first two terms vanish (since $\omega^i$ annihilates sections of $D$); the LHS vanishes by hypothesis; so $\omega^i([X, Y]) = 0$ for each $i$. This means $[X, Y] \in \bigcap_i \ker \omega^i = D$. Hence $D$ is involutive.
>
> **(b) $\Longleftrightarrow$ (c):** By Lemma 2 applied with $\eta = $ any element of $\mathcal{I}^p(D)$, $\eta$ annihilates $D$ iff $\eta = \sum_i \omega^i \wedge \beta^i$. Then $d\eta = \sum_i (d\omega^i \wedge \beta^i - \omega^i \wedge d\beta^i)$. The second sum lies in $\mathcal{I}(D)$ (each term has an $\omega^i$ factor). The first sum lies in $\mathcal{I}(D)$ iff each $d\omega^i \wedge \beta^i$ does, iff each $d\omega^i \in \mathcal{I}^2(D)$ (since the $\beta^i$ are arbitrary).
>
> **(b) $\Longleftrightarrow$ (d):** By Lemma 2 with $\eta = d\omega^i$, $d\omega^i$ annihilates $D$ iff $d\omega^i = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j$ for some $1$-forms $\alpha^i_j$.
>
> **(d) $\Longleftrightarrow$ (e):** If $d\omega^i = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j$, then $d\omega^i \wedge \omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k} = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j \wedge \omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k}$, and each summand has $\omega^j$ already in $\omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k}$, so each is zero. Conversely, given the wedge condition, expand $d\omega^i$ in a coframe $\omega^1, \dots, \omega^{n-k}, \xi^1, \dots, \xi^k$: the wedge with $\omega^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega^{n-k}$ picks out exactly the $\xi^l \wedge \xi^m$ components of $d\omega^i$, forcing them to vanish; hence $d\omega^i$ lies in the ideal. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Pfaffian systems and integrating factors in classical mechanics.** For a velocity constraint $\omega(\dot q) = 0$ on configuration space $Q$, holonomicity (existence of a configuration submanifold realizing the constraint) is equivalent to $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$. When this holds, $\omega = \lambda\,df$ locally for an integrating factor $\lambda > 0$ and a function $f$, and the constraint surface is $\{f = c\}$. *Example:* a particle constrained to a surface $f(q) = c$ has $\omega = df$, automatically involutive.

**Thermodynamic state functions via $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$.** Infinitesimal heat $\delta Q$ on the state space of a thermodynamic system is *not* closed (entropy is generated, not just transferred). But $\delta Q/T$ has $d(\delta Q/T) = 0$ as a consequence of the second law, so $\omega = \delta Q/T \wedge d\omega = 0$ vacuously (since $d\omega = 0$ implies $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$). Frobenius then produces $S$ with $dS = \delta Q/T$ — the entropy function. The integrating factor $1/T$ is what makes the heat $1$-form integrable.

**Contact $1$-forms.** The opposite of involutive: a $1$-form $\alpha$ on a $(2n+1)$-manifold is **contact** if $\alpha \wedge (d\alpha)^n \neq 0$ everywhere — maximal non-degeneracy. The kernel distribution $D = \ker \alpha$ is *maximally non-involutive*. The standard contact $1$-form $\alpha = dz - y\,dx$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the prototype; see [[Ex - A Non-Integrable Distribution on R^3 from the Standard Contact Form]].

**Cartan's structure equations for connections.** For a connection on a principal bundle, the connection $1$-form $\omega$ and curvature $2$-form $\Omega$ satisfy $\Omega = d\omega + \frac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$. Flatness is $\Omega = 0$, which gives $d\omega = -\frac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$ — a Frobenius-style equation. The horizontal distribution is involutive iff curvature is zero iff the connection is flat iff Frobenius applies. This is the form-language Frobenius applied to the principal-bundle setting.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - The Frobenius Theorem|Frobenius theorem — vector field version]]** — the dual statement, with the same content. Both versions are equivalent, and the choice between them is dictated by how the distribution is presented: spanning vector fields call for the vector-field version, annihilating $1$-forms call for the forms version. The bridge between them is Cartan's identity.

- **[[Thm - Cartan's Magic Formula|Cartan's Magic Formula]]** *(from `Differential Geometry VIII`)* — closely related to Cartan's identity for $d$ of a $1$-form. The magic formula $\mathcal{L}_X = d\iota_X + \iota_X d$ is the general form-level identity that contains Cartan's identity as the special case "evaluate the magic formula on a $1$-form."

- **The de Rham complex and its sub-complexes** — the annihilating ideal $\mathcal{I}(D)$ forms a sub-complex of the de Rham complex (closed under $d$, when $D$ is involutive). The quotient complex $\Omega^*(M)/\mathcal{I}(D)$ has cohomology that is essentially the *foliated cohomology* — the cohomology of forms living on the leaves. This is the foundation of foliated de Rham theory.

- **Cartan's structure equations and connection $1$-forms** — the equations $d\omega^i = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j$ in Frobenius's forms version are the precursor to the **structure equations of Lie theory**, where $\omega^i$ are left-invariant $1$-forms on a Lie group and $\alpha^i_j$ are connection $1$-forms. The Frobenius condition for left-invariant distributions is the integrability of a Lie subalgebra, recovering the Lie correspondence.

- **The Maurer–Cartan equation** — on a Lie group $G$, the Maurer–Cartan form $\omega : TG \to \mathfrak{g}$ satisfies $d\omega + \frac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega] = 0$, which is a Frobenius-style integrability condition certifying that the Lie group structure is consistent with the Lie algebra structure. This is the master identity of Lie group theory.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] **Integrating factor for codimension-1 Pfaffian systems** *(from this same topic)*
> The Corollary: for $\omega$ a nowhere-vanishing $1$-form with $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$, there exist a positive function $\lambda$ and a function $f$ with $\omega = \lambda\,df$ locally. This is the classical theory of integrating factors — a $1$-form is exact up to multiplication by an integrating factor exactly when it is involutive.

> [!tip] **Cartan's structure equations** *(from Riemannian Geometry and Gauge Theory)*
> The identity $d\omega^i = \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \alpha^i_j$ is the prototype for **structure equations** of moving frames, in which $\omega^i$ are coframe $1$-forms and $\alpha^i_j$ are connection $1$-forms. Cartan's first structure equation $d\omega^i + \sum_j \omega^j \wedge \omega^i_j = T^i$ (with torsion $T^i$) and second structure equation $d\omega^i_j + \sum_k \omega^k_j \wedge \omega^i_k = \Omega^i_j$ (curvature) are exact analogues, run on coframes rather than annihilating forms.

> [!tip] **Maurer–Cartan equation** *(from Lie Theory)*
> On a Lie group, the Maurer–Cartan $1$-form $\omega : TG \to \mathfrak{g}$ satisfies $d\omega = -\frac{1}{2}[\omega, \omega]$, which is the integrability condition for the entire Lie group structure encoded in the Lie algebra. The Maurer–Cartan equation is the fundamental algebraic identity of Lie theory.

> [!tip] **Contact form** *(from Symplectic and Contact Geometry)*
> The *negation* of the Frobenius condition $\omega \wedge d\omega = 0$, in maximal form: a **contact $1$-form** on a $(2n+1)$-manifold satisfies $\omega \wedge (d\omega)^n \neq 0$ everywhere — maximally non-integrable. Contact manifolds are the odd-dimensional analogue of symplectic manifolds, with the Reeb vector field replacing the Hamiltonian vector field. The standard contact form $dz - y\,dx$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the local model — see [[Ex - A Non-Integrable Distribution on R^3 from the Standard Contact Form]].

> [!tip] **Exterior differential systems** *(from PDE)*
> The full Cartan–Kähler theory of overdetermined PDEs is formulated via **exterior differential systems**: ideals in the form algebra. Integrability is a generalized Frobenius condition (Cartan's test), and the integrable systems are solvable in a precise sense. This includes Frobenius (rank-$k$ distributions) as a special case, but extends to arbitrarily complicated nonlinear PDEs.
