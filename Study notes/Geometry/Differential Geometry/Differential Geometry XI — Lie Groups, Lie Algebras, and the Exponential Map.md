# Notation Registry

Throughout this topic we adopt Lee's conventions: a "Lie group" is a smooth manifold equipped with smooth multiplication and smooth inversion, "manifold" means smooth Hausdorff second-countable manifold, all Lie algebras are real and finite-dimensional unless said otherwise, and the Lie bracket of vector fields is $[X, Y] = XY - YX$ acting on smooth functions. When working with matrix groups we identify the tangent space at the identity with the matrix algebra, and the Lie bracket then becomes the matrix commutator $[A, B] = AB - BA$.

- $G, H, K$ — Lie groups
- $e$ or $1_G$ — the identity element of $G$
- $\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{h}, \mathfrak{k}$ — the corresponding Lie algebras (Fraktur lowercase of the group name)
- $\mathrm{Lie}(G)$ — the Lie algebra of $G$, used interchangeably with $\mathfrak{g}$
- $T_e G$ — the tangent space to $G$ at the identity, canonically identified with $\mathfrak{g}$
- $L_g, R_g : G \to G$ — left and right translation by $g$, $L_g(h) = gh$, $R_g(h) = hg$
- $C_g : G \to G$ — conjugation by $g$, $C_g(h) = ghg^{-1}$
- $X^L$ or $\tilde X$ — the left-invariant vector field on $G$ generated by $X \in T_e G$, $X^L|_g = d(L_g)_e(X)$
- $[X, Y]$ — the Lie bracket; for vector fields it is the commutator of derivations, for matrices the commutator $AB - BA$
- $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ — the exponential map, $\exp(X) = \gamma_X(1)$ where $\gamma_X$ is the one-parameter subgroup with initial velocity $X$
- $e^A$ — the matrix exponential, $\sum_{k \geq 0} A^k / k!$; equals $\exp(A)$ when $G = \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$
- $\mathrm{Ad}_g : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ — the adjoint representation of $g \in G$, $\mathrm{Ad}_g = d(C_g)_e$
- $\mathrm{ad}_X : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ — the adjoint representation of $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, $\mathrm{ad}_X(Y) = [X, Y]$
- $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{C})$ — real and complex general linear groups
- $\mathrm{O}(n), \mathrm{SO}(n)$ — orthogonal and special orthogonal groups
- $\mathrm{U}(n), \mathrm{SU}(n)$ — unitary and special unitary groups
- $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{R})$ — real symplectic group
- $\mathrm{Heis}(3)$ — the $3$-dimensional Heisenberg group of $3 \times 3$ upper-triangular real matrices with $1$s on the diagonal
- $G \cdot p$ — the orbit of $p \in M$ under a group action
- $G_p$ or $\mathrm{Stab}_G(p)$ — the stabilizer (isotropy group) of $p$
- $G/H$ — the homogeneous space of left cosets, equipped with its smooth manifold structure when $H$ is closed

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: a **Lie group** is what you get when you ask the group axioms to hold in the category of smooth manifolds — multiplication and inversion are required to be smooth maps — and the **Lie algebra** is the linearization at the identity that captures, in a finite-dimensional vector space with a single bilinear operation, almost everything about how the group composes near $e$.

Lie groups are the meeting place of two halves of mathematics that until this chapter have lived apart. From [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2|group theory]] we have the abstract algebra of composition, identity, and inverses; from [[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases|smooth manifold theory]] we have the calculus of tangent vectors, vector fields, and flows. A Lie group is the simultaneous realization of both structures on the same set, with the requirement that they be compatible — that the group operations themselves be smooth maps in the manifold sense. The result is an object on which one can simultaneously *multiply* and *differentiate*, and the principal lesson of the chapter is that doing both at once is enormously more powerful than either alone.

The central discovery, due to Sophus Lie, is that a Lie group $G$ is almost entirely determined by what happens at the single point $e$. The tangent space $T_e G$ inherits a bracket from the manifold structure — the Lie bracket of vector fields restricted to those vector fields invariant under left translation — and the resulting algebraic object $\mathfrak{g} = (T_e G, [\cdot, \cdot])$ is a **Lie algebra**: a vector space with a bilinear antisymmetric bracket satisfying the Jacobi identity. The miracle is that this purely linear object, finite-dimensional and combinatorial, encodes the curvature of the group multiplication to all orders. The **exponential map** $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is the bridge that makes this concrete: it takes a vector $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, regards it as the initial velocity of a curve, integrates the group law along that curve, and produces a one-parameter subgroup $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ of $G$. Near the identity, $\exp$ is a [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]], so $G$ looks locally like its Lie algebra; the deviation between $\exp(X) \exp(Y)$ and $\exp(X + Y)$ is governed by the bracket via the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula, whose leading correction is exactly $\tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y]$.

The structural backbone of the topic is the **Lie correspondence** — the categorical statement that on the connected, simply connected side, Lie groups and finite-dimensional Lie algebras are the same thing:

$$\{\text{simply connected Lie groups}\} \; \stackrel{\mathrm{Lie}}{\longleftrightarrow} \; \{\text{finite-dimensional Lie algebras}\}.$$

The functor $G \mapsto \mathrm{Lie}(G)$ is an equivalence of categories on the simply connected side, and every Lie group is recovered from its Lie algebra up to coverings. This converts the classification of Lie groups (a problem about smooth manifolds with group structure) into the classification of Lie algebras (a problem about finite-dimensional vector spaces with bilinear brackets), and the latter is the kind of problem that linear algebra is built to solve. The classification of *simple* Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$ — the celebrated $A_n, B_n, C_n, D_n, E_6, E_7, E_8, F_4, G_2$ list of Killing and Cartan — is the prototype, and it is the engine behind the classification of fundamental forces in particle physics, the representation theory of $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ and $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ in quantum mechanics, and the geometry of symmetric spaces.

Three other themes weave through the chapter. The first is **the closed subgroup theorem (Cartan)**: any topologically closed subgroup of a Lie group is automatically an embedded Lie subgroup, with no smoothness assumption needed at all. This is the magic that lets us write down concrete Lie groups by specifying matrix equations — $A^T A = I$ for $\mathrm{O}(n)$, $\det A = 1$ for $\mathrm{SL}(n)$ — and trust that they automatically carry compatible smooth structures. The second is the **adjoint representation** $\mathrm{Ad} : G \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$ which sends each $g \in G$ to the linearization of the conjugation map $C_g(h) = ghg^{-1}$ at the identity. The adjoint representation is the natural action of $G$ on its own Lie algebra, and its derivative $\mathrm{ad}_X(Y) = [X, Y]$ is the Lie algebra adjoint, the algebraic shadow of conjugation. The third is **smooth group actions and homogeneous spaces**: when $G$ acts smoothly and transitively on a manifold $M$, $M$ is identified with the quotient $G/G_p$ for any choice of basepoint $p$, and the closed subgroup theorem provides the smooth structure on this quotient. Spheres, projective spaces, Grassmannians, flag manifolds, and the symmetric spaces of Riemannian geometry are all of this form.

A reader is assumed to have refreshed the foundations: [[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases|smooth manifolds]], [[Differential Geometry III — Tangent Vectors and the Differential|tangent vectors and differentials]], [[Differential Geometry IV — Submersions, Immersions, Embeddings, and Submanifolds|immersions and embeddings]], [[Differential Geometry V — Vector Fields, Flows, and the Lie Bracket|vector fields, flows, and the Lie bracket]], and basic [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2|group theory]] ([[Def - Subgroup|subgroups]], normal [[Def - Subgroup|subgroups]], quotient groups, [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphisms]], kernels). The [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius|Frobenius theorem]] is used once, in the proof of the fundamental theorem on Lie algebra actions, but its statement is enough — no internals of the proof are needed.

# Concept Map

## §11.1 Lie Groups

- **[[Def - Lie Group]]**
	- A **Lie group** is a smooth manifold $G$ equipped with a group structure such that multiplication $m : G \times G \to G$ and inversion $i : G \to G$ are smooth maps. Equivalently, $G$ is a group object in the category of smooth manifolds — every diagram expressing associativity, the unit, and the inverse, originally in $\mathbf{Set}$, now lives in $\mathbf{Man}$. The principal examples are matrix groups: $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ as an open submanifold of $M(n, \mathbb{R})$, and its closed subgroups $\mathrm{O}(n)$, $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{U}(n)$, $\mathrm{SU}(n)$, $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{R})$. Additional examples include the additive group $(\mathbb{R}^n, +)$, the circle $S^1$, the $n$-torus $T^n$, the $3$-sphere $S^3 \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)$, and the Heisenberg group $\mathrm{Heis}(3)$.

- **[[Def - Lie Group Homomorphism]]**
	- A **Lie group homomorphism** $F : G \to H$ is a smooth map that is also a group homomorphism. Equivalently, it is a morphism in the category of Lie groups. A Lie group isomorphism is a bijective Lie group homomorphism whose inverse is also a Lie group homomorphism (automatic — Lee Corollary 7.6 uses the constant-rank theorem to show that every Lie group homomorphism has constant rank and that a bijective one is a [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]]). Examples: $\det : \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}^\times$, the inclusion $S^1 \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}^\times$, the projection $\mathbb{R} \to S^1$, $t \mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$, with kernel $\mathbb{Z}$.

- **[[Def - Lie Subgroup]]**
	- A **Lie subgroup** of $G$ is a subgroup $H \leq G$ equipped with a smooth manifold structure making it an immersed submanifold and a Lie group on its own. An **embedded Lie subgroup** is one where the inclusion is an embedding. Embedded Lie subgroups are closed (Lee Thm 7.21), and conversely the closed subgroup theorem (§11.3) says every topologically closed subgroup is automatically an embedded Lie subgroup. Not every Lie subgroup is embedded: the irrational winding $\mathbb{R} \hookrightarrow T^2$, $t \mapsto (e^{2\pi i t}, e^{2\pi i \alpha t})$, with $\alpha$ irrational, is a Lie subgroup whose image is dense in the torus and is not embedded.

- **[[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]]** *(also drilled in §11.3)*
	- *(Cartan.)* If $G$ is a Lie group and $H \leq G$ is a subgroup that is topologically a closed subset, then $H$ is an embedded Lie subgroup of $G$. This is the workhorse: it converts the manifold question "is this subgroup smooth?" into the topological question "is it closed?", which is usually trivial. It is the reason $\mathrm{O}(n) = \{A : A^T A = I\}$, $\mathrm{SL}(n) = \{A : \det A = 1\}$, and $\mathrm{U}(n) = \{A : A^*A = I\}$ are automatically smooth submanifolds of $\mathrm{GL}(n)$ — they are closed, so the theorem does all the work.

- **[[Ex - The Lie Algebra of GL(n,R) is the Space of n by n Matrices]]** (⭐) *(drilled in §11.2 but motivated here)*
	- Compute the tangent space $T_I \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ and verify it equals the full matrix space $M(n, \mathbb{R})$.

- **[[Ex - The General Linear Group is a Smooth Manifold]]** *(from [[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases|DG I]])*
	- Already established that $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ is open in $M(n, \mathbb{R})$ and hence an $n^2$-dimensional smooth manifold; multiplication and inversion are smooth because matrix products and Cramer's rule are polynomial in the entries.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Principal Bundle *(from Gauge Theory and [[Def - Fibre Bundle|Fibre Bundles]])*
> A **principal $G$-bundle** is a smooth manifold $P$ with a free smooth right action of a Lie group $G$ such that $P \to P/G$ is a fibre bundle with fibre $G$. The Lie group $G$ acts as the "internal symmetry" at each point of the base, and a **connection** on $P$ is a smoothly varying choice of horizontal [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] transverse to the orbits. Principal bundles are the geometric foundation of gauge theory in physics — the structure group $\mathrm{U}(1)$ gives electromagnetism, $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ the weak interaction, $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ the strong interaction, and the curvature of the connection is the field strength.

## §11.2 The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group

- **[[Def - Left-Invariant Vector Field]]**
	- A vector field $X \in \mathfrak{X}(G)$ on a Lie group is **left-invariant** if it is invariant under every left translation: $d(L_g)_{g'}(X_{g'}) = X_{gg'}$ for all $g, g' \in G$, equivalently $(L_g)_* X = X$ for every $g$. The left-invariant vector fields form a finite-dimensional vector space isomorphic to $T_e G$ via $X \mapsto X_e$: any tangent vector at $e$ extends uniquely to a left-invariant vector field $X^L|_g = d(L_g)_e(X_e)$ on the whole group. The right-invariant vector fields form an isomorphic but distinct copy.

- **[[Def - Lie Algebra]]**
	- A **Lie algebra** over $\mathbb{R}$ is a real vector space $\mathfrak{g}$ equipped with a bilinear map $[\cdot, \cdot] : \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ (the **bracket**) satisfying **antisymmetry** $[X, Y] = -[Y, X]$ and the **Jacobi identity** $[X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]] = 0$. The Jacobi identity is the bracket's substitute for associativity. Examples: the space $\mathfrak{X}(M)$ of smooth vector fields on a manifold under the Lie bracket of vector fields; the space $\mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{R}) = M(n, \mathbb{R})$ of $n \times n$ matrices under the commutator $[A, B] = AB - BA$; any vector space with the trivial bracket $[X, Y] = 0$ (an **abelian** Lie algebra).

- **[[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group]]**
	- The **Lie algebra of a Lie group** $G$, denoted $\mathfrak{g}$ or $\mathrm{Lie}(G)$, is the vector space of smooth left-invariant vector fields on $G$, with bracket the restriction of the Lie bracket on $\mathfrak{X}(G)$. It is canonically isomorphic to $T_e G$ as a vector space (Lee Thm 8.37), and the bracket transports to $T_e G$ via this isomorphism. Its [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] equals $\dim G$. For matrix Lie groups the bracket on $T_e G \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{R})$ is exactly the matrix commutator.

- **[[Thm - Left-Invariant Vector Fields Form a Lie Algebra]]**
	- The set of smooth left-invariant vector fields on a Lie group $G$ is closed under the Lie bracket of vector fields. Combined with the evaluation isomorphism $X \mapsto X_e$ this gives $\mathfrak{g}$ the structure of a Lie algebra of [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] equal to $\dim G$. The proof is short: pushforward respects the bracket, so $(L_g)_*[X, Y] = [(L_g)_* X, (L_g)_* Y] = [X, Y]$, and left-invariance is preserved.

- **[[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]]**
	- Every Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$ has a unique Lie algebra homomorphism $F_* = dF_e : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ as its derivative at the identity, and the assignment $G \mapsto \mathfrak{g}$, $F \mapsto F_*$ is a **functor** from the category of Lie groups to the category of finite-dimensional Lie algebras. In particular it sends isomorphic Lie groups to isomorphic Lie algebras. The converse — recovering $G$ from $\mathfrak{g}$ — is the Lie correspondence in §11.3.

- **[[Ex - The Lie Bracket on a Matrix Lie Algebra is the Commutator]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For a matrix Lie group $G \leq \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, the Lie bracket on $\mathfrak{g} = T_I G \subseteq \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{R})$ inherited from the left-invariant-vector-field construction coincides with the matrix commutator $[A, B] = AB - BA$.

- **[[Ex - The Lie Algebra of SO(3) is Antisymmetric Matrices]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute $\mathfrak{so}(3) = T_I \mathrm{SO}(3)$ and identify it as the space of $3 \times 3$ antisymmetric matrices, with the matrix commutator as bracket. Identify $\mathfrak{so}(3) \cong (\mathbb{R}^3, \times)$ where $\times$ is the cross product.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Killing Form *(from Representation Theory of [[Def - Lie Algebra|Lie Algebras]])*
> On any Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, the **Killing form** is the symmetric bilinear form $B(X, Y) = \mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}_X \circ \mathrm{ad}_Y)$ on $\mathfrak{g}$. **Cartan's criterion** says a Lie algebra is **semisimple** if and only if its Killing form is non-degenerate, and this single algebraic test is what makes the classification of semisimple Lie algebras (Cartan–Killing) tractable. On a semisimple Lie algebra, the Killing form is the natural invariant inner product, and on the corresponding compact Lie group it integrates up to a bi-invariant Riemannian metric.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Universal Enveloping Algebra *(from Representation Theory)*
> The **universal enveloping algebra** $U(\mathfrak{g})$ is the associative algebra generated by $\mathfrak{g}$ subject to the relations $XY - YX = [X, Y]$, satisfying the universal property that every Lie algebra homomorphism $\mathfrak{g} \to A$ into an associative algebra (with $A$ regarded as a Lie algebra under its commutator) factors uniquely through $U(\mathfrak{g})$. The **Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem** gives a basis, and representations of $\mathfrak{g}$ are exactly [[Def - Module|modules]] over $U(\mathfrak{g})$. This is the algebraic engine of Lie algebra representation theory.

## §11.3 The Exponential Map and Closed Subgroups

- **[[Def - One-Parameter Subgroup]]**
	- A **one-parameter subgroup** of $G$ is a Lie group homomorphism $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to G$. By the existence-and-uniqueness theorem for integral curves, one-parameter subgroups are in canonical bijection with elements of $\mathfrak{g}$: each $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ generates the unique one-parameter subgroup whose initial velocity is $X_e$, namely the integral curve of $X^L$ through $e$. For $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ the one-parameter subgroups are $\gamma(t) = e^{tA}$ for $A \in \mathfrak{gl}(n, \mathbb{R})$, the standard matrix exponential.

- **[[Def - Exponential Map of a Lie Group]]**
	- The **exponential map** $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is defined by $\exp(X) = \gamma_X(1)$ where $\gamma_X$ is the one-parameter subgroup with $\gamma_X'(0) = X_e$, equivalently the integral curve of the left-invariant vector field $X^L$ starting at $e$, evaluated at time $1$. Equivalently $\exp(tX) = \gamma_X(t)$ — the entire one-parameter subgroup is read off from the line through $X$ in $\mathfrak{g}$. The exponential map is smooth, its differential at $0$ is the identity $\mathfrak{g} \to T_e G = \mathfrak{g}$, and therefore by the inverse function theorem it restricts to a diffeomorphism between some neighbourhood of $0 \in \mathfrak{g}$ and some neighbourhood of $e \in G$.

- **[[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map]]**
	- For any Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$, the diagram $\exp_H \circ F_* = F \circ \exp_G$ commutes. That is, $\exp$ is a **natural transformation** from the Lie functor (regarded as an identity on objects) to itself — it commutes with every morphism. As a consequence, $F$ is completely determined by $F_*$ on the connected component of $e$, since the image of $\exp_G$ generates the identity component (Lee Prop 7.14).

- **[[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]]**
	- *(Cartan, full theorem.)* If $G$ is a Lie group and $H \leq G$ is a topologically closed subgroup, then $H$ is an embedded Lie subgroup. The proof identifies $\mathfrak{h} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$ as $\{X \in \mathfrak{g} : \exp(tX) \in H \text{ for all } t \in \mathbb{R}\}$, shows $\mathfrak{h}$ is a linear [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] using the BCH-leading-order formula $\exp((t/n)X) \exp((t/n)Y))^n \to \exp(t(X+Y))$, and constructs a slice chart for $H$ near $e$ using the diffeomorphism $\mathfrak{h} \oplus \mathfrak{b} \to G$, $(X, Y) \mapsto \exp X \exp Y$, where $\mathfrak{b}$ is a complement to $\mathfrak{h}$ in $\mathfrak{g}$. Translates of this slice chart by left translation give the full slice atlas.

- **[[Ex - The Exponential Map of GL(n,R) is the Matrix Exponential]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Verify that the abstract exponential map for $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ coincides with the matrix exponential $e^A = \sum_{k \geq 0} A^k/k!$, both in the sense of one-parameter subgroups $\gamma_A(t) = e^{tA}$ and the integral curve definition $\exp(A) = \gamma_A(1) = e^A$.

- **[[Ex - SU(2) is Diffeomorphic to S^3]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Construct an explicit diffeomorphism between $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ — the group of unitary $2 \times 2$ matrices with determinant $1$ — and the unit $3$-sphere $S^3 \subseteq \mathbb{C}^2$. Identify $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ as the space of traceless skew-Hermitian $2 \times 2$ matrices and show the connecting double cover $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff Formula *(from [[Def - Lie Group|Lie Groups]], Advanced)*
> The **Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula** is the explicit Taylor expansion of $\log(\exp X \exp Y)$ in terms of nested brackets of $X$ and $Y$:
> $$\log(\exp X \exp Y) = X + Y + \tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y] + \tfrac{1}{12}([X, [X, Y]] + [Y, [Y, X]]) + \cdots,$$
> with all higher terms involving only iterated brackets. This is the precise statement that the bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$ controls the deviation of group multiplication from vector-space addition; on the locus where $\exp$ is a diffeomorphism, BCH **completely** reconstructs the multiplication on $G$ from the bracket on $\mathfrak{g}$. The formula is the analytic core of the Lie correspondence.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Lie Correspondence *(from [[Def - Lie Group|Lie Groups]], Advanced)*
> Restricting attention to **connected, simply connected** Lie groups, the functor $G \mapsto \mathfrak{g}$ is an equivalence of categories with the category of finite-dimensional Lie algebras. Every finite-dimensional Lie algebra is the Lie algebra of a unique simply connected Lie group (the "third fundamental theorem"; Ado + integration of the resulting matrix Lie subalgebra), and Lie algebra [[Def - Homomorphism|homomorphisms]] lift to Lie group homomorphisms whenever the source is simply connected. Non-simply-connected Lie groups with the same algebra differ by discrete central quotients (covers): for instance $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ have the same Lie algebra, but $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is the simply connected double cover. The classification of connected Lie groups thus reduces to: (i) classify Lie algebras; (ii) classify discrete central subgroups of the corresponding simply connected group.

## §11.4 Group Actions and Homogeneous Spaces

- **[[Def - Adjoint Representation]]**
	- The **adjoint representation** of a Lie group $G$ is the Lie group homomorphism $\mathrm{Ad} : G \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$ defined by $\mathrm{Ad}_g = d(C_g)_e$, where $C_g(h) = ghg^{-1}$ is conjugation by $g$. It is the natural linear action of $G$ on its Lie algebra. Its differential at $e$ is the Lie algebra adjoint $\mathrm{ad} : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$, $\mathrm{ad}_X(Y) = [X, Y]$, and the naturality square gives $\mathrm{Ad}_{\exp X} = \exp(\mathrm{ad}_X)$ — conjugation by $\exp(X)$ on the Lie algebra equals the matrix exponential of the bracket-with-$X$ operator. For matrix groups, $\mathrm{Ad}_g(A) = g A g^{-1}$.

- **[[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group]]**
	- A **smooth (left) action** of a Lie group $G$ on a smooth manifold $M$ is a smooth map $\theta : G \times M \to M$ satisfying $\theta(e, p) = p$ and $\theta(g_1, \theta(g_2, p)) = \theta(g_1 g_2, p)$. Right actions are defined analogously. The orbit $G \cdot p = \{g \cdot p : g \in G\}$ and stabilizer (isotropy group) $G_p = \{g : g \cdot p = p\}$ are defined as in [[Def - Group Action|abstract group theory]]. The action is **free** if every stabilizer is trivial, **transitive** if there is a single orbit, **proper** if the map $(g, p) \mapsto (g \cdot p, p)$ is proper (preimages of compacts are compact).

- **[[Def - Equivariant Map]]**
	- A smooth map $F : M \to N$ between $G$-manifolds is **equivariant** if it commutes with the actions: $F(g \cdot p) = g \cdot F(p)$ for all $g, p$ (for left actions; analogous for right). Equivariant maps are the morphisms in the category of $G$-manifolds. The **equivariant rank theorem** (Lee Thm 7.25) says every equivariant map between $G$-spaces with transitive $G$-action has constant rank — a powerful structural fact since constant-rank maps then drop to immersions/submersions/embeddings via the rank theorem.

- **[[Def - Homogeneous Space]]**
	- A **homogeneous space** of a Lie group $G$ is a smooth manifold $M$ equipped with a transitive smooth left $G$-action. By the orbit-stabilizer theorem applied at the manifold level (§11.4), every homogeneous space is diffeomorphic to a quotient $G/H$ where $H = G_p$ is the stabilizer of any point $p$ — a closed subgroup of $G$. Conversely, $G/H$ is itself a smooth manifold for any closed subgroup $H \leq G$ (homogeneous-space construction, Lee Thm 21.17).

- **[[Thm - Orbit-Stabilizer for Lie Group Actions]]**
	- *(Smooth orbit-stabilizer.)* If $G$ acts smoothly and transitively on a smooth manifold $M$, then for any $p \in M$ the orbit map $\theta^{(p)} : G \to M$, $g \mapsto g \cdot p$, descends to a $G$-equivariant diffeomorphism $G/G_p \to M$. In particular $\dim M = \dim G - \dim G_p$, the smooth analogue of $|G \cdot p| = |G|/|G_p|$ from [[Thm - Orbit-Stabiliser Theorem|finite group theory]].

- **[[Thm - Homogeneous Space is a Smooth Manifold]]**
	- For any Lie group $G$ and closed subgroup $H \leq G$, the quotient $G/H$ (set of left [[Def - Coset|cosets]]) carries a unique smooth manifold structure of dimension $\dim G - \dim H$ such that the projection $\pi : G \to G/H$ is a smooth submersion, and the left action of $G$ on $G/H$ is smooth. The proof uses the closed subgroup theorem (to get $H$ embedded), a local slice through $e$ transverse to $H$, and translation to produce charts at every coset.

- **[[Ex - S^2 as a Homogeneous Space of SO(3)]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that the natural action of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ on $S^2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ is smooth and transitive with stabilizer at the north pole isomorphic to $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, and conclude $S^2 \cong \mathrm{SO}(3)/\mathrm{SO}(2)$ as smooth manifolds.

- **[[Ex - The Adjoint Representation of SO(3) is the Defining Representation]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Compute $\mathrm{Ad} : \mathrm{SO}(3) \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{so}(3))$ explicitly under the identification $\mathfrak{so}(3) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$ via the hat map, and show that $\mathrm{Ad}_g(\widehat v) = \widehat{gv}$ for $g \in \mathrm{SO}(3)$ — i.e., $\mathrm{Ad}$ factors as $\mathrm{SO}(3) \cong \mathrm{Ad}(\mathrm{SO}(3)) \subseteq \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{so}(3))$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Symmetric Space *(from Riemannian Geometry, Advanced)*
> A **Riemannian symmetric space** is a connected Riemannian manifold $M$ such that each point has an involutive [[Def - Isometry|isometry]] fixing it. Every Riemannian symmetric space is a homogeneous space $G/H$ where $G$ is the [[Def - Isometry|isometry]] group (or its identity component) and $H$ is the stabilizer of a chosen basepoint, with $H$ being the fixed-point set of a Lie group involution $\sigma : G \to G$. The classification of symmetric spaces (Cartan, 1926) is parallel to and uses the classification of semisimple Lie algebras; it includes the spheres $S^n = \mathrm{SO}(n+1)/\mathrm{SO}(n)$, hyperbolic spaces $H^n = \mathrm{SO}(n, 1)^0/\mathrm{SO}(n)$, projective spaces, Grassmannians, and the bounded symmetric domains of complex analysis.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Maurer–Cartan Form *(from Lie [[Def - Group|Groups]], Advanced and Geometric Mechanics)*
> The **Maurer–Cartan form** on a Lie group $G$ is the $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form $\theta \in \Omega^1(G, \mathfrak{g})$ defined by $\theta_g(v) = d(L_{g^{-1}})_g(v)$ — it transports each tangent vector back to the identity. It is the canonical "left-invariant identity" on $G$: a left-invariant vector field has constant Maurer–Cartan form equal to its value at the identity, and the **Maurer–Cartan equation** $d\theta + \tfrac{1}{2}[\theta, \theta] = 0$ encodes the entire structure of the Lie group in differential-form language. The Maurer–Cartan form is the prototype for **connections on principal bundles**.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Representation Theory of Compact Lie Groups *(from Representation Theory)*
> A **representation** of a Lie group $G$ on a vector space $V$ is a Lie group homomorphism $\rho : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$. The **Peter–Weyl theorem** decomposes $L^2(G)$ for compact $G$ as a Hilbert-space direct sum of finite-dimensional irreducible representations, each appearing with multiplicity equal to its dimension. This generalizes Fourier analysis on the circle ($G = S^1$, irreducibles are $z \mapsto z^n$) to all compact groups, and is the foundation of harmonic analysis, automorphic forms, and the representation theory of quantum-mechanical symmetry groups like $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ (spin) and $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ (the quark model).

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises and theorems of this topic chase a recognizable set of goals. The most fundamental is **identifying the Lie algebra** of a concrete Lie group — computing $T_e G$ explicitly, working out the bracket, and recognizing it as a known matrix algebra. This is the workhorse target: $\mathfrak{gl}(n)$, $\mathfrak{sl}(n)$, $\mathfrak{o}(n)$, $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, $\mathfrak{u}(n)$, $\mathfrak{su}(n)$, $\mathfrak{sp}(2n)$ are computed by differentiating the defining equation of $G$ at the identity. A second target is **computing the exponential map** of a specific Lie group — for matrix groups it is the matrix exponential, and the resulting infinite series can be summed in closed form for nilpotent and certain semisimple cases. A third is **establishing structural properties of $G$ via $\mathfrak{g}$**: whether $G$ is abelian (iff $\mathfrak{g}$ is abelian, for connected $G$), whether $G$ contains a given subgroup (translating to whether $\mathfrak{g}$ contains a given subalgebra), whether $H \trianglelefteq G$ is normal (iff $\mathfrak{h} \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$ is an ideal). A fourth is **identifying a manifold as a homogeneous space**: showing $M \cong G/H$ for some natural Lie group $G$ acting transitively on $M$ with stabilizer $H$ — this is how $S^n$, $\mathbb{RP}^n$, the Grassmannians, and the flag manifolds become tractable. A fifth is **computing or recognizing the adjoint representation**, especially for compact groups where $\mathrm{Ad}$ is the natural action of $G$ on its Lie algebra by orthogonal transformations relative to the Killing form. The five targets — Lie algebra, exponential, structure-via-algebra, homogeneous-space identification, adjoint representation — recur because each is a different mode in which Lie group theory translates between the manifold side and the algebraic side.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The recurring assumptions are equally stereotyped. **A matrix Lie group given by a defining equation** is the richest source: $\mathrm{O}(n) = \{A : A^T A = I\}$, $\mathrm{SL}(n) = \{A : \det A = 1\}$, $\mathrm{U}(n) = \{A : A^* A = I\}$, $\mathrm{Sp}(2n) = \{A : A^T J A = J\}$. The closed subgroup theorem makes the manifold structure automatic, and the Lie algebra is computed by differentiating the defining equation. **A Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$** is a second source: $F_* = dF_e : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism, its kernel is a Lie algebra ideal corresponding to a closed normal subgroup of $G$, and naturality of $\exp$ ($F \circ \exp_G = \exp_H \circ F_*$) lets one compute $F$ on the image of $\exp$ knowing only $F_*$. **A smooth transitive group action of $G$ on a manifold $M$** is the third — this routes through the equivariant rank theorem and orbit-stabilizer to identify $M \cong G/G_p$. **A topologically closed subgroup** $H \leq G$ — by the closed subgroup theorem it is automatically embedded, with $\mathfrak{h} = \{X \in \mathfrak{g} : \exp(tX) \in H \text{ for all } t\}$. **The simple connectivity of $G$** is the fifth — for connected simply connected $G$ the Lie correspondence is bijective, so a Lie algebra homomorphism $\mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ integrates to a unique Lie group homomorphism $G \to H$. The unifying observation is that every source in this topic is converted to a target by the same two-step move: pass to the Lie algebra (via $\exp$ or differentiation), do the linear computation, transport back (via $\exp$ or integration). The Lie algebra is where the work is done; the exponential map is the bridge.

# Legal Operations

The operations below cover essentially every move in §11.1–§11.4 problems. Each is self-contained.

1. **Compute the Lie algebra by differentiating the defining equation.** For a matrix Lie group $G = \{A : \Phi(A) = c\}$ defined as the level set of a smooth $\Phi : \mathrm{GL}(n) \to V$, the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_I G$ is the kernel of $d\Phi_I : \mathfrak{gl}(n) \to V$. Concretely: write $A(t) = I + tX + O(t^2)$, substitute into $\Phi(A(t)) = c$, expand to first order, and read off the constraint on $X$. *Trigger:* any problem asking for the Lie algebra of a matrix group given by equations. *Pattern:* for $\mathrm{O}(n)$, $\Phi(A) = A^T A$ and $A(t)^T A(t) = I + t(X^T + X) + O(t^2)$ forces $X + X^T = 0$, so $\mathfrak{o}(n) = \{X : X^T = -X\}$.

2. **Compute the exponential map as the matrix exponential.** For any matrix Lie group $G \leq \mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, the abstract exponential $\exp_G : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is the restriction of the matrix exponential $A \mapsto e^A = \sum_{k \geq 0} A^k/k!$. *Trigger:* any concrete computation of $\exp$ on a matrix group. *Pattern:* compute $e^{tA}$ in closed form using diagonalization or Jordan form when available, or sum the series when $A$ is nilpotent or has a clean eigenstructure (rotations in $\mathfrak{so}(2)$, for example, give $e^{t\theta J} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos t\theta & -\sin t\theta \\ \sin t\theta & \cos t\theta \end{pmatrix}$).

3. **Use naturality of $\exp$ to compute Lie group homomorphisms.** If $F : G \to H$ is a Lie group homomorphism, then $F(\exp X) = \exp(F_* X)$ for all $X \in \mathfrak{g}$. *Trigger:* you know $F_*$ on $\mathfrak{g}$ and want to compute $F$ on points of $G$ near $e$. *Pattern:* since $\exp$ is a local diffeomorphism near $0 \in \mathfrak{g}$, every $g \in G$ in a neighbourhood of $e$ is $g = \exp X$ for a unique $X$, and $F(g) = \exp(F_* X)$. The image of $\exp$ generates the identity component (Lee Prop 7.14), so this determines $F$ on the whole identity component.

4. **Invoke the closed subgroup theorem.** Whenever a subset $H \subseteq G$ is shown to be a topologically closed subgroup, conclude immediately that $H$ is an embedded Lie subgroup. *Trigger:* a problem provides or constructs a subgroup defined by an equation (closed under group operations and a closed subset). *Pattern:* "Since $H$ is closed and a subgroup, the closed subgroup theorem [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]] gives that $H$ is an embedded Lie subgroup; in particular it is a smooth submanifold and its Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{h} = \{X \in \mathfrak{g} : \exp tX \in H \text{ for all } t\}$."

5. **Identify a manifold as a homogeneous space.** Given a smooth manifold $M$ and a transitive smooth action of a Lie group $G$, pick any $p \in M$, identify the stabilizer $H = G_p$, and conclude $M \cong G/H$ as smooth manifolds. *Trigger:* a familiar manifold (a sphere, projective space, Grassmannian, flag manifold) has a recognizable transitive group of symmetries. *Pattern:* "The natural action of $\mathrm{SO}(n+1)$ on $S^n$ is transitive, the stabilizer of $e_{n+1}$ is the block-diagonal copy of $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, so $S^n \cong \mathrm{SO}(n+1)/\mathrm{SO}(n)$."

6. **Use the adjoint representation to convert group conjugation to a linear action.** The adjoint representation $\mathrm{Ad} : G \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$ converts the (non-linear) conjugation action of $G$ on itself into a linear action of $G$ on $\mathfrak{g}$. *Trigger:* a problem involves $ghg^{-1}$ near the identity or asks about the structure of conjugacy classes. *Pattern:* write $h = \exp Y$ for $Y \in \mathfrak{g}$; then $ghg^{-1} = g (\exp Y) g^{-1} = \exp(\mathrm{Ad}_g Y)$, and the conjugacy class structure is governed by the orbits of $\mathrm{Ad}$ on $\mathfrak{g}$.

7. **Pass between left-invariant and right-invariant vector fields via inversion.** A left-invariant vector field $X^L$ and a right-invariant vector field $X^R$ with the same value at $e$ are related by the inversion map $\iota : G \to G$, $g \mapsto g^{-1}$: $d\iota$ at the identity is $-\mathrm{id}$, and pushforward by $\iota$ sends left-invariant to right-invariant up to a sign on the Lie bracket. *Trigger:* a problem requires switching the convention or distinguishing left from right actions. *Pattern:* the Lie bracket of right-invariant vector fields equals the *negative* of the matrix commutator, while left-invariant gives the positive commutator; this is the origin of the antihomomorphism in Lee Thm 20.18.

8. **Reduce normality of a connected subgroup to ideal structure on the algebra.** For a connected Lie subgroup $H \leq G$ of a connected Lie group, $H$ is normal in $G$ if and only if $\mathfrak{h}$ is an **ideal** in $\mathfrak{g}$ (closed under bracket with all of $\mathfrak{g}$). *Trigger:* a problem asks about normal subgroups of a Lie group. *Pattern:* compute the bracket $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{h}]$; if it lies in $\mathfrak{h}$, $H$ is normal, otherwise it is not. This is Lee Theorem 20.28.

9. **Use the orbit map to embed a homogeneous space.** When $G$ acts smoothly and transitively on $M$, the orbit map $\theta^{(p)} : G \to M$ has constant rank (by the equivariant rank theorem) equal to $\dim M$, hence factors through the quotient $G/G_p$ to give a smooth bijection $\bar\theta : G/G_p \to M$. *Trigger:* you want to put a smooth structure on a quotient $G/H$ or identify a known quotient. *Pattern:* show the orbit map descends, check smoothness from the universal property of the quotient, then invoke proper action + closed stabilizer to upgrade to a diffeomorphism.

10. **Recognize a one-parameter subgroup and extract its generator.** A smooth curve $\gamma : \mathbb{R} \to G$ with $\gamma(0) = e$ and $\gamma(s + t) = \gamma(s) \gamma(t)$ is a one-parameter subgroup; it must equal $\exp(tX)$ where $X = \gamma'(0) \in \mathfrak{g}$. *Trigger:* a homomorphism out of $\mathbb{R}$ or a flow on $G$ by left translations. *Pattern:* compute $\gamma'(0)$ in coordinates, identify $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, and conclude $\gamma(t) = \exp(tX)$. Conversely, given $X$, the integral curve of $X^L$ through $e$ is $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$.

11. **Apply the equivariant rank theorem.** Any smooth equivariant map between $G$-manifolds with $G$ acting transitively on the source has constant rank, hence is a smooth subimmersion, hence factors as a submersion followed by an immersion. *Trigger:* a smooth map respects two group actions. *Pattern:* establish equivariance, conclude constant rank, then deploy the rank theorem to obtain submersion/immersion/embedding structure as the problem demands.

12. **Use the matrix exponential identities.** For matrices: $e^{A + B} = e^A e^B$ if $[A, B] = 0$; $\det(e^A) = e^{\mathrm{tr} A}$ (Lee Problem 20-4); $e^A$ is invertible for any $A$, with $(e^A)^{-1} = e^{-A}$. *Trigger:* matrix computations involving exponentials. *Pattern:* the trace-determinant identity is the most-used: it instantly implies $\exp(\mathfrak{sl}(n)) \subseteq \mathrm{SL}(n)$ (since $\mathrm{tr} A = 0 \implies \det e^A = 1$) and is the bridge between trace-free matrices and unit-determinant matrices.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Assuming $\exp(X + Y) = \exp(X) \exp(Y)$
> The exponential map is **not** a homomorphism unless $G$ is abelian (Lee Problem 20-8). The standard counterexample is $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ or any non-abelian matrix group: take $X = \tfrac{i\pi}{2} \sigma_1$ and $Y = \tfrac{i\pi}{2} \sigma_2$ in $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, then $\exp X$ and $\exp Y$ anticommute as the Pauli matrix multiplication shows, and $\exp X \exp Y \neq \exp Y \exp X$, whereas $\exp(X + Y)$ is unambiguous. **The repair condition** that makes the formula legal: either $[X, Y] = 0$ (in which case $\exp(X + Y) = \exp X \exp Y$ by direct computation), or $G$ is abelian (in which case the bracket is always zero), or one uses the **Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff** correction $\log(\exp X \exp Y) = X + Y + \tfrac{1}{2}[X, Y] + \cdots$ to all orders.

> [!warning] 2. Assuming $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is surjective
> The exponential map is a diffeomorphism only **near** $0 \in \mathfrak{g}$, and is generally not even surjective onto the identity component. The classical counterexample is $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ — the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ is not in the image of $\exp$ from $\mathfrak{sl}(2, \mathbb{R})$ (Lee Problem 20-6), and yet $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{R})$ is connected. **The repair condition**: $\exp$ *is* surjective for connected **compact** Lie groups (a theorem of Hopf-Rinow style, via the bi-invariant metric), and for connected nilpotent Lie groups (where BCH terminates). For general connected $G$, only a finite product of exponentials is guaranteed to cover the identity component (Lee Prop 7.14).

> [!warning] 3. Treating an immersed Lie subgroup as embedded
> A Lie subgroup is by definition only an *immersed* submanifold; the standard example of failure is the irrational line in the torus, $\mathbb{R} \hookrightarrow T^2$, $t \mapsto (e^{2\pi i t}, e^{2\pi i \alpha t})$ with $\alpha$ irrational, whose image is dense and is *not* an embedded submanifold of $T^2$ (Lee Example 7.19). The induced topology on the image as a subspace of $T^2$ differs from the topology making it a Lie group. **The repair condition**: the [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem|closed subgroup theorem]] says topological closure is exactly the condition that promotes immersed to embedded. So restrict attention to closed subgroups, or apply Lee Problem 20-10 (the closure of any Lie subgroup is itself a Lie subgroup, properly embedded).

> [!warning] 4. Forgetting that "Lie algebra of a Lie subgroup" means the tangent space at the identity
> When $H \leq G$ is a closed subgroup, $\mathfrak{h}$ sits inside $\mathfrak{g}$ as the subspace $T_e H \subseteq T_e G$ — *not* as some abstract subalgebra to be constructed independently. Identifying $\mathfrak{h}$ requires computing tangent vectors to curves $\gamma(t) \in H$ at $\gamma(0) = e$, which usually means writing $\gamma(t) = I + tX + O(t^2)$ and reading off the constraint on $X$ from the equations defining $H$. The temptation is to identify $\mathfrak{h}$ via $H$'s own group operations and then ask whether it sits inside $\mathfrak{g}$ correctly; the right move is the opposite — first sit $\mathfrak{h}$ inside $\mathfrak{g}$ as $T_e H$, then observe that the bracket restricts.

> [!warning] 5. Confusing the adjoint representation $\mathrm{Ad}$ with the Lie algebra adjoint $\mathrm{ad}$
> $\mathrm{Ad} : G \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$ is a **Lie group** representation of $G$ on its algebra; $\mathrm{ad} : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$, $\mathrm{ad}_X(Y) = [X, Y]$, is a **Lie algebra** representation. They are related by $\mathrm{ad} = d(\mathrm{Ad})_e$, equivalently $\mathrm{Ad}_{\exp X} = \exp(\mathrm{ad}_X)$, but they are not the same object — one takes group elements as input, the other algebra elements. **The repair condition**: keep track of the input type. If you see $g \in G$ acting, you mean $\mathrm{Ad}$; if you see $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ bracketed, you mean $\mathrm{ad}$. The exponential map intertwines them, but they live in different categories.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

Every problem in this topic begins with the same question: **which side am I on, group or algebra?** Lie group theory is a constant translation between two languages — the geometric/global language of the group $G$ and the linear/local language of the algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ — and the strategic skill is recognizing which side a problem is naturally posed on, which side the answer is most easily computed on, and how to route between them using $\exp$, $d(\cdot)_e$, $\mathrm{Ad}$, and $\mathrm{ad}$. The candidates are short, and you should run through them deliberately.

If the problem **asks for the Lie algebra of a specific matrix Lie group**, the routine is mechanical: write the defining equation $\Phi(A) = c$, substitute $A(t) = I + tX + O(t^2)$, expand to first order in $t$, and read off the linear constraint on $X \in \mathfrak{gl}(n)$. The Lie bracket is the matrix commutator [[Ex - The Lie Bracket on a Matrix Lie Algebra is the Commutator|automatically]]. The standard computations — $\mathfrak{o}(n) = \mathfrak{so}(n) = \{X : X + X^T = 0\}$, $\mathfrak{sl}(n) = \{X : \mathrm{tr} X = 0\}$, $\mathfrak{u}(n) = \{X : X + X^* = 0\}$, $\mathfrak{su}(n) = \{X : X + X^* = 0, \mathrm{tr} X = 0\}$, $\mathfrak{sp}(2n) = \{X : X^T J + J X = 0\}$ — are all instances of this single first-order calculation, and you should be able to produce any of them in one minute.

If the problem **gives you a Lie algebra homomorphism $\varphi : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ and asks for the corresponding Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$**, the technique is integration via the [[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map|naturality of exp]]. When $G$ is simply connected this is the [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism|integration theorem]]: $\varphi$ lifts uniquely to $F$ with $F(\exp X) = \exp(\varphi(X))$. When $G$ is not simply connected, lift to the universal cover $\widetilde G$, integrate there, and descend only if the kernel of $\widetilde G \to G$ is preserved. The double cover $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$ is the canonical example of why this matters: the Lie algebras are isomorphic ($\mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathfrak{so}(3)$), and the simply connected one ($\mathrm{SU}(2)$) is the one from which all Lie group homomorphisms originate.

If the problem **asks whether a Lie group $G$ has some property** (abelian, simple, normal subgroup of a given type, compact, etc.), check whether the corresponding property of $\mathfrak{g}$ is easier: for connected $G$, $G$ is abelian iff $\mathfrak{g}$ is abelian (Lee Problem 20-7), $H \trianglelefteq G$ normal iff $\mathfrak{h}$ is an ideal (Lee Thm 20.28), $G$ semisimple iff the Killing form of $\mathfrak{g}$ is non-degenerate, $G$ compact often iff the Killing form is negative-definite. The general principle is that *structural* questions about connected Lie groups reduce to linear-algebraic questions about their algebras, and the latter are tractable.

If the problem **gives you a smooth manifold $M$ with a group of symmetries and asks for the structure of $M$**, look for a transitive group action and compute the stabilizer: $M \cong G/H$ by [[Thm - Orbit-Stabilizer for Lie Group Actions|smooth orbit-stabilizer]]. The standard examples to internalize are $S^n = \mathrm{SO}(n+1)/\mathrm{SO}(n)$, $\mathbb{RP}^n = \mathrm{SO}(n+1)/\mathrm{O}(n) = \mathrm{O}(n+1)/(\mathrm{O}(n) \times \mathrm{O}(1))$, $\mathrm{Gr}_k(\mathbb{R}^n) = \mathrm{O}(n)/(\mathrm{O}(k) \times \mathrm{O}(n - k))$, hyperbolic space $H^n = \mathrm{SO}(n, 1)^0/\mathrm{SO}(n)$, and Minkowski space's orbits $\mathrm{SO}^+(3, 1)/\mathrm{SO}(3)$ giving the mass shell. Once $M$ is realized as $G/H$, all of $G$-equivariant geometry on $M$ — invariant metrics, invariant connections, invariant volume forms — can be computed at a single point of $M$ using the linear-algebraic structure of $\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}$, which is enormously simpler than working on $M$ directly.

If the problem **involves the adjoint representation** $\mathrm{Ad} : G \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$, remember the central identity $\mathrm{Ad}_{\exp X} = \exp(\mathrm{ad}_X)$, equivalently $\mathrm{Ad}_g(Y) = g Y g^{-1}$ for matrix groups. The orbits of $\mathrm{Ad}$ on $\mathfrak{g}$ are the **adjoint orbits**; they correspond to conjugacy classes of $G$ near the identity. For semisimple Lie groups, adjoint orbits have a symplectic structure (Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau), and they are the geometric foundation of quantization theory. For $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ the orbits in $\mathfrak{so}(3) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$ are the spheres centered at the origin (and the origin itself), which is the geometric content of "rotations are classified by axis and angle" — see [[Ex - The Adjoint Representation of SO(3) is the Defining Representation]].

A meta-strategy underlies all of these: **the Lie algebra is where the work is done; the exponential map is the bridge**. The unifying question of the chapter is "what is the first-order behaviour of this group operation, and how does it determine the group?" Every theorem in this chapter is an answer at some level: Theorem 8.37 says the first-order behaviour at $e$ is the *whole* of the Lie algebra; Theorem 20.27 says the first-order behaviour of conjugation is exactly $\mathrm{ad}$; the closed subgroup theorem says first-order linear data on a subgroup integrates back to smooth manifold data; and the Lie correspondence (preview) says first-order data is the complete invariant for simply connected groups. The chapter is a sustained demonstration that linearization at the identity, despite its apparent loss of information, captures essentially everything when the group structure forces enough rigidity — and this is the principle that powers all subsequent applications of Lie theory in geometry, physics, and analysis.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem|The Closed Subgroup Theorem]]** *(Cartan)*: A topologically closed subgroup of a Lie group is automatically an embedded Lie subgroup. **Typical use:** this is the universal mechanism for putting smooth manifold structure on subgroups. Whenever a subgroup is defined by an equation $\Phi(A) = c$ in $G$, the level set is closed by continuity, so by the closed subgroup theorem it is automatically a Lie subgroup with manifold structure. This is why $\mathrm{O}(n)$, $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, $\mathrm{U}(n)$, $\mathrm{SU}(n)$, $\mathrm{Sp}(2n)$, $\mathrm{SL}(n)$ are all smooth manifolds — closed subgroup theorem, applied to determinant, transpose, and conjugate-transpose equations. It is also the linchpin of the homogeneous-space construction.

- **[[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map|Naturality of exp]]**: For any Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$, $F \circ \exp_G = \exp_H \circ F_*$. **Typical use:** this is the conversion device between the group and algebra sides. Whenever a calculation is more easily done in $\mathfrak{g}$, do it there and transport back via $\exp$; whenever a group-level question reduces to a question about $F_*$, naturality says the conversion is correct. It is also the key ingredient in showing that Lie group homomorphisms are determined by their derivatives — since $\exp$ generates the identity component (Lee Prop 7.14), $F$ on the identity component is completely determined by $F_*$.

- **The identification $\mathfrak{g} \cong T_e G$**: The evaluation isomorphism $X \mapsto X_e$ between left-invariant vector fields and tangent vectors at the identity. **Typical use:** this is the practical definition that lets one compute $\mathfrak{g}$ as a familiar tangent space — for matrix groups, $T_I G$ sits as a subspace of $\mathfrak{gl}(n)$, and the Lie bracket transports to the matrix commutator. Whenever you want to compute $\mathfrak{g}$, compute $T_e G$ instead. The full force of the identification is the rigidity statement: a left-invariant vector field on $G$ is determined by its value at a single point ($e$), so the infinite-dimensional space $\mathfrak{X}(G)$ contains a canonical finite-dimensional copy of $T_e G$.

- **[[Thm - Orbit-Stabilizer for Lie Group Actions|Orbit-stabilizer for Lie group actions]]**: A transitive smooth action of $G$ on $M$ realizes $M \cong G/G_p$ as smooth manifolds. **Typical use:** this is the universal way to identify a manifold as a homogeneous space. Whenever a familiar manifold has a recognizable transitive Lie group action, orbit-stabilizer expresses it as a quotient, and the quotient then inherits all of $G$'s structure: invariant metrics, invariant connections, invariant volume forms. Spheres, projective spaces, Grassmannians, flag manifolds, Stiefel manifolds, and the Hermitian symmetric domains are all of this form.

- **The integration of Lie algebra to Lie group, for simply connected $G$**: A Lie algebra homomorphism $\varphi : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ integrates to a unique Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$ when $G$ is simply connected. **Typical use:** this is one half of the Lie correspondence; it is how Lie algebra representations are upgraded to Lie group representations. The proof uses the fundamental theorem on Lie algebra actions (Lee Thm 20.16) to turn $\varphi$ into a flow on $H$, and the orbit through $e$ traces out the homomorphism $F$. The simple-connectivity hypothesis is essential: without it, the homomorphism might be multi-valued on $G$ — which is precisely the failure mode of the $\mathrm{SO}(3) \to \mathrm{SU}(2)$ direction.

# Bridges

1. **Group theory — Lie groups as group objects in the manifold category.** The abstract notion of [[Def - Group|group]] given by associativity, identity, and inverses is, by [[Def - Lie Group|definition]], promoted to a Lie group by requiring multiplication and inversion to be smooth maps rather than mere functions. This is a single instance of the general categorical pattern "a group object in $\mathcal{C}$": where the diagrams expressing the group axioms — multiplication, unit, inverse — are reinterpreted in $\mathcal{C}$, with the product replaced by the Cartesian product of $\mathcal{C}$. In $\mathbf{Set}$ one recovers ordinary groups; in $\mathbf{Top}$ one gets topological groups; in $\mathbf{Man}$ one gets Lie groups; in the category of varieties over a field one gets algebraic groups. The Lie functor $G \mapsto \mathrm{Lie}(G)$ is the linearization-at-the-identity functor; it is a functor from Lie groups to Lie algebras, with $F : G \to H$ sending to $dF_e : T_e G \to T_e H$.

2. **Group action theory — Lie group actions as smooth refinements of abstract actions.** A smooth Lie group action of $G$ on $M$ is the same data as an abstract [[Def - Group Action|group action]] $G \times M \to M$ subject to the additional requirement that the action map be smooth. Most of the abstract orbit-stabilizer-theoretic structure carries over: orbits partition $M$, stabilizers are subgroups, and the [[Thm - Orbit-Stabiliser Theorem|abstract orbit-stabilizer theorem]] $|G \cdot p| = |G|/|G_p|$ from finite group theory has the smooth analogue $\dim M = \dim G - \dim G_p$ (for transitive actions). The smooth setting adds the [[Thm - Homogeneous Space is a Smooth Manifold|homogeneous-space theorem]]: for a closed subgroup $H \leq G$, the quotient $G/H$ has a canonical smooth manifold structure of dimension $\dim G - \dim H$ — finite-dimensional Lagrange's theorem is now a dimension equation between real numbers, not a divisibility statement.

3. **Linear algebra — the Lie algebra as a tangent-space-with-bracket.** The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is, as a vector space, simply the tangent space $T_e G$ — a finite-dimensional [[Def - Vector Space|vector space]] of dimension $\dim G$. What makes it a Lie algebra and not just a vector space is the bracket, which is the infinitesimal trace of the group's non-commutativity: $[X, Y] = (XY - YX)|_{\text{first order}}$ in a sense made precise by the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula. For matrix Lie groups, the bracket is literally the matrix commutator $[A, B] = AB - BA$, which is a [[Def - Bilinear Form|bilinear]] operation on the matrix space — so the Lie algebra is a vector space with an extra piece of multilinear structure, exactly the kind of object that linear algebra studies. The Killing form $B(X, Y) = \mathrm{tr}(\mathrm{ad}_X \mathrm{ad}_Y)$ is a natural [[Def - Bilinear Form|symmetric bilinear form]] on $\mathfrak{g}$, and its signature controls whether $G$ is compact, semisimple, etc.

4. **Special relativity — the Lorentz group as a Lie group.** The [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]] $\mathrm{O}(3, 1)$ — the isometry group of [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|Minkowski space]] $(\mathbb{R}^4, \eta)$ where $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ — is a $6$-dimensional Lie group: a closed subgroup of $\mathrm{GL}(4, \mathbb{R})$ by the closed subgroup theorem, with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{o}(3, 1) = \{X \in \mathfrak{gl}(4, \mathbb{R}) : \eta X + X^T \eta = 0\}$. The four connected components (full Lorentz group / orthochronous / proper / proper-orthochronous) correspond to choices of time-orientation and orientation; the identity component $\mathrm{SO}^+(3, 1)$ is the proper-orthochronous Lorentz group, and its exponential map sends $\mathfrak{o}(3, 1)$ to boosts and rotations. The double cover $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C}) \to \mathrm{SO}^+(3, 1)$ is the four-dimensional analogue of $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$, and is the origin of the **spinor** in quantum field theory: the Dirac equation is $\mathrm{SL}(2, \mathbb{C})$-equivariant, not just Lorentz-equivariant, and Dirac spinors transform under representations of the double cover that have no analogue at the Lorentz-group level.

5. **Riemannian geometry — Lie groups as homogeneous Riemannian manifolds.** Every Lie group admits a **left-invariant Riemannian metric** by choosing any inner product on $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$ and transporting it by $d(L_g)_e$ to every other tangent space. When $G$ is compact, one can average to produce a **bi-invariant** metric, and then the geodesics through $e$ are exactly the one-parameter subgroups $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ — the Riemannian-geometric exponential map coincides with the Lie-group exponential. Bi-invariant Riemannian metrics on compact Lie groups are the simplest non-flat **symmetric spaces**, and their curvature is computed entirely from the bracket: $R(X, Y) Z = -\tfrac{1}{4} [[X, Y], Z]$. Forward: **Riemannian Geometry** and **Symmetric Spaces** develop this connection, building isometry groups as Lie groups and using their Lie-algebra structure to compute curvature and geodesics.

6. **Quantum mechanics — Lie algebras as observable algebras.** The angular momentum operators $L_x, L_y, L_z$ in quantum mechanics satisfy the commutation relations $[L_x, L_y] = i\hbar L_z$ and cyclic, which is exactly the structure constants of $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ (or equivalently $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, up to factors of $i\hbar$). The angular momentum algebra of QM is the Lie algebra of the rotation group, and the "raising and lowering operators" $L_\pm = L_x \pm i L_y$ are a Cartan-like basis. The spin-$j$ irreducible representations of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ classify the quantum-mechanical particles by their angular momentum content; that the half-integer spins exist comes from the fact that $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, not $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, is the simply connected covering group — physical particles transform under representations of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, with rotations by $2\pi$ acting by $\pm 1$ depending on whether the spin is integer or half-integer.

7. **Stochastic analysis on Lie groups — left-invariant Brownian motion.** A Brownian motion on a Lie group $G$ that is invariant under left translation is constructed from a Brownian motion $B_t$ on $\mathfrak{g}$ via the Itô equation $dg_t = g_t \, dB_t$, which has the closed-form solution involving the stochastic exponential (and reduces to the matrix exponential when $B_t$ is replaced by $tA$ deterministic). The generator of left-invariant Brownian motion is the Laplace–Beltrami operator with respect to a left-invariant metric, and on compact $G$ this operator is diagonalized by the irreducible representations of $G$ via the Peter–Weyl theorem. Bridge to user's research: continuous dynamic Bayesian networks on Lie group state spaces use this structure, with the Lie algebra encoding linearized infinitesimal dynamics.

8. **Information geometry — Lie groups as statistical manifolds.** Statistical models parametrized by a Lie group $G$ inherit a natural geometric structure: the Fisher information metric becomes a left-invariant Riemannian metric on $G$ (when the family is "exponentially structured" in the Lie-algebra sense). The dual connections of information geometry — the $e$-connection and $m$-connection — are both flat for exponential families, and on Lie groups they admit explicit formulas in terms of the bracket. The **Wasserstein metric** on probability measures over a Lie group involves the Lie algebra in an essential way through the displacement interpolation, and recent work connects this to mean-field limits of particle systems with Lie-group state spaces.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a Lie group is its Lie algebra plus a discrete topological correction.** The Lie correspondence states this precisely: the functor $G \mapsto \mathfrak{g}$ is *injective up to coverings* — two connected Lie groups with isomorphic Lie algebras differ only by a discrete central quotient. The Lie algebra captures everything that is **infinitesimally local** about $G$: the dimension, the bracket structure, the structural properties (abelian, nilpotent, solvable, semisimple, simple), the local model around any point (which is the same as the local model at $e$ by left-translation). What the Lie algebra does **not** see is the global topology: the fundamental group $\pi_1(G)$, the number of connected components, the discrete center. The slogan is: **Lie algebra = local structure; topology = global structure; together they determine the group.** This is the parallel with how a manifold is determined by its tangent bundle plus its differentiable structure — the tangent bundle is local, the differentiable structure is global, both are needed.

**The true name of the exponential map is "the flow of a left-invariant vector field at time $1$, starting from $e$".** The definition $\exp(X) = \gamma_X(1)$ where $\gamma_X$ is the one-parameter subgroup is sometimes presented as primary, but the operational understanding is via integration of vector fields: $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ corresponds to a left-invariant vector field $X^L$ on $G$, this vector field has a flow $\phi_t^{X^L}$, and $\exp(X) = \phi_1^{X^L}(e)$. This perspective makes the naturality of $\exp$ obvious: a Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$ takes integral curves of $X^L$ to integral curves of $(F_* X)^L$ (because $F$ is $L_g \leftrightarrow L_{F(g)}$-equivariant), so $F(\exp X) = \exp(F_* X)$. It also makes the matrix-exponential identification immediate: on $\mathrm{GL}(n)$, the left-invariant vector field $X^L$ generates the ODE $\dot g = g X$ (right-multiplication by $X$), whose solution from $g_0 = I$ is $g(t) = e^{tX}$.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: when you see a defining equation for a matrix group, differentiate to find the Lie algebra.** This is the single most-used computation in the topic. Given $G = \{A : \Phi(A) = c\}$, substitute $A(t) = I + tX$, expand $\Phi(A(t)) = c$ to first order in $t$, set the $O(t)$ coefficient to zero, read off the linear constraint on $X$. For $\mathrm{O}(n)$, $\Phi(A) = A^T A - I$ gives $X^T + X = 0$. For $\mathrm{SL}(n)$, $\Phi(A) = \det(A) - 1$ gives $\mathrm{tr} X = 0$ (using $\det(I + tX) = 1 + t \mathrm{tr} X + O(t^2)$). For $\mathrm{Sp}(2n)$, $\Phi(A) = A^T J A - J$ gives $X^T J + J X = 0$. For $\mathrm{U}(n)$, $\Phi(A) = A^* A - I$ gives $X^* + X = 0$ (skew-Hermitian). Once you internalize this, computing any classical-matrix Lie algebra is a one-line calculation.

**Conjugation is the bridge from non-commutativity to representation theory.** A Lie group's failure to be abelian is precisely measured by the conjugation action $C_g(h) = ghg^{-1}$, which is non-trivial iff there exist non-commuting $g, h \in G$. Linearizing $C_g$ at $e$ gives $\mathrm{Ad}_g \in \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$; collecting these over all $g$ gives the adjoint representation $\mathrm{Ad} : G \to \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$, a linear action of $G$ on its own algebra. The kernel of $\mathrm{Ad}$ is the center of $G$ (Lee Problem 20-20), and so $G/Z(G) \hookrightarrow \mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$ as the adjoint group $\mathrm{Inn}(G)$. **The whole of representation theory begins here**: a representation of $G$ is a Lie group homomorphism $\rho : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V)$, and the adjoint representation is the canonical example, available on every Lie group. The fact that $G/Z(G)$ embeds in $\mathrm{GL}(\mathfrak{g})$ is what makes $\mathrm{Ad}$ a faithful representation modulo the center — and is the reason centerless simple Lie groups are essentially matrix groups.

**The double cover $\mathrm{SU}(2) \to \mathrm{SO}(3)$ explains why spinors exist.** $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is the simply connected double cover of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ — both have the same Lie algebra $\mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathfrak{so}(3) \cong (\mathbb{R}^3, \times)$, but $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is connected and simply connected while $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ has fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}/2$. The covering map sends a unit quaternion (an element of $S^3 \cong \mathrm{SU}(2)$) to the rotation $v \mapsto q v q^{-1}$ on $\mathbb{R}^3 \cong \mathrm{Im}(\mathbb{H})$, and the kernel is $\{\pm 1\}$. The Lie correspondence on the simply-connected side then says: every irreducible representation of $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ integrates to a unique representation of $\mathrm{SU}(2)$, but only those representations whose value on $-1 \in \mathrm{SU}(2)$ is the identity descend to representations of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$. The "half-integer spin" representations of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ — those of odd-dimensional weight space — fail this criterion and live only on $\mathrm{SU}(2)$; physically, a $2\pi$ rotation acts by $-1$ on a half-integer-spin state, which is the geometric origin of the Pauli exclusion principle when combined with the spin-statistics theorem. The exercise $\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong S^3$ in this chapter is the geometric prerequisite for all of this.

**A Lie group is rigid in a way no other manifold is.** Knowing what happens in an arbitrarily small neighbourhood of one point — the identity $e$ — determines the entire group structure on the identity component, because left translations are diffeomorphisms and any neighbourhood of $e$ generates the identity component (Lee Prop 7.14). For a generic smooth manifold, local data near one point says nothing about behaviour elsewhere; for a Lie group, local data at $e$ propagates everywhere via translation, and the propagation respects the group multiplication. This is the **homogeneity** of a Lie group: every point looks like every other point, in a smooth-manifold-with-extra-structure sense, and the only obstruction to recovering the global group from infinitesimal data is the global topology (fundamental group, components). This rigidity is what makes the classification of Lie groups so much more tractable than the classification of general manifolds — and is why every classification result in Lie theory is ultimately a finite-dimensional linear-algebraic problem on the Lie algebra.
