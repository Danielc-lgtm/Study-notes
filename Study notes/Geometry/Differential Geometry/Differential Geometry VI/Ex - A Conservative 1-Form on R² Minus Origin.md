# Problem Statement

Let $M = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$, the punctured plane, with standard coordinates $(x, y)$. Consider the smooth 1-form
$$\omega := \frac{x \, dy - y \, dx}{x^2 + y^2} \in \Omega^1(M).$$
Show that:
(a) $\omega$ is **closed**: $d\omega = 0$ on $M$.
(b) $\omega$ is **not exact**: there is no smooth function $f : M \to \mathbb{R}$ with $\omega = df$.
(c) For the unit circle $\gamma(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$, $\int_\gamma \omega = 2\pi$.

**Recall:**

For a 1-form $\omega = P \, dx + Q \, dy$ on an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$, $d\omega = (\partial Q / \partial x - \partial P / \partial y) \, dx \wedge dy$. So $\omega$ is closed iff $\partial Q / \partial x = \partial P / \partial y$.

A 1-form $\omega$ is **exact** if $\omega = df$ for some smooth function $f$. By the fundamental theorem of line integrals ([[Def - Line Integral of a 1-Form]]), exact forms have path-independent integrals: $\int_\gamma df = f(\gamma(b)) - f(\gamma(a))$.

By [[Thm - A Closed 1-Form on a Simply Connected Manifold is Exact]], closed 1-forms on simply connected manifolds are exact. The punctured plane $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ is **not simply connected** — the unit circle is a loop that does not contract to a point.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** Constructing and analyzing a closed-but-not-exact 1-form. This is the prototypical example of a topological obstruction to exactness, and the canonical case used to motivate de Rham cohomology.

**Assumption pattern:** $\omega = (x \, dy - y \, dx)/(x^2 + y^2)$ is a specific smooth 1-form on the punctured plane. The denominator $x^2 + y^2$ is nonzero on $M$, so the form is smooth. The closedness will be verified by direct partial-derivative computation. The non-exactness will be shown by computing a loop integral and observing it is nonzero.

**Theorem routing:** For closedness, apply the coordinate formula $d\omega = (\partial Q / \partial x - \partial P / \partial y) dx \wedge dy$ with $P = -y/(x^2+y^2), Q = x/(x^2+y^2)$. The mixed partial derivatives are equal by direct computation.

For non-exactness, use the contrapositive of the fundamental theorem: if $\omega$ were exact, every closed-loop integral of $\omega$ would vanish. Compute $\int_\gamma \omega$ around the unit circle and show it is $2\pi \neq 0$.

**Key decision point:** The non-obvious step is recognizing that on $M = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$, $\omega$ is **locally** of the form $d\theta$ (the angle function $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$ is locally well-defined), but $\theta$ is not globally well-defined as a single-valued function. So $\omega$ is "locally exact" but not globally exact — and the obstruction is exactly the topological fact that $\pi_1(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}) \neq 0$ (the punctured plane has a nontrivial fundamental [[Def - Group|group]], $\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the unit circle).

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Operation 5 from the topic page (differentiate a function to produce a 1-form).** The angle function $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$ locally gives $d\theta = \omega$ — but only locally.

2. **Operation 7 from the topic page (test exactness by integrating around a loop).** Compute $\int_\gamma \omega = 2\pi$ to refute exactness.

3. **Coordinate formula for $d\omega$.** $d\omega = (\partial Q / \partial x - \partial P / \partial y) dx \wedge dy$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

4. **Illegal but tempting operation 4 (concluding a closed 1-form is exact without checking topology).** The exercise is a direct illustration: $\omega$ is closed, yet not exact, because $M$ is not simply connected.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Compute $d\omega$ in coordinates: with $P = -y/(x^2+y^2)$ and $Q = x/(x^2+y^2)$, find $\partial Q/\partial x$ and $\partial P/\partial y$.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Both partials should equal $(y^2 - x^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2$. So $d\omega = 0$ and $\omega$ is closed.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For the line integral around the unit circle: parameterize as $\gamma(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$, $\gamma'(t) = (-\sin t, \cos t)$. Substitute into $\omega = (x dy - y dx)/(x^2+y^2)$ and simplify using $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ on the unit circle.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> The integrand simplifies to $\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$. So $\int_\gamma \omega = \int_0^{2\pi} 1 \, dt = 2\pi$.

> [!note]- Hint 5
> If $\omega = df$ were exact, then $\int_\gamma df = f(\gamma(2\pi)) - f(\gamma(0)) = 0$ since $\gamma(2\pi) = \gamma(0)$ (closed loop). But $\int_\gamma \omega = 2\pi \neq 0$, contradiction. So $\omega$ is not exact.

# Solution

**Plan:** (a) Compute $d\omega = 0$ directly. (b) Compute $\int_\gamma \omega = 2\pi$ around the unit circle. (c) Use the contrapositive of the fundamental theorem for exact 1-forms to conclude non-exactness.

**Step 1: $\omega$ is closed.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Write $\omega = P dx + Q dy$ with
> $$P(x, y) = \frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}, \quad Q(x, y) = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}.$$
> Compute partial derivatives:
> $$\frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{-(x^2 + y^2) - (-y)(2y)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{-(x^2 + y^2) + 2y^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2},$$
> $$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} = \frac{(x^2 + y^2) - x(2x)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{x^2 + y^2 - 2x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}.$$
> So $\partial Q / \partial x = \partial P / \partial y$, hence $d\omega = (\partial Q / \partial x - \partial P / \partial y) dx \wedge dy = 0$.

**Step 2: Compute $\int_\gamma \omega$ around the unit circle.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Parameterize $\gamma(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$ for $t \in [0, 2\pi]$, with $\gamma'(t) = (-\sin t, \cos t)$. On the unit circle, $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, so the denominator simplifies.
>
> Substitute into the line-integral formula:
> $$\omega_{\gamma(t)}(\gamma'(t)) = \frac{\cos t \cdot \cos t - \sin t \cdot (-\sin t)}{1} = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1.$$
> So
> $$\int_\gamma \omega = \int_0^{2\pi} \omega_{\gamma(t)}(\gamma'(t)) \, dt = \int_0^{2\pi} 1 \, dt = 2\pi.$$

**Step 3: $\omega$ is not exact.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Suppose for contradiction that $\omega = df$ for some smooth $f : M \to \mathbb{R}$. By the fundamental theorem for line integrals ([[Def - Line Integral of a 1-Form]]),
> $$\int_\gamma df = f(\gamma(2\pi)) - f(\gamma(0)) = f(1, 0) - f(1, 0) = 0$$
> since $\gamma$ is a closed loop ($\gamma(2\pi) = \gamma(0) = (1, 0)$). But by Step 2, $\int_\gamma \omega = 2\pi \neq 0$. Contradiction.
>
> Therefore no such $f$ exists, and $\omega$ is not exact.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Closedness.** Let $P = -y/(x^2+y^2)$ and $Q = x/(x^2+y^2)$, so $\omega = P dx + Q dy$. Direct computation: $\partial P/\partial y = (y^2 - x^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2 = \partial Q/\partial x$. So $d\omega = 0$ on $M = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$, i.e., $\omega$ is closed.
>
> **Loop integral.** For $\gamma(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)$, $t \in [0, 2\pi]$: $\gamma'(t) = (-\sin t, \cos t)$, $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, so
> $$\omega(\gamma'(t)) = \cos t \cdot \cos t - \sin t \cdot (-\sin t) = 1.$$
> Therefore $\int_\gamma \omega = \int_0^{2\pi} 1 \, dt = 2\pi$.
>
> **Non-exactness.** Suppose $\omega = df$ for $f \in C^\infty(M)$. By the fundamental theorem, $\int_\gamma df = f(\gamma(2\pi)) - f(\gamma(0)) = 0$ (closed loop). But $\int_\gamma \omega = 2\pi \neq 0$, contradiction. So $\omega \neq df$ for any $f \in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\})$; $\omega$ is closed but not exact.
> $\qquad\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Illegal but tempting alternative — "the angle function $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$ is the potential"
> One might be tempted to write $\omega = d\theta$ with $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$, on the grounds that locally this is correct. The trouble is that $\theta$ is not a globally well-defined smooth function on $M = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ — different branches of arctan differ by integer multiples of $\pi$, and choosing a branch requires removing a "cut" (e.g., the positive $x$-axis) from $M$, which leaves a simply connected domain on which $\theta$ is well-defined and $\omega = d\theta$. But on the punctured plane *as a whole*, no single-valued $\theta$ exists, and the failure is exactly the failure of exactness. So "$\omega = d\theta$" is a *local* identity that does not extend globally — and the loop integral $2\pi$ is exactly the increment $\theta$ would gain if it could be followed around the loop without jumping.

# Key Takeaways

**Closed plus non-exact is the prototypical "topological obstruction" example.** This exercise is the canonical demonstration that closedness is strictly weaker than exactness on non-simply-connected manifolds. The form $\omega = (x dy - y dx)/(x^2 + y^2)$ is the simplest closed-but-not-exact 1-form in nature, and its loop integral $2\pi$ is the simplest nontrivial period. Together they show that the first de Rham cohomology $H^1_{dR}(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}) \neq 0$ — and in fact equals $\mathbb{R}$, generated by $[\omega]$.

**The integral around the unit circle is the "winding number" of the loop around the origin, times $2\pi$.** For any loop $\gamma$ in $M = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$, the integral $\int_\gamma \omega = 2\pi \cdot n(\gamma, 0)$ where $n(\gamma, 0) \in \mathbb{Z}$ is the winding number — the (signed) number of times $\gamma$ goes around the origin. This makes $\omega$ a "winding-number-detector": it picks out the topological invariant. The map $H^1_{dR}(M) \to \mathbb{R}$, $[\omega] \mapsto \int_\gamma \omega$ for $\gamma$ a generator of $\pi_1(M) = \mathbb{Z}$, is an isomorphism, called the **period map** of the cohomology.

**The angle function $\theta$ is locally a potential but not globally.** On any simply connected open subset of $M$ (e.g., $M$ minus the positive $x$-axis), a branch of $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$ is a smooth function with $d\theta = \omega$. So $\omega$ is locally exact everywhere on $M$. The obstruction to global exactness is the multi-valuedness of $\theta$: traversing the unit circle once, $\theta$ would increase by $2\pi$, but any single-valued function on a closed loop must return to its starting value. The mismatch — $2\pi$ vs $0$ — is exactly the period $\int_\gamma \omega$.

**This example motivates de Rham cohomology and the structure of $H^1_{dR}$.** The form $\omega$ represents a nonzero class in $H^1_{dR}(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\})$, and the loop integral is the cohomology pairing with the generator of $H_1(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}; \mathbb{Z}) = \pi_1(M)_{\mathrm{ab}}$. The full de Rham theorem (in [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius]]) identifies $H^k_{dR}$ with singular cohomology, making this example a concrete instance of the topology-of-$M$-detected-by-forms phenomenon.
