# Notation Registry

**Standing conventions.** Throughout this topic, "manifold" means *smooth, Hausdorff, second-countable, finite-dimensional* manifold unless explicitly qualified. The word "smooth" means $C^\infty$ — infinitely differentiable in every coordinate chart — and "manifold" without further qualification means manifold *without* boundary, in line with [[Def - Smooth Manifold]] and the dichotomy in [[Def - Smooth Manifold with Boundary]]. The dimension $n$ of an $n$-manifold is a topological invariant (see [[Thm - Invariance of Dimension]]), so $n$ is unambiguously associated to $M$. The empty set is a manifold of every dimension by convention; we usually ignore it without comment.

- $M, N$ — smooth manifolds; usually $\dim M = n$, $\dim N = m$
- $p, q \in M$ — points
- $(U, \varphi)$ — a coordinate chart: $U \subseteq M$ open, $\varphi : U \to \widehat{U} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ a [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphism]] onto an open subset
- $\widehat{U} = \varphi(U) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ — the coordinate image (Lee's hat notation)
- $x^1, \dots, x^n$ — local coordinates; $\varphi(p) = (x^1(p), \dots, x^n(p))$
- $\psi \circ \varphi^{-1}$ — the **transition map** from $(U, \varphi)$ to $(V, \psi)$, a map $\varphi(U \cap V) \to \psi(U \cap V)$
- $\mathcal{A}$ — a smooth atlas; $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ — its maximal extension (the smooth structure it determines)
- $(M, \mathcal{A})$ — a smooth manifold; usually abbreviated $M$
- $\mathbb{H}^n = \{(x^1, \dots, x^n) \in \mathbb{R}^n : x^n \geq 0\}$ — the closed upper half-space; the model for manifolds with boundary
- $\partial M$, $\operatorname{Int} M$ — the (manifold) boundary and interior of a manifold with boundary
- $S^n, \mathbb{RP}^n, \mathbb{CP}^n, T^n, \mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R}), G_k(V)$ — sphere, real and complex projective spaces, $n$-torus, general linear group, Grassmannian
- $\mathbb{B}^n = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : |x| < 1\}$ — the open unit ball
- $\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{C}^n$ — Euclidean and complex $n$-spaces

> [!warning] Convention: dimension and the standing topological assumptions
> Many sources (notably Cascini) write "manifold" to mean topological manifold and add "smooth" when needed; we follow Lee and reserve "manifold" alone for the smooth case. Some authors drop Hausdorff or second countability — we never do, because partitions of unity and paracompactness depend on both, and these are the engines of the existence theorems in [[Differential Geometry II — Smooth Maps and Partitions of Unity]].

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: a *smooth manifold* is a space that locally looks like $\mathbb{R}^n$ in a way smooth enough that calculus makes sense. The whole apparatus of charts, atlases, transition functions, and smooth structures exists to make that sentence precise — to extract from "looks locally like $\mathbb{R}^n$" exactly the data that allows differentiation to be performed without committing to a specific embedding in any ambient Euclidean space.

The motivating examples are the surfaces of classical multivariable analysis. The sphere $S^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ is a two-dimensional object in three-dimensional space, and you can plainly do calculus on it: differentiate a function $S^2 \to \mathbb{R}$, take gradients, solve PDEs on the sphere. But every such operation is performed in *local* coordinates — spherical coordinates near a typical point, stereographic coordinates near the poles — and the answers had better not depend on which coordinates you used. The classical theory of surfaces handles this by working *extrinsically*: $S^2$ sits inside $\mathbb{R}^3$, and you can always fall back on the ambient calculus. But the projective plane $\mathbb{RP}^2$ — the set of lines through the origin of $\mathbb{R}^3$ — does not naturally sit inside any low-dimensional Euclidean space, and yet you want to do calculus on it. The same goes for configuration spaces in mechanics, for the orbit space of a Lie group action, for the moduli spaces of algebraic geometry. The extrinsic viewpoint is a crutch we can no longer afford.

So we abstract: take *only* the data that "calculus in local coordinates works, and the answer does not depend on the coordinates". The data turns out to be remarkably economical. A *topological manifold* (see [[Def - Topological Manifold]]) is a Hausdorff, second-countable topological space in which every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. A *chart* (see [[Def - Coordinate Chart and Atlas]]) is one such [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphism]]. The trouble is that two charts may overlap, and a function smooth in one need not be smooth in the other; the function $f \circ \varphi^{-1}$ is smooth on $\varphi(U)$ in the ordinary sense of calculus, but $f \circ \psi^{-1} = (f \circ \varphi^{-1}) \circ (\varphi \circ \psi^{-1})$ — and unless the *transition function* $\varphi \circ \psi^{-1}$ is itself smooth, smoothness will not survive a coordinate change. So we demand more: an *atlas* (covering family of charts) is *smooth* if every transition function is a [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]] between open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$. This is the content of [[Def - Smooth Atlas and Smooth Structure]]. Two smooth atlases give "the same calculus" precisely when their union is again smooth, and the equivalence classes are *smooth structures*; equivalently, each is contained in a unique *maximal* smooth atlas, the standard form of the data.

The whole construction sits one diagram deep:

$$\text{topological manifold} \;\xrightarrow{\;+\,\text{smooth atlas}\;}\; \text{smooth manifold}.$$

This is the backbone of the chapter. Every example in §1.3 — the sphere, projective space, the torus, the general linear group, the Grassmannian — is built by following this template: produce charts, check transition functions are smooth, declare victory.

What this topic is *not* about: it is not about the *geometry* of manifolds. There are no distances, angles, curvatures, or volumes yet — those require additional structure (a Riemannian metric), and they enter in [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds|DG XII]]. What we have here is *differential topology*: the bare smooth structure, the minimum data needed to talk about differentiable functions and maps. The genuinely surprising fact is how much one can do with this minimum. Calculus on manifolds — tangent vectors ([[Differential Geometry III — Tangent Vectors and the Differential|DG III]]), vector fields and flows ([[Differential Geometry V — Vector Fields, Flows, and the Lie Bracket|DG V]]), differential forms ([[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms|DG VIII]]), Stokes's theorem ([[Differential Geometry IX — Orientation, Integration, and Stokes' Theorem|DG IX]]) — is built entirely on the smooth structure, with no metric in sight.

This topic assumes the reader has refreshed the following before working through it: the definition of a [[Def - Topological Space|topological space]] and its basic properties (Hausdorff, second countability, [[Def - Subspace Topology|subspace topology]], [[Def - Product Topology|product topology]]); [[Def - Continuous Map|continuous maps]] and [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphisms]]; the multivariable calculus of smooth maps between open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$, including the chain rule and the [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]]; and basic linear algebra ([[Def - Vector Space]], [[Def - Linear Map]], [[Def - Field]]), since the model space $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a vector space and bases will reappear constantly as the coordinate maps of charts.

# Concept Map

## §1.1 Topological Manifolds

- **[[Def - Topological Manifold]]**
	- A **topological $n$-manifold** is a topological space $M$ that is Hausdorff, second-countable, and locally Euclidean of [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] $n$ — every point has an open neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. The three conditions are independent: Hausdorff rules out pathologies like the "line with two origins" where limits would not be unique; second countability ensures partitions of unity exist; locally Euclidean is the geometric content. Spheres, tori, projective spaces, open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and graphs of continuous functions are all topological manifolds. The dimension $n$ is forced to be a topological invariant by [[Thm - Invariance of Dimension]].

- **[[Def - Coordinate Chart and Atlas]]**
	- A **coordinate chart** is a pair $(U, \varphi)$ with $U \subseteq M$ open and $\varphi : U \to \widehat{U} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ a homeomorphism onto an open set; the component functions $x^i := \pi^i \circ \varphi$ are the **local coordinates**. An **atlas** is a collection of charts whose domains cover $M$. A chart $(U, \varphi)$ provides a temporary identification of the patch $U$ with the open subset $\widehat{U}$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$, letting us do Euclidean calculus on $M$ in disguise. By definition of topological manifold, atlases exist; the trick of the chapter is choosing atlases whose overlap is *smooth*.

- **[[Thm - Invariance of Dimension]]**
	- A nonempty topological $n$-manifold cannot be homeomorphic to a topological $m$-manifold unless $n = m$ — equivalently, if open subsets $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$ are homeomorphic, then $n = m$. This is what makes the integer $n$ a well-defined invariant of a manifold rather than a feature of a chosen chart. The proof requires algebraic topology (singular or de Rham cohomology, or the simpler Brouwer fixed-point argument) and is properly the content of Lee's chapter 17; we state it here and use it as a black box. Without invariance of dimension, "the dimension of $M$" would not even be a sensible phrase.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Topological Invariants from Cohomology *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> Once dimension is a topological invariant, one asks which other features of a manifold are independent of the chart structure. The answer — **Betti numbers**, **fundamental group**, **homology and cohomology [[Def - Group|groups]]** — is the substance of algebraic topology, and the proof of invariance of dimension is the first instance of this kind of argument. The de Rham cohomology of [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius|DG X]] sits on top of this idea.

## §1.2 Smooth Structures

- **[[Def - Transition Function]]**
	- For two charts $(U, \varphi)$ and $(V, \psi)$ on a topological manifold $M$ with $U \cap V \neq \emptyset$, the **transition function** from $\varphi$ to $\psi$ is the composition $\psi \circ \varphi^{-1} : \varphi(U \cap V) \to \psi(U \cap V)$. It is automatically a homeomorphism between open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$, since both $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are. The transition function records the change of coordinates between the two charts; everything you want to be coordinate-independent on $M$ must transform correctly under it. Charts $(U, \varphi)$ and $(V, \psi)$ are **smoothly compatible** if $U \cap V = \emptyset$ or the transition function is a diffeomorphism between open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ — i.e. smooth with smooth inverse, in the ordinary sense of calculus.

- **[[Def - Smooth Atlas and Smooth Structure]]**
	- A **smooth atlas** on a topological manifold $M$ is an atlas whose every pair of charts is smoothly compatible. Two smooth atlases are **compatible** (give the same smooth structure) if their union is again a smooth atlas; this is an equivalence relation, and an equivalence class is a **smooth structure**. Equivalently, every smooth atlas is contained in a unique **maximal** smooth atlas — every chart smoothly compatible with all charts in the atlas is already in it — and a smooth structure may be defined either as an equivalence class of smooth atlases or as a single maximal smooth atlas. The maximal-atlas formulation is the standard one; it lets us speak of "smooth charts" unambiguously.

- **[[Thm - Smooth Structure from Maximal Atlas]]**
	- (Lee, Proposition 1.17.) Every smooth atlas $\mathcal{A}$ on a topological manifold $M$ is contained in a *unique* maximal smooth atlas $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ — the smooth structure determined by $\mathcal{A}$. Two smooth atlases determine the same smooth structure if and only if their union is again a smooth atlas. The proof is essentially symbol-chasing: take $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ to be all charts smoothly compatible with every chart of $\mathcal{A}$, and verify it is a smooth atlas using a transitivity argument with a witness chart from $\mathcal{A}$. This is the workhorse: it means we never have to *write down* a maximal atlas — any old smooth atlas does.

- **[[Def - Smooth Manifold]]**
	- A **smooth manifold** is a pair $(M, \mathcal{A})$ where $M$ is a topological manifold and $\mathcal{A}$ is a smooth structure (equivalently, a maximal smooth atlas) on $M$. The same topological manifold may carry many distinct smooth structures (most famously, exotic $\mathbb{R}^4$'s — uncountably many smooth structures on $\mathbb{R}^4$, all topologically the same — and Kervaire's example of a 10-manifold that admits no smooth structure at all). When the smooth structure is understood, we write "$M$ is a smooth manifold" and call the charts of $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$ the **smooth charts**. The category $\mathbf{Man}$ has smooth manifolds as objects and smooth maps as morphisms; one obtains $C^k$-manifold and complex-manifold categories by varying the compatibility requirement on transition functions.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Diffeomorphism|Diffeomorphism]], $C^\infty(M)$, and the Category of Manifolds *(from this chapter forward)*
> Once you have a smooth structure, a map $f : M \to N$ between smooth manifolds is **smooth** if every chart-representation $\psi \circ f \circ \varphi^{-1}$ is smooth in the ordinary sense ([[Differential Geometry II — Smooth Maps and Partitions of Unity|DG II]]). The smooth real-valued functions $C^\infty(M)$ form a commutative $\mathbb{R}$-algebra, the most important single algebraic invariant of $M$. The whole project of differential geometry is to study the category of smooth manifolds and its functorial constructions — tangent space ([[Differential Geometry III — Tangent Vectors and the Differential|DG III]]), tangent bundle, cotangent bundle, tensor bundles ([[Differential Geometry VII — Tensors and Tensor Fields|DG VII]]).

> [!tip] Unlocked: Complex Manifolds *(from Complex Geometry)*
> The smooth-manifold definition reads: charts to $\mathbb{R}^n$, transitions $C^\infty$. Replacing $\mathbb{R}^n$ by $\mathbb{C}^n$ and demanding transitions be *holomorphic* (complex-differentiable, satisfying the Cauchy–Riemann equations in each variable) gives the definition of a **complex manifold**. The Riemann sphere $S^2 = \mathbb{CP}^1$ with its two stereographic charts whose transition is $w = 1/z$ is the prototype; **Kähler manifolds** arise by also adding a compatible Riemannian metric. The categorical pattern — set + atlas + compatibility class on the model space — is exactly the same.

## §1.3 Examples of Smooth Manifolds

- **[[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]]**
	- If $M_1, \dots, M_k$ are smooth manifolds of [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]] $n_1, \dots, n_k$, then the product space $M_1 \times \cdots \times M_k$ with the product topology is a smooth manifold of dimension $n_1 + \cdots + n_k$, with smooth structure determined by the product charts $(U_1 \times \cdots \times U_k, \varphi_1 \times \cdots \times \varphi_k)$. Hausdorff and second countability are inherited from finite products of such spaces; the locally Euclidean property comes from the product of charts, with image an open box $\widehat{U}_1 \times \cdots \times \widehat{U}_k \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n_1+\cdots+n_k}$. The torus $T^n = S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1$ is the canonical example.

- **[[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold]]**
	- (Lee, Example 1.26.) Every open subset $U$ of a smooth $n$-manifold $M$ inherits a smooth $n$-manifold structure: the atlas $\mathcal{A}_U = \{(V, \varphi|_V) : (V, \varphi) \in \overline{\mathcal{A}}, V \subseteq U\}$ is smooth on $U$, and the resulting smooth manifold is called an **open submanifold** of $M$. Hausdorff, second countability, and the locally Euclidean property are inherited; transition functions for restricted charts are restrictions of transition functions, hence smooth. This is the construction that turns the general linear group $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R}) \subset M(n, \mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ into a smooth manifold.

- **[[Ex - The Sphere as a Smooth Manifold via Stereographic Projection]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Construct an explicit two-chart smooth atlas on $S^n$ using stereographic projection from the north and south poles, compute the transition function, and verify it is smooth on its domain. This is *the* foundational example: every later construction (the round metric, the volume form, the Hopf [[Def - Fibration|fibration]]) builds on this atlas.

- **[[Ex - Real Projective Space is a Smooth Manifold]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Construct the standard $(n+1)$-chart atlas on $\mathbb{RP}^n$ using affine sets where the $i$-th homogeneous coordinate is nonzero, derive a transition function, and verify smoothness. The Hausdorff and second-countability properties for the quotient topology need separate verification — this is the deepest part.

- **[[Ex - The Torus is a Smooth Manifold via Quotient]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Realize $T^n = \mathbb{R}^n / \mathbb{Z}^n$ as a quotient of $\mathbb{R}^n$ by translations, construct an atlas using restrictions of the quotient map, and verify smoothness. Compare with the product construction $T^n = S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1$ from [[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]].

- **[[Ex - The General Linear Group is a Smooth Manifold]]** (⭐)
	- $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R}) = \det^{-1}(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\})$ is an open subset of $M(n, \mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$, hence a smooth $n^2$-manifold by [[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold]]. The smooth structure inherited this way is the one used in [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|Lie group theory]].

- **[[Ex - Compatibility of Two Atlases on the Sphere]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that the graph-coordinate atlas (Lee Example 1.4: $2n+2$ charts $U_i^\pm$) and the stereographic atlas (Lee Problem 1-7: two charts) determine the same smooth structure on $S^n$ — i.e., their union is a smooth atlas. This is the workhorse exercise for the "smooth structure as equivalence class" picture.

- **[[Ex - The Grassmannian is a Smooth Manifold]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Construct charts on the Grassmannian $G_k(V)$ of $k$-dimensional [[Def - Subspace|subspaces]] of an $n$-dimensional vector space $V$ using complementary [[Def - Subspace|subspaces]], verify transition functions are smooth (matrix-Möbius transformations $X' = (B+DX)(A+CX)^{-1}$), and conclude $G_k(V)$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $k(n-k)$. The Grassmannian is the deepest example in the chapter and the prototype for **classifying spaces** in topology.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Lie Group|Lie Group]] *(from Differential Geometry and Mathematical Physics)*
> The general linear group $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$ is the prototype of a **Lie group** — a smooth manifold equipped with smooth group operations. Adding "group structure" to "smooth manifold" gives an enormously rigid object whose entire local structure is captured by its Lie algebra. The orthogonal, unitary, symplectic, and special linear groups are all matrix Lie groups, and their representation theory is the algebraic backbone of quantum mechanics, gauge theory, and harmonic analysis. The development is in [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|DG XI]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Fibre Bundle|Fibre Bundle]] *(from Topology and Geometry)*
> The Grassmannian $G_k(\mathbb{R}^n)$ carries a **tautological bundle** — at each point (a $k$-plane $V \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$) we attach the vector space $V$ itself — and every rank-$k$ vector bundle on a paracompact space is a pullback of this tautological bundle along a map to $G_k$. Grassmannians are therefore the **classifying spaces** for vector bundles, and the cohomology classes (Chern, Stiefel–Whitney, Pontryagin) of a bundle are read off from the [[Def - Homotopy|homotopy]] class of its classifying map. The general theory of **fibre bundles** and **principal bundles** sits on top of this.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Riemannian Manifold|Riemannian Manifold]] *(from Riemannian Geometry)*
> Once smooth manifolds are in hand, the next layer of structure is a smoothly varying inner product on each tangent space — a **Riemannian metric**. The pair $(M, g)$ is a Riemannian manifold, and from $g$ one derives distance, angle, [[Def - Geodesic|geodesics]], volume, and curvature. Every paracompact smooth manifold admits a Riemannian metric (by a partition-of-unity argument); the development is in [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds|DG XII]].

## §1.4 Manifolds with Boundary

- **[[Def - Smooth Manifold with Boundary]]**
	- A **topological $n$-manifold with boundary** is a Hausdorff, second-countable space $M$ in which every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open subset of either $\mathbb{R}^n$ or the closed upper half-space $\mathbb{H}^n = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^n : x^n \geq 0\}$. A **smooth structure** on such an $M$ is a maximal smooth atlas where chart targets are open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ or $\mathbb{H}^n$ and transition functions are smooth in the sense of admitting smooth extensions across the boundary $\partial \mathbb{H}^n$. The **boundary** $\partial M$ is the set of points sent to $\partial \mathbb{H}^n = \{x^n = 0\}$ by some chart; by smooth invariance of the boundary, this property does not depend on the chart. A closed half-line, the closed unit ball $\overline{\mathbb{B}^n}$ (whose boundary is $S^{n-1}$), and any closed interval are examples.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Stokes's Theorem on Manifolds with Boundary *(from later in this subject)*
> The whole point of admitting boundaries is to be able to integrate by parts. **Stokes's theorem** for manifolds with boundary, $\int_M d\omega = \int_{\partial M} \omega$, in [[Differential Geometry IX — Orientation, Integration, and Stokes' Theorem|DG IX]] is the master integration-by-parts formula, encompassing the classical Green, Gauss, and Stokes theorems as special cases. Without boundary, there is nothing for the right-hand side to integrate over, so the theorem trivializes.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises and theorems of this chapter chase a small number of recurring goals. The single most common is **showing that a given set is a smooth manifold**: produce an atlas, check smooth compatibility of overlapping charts, verify Hausdorff and second countability, declare victory. Spheres, projective spaces, the torus, Grassmannians, matrix groups, level sets of smooth functions — the form of the argument is identical, and the difficulty is concentrated in choosing the right charts. A second target is **identifying which topological manifold a given construction produces** — for instance, recognizing that $\mathrm{SO}(3) \cong \mathbb{RP}^3$ topologically, or that $\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2$ smoothly. A third is **showing two atlases determine the same smooth structure**, equivalently that their union is smooth; this is the right way to say "the smooth structure on $S^n$ is canonical" or "the projective-space smooth structure agrees with the quotient structure". A fourth, more delicate, is **showing a topological manifold admits no smooth structure** (e.g., Kervaire's 10-manifold) or that it admits *multiple* inequivalent ones (e.g., Milnor's exotic spheres in dimension 7 and Donaldson's exotic $\mathbb{R}^4$'s); these results are deep and out of scope here, but the chapter equips the reader to even formulate them. A fifth is **producing the right notion of map between manifolds** — a smooth function $f : M \to \mathbb{R}$ or smooth map $f : M \to N$ — which is the bridge to [[Differential Geometry II — Smooth Maps and Partitions of Unity|DG II]].

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The recurring sources are equally compact. **A set with a covering by injective maps to $\mathbb{R}^n$** — typically chosen for geometric reasons (projections, parametrizations, quotient maps) — is the starting datum, and the [[Thm - Smooth Structure from Maximal Atlas|smooth manifold chart lemma]] (Lee 1.35) converts it directly into a smooth manifold structure if the overlap maps are smooth, the sets cover, the Hausdorff condition holds in a chart-theoretic form, and countably many charts suffice. **A topological space already known to be Hausdorff and second-countable** (a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^N$, a finite product of manifolds, a quotient of a paracompact space by a properly discontinuous action) is the most efficient source: only the locally Euclidean property remains to be checked. **A smooth function $\Phi : U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ defined on an open $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, with a regular value $c$** — by the implicit function theorem, the level set $\Phi^{-1}(c)$ acquires graph coordinates and is a smooth $(n-k)$-manifold; this packages most concrete examples (the sphere, the orthogonal group, the special linear group, any solution set of polynomial equations with nondegenerate gradient). **An open subset of a smooth manifold** is automatically a smooth manifold ([[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold]]); this is the source behind matrix Lie groups. **A product of smooth manifolds** is automatically a smooth manifold ([[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]]); this gives tori, products of spheres, configuration spaces in classical mechanics. The routes between these sources and the targets above run through the chart lemma in essentially every problem.

# Legal Operations

The moves that almost every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Cover with charts.** Given a candidate manifold, the first move is to write down explicit charts: open sets $U_\alpha$ covering the space, [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphisms]] $\varphi_\alpha : U_\alpha \to \widehat{U}_\alpha \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. The trigger is *any* problem of the form "show $X$ is a smooth manifold". The pattern is "name the charts, then check compatibility" — and the only freedom is in choosing the charts well, since a clever choice can collapse a transition function from a horror to a one-line check. *Trigger:* "show that ... is a smooth manifold". *Pattern:* find the natural geometric parametrizations of the space and read off charts.

2. **Compute transition functions explicitly.** Given two charts $(U, \varphi)$ and $(V, \psi)$, the transition $\psi \circ \varphi^{-1} : \varphi(U \cap V) \to \psi(U \cap V)$ is a function from open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ to open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Compute it; check smoothness using ordinary multivariable calculus. *Trigger:* "show the atlas is smooth". *Pattern:* invert $\varphi$ on $\varphi(U \cap V)$, compose with $\psi$, recognise the result as built from smooth elementary functions, polynomials, and rational functions with nonvanishing denominator.

3. **Apply the smooth manifold chart lemma (Lee 1.35).** When you have a set $M$ (no a priori topology) with maps $\varphi_\alpha : U_\alpha \to \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfying five technical conditions (bijection onto open set, smooth overlap, countable cover, Hausdorff-like separation), the lemma manufactures both a topology and a smooth structure on $M$. *Trigger:* a set defined by an algebraic or geometric construction, not yet endowed with a topology — Grassmannians, the tangent bundle, vector bundles. *Pattern:* the lemma is the cheapest route to "$X$ is a smooth manifold" when starting from a set rather than a space.

4. **Pass to an open subset.** Any open subset $U \subseteq M$ of a smooth manifold inherits a smooth structure by restricting charts ([[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold]]). *Trigger:* the space in question is naturally cut out as the locus where some continuous function is nonzero — $\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ where $\det \neq 0$, $\mathrm{GL}^+(n,\mathbb{R})$ where $\det > 0$, the upper half-space $\{x^n > 0\}$. *Pattern:* don't reprove smoothness — invoke [[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold]].

5. **Take a product.** Any finite product of smooth manifolds is a smooth manifold via product charts ([[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]]). *Trigger:* the space is visibly a Cartesian product — the torus $T^n = (S^1)^n$, configuration spaces $M \times M \times \dots \times M$, phase spaces $M \times \mathbb{R}^n$. *Pattern:* check the manifolds, multiply.

6. **Apply the regular value theorem (preview of [[Differential Geometry IV — Submersions, Immersions, Embeddings, and Submanifolds|DG IV]]).** If $\Phi : U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ is smooth on an open $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}^k$ is a regular value (the Jacobian $D\Phi$ has full rank $k$ at every preimage point), then $\Phi^{-1}(c)$ is a smooth $(n-k)$-manifold. *Trigger:* the space is the zero set of an equation or system of equations, and you have already computed the Jacobian once. *Pattern:* invoke the [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]] via its implicit-function cousin to produce graph coordinates near every point of the level set. Worked in [[Ex - The General Linear Group is a Smooth Manifold]] (open subset case) and developed fully in [[Differential Geometry IV — Submersions, Immersions, Embeddings, and Submanifolds|DG IV]].

7. **Quotient by a properly discontinuous group action.** If a discrete group $\Gamma$ acts freely and properly discontinuously on a smooth manifold $\widetilde{M}$, the quotient $\widetilde{M}/\Gamma$ inherits a smooth structure: charts are restrictions of charts of $\widetilde{M}$ to small open sets where the quotient map is a homeomorphism. *Trigger:* the space is presented as a quotient — $T^n = \mathbb{R}^n / \mathbb{Z}^n$, $\mathbb{RP}^n = S^n / \{\pm 1\}$, Klein bottle, lens spaces. *Pattern:* show the action is free + properly discontinuous, lift charts.

8. **Verify Hausdorff and second countability by inheritance.** Both conditions are inherited by subspaces (Hausdorff trivially, second countability by restricting the basis), by finite products, and — under the right conditions — by quotients. *Trigger:* you have a chart datum but need to verify the global topological conditions. *Pattern:* "$M$ embeds in $\mathbb{R}^N$" $\Rightarrow$ Hausdorff $+$ second countable for free.

9. **Replace one atlas by an equivalent one (use a maximal atlas).** A smooth structure is an *equivalence class* of compatible smooth atlases; the maximal atlas form, [[Thm - Smooth Structure from Maximal Atlas]], shows that to add a chart to the smooth structure you only need to verify smooth compatibility with charts of *any* atlas representing the class — not all of the (uncountable) maximal atlas. *Trigger:* you want to use a "nice" chart that is not in the original atlas (polar coordinates on $\mathbb{R}^2$, spherical coordinates on $S^2$). *Pattern:* show the chart is smoothly compatible with one given atlas; conclude it lies in the maximal atlas and may be used freely.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating "smooth" as a topological property
> It is tempting to think that since two charts $\varphi$ and $\varphi \circ g$ (with $g$ a homeomorphism but not diffeomorphism) both describe the *same* open subset of $M$ topologically, they should be smoothly interchangeable. They are not. The map $g(x) = x^3$ is a homeomorphism $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ but not a diffeomorphism (the inverse $y \mapsto y^{1/3}$ is not smooth at $0$); the chart $(\mathbb{R}, x \mapsto x^3)$ defines a smooth structure on $\mathbb{R}$ *distinct* from the standard one (Lee Example 1.23). What makes it legal is *smooth* compatibility, not topological compatibility — and smoothness is not a homeomorphism invariant. The repair: insist on smooth compatibility (diffeomorphism, not just homeomorphism) in the definition of compatibility.

> [!warning] 2. Forgetting to check Hausdorff or second countability
> The locally-Euclidean property looks like the substantive piece of the definition of topological manifold, and Hausdorff plus second countability look like routine technicalities. They are not. The *line with two origins* — the quotient of $\mathbb{R} \times \{0, 1\}$ by $(x, 0) \sim (x, 1)$ for $x \neq 0$ — is locally Euclidean and second countable but *not* Hausdorff: the two origins cannot be separated by disjoint open sets, and convergent sequences may have multiple limits (Lee Problem 1-1). An *uncountable disjoint union of $\mathbb{R}$'s* is locally Euclidean and Hausdorff but not second countable, and partitions of unity fail. Both pathologies break essentially every theorem of [[Differential Geometry II — Smooth Maps and Partitions of Unity|DG II]]. The repair: write down the topology explicitly, verify both conditions.

> [!warning] 3. Confusing the *manifold* dimension with the dimension of an ambient space
> The sphere $S^2$ is a 2-manifold, even though it sits inside the 3-dimensional $\mathbb{R}^3$; the Klein bottle is a 2-manifold even though it has no embedding in $\mathbb{R}^3$ at all (it embeds in $\mathbb{R}^4$). The dimension of a manifold is the dimension of the chart target $\mathbb{R}^n$, not the dimension of any ambient space. Indeed, the Whitney embedding theorem says every $n$-manifold embeds in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$, so the ambient dimension is a feature of the embedding, not of $M$. The repair: dimension is always read off from $\varphi : U \to \mathbb{R}^n$, never from any ambient embedding.

> [!warning] 4. Assuming smoothness of $f : M \to \mathbb{R}$ in one chart is enough
> Smoothness on a manifold is a *local* condition: $f$ is smooth at $p$ if $f \circ \varphi^{-1}$ is smooth on $\varphi(U)$ for *some* chart $\varphi$ containing $p$. The temptation is to check this in one chart and stop. But "the same calculation in another chart" amounts to $f \circ \psi^{-1} = (f \circ \varphi^{-1}) \circ (\varphi \circ \psi^{-1})$ — and this is smooth only because the *transition* $\varphi \circ \psi^{-1}$ is smooth, which is precisely what the smooth-atlas hypothesis guarantees. The repair: smoothness *does* propagate to all smooth charts, but the reason is the smooth structure, not magic. If you ever entertain a "smooth structure" with non-smooth transitions, smoothness in one chart fails to be smoothness in another.

> [!warning] 5. Assuming the diagonal action on a product gives the product smooth structure
> Given a smooth atlas on $M$ and on $N$, the product atlas $\{(U_\alpha \times V_\beta, \varphi_\alpha \times \psi_\beta)\}$ is smooth on $M \times N$. It is *not* in general the case that an atlas consisting only of "diagonal" charts $(U_\alpha \times U_\alpha, \varphi_\alpha \times \varphi_\alpha)$ on $M \times M$ is enough — such an atlas would only cover the neighbourhood of the diagonal in general. The repair: use the *full* product atlas $U_\alpha \times V_\beta$, not just diagonal pairs. (This is a subtle pitfall in constructing the tangent bundle's smooth structure in [[Differential Geometry III — Tangent Vectors and the Differential|DG III]].)

# Problem-Solving Strategy

Every problem in this chapter has the same essential shape: *given a candidate object, prove it is a smooth manifold (of some specified dimension), or use the smooth-manifold structure to deduce something about it.* The strategic question is how to read off, from the way the candidate is presented, which of the legal operations to deploy.

If the object is **presented as a subset of $\mathbb{R}^N$ cut out by equations** — the sphere $S^n = \{|x|^2 = 1\}$, the orthogonal group $O(n) = \{A^T A = I\}$, the special linear group $\mathrm{SL}(n) = \{\det A = 1\}$ — then the source is "level set of a smooth function with regular value", and the route runs through (or previews) the regular value theorem. You compute the Jacobian of the defining map, check that it has full rank at every preimage point, and conclude smooth manifold structure. Hausdorff and second countability come for free from the subspace topology inherited from $\mathbb{R}^N$. The skill is in the rank computation; for matrix groups, it is a determinant or transpose calculation that is usually fast once you set up the right system of coordinates. This is the source-target route most worth internalizing, because it generalizes to vastly more sophisticated settings (algebraic varieties, fibre bundles).

If the object is **presented as a quotient** — the projective space $\mathbb{RP}^n = (\mathbb{R}^{n+1} \setminus 0)/\sim$, the torus $T^n = \mathbb{R}^n/\mathbb{Z}^n$, the Klein bottle — then the source is "quotient of a manifold by a group action" or "quotient by an equivalence relation with nice local sections". The strategy is to construct charts on the quotient by *lifting* — find an open set $\widetilde{U} \subseteq \widetilde{M}$ on which the quotient map is a homeomorphism onto its image, then push forward a chart from $\widetilde{U}$. The hardest part is usually the Hausdorff property for the quotient; for free, properly discontinuous group actions on a Hausdorff space, the quotient is Hausdorff, and Cascini and Lee both give clean arguments. Cover-by-charts and verify-Hausdorff are the two halves of every quotient argument.

If the object is **presented as a set with no a priori topology** — a Grassmannian, a moduli space, the tangent bundle as a set — then you reach for the [[Thm - Smooth Structure from Maximal Atlas|smooth manifold chart lemma]] (Lee 1.35). The lemma constructs both the topology and the smooth structure simultaneously, and its five hypotheses are a checklist you go through item by item: each $\varphi_\alpha$ is a bijection onto an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$, the image sets $\varphi_\alpha(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta)$ are open, the transition maps are smooth, countably many of the $U_\alpha$ cover $M$, and any two points are separated either by a single chart or by disjoint chart domains. This is the heavyweight tool of the chapter and is reserved for cases where presenting a topology by hand is awkward.

If the object is **presented as a product** of known manifolds — $S^m \times S^n$, $T^n = S^1 \times \dots \times S^1$, the phase space $T^*M = M \times \mathbb{R}^n$ in a single trivializing chart — invoke [[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]] and move on. The smooth structure is forced; the only step is naming it.

If the object is **presented as an open subset of a manifold** — the upper half-plane, $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R}) \subset M(n, \mathbb{R})$, the complement of a closed set — invoke [[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold]]. Done in one line.

A meta-strategy threads through all of these: **the smooth structure should be the cheapest one that makes the natural maps you care about smooth.** When two atlases compete (graph coordinates vs. stereographic on $S^n$; product vs. quotient on $T^n$), the right move is to prove they are equivalent — they generate the same maximal atlas — which validates passing freely between them. The single unifying question of this chapter is: **what is the minimum coordinate-independent data needed for calculus to work?** The answer — a maximal smooth atlas, equivalently an equivalence class of compatible smooth atlases — is the entire content of the chapter, and every later chapter of differential geometry builds upon it.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Smooth Structure from Maximal Atlas|Smooth structure from a smooth atlas]]**: every smooth atlas $\mathcal{A}$ is contained in a unique maximal smooth atlas $\overline{\mathcal{A}}$, and two atlases give the same smooth structure iff their union is smooth. This is the most-used fact of the chapter because it lets you specify a smooth manifold by any convenient atlas (graph coordinates, stereographic, spherical) and then freely use any other smoothly compatible chart (polar, exponential) without rederiving anything. **Typical use:** show a single new chart is smoothly compatible with a known atlas to add it to the smooth structure; or show two seemingly different atlases give the same structure to justify mixing them in computations.

- **[[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold|Open subset is a smooth manifold]]**: any open $U \subseteq M$ inherits a smooth $n$-manifold structure. This is the workhorse for matrix Lie groups ($\mathrm{GL}(n)$, $\mathrm{GL}^+$, the upper Heisenberg-like groups), for upper half-spaces in physics, and for any local construction that does not need to extend to all of $M$. **Typical use:** identify a subset as the locus where a continuous function is nonzero or positive (so it is open), and invoke the theorem; no further verification needed.

- **[[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold|Products of smooth manifolds]]**: finite products inherit smooth structure. This gives the torus, configuration spaces, phase spaces, and in general lets us reduce $n$-dimensional constructions to 1-dimensional ones when the manifold factors. **Typical use:** decompose $T^n = (S^1)^n$ and check $n = 1$; bootstrap to multivariable settings.

- **[[Def - Smooth Atlas and Smooth Structure|Smooth compatibility of charts]]**: the transition $\psi \circ \varphi^{-1}$ being a diffeomorphism is *the* test for whether you can do calculus consistently in both charts. **Typical use:** verify a new chart fits into a given smooth structure by computing the transition function with one chart from the structure; smoothness propagates from there.

- **[[Thm - Invariance of Dimension|Dimension is a topological invariant]]**: the dimension of a manifold is the dimension of the chart target $\mathbb{R}^n$, and this number is well-defined regardless of the chart chosen. **Typical use:** any time the dimension appears in a formula (volume, degree of a covering, codimension of a submanifold), it is unambiguous; and dimension matching is a *necessary* condition for two manifolds to be diffeomorphic.

# Bridges

1. **Topology — manifolds are topological spaces with extra structure.** The definition of [[Def - Topological Manifold|topological manifold]] is built directly on [[Def - Topological Space|topological space]], with three layered conditions (Hausdorff + second countability + locally Euclidean), each enforcing a different feature. The Hausdorff axiom rules out spaces like the line with two origins where convergent sequences would have multiple limits; second countability gives metrizability (every manifold is metrizable, by the Urysohn metrization theorem, but the metric is not canonical) and is the technical hypothesis behind the existence of partitions of unity in [[Differential Geometry II — Smooth Maps and Partitions of Unity|DG II]]. The locally Euclidean condition is the geometric heart. The categorical picture is that the forgetful functor $\mathbf{Man} \to \mathbf{Top}$ from smooth manifolds to topological spaces is faithful but not full — there are continuous maps that are not smooth — and the image is exactly the topological manifolds that admit at least one smooth structure (which is a strict subset, by Kervaire).

2. **Multivariable analysis — the [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]] is the analysis backbone.** The whole construction of smooth structures rests on the diffeomorphism notion in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and the diffeomorphism notion in turn rests on the inverse function theorem: a smooth map with invertible Jacobian at a point is a local diffeomorphism near that point. When [[Differential Geometry IV — Submersions, Immersions, Embeddings, and Submanifolds|DG IV]] develops submanifold theory, the regular value theorem will be the inverse function theorem applied uniformly across the level set, producing graph coordinates at each preimage point that automatically fit together into a smooth atlas. The same logic underlies the smooth manifold chart lemma — local information assembled into a global smooth structure.

3. **Linear algebra — chart targets are open subsets of vector spaces.** The model space $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a [[Def - Vector Space|vector space]]; coordinate maps $\varphi : U \to \mathbb{R}^n$ produce $n$-tuples of real-valued functions which are, in the language of linear algebra, the components in a particular basis. Changes of basis correspond to compositions with linear isomorphisms — a particularly trivial case of a transition function (an affine diffeomorphism). The deeper bridge is that the tangent space $T_pM$ at each point of a smooth manifold is a finite-dimensional vector space of the same dimension as $M$, and the coordinate map gives a linear isomorphism $T_pM \cong \mathbb{R}^n$ — that is the content of [[Differential Geometry III — Tangent Vectors and the Differential|DG III]]. The general theme is: a manifold has Euclidean structure *locally* but not globally, and linear algebra is the local fibre.

4. **Algebraic topology — invariance of dimension and the search for invariants.** The fact that an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ cannot be homeomorphic to an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^m$ for $n \neq m$ ([[Thm - Invariance of Dimension]]) is the first nontrivial topological invariant statement — and its proof requires genuinely topological machinery (singular homology, de Rham cohomology, or the Brouwer fixed-point theorem). Once dimension is in hand, the entire enterprise of algebraic topology can begin: which other features of a space are homeomorphism-invariant? Fundamental groups, homology, cohomology, and ultimately the Atiyah–Singer index theorem all sit downstream. For manifolds specifically, [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius|de Rham cohomology]] in DG X produces invariants directly from differential forms, and the **de Rham theorem** identifies these with singular cohomology — a bridge that is impossible to state without the smooth-manifold framework.

5. **Algebraic geometry and complex geometry — variants of the smooth-manifold definition.** The definition "set, atlas, compatibility class on the model" is a template, and varying the model and compatibility gives different categories: complex manifolds (model $\mathbb{C}^n$, transitions holomorphic), real-analytic manifolds (transitions real-analytic), $C^k$-manifolds, algebraic varieties (model affine algebraic schemes, transitions algebraic). The Riemann sphere $S^2 = \mathbb{CP}^1$ with stereographic chart transition $w = 1/z$ is the prototype complex manifold; the resulting category is enormously rigid (Liouville's theorem, the maximum principle, the Riemann–Roch theorem) and is the foundation of complex geometry and algebraic geometry. The general categorical pattern — *ringed spaces* and *sheaves* — is the abstract subsuming framework, and a smooth manifold is the ringed space $(M, \mathcal{O}_M)$ where $\mathcal{O}_M$ is the sheaf of smooth functions; a complex manifold replaces $\mathcal{O}_M$ by the sheaf of holomorphic functions.

6. **Mechanics — manifolds as configuration spaces.** The configuration space of a rigid body in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is $\mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathrm{SO}(3)$, a six-dimensional manifold (position $\times$ orientation); the configuration space of a planar double pendulum is $T^2$; the configuration space of $n$ identical particles in $\mathbb{R}^3$ that may collide is a quotient of $\mathbb{R}^{3n}$ by the symmetric group action — generically a manifold, with singular strata where particles collide. Hamiltonian mechanics lives on the cotangent bundle $T^*M$, a $2n$-dimensional symplectic manifold; geodesic motion lives on the tangent bundle $TM$. The smooth structure on $M$ is what makes the equations of motion (the Euler–Lagrange and Hamilton equations) well-defined, and the rest of physics — Maxwell, Yang–Mills, general relativity — extends this to fields on $M$ rather than particles in $M$.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a smooth manifold is a set of compatible coordinate viewings.** The reader's instinct from undergraduate analysis is that a manifold *is* its underlying point set — a sphere is the set of unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and operations on the sphere are operations on the points. The right viewpoint, the one this chapter is built to install, is the opposite: a smooth manifold is not its point set but the *coordinate-compatible structure* on its point set. Two different smooth structures on the same underlying topological manifold (Lee Example 1.23: $\mathbb{R}$ with the standard chart vs. $\mathbb{R}$ with the chart $x \mapsto x^3$) are different smooth manifolds even though they are the same set. What you do *with* the manifold — define smooth functions, take derivatives, integrate — depends on the smooth structure, not on the point set. The platonic object is the smooth structure; the points are just where things happen to live. This is the same shift in viewpoint that took place in linear algebra between "vector = tuple of numbers" and "vector = element of an abstract vector space"; in both cases, the abstract structure carries the mathematics and the concrete representation is a chosen viewing.

**The true name of "smooth manifold" is "calculus, made coordinate-invariant."** The textbook definition — Hausdorff, second-countable, locally Euclidean, with a maximal smooth atlas — is the right thing to *check*, but the wrong thing to *think*. The operational meaning is: a smooth manifold is the data needed to make derivatives unambiguous. Every axiom of the definition serves this aim. Locally Euclidean lets us write down derivatives by transporting to $\mathbb{R}^n$. Smooth compatibility of charts makes those derivatives independent of the chart. Hausdorff and second countability ensure the global topology supports partitions of unity, so local derivatives can be glued into global objects. Once a reader internalizes "the definition exists to make derivatives well-defined," every later complication of the theory (tangent vectors, vector fields, exterior derivative, integration) reads as a refinement of the same idea.

**Inheritance — properties pull back to Euclidean space.** Most properties of a manifold can be checked, computed, or proved by transporting them to $\mathbb{R}^n$ via a chart. Smoothness of a function: check in a chart. Smoothness of a map between manifolds: check the chart-representation. Tangent vectors: defined via curves in a chart, or equivalently as derivations of $C^\infty(M)$, in either case reducing to Euclidean calculations. Vector fields: smooth assignments of vectors, with smoothness defined chart-by-chart. The manifold inherits its analytic structure from the simpler object — $\mathbb{R}^n$, whose calculus we already understand — and the complexity is concentrated in the *gluing*, that is, in the transition functions. This is the single most useful slogan for the rest of the subject: **a manifold is locally trivial; the geometry is in the gluing.**

**A trigger-reaction pattern: "show $X$ is a smooth manifold" $\Rightarrow$ "find charts and check transitions."** Almost every concrete problem of the form "show that the following set $X$ is a smooth manifold" reduces to a four-step routine: (i) identify a natural family of injective maps $\varphi_\alpha : U_\alpha \to \mathbb{R}^n$ from open subsets of $X$ to open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (the charts), with the $U_\alpha$ covering $X$; (ii) verify each $\varphi_\alpha$ is a homeomorphism onto its image; (iii) compute the transition functions $\varphi_\beta \circ \varphi_\alpha^{-1}$ and verify each is smooth; (iv) check the global topological conditions (Hausdorff, second countability). When the space is presented as a set with no topology, replace (i)+(ii) by the smooth manifold chart lemma. This routine is enough to handle every example in §1.3, every Cascini exercise, and most of Lee's chapter 1 problems.

**Density of examples and the rigidity of low-dimensional manifolds.** The chapter's examples cluster in low dimensions: $S^n$ in every dimension, $\mathbb{RP}^n$ and $\mathbb{CP}^n$, $T^n$, $\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$, Grassmannians, matrix Lie groups. These look like a rich zoo, but they are in fact unusually *rigid*: in low dimensions, smooth structures on a given topological manifold are typically unique (every topological surface admits a unique smooth structure, every 3-manifold admits a unique smooth structure), so the smooth-vs-topological distinction is invisible in everyday geometry. The pathologies — Kervaire's 10-manifold with no smooth structure, the 28 oriented diffeomorphism classes of homotopy $7$-spheres (27 exotic and one standard), Donaldson's uncountably many exotic $\mathbb{R}^4$'s — live in higher dimensions, where the framework of this chapter remains the only sensible language even when its predictions become surprising. The chapter is a foundation, not a theory; the theory begins downstream.
