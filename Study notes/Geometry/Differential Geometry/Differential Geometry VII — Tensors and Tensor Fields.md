# Notation Registry

This topic uses two parallel notations everywhere: a coordinate-free notation for the underlying object and an index notation for its components in a chart. The two must always agree.

**Standing convention — index placement.** Throughout, an *upper* index is contravariant (vector-like, transforms with the Jacobian of a change of coordinates) and a *lower* index is covariant (covector-like, transforms with the inverse-transpose Jacobian). The Einstein summation convention is in force: a repeated upper-lower index pair is implicitly summed over its range. So $A^i_j v^j$ means $\sum_j A^i_j v^j$. The Lee convention "covariant first" places covariant tensors as $T^k(V^*)$ — multilinear functionals of $k$ vectors — and we follow it; Frankel and Jost agree.

- $V$ — a finite-dimensional real vector space, with $\dim V = n$; in applications, $V = T_p M$.
- $V^*$ — the [[Def - Dual Space|dual space]] of linear functionals $V \to \mathbb{R}$.
- $(E_i)_{i=1}^n$ — a basis of $V$; $(\varepsilon^i)_{i=1}^n$ — the [[Def - Dual Basis|dual basis]] of $V^*$, with $\varepsilon^i(E_j) = \delta^i_j$.
- $v^i$ — components of $v \in V$ in the basis: $v = v^i E_i$. $\omega_j$ — components of $\omega \in V^*$ in the dual basis: $\omega = \omega_j \varepsilon^j$.
- $T^k(V^*) = V^* \otimes \cdots \otimes V^*$ ($k$ factors) — covariant $k$-tensors on $V$, i.e. $k$-multilinear maps $V \times \cdots \times V \to \mathbb{R}$.
- $T^k(V) = V \otimes \cdots \otimes V$ — contravariant $k$-tensors on $V$, i.e. $k$-multilinear maps $V^* \times \cdots \times V^* \to \mathbb{R}$.
- $T^{(k,\ell)}(V) = \underbrace{V \otimes \cdots \otimes V}_{k} \otimes \underbrace{V^* \otimes \cdots \otimes V^*}_{\ell}$ — mixed tensors of type $(k, \ell)$: $k$ contravariant slots, $\ell$ covariant slots, equivalently $(\ell + k)$-multilinear maps eating $\ell$ vectors and $k$ covectors.
- $\otimes$ — the [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces|tensor product]]; an *elementary tensor* in $T^k(V^*)$ has the form $\omega^1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \omega^k$ with $\omega^i \in V^*$.
- $M$ — a smooth $n$-manifold (Hausdorff, second countable); see [[Def - Smooth Manifold]].
- $T_pM, T_p^*M$ — the [[Def - The Tangent Space|tangent space]] and [[Def - Cotangent Space and Cotangent Bundle|cotangent space]] at $p$.
- $T^{(k,\ell)}M$ — the **tensor bundle** of type $(k,\ell)$, whose fibre over $p$ is $T^{(k,\ell)}(T_pM)$.
- $\mathcal{T}^k(M) = \Gamma(T^k T^*M)$ — smooth covariant $k$-tensor fields on $M$ (Lee's notation). $\mathcal{T}^{(k,\ell)}(M)$ — smooth $(k,\ell)$-tensor fields.
- $A^{i_1 \cdots i_k}_{j_1 \cdots j_\ell}$ — components of a $(k,\ell)$-tensor field in a chart $(x^1, \dots, x^n)$. The reader should imagine these as $n^{k+\ell}$ smooth functions on the chart, varying with $p$.
- $\partial_i = \partial/\partial x^i$ — the coordinate vector fields. $dx^i$ — the coordinate 1-forms, the dual coordinate frame: $dx^i(\partial_j) = \delta^i_j$.
- $C^\infty(M)$ — the [[Def - The Smooth Functions Ring|ring of smooth real-valued functions]] on $M$; sections of any tensor bundle form a *module* over this ring, and the "tensor-or-not" diagnostic of the chapter is $C^\infty(M)$-multilinearity.
- $F^*A$ — the [[Def - Pullback of a Covariant Tensor Field|pullback]] of a covariant tensor field $A$ on $N$ by a smooth map $F : M \to N$.
- $\text{Sym}, \text{Alt}$ — the symmetrization and alternation projectors on $T^k(V^*)$.
- $\delta^i_j$ — the Kronecker delta, the components of the identity-as-mixed-tensor of type $(1,1)$.

The full notation registry for differential geometry sits on [[Differential Geometry III — Tangent Vectors and the Differential]] (for $TM, T^*M$); this page extends it with tensor-product structure.

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: **a tensor field on a manifold is a smoothly varying multilinear gadget on the tangent space at each point, and the test for being one is $C^\infty(M)$-multilinearity**. Everything else — index notation, transformation rules, symmetric and alternating tensors, the Riemannian metric, the curvature tensor — is structure built on that one sentence.

Why introduce tensors at all? By the end of [[Differential Geometry VI — Vector Bundles and the Cotangent Bundle]] we have vector fields and 1-forms. These give the linear and dual-linear data at each point. But geometry and physics demand more: the **inner product** that measures lengths and angles on a Riemannian manifold pairs *two* vectors and is bilinear; the **stress tensor** in continuum mechanics, the **curvature** of a connection, the **electromagnetic field strength** $F$ — all of them are quantities that, evaluated at a point, eat several tangent vectors (and possibly some cotangent vectors), are multilinear in those slots, and produce a number. There is no chance of expressing them in terms of vector fields and 1-forms alone, because vector fields and 1-forms have *one* slot apiece. The natural home for these objects is the multilinear algebra of $T_pM$ and $T_p^*M$, repackaged for each $p$ and patched smoothly. That repackaging is precisely a tensor field.

The shape of the topic is dictated by this. We start at a single point: do multilinear algebra on a fixed vector space $V$ ([[Def - Covariant Tensor on a Vector Space]], [[Def - Contravariant Tensor on a Vector Space]], [[Def - Mixed Tensor]]). This is essentially [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants|LA IX]] read with one eye on the manifold: the tensor product $V^* \otimes V^*$ from LA IX *is* the fibre of the bundle of covariant 2-tensors that the manifold will carry. Then we glue these fibres together, one for each point of $M$, into a **tensor bundle** $T^{(k,\ell)}M$, and a tensor field of type $(k,\ell)$ is a smooth section of that bundle ([[Def - Tensor Bundle]], [[Def - Tensor Field on a Manifold]]).

The conceptual hinge is **[[Thm - Tensor Field is C-Infinity Multilinear over C-Infinity Functions|Lee's tensor characterization lemma]]**. A tensor field gives a multilinear map on vector fields by pointwise evaluation, and the lemma's content is that this multilinear map is *more* than $\mathbb{R}$-multilinear: it is multilinear *over the [[Def - Ring|ring]]* $C^\infty(M)$ — moving a smooth scalar function in or out of a slot is legal. Conversely, **every** $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear map on vector fields and 1-forms comes from a tensor field. So the true name of a tensor field is "$C^\infty(M)$-multilinear gadget on vector fields and 1-forms". This is the diagnostic used throughout the rest of differential geometry: when you write down a candidate object, you ask "is it $C^\infty(M)$-linear in each slot?"; if yes, it is a tensor field and lives at a point; if no, it is not, and it secretly involves derivatives.

The diagnostic is sharp enough to cut. The **Lie bracket** $[X,Y]$ is *not* $C^\infty(M)$-linear in its arguments — $[fX, Y]$ has an extra $(Yf)X$ term that no tensor field can produce. So $[X,Y]$ is not a tensorial operation; it is a differential operation, and this is why the Riemann curvature tensor is built out of *connections*, not out of $[X,Y]$ alone. By contrast, the metric $g(X,Y)$ *is* $C^\infty(M)$-linear in each slot: $g(fX, Y) = f g(X,Y)$, because $f$ is just a number at each point. So $g$ is a tensor field, the $(0,2)$-tensor field that defines Riemannian geometry.

The chapter closes with two operations that act on tensor fields: **contraction**, which collapses a covariant slot against a contravariant slot using the trace pairing, and **pullback**, which transports covariant tensor fields backwards along a smooth map. Contraction is the operation that takes a $(1,1)$-tensor to a scalar (the trace), or the Riemann curvature $(1,3)$-tensor to the Ricci $(0,2)$-tensor. Pullback is the operation by which a map $F : M \to N$ converts a tensor field on $N$ into one on $M$ — it is the input to virtually every coordinate calculation, and the foundation of the integral of forms in [[Differential Geometry IX — Orientation, Integration, and Stokes' Theorem]].

A backbone display, for orientation throughout the chapter:
$$\underbrace{\text{multilinear algebra on } V}_{\text{LA IX}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{tensor bundle } T^{(k,\ell)}M}_{\text{fibrewise}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\text{tensor field} = \text{smooth section}}_{=\, C^\infty(M)\text{-multilinear gadget}}.$$

**Audience-assumption paragraph.** The reader is assumed comfortable with: smooth manifolds and smooth maps ([[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases]], [[Differential Geometry II — Smooth Maps and Partitions of Unity]]); tangent and cotangent spaces and the differential ([[Differential Geometry III — Tangent Vectors and the Differential]]); vector fields, with the warning that the Lie bracket is *not* tensorial ([[Differential Geometry V — Vector Fields, Flows, and the Lie Bracket]]); vector bundles, sections, local frames, the cotangent bundle, and pullback of 1-forms ([[Differential Geometry VI — Vector Bundles and the Cotangent Bundle]]); and the multilinear-algebra package of [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants]] — [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]], [[Def - Bilinear Form]], [[Def - Multilinear Form]], [[Def - Symmetric and Alternating Bilinear Form]], [[Def - Alternating Multilinear Form]], [[Def - Dual Space]], [[Def - Dual Basis]]. The reader who has refreshed the LA IX [[Def - Module|module]] will find the first section a fast review with one extra demand — keeping covariant and contravariant indices straight — and the manifold sections will then read as "do this fibrewise, smoothly".

# Concept Map

## §7.1 Multilinear Algebra Refresher

This is multilinear algebra on a single finite-dimensional vector space $V$, which in applications will be $T_pM$. The whole section is fibre-level: everything here is in [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants|LA IX]], with the difference that we now systematically distinguish *covariant* slots (eating vectors) from *contravariant* slots (eating covectors), and we use the convention $T^k(V^*)$ for "covariant $k$-tensors".

- **[[Def - Covariant Tensor on a Vector Space]]**
	- A **covariant $k$-tensor** on $V$ is a $k$-multilinear map $\alpha : V \times \cdots \times V \to \mathbb{R}$. The space of such tensors is $T^k(V^*) = V^* \otimes \cdots \otimes V^*$ ($k$ factors). A covariant 1-tensor is a covector; a covariant 2-tensor is a [[Def - Bilinear Form|bilinear form]]; the determinant on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a covariant $n$-tensor. If $(\varepsilon^i)$ is a dual basis of $V^*$, every $\alpha \in T^k(V^*)$ expands uniquely as $\alpha = \alpha_{i_1 \cdots i_k} \varepsilon^{i_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \varepsilon^{i_k}$, with $n^k$ components $\alpha_{i_1\cdots i_k} = \alpha(E_{i_1}, \dots, E_{i_k})$.

- **[[Def - Contravariant Tensor on a Vector Space]]**
	- A **contravariant $k$-tensor** on $V$ is a $k$-multilinear map $T : V^* \times \cdots \times V^* \to \mathbb{R}$. Equivalently $T \in V \otimes \cdots \otimes V$, since a vector $v \in V$ acts on a covector $\omega \in V^*$ by $v(\omega) := \omega(v)$ (using the canonical double-dual identification $V \cong V^{**}$, which is the reason this definition works only in finite [[Def - Dimension|dimensions]]). Components: $T = T^{i_1 \cdots i_k} E_{i_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes E_{i_k}$ with $T^{i_1 \cdots i_k} = T(\varepsilon^{i_1}, \dots, \varepsilon^{i_k})$.

- **[[Def - Mixed Tensor]]**
	- A **mixed tensor of type $(k, \ell)$** on $V$ is a multilinear map $W : V^* \times \cdots \times V^* \times V \times \cdots \times V \to \mathbb{R}$ ($k$ covector slots, $\ell$ vector slots). Equivalently $W \in V^{\otimes k} \otimes (V^*)^{\otimes \ell}$, with components $W^{i_1\cdots i_k}_{j_1 \cdots j_\ell} = W(\varepsilon^{i_1}, \dots, \varepsilon^{i_k}, E_{j_1}, \dots, E_{j_\ell})$ — $n^{k+\ell}$ numbers, arranged with the contravariant indices upstairs and the covariant indices downstairs. A linear map $A : V \to V$ is a $(1,1)$-tensor with components $A^i_j$; the **Kronecker delta** $\delta^i_j$ is the identity-as-$(1,1)$-tensor.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The (p, q)-Tensor Algebra of $V$ *(from Multilinear Algebra)*
> The graded vector space $T^\bullet(V) = \bigoplus_{k,\ell \geq 0} T^{(k,\ell)}(V)$ is an associative algebra under the tensor product. Quotienting by the antisymmetrization relations on the contravariant or covariant parts produces the exterior algebra $\Lambda^\bullet V^*$ — the algebraic home of [[Def - Differential Form|differential forms]]. The whole structure of differential forms on a manifold is this algebra applied fibre-by-fibre, then patched together.

- **Basis-induced basis on $T^{(k,\ell)}(V)$**
	- A choice of basis $(E_i)$ of $V$ with dual basis $(\varepsilon^j)$ of $V^*$ induces the basis $\{E_{i_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes E_{i_k} \otimes \varepsilon^{j_1} \otimes \cdots \otimes \varepsilon^{j_\ell}\}$ of $T^{(k,\ell)}(V)$, so $\dim T^{(k,\ell)}(V) = n^{k+\ell}$. Every tensor expands uniquely as $W = W^{i_1\cdots i_k}_{j_1 \cdots j_\ell}\, E_{i_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes E_{i_k}\otimes \varepsilon^{j_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes \varepsilon^{j_\ell}$. This is the "$mn$ degrees of freedom" of [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]] applied iteratively.

- **[[Ex - The Kronecker Delta as a Mixed Tensor]]** (⭐)
	- Show that the identity map $\mathrm{id}_V : V \to V$, viewed as a $(1,1)$-tensor via $W_{\mathrm{id}}(\omega, v) = \omega(v)$, has components $\delta^i_j$ in every basis, and that this is the unique nonzero $(1,1)$-tensor with that property. A clean drill on the bilinear-form-versus-linear-map distinction.

## §7.2 Tensor Fields on Manifolds

We now patch the fibre-level construction over a manifold $M$. The bundle of $(k,\ell)$-tensors $T^{(k,\ell)}M$ is built fibrewise, with smooth transition functions inherited from the tangent and cotangent bundles. A tensor field is a smooth section; the central characterization is Lee's tensor characterization lemma.

- **[[Def - Tensor Bundle]]**
	- The **tensor bundle of type $(k, \ell)$** over $M$ is the disjoint union $T^{(k,\ell)}M = \bigsqcup_{p \in M} T^{(k,\ell)}(T_pM)$, equipped with the smooth vector-bundle structure inherited from the tangent and cotangent bundles. Special cases: $T^{(0,0)}M = M \times \mathbb{R}$ (functions), $T^{(1,0)}M = TM$ ([[Def - The Tangent Bundle|tangent bundle]]), $T^{(0,1)}M = T^*M$ ([[Def - Cotangent Space and Cotangent Bundle|cotangent bundle]]). The rank of $T^{(k,\ell)}M$ as a vector bundle is $n^{k+\ell}$. Transition functions are tensor products of the transition functions of $TM$ and $T^*M$.

- **[[Def - Tensor Field on a Manifold]]**
	- A **tensor field of type $(k, \ell)$** on $M$ is a smooth section $A : M \to T^{(k,\ell)}M$ — for each $p$, an element $A_p \in T^{(k,\ell)}(T_pM)$, varying smoothly with $p$. In a chart $(x^i)$ the field reads $A = A^{i_1\cdots i_k}_{j_1\cdots j_\ell}(x)\, \partial_{i_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\partial_{i_k}\otimes dx^{j_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes dx^{j_\ell}$, and smoothness of $A$ is equivalent to smoothness of the component functions $A^{\bullet}_{\bullet}(x)$. Covariant 1-tensor fields are [[Def - Covector Field and Differential 1-Form|1-forms]]; contravariant 1-tensor fields are [[Def - Vector Field on a Manifold|vector fields]]; $(0,0)$-tensor fields are smooth functions.

- **[[Thm - Tensor Field is C-Infinity Multilinear over C-Infinity Functions]]**
	- *Lee's tensor characterization lemma.* A map $A : \mathfrak{X}(M)^\ell \times \Omega^1(M)^k \to C^\infty(M)$ is induced (by pointwise evaluation) by a smooth $(k,\ell)$-tensor field if and only if it is multilinear *over the ring $C^\infty(M)$*: for $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and a vector field $X$, the operation $A(\dots, fX, \dots) = f A(\dots, X, \dots)$, with no extra term in $df$. This is the **operational definition of "tensorial"**: the test is whether the gadget pulls smooth functions in and out of its slots. The pointwise statement — the value depends only on the values of the inputs at the point — is the same statement read fibre-by-fibre.

- **[[Thm - Transformation Rule for Tensor Components]]**
	- Under a smooth change of coordinates $\tilde x^i = \tilde x^i(x)$, the components of a $(k,\ell)$-tensor field transform by $\tilde A^{i_1\cdots i_k}_{j_1\cdots j_\ell} = \frac{\partial \tilde x^{i_1}}{\partial x^{a_1}} \cdots \frac{\partial \tilde x^{i_k}}{\partial x^{a_k}} \frac{\partial x^{b_1}}{\partial \tilde x^{j_1}} \cdots \frac{\partial x^{b_\ell}}{\partial \tilde x^{j_\ell}}\, A^{a_1\cdots a_k}_{b_1\cdots b_\ell}$. Upper indices contract with the new-from-old Jacobian, lower indices with the old-from-new Jacobian. This is the classical definition of a tensor used by physicists, and it is exactly equivalent to the smooth-section definition and to the $C^\infty(M)$-multilinearity definition.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Riemannian Metric|Riemannian Metric]] *(from Riemannian Geometry, [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds]])*
> A **Riemannian metric** is a symmetric, positive-definite $(0,2)$-tensor field $g$ on $M$. The metric is *the* fundamental object of Riemannian geometry: it gives lengths of curves, angles between vectors, the volume form, the musical isomorphism between $TM$ and $T^*M$, and the Levi-Civita connection. Every smooth manifold admits one ([[Thm - Existence of Riemannian Metrics via Partitions of Unity]]), built by partition-of-unity-summing local Euclidean metrics. The defining identity $g(fX, Y) = f g(X, Y)$ for $f \in C^\infty(M)$ is just the $(0,2)$-tensoriality of $g$ via the characterization lemma.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Stress-Energy Tensor|Stress-Energy Tensor]] *(from General Relativity)*
> The **stress-energy tensor** $T_{\mu\nu}$ in general relativity is a symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor field on the spacetime manifold $(M, g)$, packaging the energy density, momentum density, and stress of matter into a single tensorial object. Einstein's field equation $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\, T_{\mu\nu}$ equates one symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor field (the Einstein tensor, a curvature contraction) with another (the matter content). The whole language is tensorial because both sides must transform identically under change of coordinates — the manifestly tensor-field framework is what makes coordinate-invariant physics possible.

- **[[Ex - Computing the Pullback of a Tensor Field in Coordinates]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute $F^*(x^2\, dy \otimes dy)$ where $F(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ in polar coordinates. A direct drill of the substitute-and-expand recipe; the answer is $r^2 \cos^2\theta\, (\sin\theta\, dr + r\cos\theta\, d\theta) \otimes (\sin\theta\, dr + r\cos\theta\, d\theta)$, an exercise in keeping covariant indices straight.

- **[[Ex - The Metric Tensor in Polar Coordinates]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Starting from the Euclidean metric $g = dx \otimes dx + dy \otimes dy$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ and the polar-coordinate map $F(r,\theta) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$, compute the pullback $F^*g$ and verify it equals $dr \otimes dr + r^2\, d\theta \otimes d\theta$. This is the central worked example: it shows the transformation rule in action, derives the line element $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2\, d\theta^2$ that the reader has seen many times, and demonstrates why the *components* of $g$ in polar coordinates ($g_{rr} = 1, g_{\theta\theta} = r^2$) are different from the Cartesian ones ($g_{xx} = g_{yy} = 1$) even though $g$ is the same tensor field.

## §7.3 Symmetric and Alternating Tensors

This section introduces the two natural symmetry classes of covariant tensors and the projectors onto them. Symmetric tensor fields are the home of metrics and stress tensors; alternating tensor fields are the home of differential forms, which take over in [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]]. The two classes are intentionally given separate definition pages because, despite their formal similarity, their downstream lives are very different.

- **[[Def - Symmetric Tensor Field]]**
	- A covariant $k$-tensor $\alpha$ on $V$ is **symmetric** if $\alpha(v_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, v_{\sigma(k)}) = \alpha(v_1, \dots, v_k)$ for every permutation $\sigma \in S_k$, equivalently if the components $\alpha_{i_1\cdots i_k}$ are unchanged by any permutation of the indices. A **symmetric tensor field** $A$ on $M$ is a covariant tensor field with $A_p$ symmetric for every $p$. The space of symmetric covariant $k$-tensors is denoted $\Sigma^k(V^*)$; it has [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$. The prototype is the Riemannian metric: a symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor field.

- **[[Def - Alternating Tensor Field]]**
	- A covariant $k$-tensor $\alpha$ on $V$ is **alternating** (also called *antisymmetric* or *skew-symmetric*) if $\alpha(v_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, v_{\sigma(k)}) = (\operatorname{sgn} \sigma)\, \alpha(v_1, \dots, v_k)$, equivalently if the components flip sign under any transposition of two indices. The [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] of alternating $k$-tensors is $\Lambda^k(V^*) \subset T^k(V^*)$, with $\dim \Lambda^k(V^*) = \binom{n}{k}$ (zero for $k > n$). An **alternating tensor field** is a covariant tensor field with $A_p \in \Lambda^k(T_p^*M)$ for every $p$. These are exactly the [[Def - Differential Form|differential k-forms]] of [[Multivariate Analysis IV — Differential Forms and Stokes' Theorem]] in the Euclidean case; the next chapter develops them on manifolds.

- **[[Thm - Symmetrization and Alternation Projectors]]**
	- The maps $\mathrm{Sym} : T^k(V^*) \to \Sigma^k(V^*)$ and $\mathrm{Alt} : T^k(V^*) \to \Lambda^k(V^*)$ defined by $(\mathrm{Sym}\,\alpha)(v_1, \dots, v_k) = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_\sigma \alpha(v_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, v_{\sigma(k)})$ and $(\mathrm{Alt}\,\alpha)(v_1, \dots, v_k) = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_\sigma (\operatorname{sgn}\sigma)\,\alpha(v_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, v_{\sigma(k)})$ are linear projections — they are idempotent, fix $\Sigma^k(V^*)$ and $\Lambda^k(V^*)$ respectively, and (for $k = 2$ only) decompose $T^2(V^*) = \Sigma^2(V^*) \oplus \Lambda^2(V^*)$. For $k \geq 3$ the decomposition fails: there are tensors that are neither symmetric nor alternating nor a sum of one of each.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Differential k-Form *(from [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]])*
> A **differential $k$-form** on $M$ is precisely an alternating $(0,k)$-tensor field. Everything in the next chapter — the wedge product, the exterior derivative, integration of forms, Stokes's theorem — is built on this identification. The wedge product $\omega \wedge \eta$ is the alternation of the tensor product $\omega \otimes \eta$ (up to a combinatorial factor), so the wedge is *not* an extra structure but a derived one. The reason forms get their own chapter is that the alternating structure brings new operations (exterior derivative, integration) that have no analogue for general tensor fields.

- **[[Ex - Decomposing a 2-Tensor into Symmetric and Antisymmetric Parts]]** (⭐)
	- Given a covariant 2-tensor $\beta$ on $V$, show that $\beta = \mathrm{Sym}\,\beta + \mathrm{Alt}\,\beta$, that this decomposition is unique, and that it fails to generalize to $k \geq 3$. A diagnostic exercise that demonstrates the projector identities $\mathrm{Sym}^2 = \mathrm{Sym}$ and $\mathrm{Alt}\circ\mathrm{Sym} = 0$.

- **[[Ex - The Stress Tensor as a Symmetric 2-Tensor]]** (⭐⭐)
	- The Cauchy stress tensor $\sigma_{ij}$ in continuum mechanics is a symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor field on the body manifold. Show that the symmetry $\sigma_{ij} = \sigma_{ji}$ follows from the conservation of angular momentum, and identify the eigenvalues of $\sigma$ at a point with the principal stresses, and the eigenvectors with the principal axes. A physics-bridge exercise that ties the symmetric-tensor formalism to its archetypal physical instance.

## §7.4 Pullback and Contraction of Tensor Fields

Two operations on tensor fields. **Pullback** is the manifold-level extension of the LA pullback of a multilinear form: a smooth map $F : M \to N$ converts a covariant tensor field on $N$ into one on $M$ by pulling the inputs back via the differential. **Contraction** uses the trace pairing $V \otimes V^* \to \mathbb{R}$ to collapse one covariant slot of a tensor against one contravariant slot, producing a tensor of strictly lower rank. Together with the tensor product, these are the operations the rest of differential geometry runs on.

- **[[Def - Contraction of a Tensor]]**
	- The **contraction** $C^i_j : T^{(k,\ell)}(V) \to T^{(k-1, \ell-1)}(V)$ collapses the $i$-th contravariant slot against the $j$-th covariant slot by inserting the trace pairing $V \otimes V^* \to \mathbb{R}$, $v \otimes \omega \mapsto \omega(v)$. In components: $(CT)^{a_1\cdots\hat{a_i}\cdots}_{b_1\cdots\hat{b_j}\cdots} = T^{a_1 \cdots c \cdots}_{b_1 \cdots c \cdots}$ — sum the chosen contravariant index over the chosen covariant index. The contraction of the $(1,1)$-tensor $W^i_j$ is the trace $W^i_i$, a scalar. Crucially, contraction is **coordinate-invariant**: the result is a tensor of well-defined type, not just a chart-dependent number.

- **[[Def - Pullback of a Covariant Tensor Field]]**
	- For a smooth map $F : M \to N$ and a covariant tensor field $A$ on $N$, the **pullback** $F^*A$ is the covariant tensor field on $M$ defined by $(F^*A)_p(v_1, \dots, v_k) = A_{F(p)}(dF_p(v_1), \dots, dF_p(v_k))$. In coordinates, with $A = A_{i_1\cdots i_k}(y)\, dy^{i_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes dy^{i_k}$ on $N$ and $F^i(x)$ the coordinate functions of $F$, the pullback is $F^*A = (A_{i_1\cdots i_k}\circ F)\, dF^{i_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes dF^{i_k}$ — "substitute $y^i = F^i(x)$ and expand". Pullback exists for *covariant* tensors only (and for contravariant ones when $F$ is a [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]]), because $dF_p$ pushes vectors forward and so pulls covariant arguments backward.

- **[[Thm - Pullback Commutes with Tensor Product]]**
	- For a smooth map $F : M \to N$ and covariant tensor fields $A, B$ on $N$, $F^*(A \otimes B) = F^*A \otimes F^*B$, $F^*(A + B) = F^*A + F^*B$, $F^*(fB) = (f \circ F)\, F^*B$, $(G \circ F)^* = F^* \circ G^*$, and pullback preserves smoothness. So pullback is a *functorial* operation: it respects all the algebraic structure on covariant tensor fields and reverses arrows like any contravariant functor.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Pullback of [[Def - Differential Form|Differential Forms]] and the Integral *(from [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]], [[Differential Geometry IX — Orientation, Integration, and Stokes' Theorem]])*
> The pullback restricted to alternating tensor fields is the pullback of differential forms. The integral $\int_M F^*\omega$ over a manifold is then *defined* using pullback to bring the form back to a chart in $\mathbb{R}^n$ where Lebesgue integration is available. Pullback also commutes with the exterior derivative ($F^*d = dF^*$), which is one of the two facts that make Stokes's theorem on manifolds work. So the modest-looking pullback defined here is the load-bearing operation of the entire integration theory.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The [[Def - Ricci Tensor|Ricci Tensor]] as a Contraction of the Riemann Curvature *(from Riemannian Geometry)*
> The **Riemann curvature tensor** $R^i_{jk\ell}$ is a $(1,3)$-tensor field on a Riemannian manifold. Contracting the first contravariant index against the second covariant index gives the **Ricci tensor** $R_{j\ell} = R^i_{ji\ell}$, a symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor field. Contracting Ricci with the inverse metric gives the **scalar curvature** $R = g^{j\ell}R_{j\ell}$, a smooth function. The whole hierarchy of curvature invariants in general relativity is generated by repeated contractions of one master tensor, and the legality of those contractions is the content of this chapter.

- **[[Ex - The Kronecker Delta as a Mixed Tensor]]** (⭐) — already in §7.1 above; relevant here because its contraction $\delta^i_i = n$ exemplifies tensor contraction in its simplest form.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises in this chapter pursue five recurring goals. The first is **verifying that a candidate object is a tensor field** — typically by checking $C^\infty(M)$-multilinearity directly, or, in coordinate calculations, by checking the components transform by the tensor rule. This is the gatekeeper question: nothing else in differential geometry is allowed to proceed until you know your object is a tensor. The second is **computing components in a chart** — given an abstract description ($g$ is the metric induced by the standard embedding of $S^2$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$, say), find the matrix of components in some convenient coordinate system. This is what makes geometry concrete and is the bridge to the physicist's index-shuffling formalism. The third is **computing the pullback** in coordinates, which is the substitute-and-expand recipe and the input to every change-of-variable computation. The fourth is **identifying the symmetry class** of a tensor field — symmetric, alternating, or neither — and using the symmetry to simplify a count or a sum: a symmetric 2-tensor in dimension $n$ has $\binom{n+1}{2}$ independent components, not $n^2$, and many a computation hinges on remembering this. The fifth is **using the tensor characterization lemma in reverse**: given a $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear gadget defined on vector fields, produce the tensor field it secretly is, then read off its components — this is how the Riemann curvature tensor is constructed from a connection in later chapters.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The recurring sources are five also. **A tensor field is given on $N$ and a smooth map $F : M \to N$**, in which case pullback is available and the strategy is "pull back to $M$, then compute in $M$'s coordinates". **A linear-algebraic description is given on each tangent space** — for instance, "$g_p$ is the restriction of the ambient Euclidean inner product" — in which case the strategy is to compute $g_p(\partial_i|_p, \partial_j|_p)$ explicitly using the embedding. **A $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear map on vector fields is given**, in which case Lee's lemma converts it into a tensor field whose components are known by evaluating on coordinate frames. **A coordinate description with components is given**, in which case the transformation rule lets you translate it to any other chart, and the existence of the global tensor field is the assertion that the components in any *one* chart determine it everywhere. **A pair of tensor fields is given together with an operation that combines them** — tensor product, contraction, symmetrization, alternation, pullback — in which case the result is automatically a tensor field, by closure of the relevant operations on the appropriate categories of fields. The routing logic is: a $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear gadget routes through Lee's lemma to a tensor field; a tensor field with a coordinate expression routes through the transformation rule to any other chart; a tensor field on $N$ together with a smooth map $F : M \to N$ routes through pullback to a tensor field on $M$; two tensor fields route through tensor product, symmetrization, alternation, or contraction to a tensor field of the appropriate type.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves of tensor-field calculus. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Each item is self-contained: a reader with no differential-geometry background should be able to follow the operation from the description alone.

1. **Take the tensor product of two tensor fields.** Given $A \in \mathcal{T}^{(k_1, \ell_1)}(M)$ and $B \in \mathcal{T}^{(k_2, \ell_2)}(M)$, their **tensor product** $A \otimes B$ is the $(k_1 + k_2, \ell_1 + \ell_2)$-tensor field defined pointwise via the algebraic tensor product on each fibre. In components, $(A \otimes B)^{\cdots}_{\cdots}$ is simply the product of the components of $A$ and $B$ with the index sets concatenated. *Trigger:* you want to build a higher-rank tensor field from lower-rank ones. *Pattern:* concatenate slots, multiply components. This is the most basic constructive operation.

2. **Check tensoriality via $C^\infty(M)$-multilinearity.** When you write down a candidate multilinear operation on vector fields and 1-forms, test whether it pulls smooth functions in and out of its slots — i.e. whether $A(\dots, fX, \dots) = f\, A(\dots, X, \dots)$ for every $f \in C^\infty(M)$. If yes, [[Thm - Tensor Field is C-Infinity Multilinear over C-Infinity Functions|Lee's lemma]] guarantees a tensor field; if no, you have a differential operator, not a tensor. *Trigger:* "is this thing tensorial?" *Pattern:* substitute $fX$ for $X$ in a slot and watch for stray $df$ terms. This is the single most-used diagnostic in differential geometry.

3. **Compute components in a chart.** With a chart $(x^i)$ and the induced frame $(\partial_i)$ and coframe $(dx^j)$, the components of a $(k,\ell)$-tensor field $A$ are $A^{i_1\cdots i_k}_{j_1\cdots j_\ell} = A(dx^{i_1}, \dots, dx^{i_k}, \partial_{j_1}, \dots, \partial_{j_\ell})$. *Trigger:* you have an abstract tensor field and need a number to feed to a physicist. *Pattern:* evaluate on basis covectors and basis vectors and read off the matrix-of-matrices of values. The component functions determine the tensor field uniquely on the chart.

4. **Change coordinates using the transformation rule.** Under $\tilde x = \tilde x(x)$, the components transform by Jacobian factors — upper indices with $\partial \tilde x^i / \partial x^a$, lower with $\partial x^b / \partial \tilde x^j$. *Trigger:* given components in chart $A$ you want them in chart $B$. *Pattern:* lay out one Jacobian factor per index, contract on the corresponding slot, and apply the chain rule wherever a function of $x$ is being differentiated with respect to $\tilde x$. The classical "rule by which a tensor transforms" *is* the tensor.

5. **Pull back covariant tensor fields.** For $F : M \to N$ smooth and $A$ a covariant tensor field on $N$, the pullback $F^*A$ is a covariant tensor field on $M$, defined by $(F^*A)_p(v_1,\dots,v_k) = A_{F(p)}(dF_p(v_1),\dots,dF_p(v_k))$. In coordinates this is "substitute $y^i = F^i(x)$ and expand $dy^i \to dF^i$". *Trigger:* a tensor on the target, a map to it. *Pattern:* substitute and expand; the Leibniz rule on $d$ does the rest of the work. The Jacobian appears automatically.

6. **Contract a covariant against a contravariant index.** Contraction $C^i_j$ collapses the $i$-th upper slot against the $j$-th lower slot of a $(k,\ell)$-tensor field, producing a $(k-1, \ell-1)$-tensor field. In components, set the chosen upper index equal to the chosen lower index and sum (Einstein convention). *Trigger:* you have a mixed tensor and want a lower-rank tensor or a function. *Pattern:* trace one upper against one lower. Contraction is the only way to extract numbers (the trace) or low-rank tensors (Ricci from Riemann) from a high-rank mixed tensor.

7. **Symmetrize or alternate a covariant tensor.** Apply $\mathrm{Sym}$ to extract the symmetric part, $\mathrm{Alt}$ to extract the alternating part. For $k = 2$ this decomposes the tensor into its two parts; for $k \geq 3$ it produces *some* part but loses information that lives in neither [[Def - Subspace|subspace]]. *Trigger:* you only need the symmetric or antisymmetric content (e.g. when wedging or when taking a quadratic form). *Pattern:* average over $S_k$ with or without signs of permutations. This is the gateway to forms and to quadratic-form analysis.

8. **Use coordinate vector fields and coordinate 1-forms as a local frame.** On a chart, $(\partial_i)$ is a basis of $T_pM$ at each $p$, $(dx^j)$ is a basis of $T_p^*M$, and tensor products of these give a basis of every tensor space — the **coordinate frame** for tensor fields. Any tensor field is a sum, with smooth coefficients, of tensor products of coordinate frame elements. *Trigger:* you need to write a tensor field explicitly in formulas. *Pattern:* expand $A = A^{\cdots}_{\cdots} \partial_{i_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes dx^{j_\ell}$ and treat the components as $n^{k+\ell}$ smooth functions. Coordinate frames are local, not global; if a global frame existed everywhere, the bundle would be trivial.

9. **Multiply a tensor field by a smooth function.** Pointwise scalar multiplication: $(fA)_p = f(p) A_p$. This is the only way $C^\infty(M)$-multilinearity ever appears in practice — moving $f$ from in front of a tensor field to inside one of its slots. *Trigger:* always available. *Pattern:* $C^\infty(M)$-[[Def - Module|module]] structure of tensor fields.

10. **Restrict a tensor field to an open subset.** If $A$ is a tensor field on $M$ and $U \subseteq M$ is open, $A|_U$ is a tensor field on $U$. *Trigger:* you want to work in a coordinate chart. *Pattern:* manifold-level operations are local, so every chart computation is really a chart-level computation of a restricted tensor field; the global field is then recovered by checking that the chart-level expressions glue.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Pulling back a contravariant tensor field along a non-[[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]]
> The pullback $F^*$ is well-defined only on *covariant* tensor fields when $F$ is merely smooth; for contravariant tensor fields one needs $F$ to be a diffeomorphism in order to make sense of pushforward and then take its inverse. **Concrete counterexample:** if $F : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ is the projection $(x, y) \mapsto (x, 0)$, the vector field $\partial_y$ on the target has no pullback to the source — at $(0, 0)$, $dF$ has a kernel, and there is no way to choose a vector at the source that maps to $\partial_y$. **Repair condition:** the operation becomes legal exactly when $F$ is a diffeomorphism, in which case the pullback of a contravariant tensor field is defined by pushforward under $F^{-1}$.

> [!warning] 2. Treating the Lie bracket as a $(1,2)$-tensor field
> One is tempted to view $(X, Y) \mapsto [X, Y]$ as a $(1,2)$-tensor field — it takes two vector fields in and produces a vector field, with values that look local. **Concrete counterexample:** $[fX, Y] = f[X, Y] - (Yf)X$, with the $-(Yf)X$ term killing $C^\infty(M)$-linearity. The bracket therefore depends on more than the values of $X$ and $Y$ at a point — it depends on their first derivatives. **Repair condition:** there is no repair. The bracket is genuinely a *differential* operation, not tensorial; the way differential geometry recovers a tensorial curvature object is to use a [[Def - Vector Field on a Manifold|connection]] $\nabla$ in place of the bare bracket, and the Riemann tensor $R(X,Y)Z = \nabla_X \nabla_Y Z - \nabla_Y \nabla_X Z - \nabla_{[X,Y]}Z$ is $C^\infty(M)$-linear in all three slots precisely because the brackets cancel inside.

> [!warning] 3. Adding tensors of different type
> One sees $A^i_j$ and $A^{ij}$ and is tempted to sum them. **Concrete counterexample:** in 2D, the identity-as-$(1,1)$-tensor has matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ in every chart, but the same matrix read as a *contravariant* $(2,0)$-tensor transforms differently under change of coordinates and so describes a different geometrical object. Adding them is meaningless because the two transform differently. **Repair condition:** the operation becomes legal only after one of the indices is *raised* or *lowered* via a metric tensor — see [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds]]. The metric is exactly the structure that lets you identify $V$ with $V^*$ and so type-convert tensors.

> [!warning] 4. Treating a coordinate frame as a global frame
> The frame $(\partial_i)$ exists *within a chart* and depends on the chart. On a non-parallelizable manifold like $S^2$, there is no global frame at all — the hairy ball theorem says any continuous vector field on $S^2$ vanishes somewhere, hence no global basis of $TS^2$ exists. **Concrete counterexample:** a tensor field whose component matrix is the identity in *every* coordinate chart on $S^2$ cannot exist, because gluing across the equator would require the frame to extend consistently, and it does not. **Repair condition:** local frames exist on every chart by construction; global frames exist exactly on **parallelizable** manifolds (Lie [[Def - Group|groups]], $\mathbb{R}^n$, tori). On a general manifold, every global construction must be a tensor field, not a frame.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic fall into a small number of types, and recognizing the type up front saves work. Below, each type is named and the recipe sketched.

If the problem **asks you to verify that an object is a tensor field**, the route is one of two equivalent paths. The high-tech path is to write the candidate as a multilinear map on vector fields and 1-forms and check $C^\infty(M)$-multilinearity — substitute $fX$ for $X$ in a slot and see whether the answer is $f$ times the original, with no extra term. This is the application of [[Thm - Tensor Field is C-Infinity Multilinear over C-Infinity Functions|Lee's tensor characterization lemma]] in its forward direction. The low-tech path is to pick coordinates, compute the components, change to another chart, and verify that the components transform by the rule of [[Thm - Transformation Rule for Tensor Components]]. The two paths give the same answer because the tensor characterization lemma is the global statement of the transformation rule. The high-tech path is faster when the object is described abstractly (e.g. "$T(X, Y) = X(g(Y, Z))$" — quickly seen to be non-tensorial because $X$ differentiates a function); the low-tech path is faster when components are already in hand and the abstract description is unclear.

If the problem **asks for the components of a tensor field in a chart**, evaluate the tensor field on the coordinate frame and coframe: $A^{i_1 \cdots i_k}_{j_1\cdots j_\ell} = A(dx^{i_1}, \dots, dx^{i_k}, \partial_{j_1}, \dots, \partial_{j_\ell})$. The result is $n^{k+\ell}$ smooth functions on the chart, which together encode $A$ on that chart. The whole challenge is in choosing the chart well: an embedded submanifold is usually best handled with parameterization-induced coordinates, a symmetric problem with adapted symmetric coordinates (polar, cylindrical, spherical), and a Lie [[Def - Group|group]] with left-invariant coordinates. The Metric Tensor in Polar Coordinates exercise is the prototype: the Euclidean metric is hard in polar coordinates if you try to start from the polar definitions, but is trivial if you pull back the Cartesian metric.

If the problem **asks you to pull back a tensor field**, the recipe is mechanical: substitute the map's coordinate functions $y^i = F^i(x)$ in the components, then expand $dy^i = (\partial F^i / \partial x^j)\, dx^j$ using $d$'s linearity and Leibniz rule. The Jacobian appears as a coefficient automatically. The only thing to watch is that pullback is *covariant* — it acts on covariant tensor fields by pulling inputs back through $dF$. If your tensor field has contravariant components, pullback is not defined unless $F$ is a diffeomorphism, in which case you push forward via $F^{-1}$.

If the problem **involves a symmetry property**, apply $\mathrm{Sym}$ or $\mathrm{Alt}$ to extract the relevant part. For 2-tensors the decomposition $\beta = \mathrm{Sym}\,\beta + \mathrm{Alt}\,\beta$ is unique and complete. For higher rank, the decomposition is partial: there are tensors that vanish under both projectors (the "mixed-Young-diagram" tensors), so for $k \geq 3$ one must work with the full $\mathrm{Sym}$ and $\mathrm{Alt}$ projectors as ends, not as a basis.

If the problem **involves contraction**, the recipe is to set one upper index equal to one lower index and sum. The result is a tensor of strictly lower rank, and the operation is coordinate-independent — this is the content of the chapter's "contraction is well-defined" theorem. Contraction is the only way to *reduce* the rank of a tensor without losing the multilinear structure (averaging or substituting destroys the multilinearity). The standard targets of contraction are the trace of a $(1,1)$-tensor, the Ricci tensor as a contraction of Riemann, and the scalar curvature as the metric-trace of Ricci.

The meta-strategy that threads through all of the above: **everything in this chapter is fibrewise multilinear algebra, plus smoothness**. Every theorem is "do this on each fibre and check it varies smoothly". Every diagnostic is "is this $C^\infty(M)$-linear?" because $C^\infty(M)$-linearity in a slot is the categorical statement that the operation is fibrewise. The question that every exercise in this chapter secretly asks is "does this gadget live at a point, or does it secretly involve derivatives across points?" — tensors live at a point, the Lie bracket does not, the metric does, the connection does not, the curvature tensor does.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Tensor Field is C-Infinity Multilinear over C-Infinity Functions|Tensor Characterization Lemma]]**: a multilinear map on vector fields and 1-forms is a tensor field iff it is $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear. **Typical use:** the single most-used diagnostic in differential geometry. Recognize when an object is tensorial: check $C^\infty(M)$-linearity in each slot. Recognize when it is *not*: find an $f$ for which the linearity fails (which is always how the Lie bracket gets discovered to be non-tensorial). The lemma is also used in reverse: given an abstract $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear gadget, you have a tensor field whose components can be computed by evaluation on the coordinate frame. This is how the curvature tensor of a connection is constructed in Riemannian geometry.

- **[[Thm - Transformation Rule for Tensor Components|Transformation Rule]]**: upper indices transform with the new-from-old Jacobian, lower indices with the old-from-new Jacobian. **Typical use:** the "classical" definition of a tensor and the bridge to the index notation used in physics. Whenever a physicist writes a quantity with up/down indices and claims it is "a tensor", what is being asserted is that the components transform by this rule under change of coordinates. The rule also generates the **distinction between tensors and pseudo-tensors**: pseudo-tensors transform by the rule plus an extra factor of $\mathrm{sgn}(\det J)$, and they show up in volume-form contexts on non-orientable manifolds.

- **The tensor product on fibres**: $T^{(k,\ell)}(V) = V^{\otimes k} \otimes (V^*)^{\otimes \ell}$. **Typical use:** the algebraic foundation. Every theorem about tensor *fields* reduces, at a point, to a theorem about tensor *spaces*, and the algebra of tensor spaces is the algebra of [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces|tensor products]] from LA IX. The fibre-level statements ($\dim T^{(k,\ell)} = n^{k+\ell}$, the basis-induced basis, the universal property) all transfer verbatim. The smoothness of the bundle structure is what gives the manifold-level theorems extra content.

- **Pullback for covariant tensor fields**: $F^* : \mathcal{T}^k(N) \to \mathcal{T}^k(M)$, contravariant functor in $F$. **Typical use:** every coordinate calculation. To compute a tensor field's components in a non-obvious chart, pull it back from a chart where the components are simple. To prove a relation between tensor fields, often pull back to a particularly simple manifold (a chart, a point). Pullback also commutes with the tensor product and (in the next chapter) with the exterior derivative, so it carries enormous algebraic structure.

- **Contraction**: $C^i_j : \mathcal{T}^{(k,\ell)}(M) \to \mathcal{T}^{(k-1,\ell-1)}(M)$, coordinate-invariant. **Typical use:** every scalar invariant in differential geometry — trace, scalar curvature, divergence — is a contraction of a higher-rank tensor field. The Ricci tensor is a contraction of the Riemann curvature; the scalar curvature is a contraction of the Ricci; the divergence of a vector field is a contraction of $\nabla X$. Whenever you need a number out of a tensor field, contraction is the way to get it.

# Bridges

1. **Linear Algebra IX — multilinear algebra is the fibre.** The whole opening of this chapter is [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants|LA IX]] read again with a single change of emphasis: we now systematically separate covariant ($V^*$) from contravariant ($V$) factors and track up-versus-down indices. The [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces|tensor product]] $V \otimes W$ is the fibre of the $(k, \ell) = (2, 0)$ tensor bundle (with $W = V$); the [[Def - Bilinear Form|bilinear form]] space $\mathcal{B}(V, V)$ is the fibre of the $(0,2)$-tensor bundle. The whole structure of forms (alternating multilinear) and of symmetric tensors ($\mathrm{Sym}$ projector) carries over verbatim, *and* the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|universal property]] of $V \otimes W$ specializes to give the universal property of the $(k,\ell)$-tensor fibre. To prove a fibre-level statement, look it up in LA IX; to extend to fields, check that it varies smoothly with $p$, which is automatic when the fibre construction is functorial.

2. **Multivariate Analysis IV — differential forms generalize from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to manifolds.** [[Multivariate Analysis IV — Differential Forms and Stokes' Theorem|MA IV]] develops [[Def - Differential Form|differential k-forms]] on $\mathbb{R}^n$ — alternating multilinear functionals on tangent vectors — together with the [[Def - The Wedge Product|wedge product]], the [[Def - The Exterior Derivative|exterior derivative]], pullback, and Stokes's theorem. These are exactly alternating $(0,k)$-tensor fields, defined locally on Euclidean space. The present chapter (and the next, [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]]) lifts this entire structure to a smooth manifold. The bridge is exact: in any single coordinate chart on $M$, a differential form on $M$ is literally a differential form on the chart's image in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and the operations agree. What is new at the manifold level is the patching condition between charts, which is automatic because the operations (wedge, exterior derivative, pullback) are all natural under coordinate changes.

3. **Special Relativity — the Minkowski metric is a constant $(0,2)$-tensor field on $\mathbb{R}^4$.** The [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|Minkowski metric]] $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$ (Lee's signature) is a symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor field on $\mathbb{R}^4$ whose components are constant in inertial coordinates. The change between inertial frames is a [[Def - The Lorentz Transformation|Lorentz transformation]], and the statement that $\eta$ is a tensor — that its components transform by the rule of [[Thm - Transformation Rule for Tensor Components]] — *is* the statement that the spacetime interval $\eta_{\mu\nu} dx^\mu dx^\nu$ is Lorentz-invariant. The four-velocity $u^\mu$ is a contravariant 1-tensor, the four-momentum $p^\mu = mu^\mu$ similarly, and the electromagnetic field strength $F_{\mu\nu}$ is an antisymmetric $(0,2)$-tensor — an alternating $(0,2)$-tensor field, the basic example of the alternating tensors of §7.3. General relativity replaces this constant tensor field with a curved one and lets the geometry be dynamic; the tensorial framework is the only one in which the principle of general covariance has a well-defined meaning.

4. **Continuum mechanics — the stress tensor.** In a deformable medium, the **Cauchy stress tensor** $\sigma$ at a point is a symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor (more precisely, a $(1,1)$-tensor that becomes symmetric after lowering an index with the spatial metric) representing the force per unit area transmitted across an infinitesimal surface. The traction $t = \sigma(n, \cdot)$ on a surface with unit normal $n$ is a covector, and the symmetry $\sigma_{ij} = \sigma_{ji}$ is the manifestation of conservation of angular momentum (Cauchy's second law). This is the archetypal physical application of symmetric 2-tensor fields, and it predates the abstract definition by half a century: Cauchy's 1822 introduction of $\sigma$ was the origin of the very word *tensor* (German *Spannungstensor*, "tension tensor").

5. **Rings and Modules — sections form modules over $C^\infty(M)$.** The space $\mathcal{T}^{(k,\ell)}(M)$ of smooth $(k,\ell)$-tensor fields is naturally a *module* over the [[Def - Ring|commutative ring]] $C^\infty(M)$ — see [[Def - The Smooth Functions Ring]] — by pointwise scalar multiplication. The tensor characterization lemma can be stated in this language: the tensor fields are precisely the $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear maps among modules of sections. This puts differential geometry into the framework of [[Def - Module|module theory]]: vector fields are a module ($TM$'s module of sections), 1-forms are its dual module (in the projective sense), and tensor fields of type $(k,\ell)$ are the iterated tensor-product modules over $C^\infty(M)$. The Serre–Swan theorem makes this precise: finitely-generated projective modules over $C^\infty(M)$ are exactly sections of smooth vector bundles.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a tensor field is fibrewise multilinear algebra varying smoothly.** Every theorem in this chapter has the shape "do this on $V = T_pM$, then check it varies smoothly with $p$". The fibrewise step is multilinear algebra ([[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants|LA IX]]); the smoothness step is automatic when the fibrewise construction is functorial, which it is for tensor product, dualization, symmetrization, alternation, contraction, and pullback. So there are really *two* layers of insight to maintain. The fibre-level layer is "what is the linear-algebraic object" — a metric is a symmetric bilinear form, the curvature is a $(1,3)$-multilinear gadget on vectors, the stress is a symmetric bilinear form. The bundle-level layer is "how does it patch" — the answer is always "smoothly, by transition functions inherited from $TM$ and $T^*M$". Whenever a problem feels heavy, separate the layers: solve the fibrewise problem on $V$ using LA IX (which is sober and finite-dimensional), then attach the smoothness afterward (which is usually a matter of checking a partition of unity argument or a chart change).

**The true name of a tensor field is a $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear gadget.** The standard definition — "a smooth section of $T^{(k,\ell)}M$" — is the right thing to *construct* but the wrong thing to *think*. The operational meaning, the one that drives every proof and every diagnostic, is **the gadget pulls smooth functions in and out of its slots without producing derivative terms**. This is what [[Thm - Tensor Field is C-Infinity Multilinear over C-Infinity Functions|Lee's lemma]] says, and it converts every "is this a tensor?" question into a one-line check. When you see a candidate $T(X, Y, \dots)$, substitute $fX$ for $X$ and watch what happens. If you get $f \cdot T(X, Y, \dots)$ and nothing else, $T$ is a tensor and lives at a point. If you get an extra term involving $df$ or $Xf$ or $Yf$, $T$ is *not* a tensor and you have a differential operator on your hands. The Lie bracket fails the test ($[fX, Y] = f[X,Y] - (Yf)X$); the metric passes ($g(fX, Y) = f g(X, Y)$); the covariant derivative $\nabla_X Y$ fails in the $X$ slot but passes in the $Y$ slot (asymmetric tensoriality, which is exactly why $\nabla$ is called a *connection* and not a tensor). The true-name diagnostic is what separates the tensorial parts of differential geometry from the non-tensorial parts, and it is the question to ask before any other.

**Upper indices contract with new-from-old; lower indices with old-from-new — and that is what "covariant" and "contravariant" *mean*.** The terminology is the reverse of what one initially expects: a *covariant* quantity transforms *contrary* to the basis change (with the inverse Jacobian), and a *contravariant* quantity transforms *with* the basis change (with the Jacobian). The names come from the older usage where "covariant" meant "transforms like the basis": a basis vector $E_i$ in the old chart maps to $E_i = (\partial x^a / \partial \tilde x^i) E_a$ in the new — the old-from-new Jacobian acts on a lower index, hence "lower index = co-variant = basis-like". A tangent vector $v = v^i E_i$ then has components that must transform inverse-to-basis to leave $v$ invariant, hence "upper index = contra-variant = inverse-basis-like". The whole confusion of the index notation evaporates once this is internalized: the indices are telling you exactly how the components must adjust to keep the underlying tensor field unchanged. If a candidate quantity has up/down indices but does not transform this way, it is not a tensor; the labelling is a *promise* about transformation behaviour.

**The Lie bracket's failure to be a tensor is the seed of curvature.** It is tempting to dismiss the failure of $[X, Y]$ to be tensorial as a technical annoyance, but it is the seed from which Riemannian curvature grows. A tensor field measures something *at a point*, but the bracket measures the failure of two flows to commute, which is a *next-order* effect. Whenever you have two non-commuting differential operations on a manifold (covariant differentiation along $X$ then $Y$ versus along $Y$ then $X$), the difference is, schematically, the Lie bracket plus a tensorial correction. Tweaking the differential structure so that the bracket-piece cancels — by adding a connection — produces a genuinely tensorial measure of non-commutation, namely the Riemann curvature. The whole hierarchy of geometric invariants (curvature, torsion, holonomy) lives in the gap between "differential" operations and the "tensorial" corrections of them. This chapter equips the reader with the language of tensoriality precisely so that this gap can be measured in later chapters.

**Symmetric and alternating tensors live different downstream lives.** Despite their formal similarity — both are sub-quotients of $T^k(V^*)$, both have idempotent projectors, both come with natural products (symmetric product and wedge product) — symmetric and alternating tensor fields lead radically different downstream lives. Symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor fields give *metrics* (when positive definite) and *quadratic functionals* — the language of Riemannian geometry, lengths and angles, the kinetic-energy bilinear form in mechanics, the stress and strain of materials. Alternating $(0,k)$-tensor fields give *differential forms* — the language of integration over submanifolds, of de Rham cohomology, of gauge theory and the field strengths of electromagnetism and Yang–Mills. The two strands together cover most of differential geometry, and they almost never mix: a problem about lengths and angles is symmetric, a problem about flux and integration is alternating. The reason they get separate definition pages in this chapter, despite being defined in parallel, is that the rest of the curriculum splits along this line.
