# Notation Registry

A standing convention for this topic: **a smooth manifold $M$ is always Hausdorff, second-countable, and without boundary unless otherwise stated.** This is what makes [[Thm - Existence of Smooth Partitions of Unity|partitions of unity]] available, and through them the existence of Riemannian metrics. Where boundary cases differ — they almost never do, in this chapter — the difference is noted explicitly. A second standing convention concerns the Lorentzian signature: Lee uses **signature $(1, n-1)$** ("mostly minus", $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,\ldots,-1)$), and so do we, to match [[Special Relativity I — Postulates and Lorentz Transformations]]; many physics texts use the opposite "mostly plus" $(-,+,+,+)$ convention, which differs by an overall sign of $g$ and swaps the sign convention for timelike and spacelike. Where a formula is convention-sensitive, both versions are noted.

- $M, N$ — smooth manifolds; $\dim M = n$
- $p, q$ — points of $M$
- $T_pM$ — the tangent space to $M$ at $p$
- $T_p^*M$ — the cotangent space; $TM$, $T^*M$ — the tangent and cotangent bundles
- $X, Y, Z$ — smooth vector fields on $M$; $\mathfrak{X}(M) = \Gamma(TM)$
- $\omega, \eta, \alpha$ — covector fields (1-forms); $\Omega^1(M) = \Gamma(T^*M)$
- $g, h, \tilde g$ — Riemannian or pseudo-Riemannian metrics on $M$
- $g_p(\cdot, \cdot) = \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{g,p}$ — the inner product on $T_pM$ induced by $g$
- $g_{ij}(x) = g_p(\partial_i, \partial_j)$ — components of $g$ in local coordinates $x^i$
- $g^{ij}$ — components of the inverse matrix, so $g^{ij} g_{jk} = \delta^i_k$
- $|v|_g = \sqrt{g_p(v,v)}$ — the length of a tangent vector (only meaningful when $g_p(v,v) \geq 0$)
- $L_g(\gamma) = \int_a^b |\dot\gamma(t)|_g\, dt$ — the length of a smooth curve $\gamma$
- $d_g(p, q)$ — the Riemannian distance from $p$ to $q$, the infimum of $L_g$ over piecewise smooth curves
- $\flat : TM \to T^*M$, $\sharp : T^*M \to TM$ — the **musical isomorphisms** (index-lowering and index-raising)
- $X^\flat$, $\omega^\sharp$ — the covector obtained by lowering $X$, the vector obtained by raising $\omega$
- $\mathrm{grad}\, f = (df)^\sharp$ — the **gradient** of a smooth function $f$ on a Riemannian manifold
- $(p, q)$ or $(r, s)$ — the **signature** of a pseudo-Riemannian metric: $p$ positive eigenvalues, $q$ negative; $p + q = n$
- $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ — the **Minkowski metric** on $\mathbb{R}^4$; the canonical signature-$(1,3)$ form
- $\mathbb{R}^{1, n-1}$ — flat $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the Lorentzian metric of signature $(1, n-1)$; $\mathbb{R}^{1,3} = \mathbb{M}$
- $\iota : S \hookrightarrow M$ — the inclusion of a [[Def - Embedded Submanifold|submanifold]]
- $F^*g$ — the **pullback** of a tensor field $g$ on $N$ to $M$ along a smooth map $F : M \to N$
- $\mathrm{Isom}(M, g)$ — the group of [[Def - Isometry of Riemannian Manifolds|isometries]] of $(M, g)$
- $\nabla$ — a **connection** on $TM$ (introduced as a forward concept; full development belongs to Riemannian Geometry)
- $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ — the **Christoffel symbols** of a connection (forward concept)

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: a Riemannian metric is an inner product placed in every tangent space, varying smoothly from point to point. That is the whole of the definition, but the consequences are enormous, because almost every geometric notion one cares about — length, angle, distance, area, volume, gradient, divergence, curvature — is built from an inner product. A smooth manifold by itself has no geometry: it has a topology and a calculus, you can differentiate smooth maps and integrate forms, but you cannot say how long a curve is or how far apart two points are. The data needed to install geometry is exactly an inner product in each $T_pM$, and a Riemannian metric is the smooth field of such inner products.

So far the development of [[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases|smooth manifolds]] has produced [[Def - The Tangent Space|tangent vectors]], [[Def - Vector Field on a Manifold|vector fields]], [[Def - Cotangent Space and Cotangent Bundle|covectors]], [[Def - Tensor Field on a Manifold|tensor fields]], and [[Def - Differential k-Form on a Manifold|differential forms]] — every object the calculus of manifolds requires. None of this is geometry. The Lie bracket of vector fields, the exterior derivative of a form, Stokes' theorem on a manifold — all of these are smooth-structure invariants alone, defined without reference to any inner product. What is missing is *quantitative metric structure*: how to assign a number to the length of a curve, an angle between two tangent vectors, a distance between two points. The trouble is that the cleanest way to do all of this on $\mathbb{R}^n$ — the Euclidean dot product $\langle v, w \rangle = \sum v^i w^i$ — uses the standard inner product, an object that lives canonically on $\mathbb{R}^n$ but does *not* transport to a manifold along charts: a chart map is not in general an isometry of the Euclidean structure, so the Euclidean inner product depends on the chart and is not a manifold-intrinsic object. The fix is to *install* an inner product manifold-intrinsically, by giving a smoothly varying inner product in each tangent space. Once installed, this single piece of additional data — the **Riemannian metric** $g$ — converts the manifold into a *geometric* object: lengths, angles, distances, volumes, gradients, [[Def - Isometry|isometries]], all become well defined, and Riemannian geometry is the study of what they look like.

The plan of the chapter is laid out as a single arc. Section §12.1 introduces the Riemannian metric itself: a smooth, symmetric, positive-definite $(0,2)$-tensor field $g$ on $M$. We give the formal definition, exhibit the canonical examples (the round sphere, the hyperbolic plane, flat tori, the induced metric on any submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^N$), and prove that every smooth manifold admits at least one Riemannian metric, via a [[Thm - Existence of Smooth Partitions of Unity|partition of unity]] argument that is the prototypical "patch local data together using a partition of unity" construction. Section §12.2 uses the metric to define the length of a curve and the **Riemannian distance function** $d_g(p,q) = \inf_\gamma L_g(\gamma)$, and proves the central theorem of the section: this distance function turns $M$ into an honest metric space whose induced topology coincides with the manifold topology. This compatibility is the bridge from differential geometry to point-set topology: every Riemannian manifold is automatically a metric space, and the topology nothing surprises. Section §12.3 introduces the **musical [[Def - Isomorphism|isomorphisms]]** $\flat : TM \to T^*M$ and $\sharp : T^*M \to TM$, the canonical [[Def - Isomorphism|isomorphisms]] between tangent and cotangent bundles induced by the metric. They formalise the index gymnastics of "raising and lowering indices" universally used in physics and let us define the gradient $\mathrm{grad}\, f = (df)^\sharp$ of a smooth function as a vector field on a Riemannian manifold — a definition that depends on $g$, unlike the chart-dependent gradient of calculus.

Section §12.4 generalises by relaxing positive-definiteness while keeping non-degeneracy: a **semi-Riemannian (pseudo-Riemannian) metric** is a smooth, symmetric, non-degenerate $(0,2)$-tensor field, characterised at each point by its **signature** $(p, q)$ — the number of positive and negative eigenvalues. By Sylvester's law of inertia this signature is a coordinate-free invariant. The Riemannian case is signature $(n, 0)$; the case of overwhelming physical interest is signature $(1, n-1)$, the **Lorentzian** case. A Lorentzian manifold has a causal structure built into every tangent space: tangent vectors split into **timelike** ($g(v,v) > 0$), **spacelike** ($g(v,v) < 0$), and **null** or **lightlike** ($g(v,v) = 0$, $v \neq 0$) classes, and this trichotomy is the local model of the light-cone structure of spacetime. The flat Lorentzian manifold $(\mathbb{R}^4, \eta)$ — Minkowski space, the entire content of [[Special Relativity I — Postulates and Lorentz Transformations|special relativity]] — is the simplest example, and the Lorentz group $O(1,3)$ is its isometry group. Curved Lorentzian manifolds are the spacetimes of **general relativity**, and the step from §12.4 to GR is the step from a flat to a curved Lorentzian metric — the central conceptual move of twentieth-century physics. One important asymmetry to note: a Riemannian metric exists on every smooth manifold, but a Lorentzian one does not. The obstruction is the existence of a nowhere-vanishing line field, which fails for $S^2$ (the [[Thm - A Lorentzian Manifold Need Not Exist on Every Smooth Manifold|hairy-ball-style obstruction]]).

Section §12.5 is a forward bridge. The metric tensor alone is not enough to do all of differential geometry — to define geodesics, to compare tangent vectors at neighbouring points, to differentiate vector fields covariantly — we need a **connection** on $TM$, and the central theorem of Riemannian geometry, the **fundamental theorem of Riemannian geometry**, asserts that a Riemannian metric uniquely selects one connection (the **Levi-Civita connection**) among all possible ones by requiring two properties: torsion-freeness and metric compatibility. We state the theorem here without proof; the proof — and the whole resulting development of geodesics, curvature, sectional curvature, Jacobi fields, the exponential map, Bonnet–Myers, Cartan–Hadamard — belongs to a future "Riemannian Geometry I" topic. What §12.5 establishes is the *existence and uniqueness* of the right connection, so that the reader knows what is unlocked and where the next chapter begins.

$$
\text{smooth manifold} \;\xrightarrow{\text{add }g}\; \text{Riemannian (or Lorentzian) manifold} \;\xrightarrow{\text{Fund.\,Thm.}}\; \text{Riemannian geometry}
$$

The reader is assumed to have worked through [[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases|smooth manifolds]], [[Differential Geometry III — Tangent Vectors and the Differential|tangent vectors]], [[Differential Geometry VI — Vector Bundles and the Cotangent Bundle|vector bundles and the cotangent bundle]], [[Differential Geometry VII — Tensors and Tensor Fields|tensor fields]], and the partition-of-unity material from [[Differential Geometry II — Smooth Maps and Partitions of Unity]]. The linear-algebra backbone is [[Def - Inner Product Space|inner product spaces]] and [[Def - Bilinear Form|bilinear forms]], including Sylvester's law of inertia for the signature of a non-degenerate symmetric form. For the Lorentzian half of the chapter, fluency with [[Special Relativity I — Postulates and Lorentz Transformations]] is the right preparation: it is exactly the constant-metric, flat case of what §12.4 develops at the level of general manifolds.

# Concept Map

## §12.1 Riemannian Metrics

- **[[Def - Riemannian Metric]]**
	- A Riemannian metric on a smooth manifold $M$ is a smooth covariant $(0,2)$-tensor field $g$ that is symmetric and positive-definite at every point: $g_p(v, w) = g_p(w, v)$ and $g_p(v, v) > 0$ for every nonzero $v \in T_pM$. Equivalently, $g$ assigns a smoothly-varying inner product to each tangent space $T_pM$. In local coordinates $x^i$, $g = g_{ij}(x)\, dx^i \otimes dx^j$ with $g_{ij}$ a smooth symmetric positive-definite matrix-valued function. The Euclidean metric $\bar g = \sum (dx^i)^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the prototype.

- **[[Def - Riemannian Manifold]]**
	- A Riemannian manifold is a pair $(M, g)$ where $M$ is a smooth manifold and $g$ is a Riemannian metric on $M$. The terminology promotes the metric from "additional data" to "part of the object": [[Def - Isometry|isometries]], geodesics, and curvature all refer to $(M, g)$ as a whole. The same smooth manifold can carry many genuinely different Riemannian metrics — the round sphere and a flattened ellipsoid are the same smooth $S^2$ but distinct Riemannian manifolds.

- **[[Def - Induced Metric on a Submanifold]]**
	- If $(M, g)$ is a Riemannian manifold and $\iota : S \hookrightarrow M$ is an immersed or embedded submanifold, the **induced metric** on $S$ is the pullback $\iota^* g$, which restricts $g$ to pairs of tangent vectors in $T_pS \subseteq T_pM$. This is always a Riemannian metric on $S$ because the restriction of a positive-definite form to a [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] is still positive-definite. The round metric on $S^n$ is the induced metric from the inclusion $S^n \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.

- **[[Thm - Existence of Riemannian Metrics via Partitions of Unity]]**
	- Every smooth manifold (with or without boundary) admits a Riemannian metric. The proof is the prototype "patch local data via a partition of unity" construction: cover $M$ by coordinate charts, pull back the Euclidean metric on each chart, and combine them using a smooth partition of unity. The convex-combination structure of positive-definite forms guarantees the result is again positive-definite. The same argument fails for Lorentzian metrics, which is why semi-Riemannian existence is genuinely different.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The [[Def - Riemannian Volume Form|Riemannian Volume Form]] *(from Differential Geometry IX)*
> Once a Riemannian metric is in hand, an oriented Riemannian manifold carries a canonical [[Def - Riemannian Volume Form|Riemannian volume form]] $dV_g$, locally $\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\, dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n$. This is the integration-theory entrypoint: every Riemannian manifold is automatically a measure space, and integrals of smooth functions $\int_M f\, dV_g$ are well defined. The bridge to [[Multivariate Analysis IV — Differential Forms and Stokes' Theorem|forms on Euclidean space]] is that the Euclidean volume form $dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n$ is the special case $g = \delta_{ij}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita Connection]] *(from Riemannian Geometry)*
> A Riemannian metric uniquely selects one connection on $TM$ — the **Levi-Civita connection** — by demanding torsion-freeness and metric compatibility. This is the fundamental theorem of Riemannian geometry, the central existence-and-uniqueness result on which the entire subsequent development rests; see §12.5.

- **[[Ex - The Round Metric on the Sphere via Restriction]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute the induced metric on $S^2 \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ in spherical-coordinate parametrisation $X(\theta, \varphi) = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \sin\theta\sin\varphi, \cos\theta)$ and verify it equals $g = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\, d\varphi^2$.

- **[[Ex - The Hyperbolic Plane as a Riemannian Manifold]]** (⭐⭐)
	- The upper half-plane $\mathbb{H}^2 = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : y > 0\}$ with metric $g = (dx^2 + dy^2)/y^2$ is a Riemannian manifold (verify smoothness, symmetry, positive-definiteness, and that it is *not* the Euclidean metric).

## §12.2 The Riemannian Distance Function

- **[[Def - Length of a Curve and Riemannian Distance]]**
	- For a piecewise smooth curve $\gamma : [a, b] \to M$ on a Riemannian manifold, the **length** is $L_g(\gamma) = \int_a^b |\dot\gamma(t)|_g\, dt$, where $|\dot\gamma|_g = \sqrt{g_{\gamma(t)}(\dot\gamma, \dot\gamma)}$. Length is independent of parametrisation. The **Riemannian distance** between $p, q \in M$ (with $M$ connected) is $d_g(p,q) = \inf L_g(\gamma)$ over all piecewise smooth curves from $p$ to $q$. For Euclidean $(\mathbb{R}^n, \bar g)$, $d_{\bar g}$ is the ordinary Euclidean distance.

- **[[Thm - The Riemannian Distance Makes M a Metric Space]]**
	- For a connected Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$, the function $d_g$ is a genuine metric: it is non-negative, symmetric, satisfies the triangle inequality, and $d_g(p,q) = 0$ iff $p = q$. Moreover the metric-space topology induced by $d_g$ coincides with the original manifold topology. The non-trivial part is positivity ($p \neq q$ implies $d_g > 0$); the rest is straightforward from the definition.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - Geodesic|Geodesics]] *(from Riemannian Geometry)*
> Curves that locally minimise $L_g$ — the **geodesics** — are the Riemannian analogues of straight lines and the central objects of Riemannian geometry. They are characterised either variationally (extrema of the length or energy functional) or via the **geodesic equation** $\ddot\gamma^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\dot\gamma^i\dot\gamma^j = 0$ involving the Christoffel symbols of the Levi-Civita connection. The fundamental theorems of geodesic completeness — **Hopf–Rinow** (metric completeness, geodesic completeness, and the existence of length-minimising geodesics all coincide) — sit on top of this.

	- Prove that for a connected Riemannian manifold, the metric topology generated by $d_g$ equals the original manifold topology, using the local comparability of $g$ with the Euclidean metric.

## §12.3 The Musical Isomorphism

- **[[Def - Musical Isomorphism (Flat and Sharp)]]**
	- The **flat** map $\flat : TM \to T^*M$ sends a tangent vector $v$ to the covector $\flat(v) = v^\flat = g(v, \cdot)$, the linear functional "take the inner product with $v$". The **sharp** map $\sharp : T^*M \to TM$ is the inverse: $\omega^\sharp$ is the unique tangent vector with $g(\omega^\sharp, w) = \omega(w)$ for every $w$. In coordinates these are index-lowering $v_i = g_{ij}v^j$ and index-raising $\omega^i = g^{ij}\omega_j$. The pair are smooth bundle isomorphisms because $g$ is non-degenerate at every point.

- **[[Thm - Musical Isomorphism Identifies Tangent and Cotangent Bundles]]**
	- The flat map $\flat : TM \to T^*M$ is a smooth bundle isomorphism. Equivalently the cotangent bundle and the tangent bundle of a Riemannian manifold are canonically isomorphic via $g$. In particular, the **gradient** of a smooth function $f \in C^\infty(M)$ is defined as $\mathrm{grad}\, f := (df)^\sharp$, the vector field metric-dual to the 1-form $df$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - The Hodge Star Operator|The Hodge Star Operator]] *(from [[Def - de Rham Cohomology|de Rham Cohomology]] and Hodge Theory)*
> Combined with the [[Def - Riemannian Volume Form|volume form]], the musical isomorphisms generate the **Hodge star** $\star : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{n-k}(M)$, an isomorphism between $k$-forms and $(n-k)$-forms that depends on both the metric and the orientation. From it comes the **[[Def - Hodge Laplacian|Hodge Laplacian]]** $\Delta = d\delta + \delta d$ on forms, and the **Hodge decomposition theorem** on a compact oriented Riemannian manifold: every form decomposes uniquely as exact $\oplus$ co-exact $\oplus$ harmonic. This is the analytic/PDE side of the de Rham theorem.

- **[[Ex - Raising and Lowering Indices in Polar Coordinates]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute $g_{ij}$ and $g^{ij}$ for the Euclidean metric in polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ on $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ and use them to lower the covector $df$ for $f(r, \theta) = r^2 \cos\theta$.

## §12.4 Semi-Riemannian and Lorentzian Manifolds

- **[[Def - Semi-Riemannian Metric and Signature]]**
	- A semi-Riemannian (pseudo-Riemannian) metric on $M$ is a smooth symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor field $g$ that is **non-degenerate** at every point — meaning the map $v \mapsto g(v, \cdot)$ from $T_pM$ to $T_p^*M$ is an isomorphism — but not necessarily positive-definite. By Sylvester's law of inertia, $g_p$ has a well-defined **signature** $(p, q)$ with $p + q = n$: the numbers of positive and negative eigenvalues in any diagonalisation. The signature is required to be constant across $M$.

- **[[Def - Lorentzian Manifold]]**
	- A Lorentzian manifold is a semi-Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$ of signature $(1, n-1)$ — one positive and $n-1$ negative eigenvalues (Lee's "mostly minus" convention; the opposite "mostly plus" $(n-1, 1)$ convention is also common in physics). The Lorentzian case is the geometric model of spacetime: there is one "timelike" direction and $n-1$ "spacelike" directions, and the indefiniteness of $g$ produces a causal structure on every tangent space — the light cone.

- **[[Def - Causal Classification of Tangent Vectors]]**
	- On a Lorentzian manifold, a nonzero tangent vector $v \in T_pM$ is **timelike** if $g_p(v, v) > 0$, **spacelike** if $g_p(v, v) < 0$, and **null** (or **lightlike**) if $g_p(v, v) = 0$ (with $v \neq 0$). The set of null vectors forms the **light cone** at $p$. This is the geometric heart of relativity: timelike vectors are tangent to worldlines of massive particles, null vectors to those of light, spacelike vectors to instantaneous spatial directions.

- **[[Def - Isometry of Riemannian Manifolds]]**
	- An isometry between $(M, g)$ and $(N, h)$ is a [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]] $F : M \to N$ with $F^*h = g$. Equivalently, $dF_p : T_pM \to T_{F(p)}N$ is a linear isometry of inner product spaces for every $p$. The isometries of a fixed $(M, g)$ form a group $\mathrm{Isom}(M, g)$. For Minkowski space $(\mathbb{R}^n, \eta)$, this group is the Poincaré group; restricted to fixing the origin, it is the Lorentz group.

- **[[Thm - A Lorentzian Manifold Need Not Exist on Every Smooth Manifold]]**
	- Unlike Riemannian metrics, Lorentzian metrics do not exist on every smooth manifold. The obstruction is the existence of a **nowhere-vanishing line field** (a rank-one [[Def - Subbundle|subbundle]] of $TM$): a Lorentzian metric uses such a field to single out the timelike direction at each point. The sphere $S^2$ has no nowhere-vanishing line field (the hairy-ball theorem says it has no nowhere-vanishing vector field, and a line field is even weaker but still obstructed in even-dimensional spheres), so $S^2$ admits no Lorentzian metric.

> [!tip] Unlocked: General Relativity *(from General Relativity)*
> The fundamental object of general relativity is a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold $(M, g)$ — **spacetime** — whose metric $g$ satisfies the **Einstein field equations** $R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac{1}{2} R\, g_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\, T_{\mu\nu}$. Matter and energy ($T_{\mu\nu}$) curve the metric; the curved metric, in turn, dictates how matter moves — geodesics of $g$ are the worldlines of free-falling test particles. Minkowski space is the simplest solution (the empty, flat case), and **special relativity** is the chapter of GR in which the metric happens to be the constant $\eta_{\mu\nu}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: [[Def - The Lorentz Group|The Lorentz Group]] as Isometry [[Def - Group|Group]] *(from Special Relativity and Group Theory)*
> The isometry group of $(\mathbb{R}^4, \eta)$ fixing the origin is exactly the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]] $O(1, 3)$ — the orthogonal group of the indefinite form $\eta$. This is the bridge from differential-geometric "isometry" to physical "frame change", and it identifies special relativity as the linear-algebra chapter of the geometry of a single fixed indefinite form. The full isometry group, allowing translations, is the **Poincaré group**, the semidirect product $O(1,3) \ltimes \mathbb{R}^{1,3}$.

- **[[Ex - Minkowski Space as the Flat Lorentzian Manifold]]** (⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Isometries Send Geodesics to Geodesics]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that an isometry $F : (M, g) \to (N, h)$ between Riemannian manifolds sends geodesics of $g$ to geodesics of $h$ — equivalently, geodesics are invariants of Riemannian isometries.

## §12.5 Forward Bridge — The Levi-Civita Connection and Geodesics

- **[[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Statement)]]**
	- On every Riemannian (or semi-Riemannian) manifold $(M, g)$ there is a unique connection $\nabla$ on $TM$ that is both **torsion-free** ($\nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X = [X, Y]$) and **metric-compatible** ($Xg(Y, Z) = g(\nabla_X Y, Z) + g(Y, \nabla_X Z)$). This connection is the **Levi-Civita connection**. Its uniqueness is encoded in the **Koszul formula**, an explicit expression for $g(\nabla_X Y, Z)$ in terms of $g$ and Lie brackets alone, which immediately gives existence by reading off $\nabla_X Y$ from $\flat$-duality.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Riemannian Curvature *(from Riemannian Geometry)*
> Once the Levi-Civita connection is in hand, all of Riemannian geometry follows. The **Riemann curvature tensor** $R(X, Y)Z = \nabla_X\nabla_Y Z - \nabla_Y\nabla_X Z - \nabla_{[X,Y]} Z$ measures the failure of $\nabla$ to commute — equivalently, the obstruction to the metric being locally Euclidean. From it come the **sectional curvature** (curvature in a 2-plane, generalising Gaussian curvature of a surface), the **Ricci tensor** $\mathrm{Ric}_{ij} = R^k{}_{ikj}$ (an averaged curvature), and the **scalar curvature** $R = g^{ij}\mathrm{Ric}_{ij}$ (the fully traced curvature). The **Bonnet–Myers theorem** (positive Ricci curvature with a lower bound implies compactness and a diameter bound), the **Cartan–Hadamard theorem** (non-positive curvature implies the universal cover is diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n$ via the exponential map), and the **Synge theorem** (positive sectional curvature on an even-dimensional orientable manifold implies simple connectivity) are paradigmatic curvature-to-topology results. The Riemannian **exponential map** $\exp_p : T_pM \to M$ (distinct from the Lie-group exponential of [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|DG XI]]) sends $v \in T_pM$ to $\gamma_v(1)$, the value at parameter $1$ of the unique geodesic with initial velocity $v$; it gives normal coordinates around any point and is the geometric workhorse of comparison theorems. **Jacobi fields** — variations of geodesics — measure how curvature spreads nearby geodesics, and underlie the second variation of length, conjugate points, the Morse index theorem, and the Bonnet–Myers proof.
>
> The full development of this material — the Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^k_{ij} = \tfrac{1}{2} g^{kl}(\partial_i g_{jl} + \partial_j g_{il} - \partial_l g_{ij})$, the geodesic equation, parallel transport, the Riemann tensor and its symmetries, sectional and Ricci curvature, the exponential map, Hopf–Rinow, Bonnet–Myers, Cartan–Hadamard, Synge, and the comparison theorems — is the content of a future **Riemannian Geometry I** topic. What §12.5 establishes is the unique connection that makes all of it possible. The Koszul formula, $2g(\nabla_X Y, Z) = Xg(Y, Z) + Yg(X, Z) - Zg(X, Y) + g([X, Y], Z) - g([X, Z], Y) - g([Y, Z], X)$, is the formula one writes down once and computes from forever after.
>
> The Lorentzian case is parallel — the same theorem holds for semi-Riemannian manifolds, and the resulting Levi-Civita connection on a Lorentzian manifold is the connection of **general relativity**. Geodesics of a Lorentzian metric are the worldlines of free-falling particles (timelike geodesics for massive particles, null geodesics for light), and the Einstein field equations $R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac{1}{2}R\, g_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\, T_{\mu\nu}$ relate the Ricci tensor of the Levi-Civita connection to the energy–momentum tensor of matter. The full gravitational theory — Schwarzschild and Kerr solutions, the singularity theorems of Hawking and Penrose, gravitational waves, the cosmological FRW models — is the Lorentzian shadow of the Riemannian developments above.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises and theorems of this topic chase a small, recognisable set of goals. The most common is to **compute a metric in coordinates**: given a manifold (sphere, hyperbolic plane, torus, Lie group, surface of revolution) and a parametrisation, produce the explicit matrix $g_{ij}(x)$. This is typically done by pulling back from an ambient Euclidean metric, and the entire skill is in expanding $X^*g$ correctly. A second target is to **verify that a candidate tensor is a Riemannian or semi-Riemannian metric**: check smoothness, symmetry, and either positive-definiteness or non-degeneracy. A third is to **compute the Riemannian distance** between specified points, often achieved by recognising the minimising curve and computing its length (a much weaker form of the geodesic problem that lies fully in Riemannian Geometry). A fourth is to **establish a structural property of the manifold** — that it is a metric space, that two metrics give the same topology, that the induced metric on a submanifold is well defined, that a map between two manifolds is or is not an isometry. A fifth is **causal** in the Lorentzian setting: classify a tangent vector or worldline as timelike, spacelike, or null; decide whether two events can be causally connected. Across all five the unifying observation is that *a Riemannian metric converts geometric questions into linear-algebraic ones at the tangent space*, and the work of the chapter is in checking that this conversion is smooth and varies coherently across $M$.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses fall into a small number of stereotyped patterns. **A smooth structure is given**, which already supplies tangent spaces, smooth functions, and partitions of unity — enough to prove existence of Riemannian metrics by patching. **An embedding into Euclidean space is given**, immediately producing an induced metric by pulling back the ambient Euclidean structure; this is how every concrete example in the chapter (sphere, torus, surface of revolution, hyperbolic plane via the upper-half-plane model, real projective space via $S^n$) is built. **A local parametrisation is given**, allowing direct computation of $g_{ij}$ in coordinates by substituting the parametrisation into the ambient metric expression. **A metric is given in coordinates**, allowing direct invocation of the musical isomorphisms (raise/lower with $g^{ij}, g_{ij}$) and the gradient formula. **A symmetry group is given** — for instance a Lie group acts on $M$ — allowing the construction of an invariant metric by averaging (when the group is compact). **A causal classification of vectors is given** in the Lorentzian case, immediately routing to the light cone and the trichotomy of vectors. The recurring move is to route a source to a target via pullback and the partition-of-unity construction: a submanifold's metric is the pullback; a global metric on $M$ is built from local Euclidean ones glued together. The [[Differential Geometry XII — Riemannian and Semi-Riemannian Manifolds#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in the topic routes through. When stuck, scan and try each one.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Pull back an ambient metric along an immersion or embedding.** Given a smooth map $F : M \to N$ and a (semi-)Riemannian metric $g$ on $N$, the pullback $F^*g$ is a $(0,2)$-tensor field on $M$ with $(F^*g)_p(v, w) = g_{F(p)}(dF_p(v), dF_p(w))$, and it is a Riemannian metric on $M$ if and only if $F$ is a smooth [[Def - Immersion, Submersion, and Embedding|immersion]]. *Trigger:* you have $M$ presented as a submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^N$ or of some larger Riemannian manifold. *Pattern:* substitute the coordinate expression of $F$ into $g_N$ and expand using $d(F^i F^j) = dF^i\, dF^j$.

2. **Compute $g$ in coordinates by substitution.** For a Riemannian manifold given by a parametrisation $X : U \to M$ with $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ open and $M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^N$, write $g = \sum (dF^i)^2$ in the ambient coordinates, then substitute $F = X(u)$ to obtain $g_{ij}(u) = \sum_k \partial_i X^k \partial_j X^k$. *Trigger:* any concrete Riemannian metric on a parametrised submanifold. The classical computations of the round metric, hyperbolic metric, and surfaces of revolution all run through this.

3. **Construct a global Riemannian metric via partition of unity.** Cover $M$ by coordinate charts $(U_\alpha, \varphi_\alpha)$; on each chart, the pullback $\varphi_\alpha^* \bar g$ of the Euclidean metric is a local Riemannian metric $g_\alpha$. Take a [[Thm - Existence of Smooth Partitions of Unity|smooth partition of unity]] $\{\psi_\alpha\}$ subordinate to the cover and set $g = \sum_\alpha \psi_\alpha g_\alpha$. *Trigger:* you need to know a Riemannian metric exists on a manifold given to you abstractly. *Pattern:* the key step is the convexity of "positive-definite symmetric forms" — a convex combination of positive-definite forms is positive-definite — which is why the gluing succeeds.

4. **Use the musical isomorphism to convert between vectors and covectors.** Lowering: $v^\flat_i = g_{ij} v^j$. Raising: $\omega^{\sharp,i} = g^{ij}\omega_j$. *Trigger:* a problem mixes vectors and covectors, or asks for the gradient of a function (which is $(df)^\sharp$), or asks for the divergence (which uses the inverse). *Pattern:* whenever a formula has a free index appearing with the "wrong" position relative to the surrounding indices, raise or lower it with the metric.

5. **Verify positive-definiteness or non-degeneracy in coordinates.** A symmetric matrix $g_{ij}(x)$ is positive-definite iff all eigenvalues are positive iff all principal minors are positive (Sylvester's criterion). It is non-degenerate iff $\det(g_{ij}) \neq 0$. *Trigger:* checking whether a candidate $(0,2)$-tensor field is a Riemannian or semi-Riemannian metric.

6. **Read off the signature in any convenient basis.** By Sylvester's law of inertia, the signature $(p, q)$ of a symmetric bilinear form is basis-independent. *Trigger:* you have a semi-Riemannian metric and want its signature; diagonalise in any one basis and count signs, then conclude the count is invariant.

7. **Bound a metric locally by the Euclidean one.** In any coordinate chart, on any compact subset $K$ of the chart, there exist constants $c, C > 0$ with $c |v|_{\bar g} \leq |v|_g \leq C |v|_{\bar g}$ for all $v \in T_xM$, $x \in K$ — by continuity and compactness on the unit sphere. *Trigger:* you need to compare lengths or distances in $g$ to lengths or distances in the chart. *Pattern:* the entire proof that $d_g$ induces the manifold topology uses this — distances in $g$ are pinched between scaled Euclidean distances.

8. **Restrict a metric to a submanifold.** The pullback $\iota^* g$ along an immersion $\iota : S \hookrightarrow M$ is a Riemannian metric on $S$, automatically — positive-definiteness is preserved under restriction to a subspace. *Trigger:* a submanifold appears; immediately equip it with the induced metric and you have a new Riemannian manifold to work with.

9. **Use an isometry to transport problems.** A Riemannian isometry $F : (M, g) \to (N, h)$ preserves lengths of curves, distances, gradients, the musical isomorphisms, geodesics, and curvature. *Trigger:* a problem on $(M, g)$ becomes trivial in some other model $(N, h)$ — for instance, computing a metric in a chart by writing it as the pullback of a known metric.

10. **Classify a Lorentzian vector by the sign of its norm.** On a Lorentzian manifold, $g_p(v, v) > 0$ is timelike, $< 0$ spacelike, $= 0$ (with $v \neq 0$) null. *Trigger:* any causal question — can $A$ influence $B$, is a worldline allowed, can a particle be at rest. The trichotomy is invariant under isometries and frame-independent.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating a semi-Riemannian metric as positive-definite
> The temptation is to carry over Riemannian intuition: "the metric is non-degenerate, so $g(v, v) = 0$ should force $v = 0$, and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality should hold." Neither is true for indefinite metrics. In Minkowski space the vector $v = (1, 1, 0, 0)$ has $\eta(v, v) = 1 - 1 = 0$ but $v \neq 0$ — a [[Def - Causal Classification of Tangent Vectors|null vector]]. Cauchy–Schwarz $|g(v, w)| \leq |v|\,|w|$ fails, and the ordinary triangle inequality is replaced by the *reverse* triangle inequality for timelike vectors (see [[Thm - The Reversed Triangle Inequality]] in the SR notes). The operation becomes legal exactly when the metric is positive-definite (or, more generally, when restricted to a subspace on which it is positive-definite).

> [!warning] 2. Assuming every smooth manifold admits a Lorentzian metric
> Every smooth manifold admits a Riemannian metric — this is the partition-of-unity argument — and the temptation is to assume the same for Lorentzian metrics. It fails: the partition-of-unity argument breaks because a convex combination of non-degenerate indefinite forms can be degenerate. The concrete obstruction is the existence of a nowhere-vanishing line field on $M$; for $S^2$ (and every even-dimensional sphere $S^{2k}$) no such line field exists, and $S^2$ admits no Lorentzian metric. To fix the temptation, check (or assume) the manifold has Euler characteristic zero (a necessary condition in the compact case).

> [!warning] 3. Confusing the gradient on a Riemannian manifold with the calculus gradient
> In a chart, the gradient of $f$ is *not* $(\partial_1 f, \ldots, \partial_n f)$ unless the metric is the Euclidean one. The correct formula is $\mathrm{grad}\, f = g^{ij}\partial_j f\, \partial_i$, with the inverse metric raising the index of $df$. In polar coordinates on $\mathbb{R}^2$ the Euclidean metric is $g = dr^2 + r^2 d\theta^2$, so $g^{ij} = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1/r^2)$ and $\mathrm{grad}\, f = \partial_r f\, \partial_r + (1/r^2)\partial_\theta f\, \partial_\theta$ — the second component has a factor of $1/r^2$ that is invisible if one naively raises by the identity. The operation is legal once you compute $g^{ij}$ explicitly and use it.

> [!warning] 4. Identifying $TM$ with $T^*M$ without using the metric
> Without a metric, $TM$ and $T^*M$ are genuinely different bundles — they are dual, but not naturally isomorphic. The musical isomorphism $\flat$ depends on $g$, and a different $g$ gives a different isomorphism. The temptation, common from working in coordinates where $\partial_i$ and $dx^i$ look "dual by name", is to identify them silently — this is correct for the Euclidean metric and only for it. On a curved Riemannian manifold the components of $X^\flat$ and $X$ differ by factors of $g_{ij}$, and the distinction is what raising and lowering indices *is*.

> [!warning] 5. Assuming the distance function is realised by a straight line
> The Riemannian distance $d_g(p, q)$ is an *infimum* over piecewise smooth curves, not a minimum: there may be no curve actually achieving it, and even when one exists, it is rarely a "straight line" in the chart sense. On the round sphere the minimising curves are great-circle arcs, not chart-Euclidean lines; on a punctured plane $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ with the Euclidean metric there are pairs of points whose infimum distance is not achieved by any curve. The fix: when computing distances, the right tool is the **geodesic equation** of the [[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Statement)|Levi-Civita connection]] (a forward concept), or in completed manifolds, the Hopf–Rinow theorem; or, in well-behaved special cases, a clever choice of comparison curve.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic are won at the moment you decide which of two routes a question is on: *build a metric* or *compute with a given metric*. The build-a-metric problems are settled by either pulling back from an ambient space (typically Euclidean) or by patching local pieces with a partition of unity. The compute-with-a-given-metric problems are settled by reading off $g_{ij}$ in coordinates and then routing through standard formulas (length, distance, musical isomorphism, gradient).

If the problem **asks to construct or exhibit a Riemannian metric**, the first move is to check whether the manifold is naturally embedded somewhere. If so, take the induced metric — this is how the round sphere, the flat torus, and every surface of revolution are best handled (operation 1, [[Def - Induced Metric on a Submanifold]]). If no natural embedding is available, use the partition-of-unity construction (operation 3, [[Thm - Existence of Riemannian Metrics via Partitions of Unity]]) and accept that the result is non-canonical. The decision is therefore "natural source available?" — if yes, pull back; if no, partition.

If the problem **asks for $g$ in explicit coordinates** for a parametrised surface or chart, the route is mechanical: substitute the parametrisation into the ambient metric and expand $d(\cdot)^2$ terms (operation 2). The structural insight is that for a parametrisation $X : U \to M \subseteq \mathbb{R}^N$, the induced metric matrix is $g_{ij}(u) = \langle \partial_i X, \partial_j X \rangle_{\bar g}$ — the Gram matrix of the coordinate tangent vectors in the ambient Euclidean inner product. This single formula handles the round metric, the hyperbolic metric, the metric of a surface of revolution, and almost every concrete example one ever needs.

If the problem **asks to compute a gradient, divergence, Laplacian, or to raise/lower indices**, the metric matrix and its inverse are needed; route through the [[Def - Musical Isomorphism (Flat and Sharp)|musical isomorphisms]] (operation 4). The trap is to forget that the gradient is $g^{ij}\partial_j f\, \partial_i$ rather than the coordinate gradient (illegal operation 3). In any non-Cartesian coordinate system the factors of $g^{ij}$ matter; in polar, spherical, or cylindrical coordinates these are nontrivial.

If the problem **asks for a property of the Riemannian distance** — that $d_g$ is a metric, that distances are positive, that the topology matches the manifold topology — the route is through the local-comparability lemma: on any compact set in a chart, $g$ is uniformly comparable to the Euclidean metric, so $g$-lengths are within constant factors of Euclidean lengths. This converts a manifold-distance question into a Euclidean-distance question plus a uniform-comparison step. The [[Thm - The Riemannian Distance Makes M a Metric Space|metric-space theorem]] proves $d_g(p, q) > 0$ for $p \neq q$ this way: a curve from $p$ to $q$ must cross out of any small Euclidean ball around $p$, and crossing has length at least a constant times the Euclidean radius.

If the problem **is Lorentzian**, the bookkeeping is causal: classify each vector as timelike, spacelike, or null (operation 10, [[Def - Causal Classification of Tangent Vectors]]), and remember the trichotomy is Lorentz-invariant. Whenever simultaneity, time-ordering, or "before/after" enters, the [[Special Relativity I — Postulates and Lorentz Transformations#Problem-Solving Strategy|relativistic bookkeeping]] of the SR notes applies — the present chapter generalises that bookkeeping from flat $\mathbb{R}^4$ to curved spacetimes, but the local structure is identical.

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle: **a Riemannian metric is a smooth field of inner products, and almost every operation reduces to a linear-algebra operation in a single tangent space**. The "smooth field" part is bookkeeping — partition of unity, smooth dependence, coordinate compatibility — and the "inner product" part is where the geometry lives. Every question in this chapter is some version of "what does this inner product compute, and how does it vary across $M$?"

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Existence of Riemannian Metrics via Partitions of Unity|Existence of Riemannian metrics]]**: every smooth manifold admits a Riemannian metric. **Typical use:** this is the licence to assume a metric is available even when one is not specified. Whenever a result requires "for some choice of Riemannian metric", the existence theorem makes the requirement free. The construction is also a template — the partition-of-unity-of-positive-definite-forms argument is reusable for any "convex" geometric structure (volume forms on oriented manifolds, fibre metrics on vector bundles, Hermitian metrics on complex vector bundles), and recognising the pattern is the first step in extending the result to new contexts.

- **[[Thm - The Riemannian Distance Makes M a Metric Space|The Riemannian distance is a metric, with topology equal to the manifold topology]]**: $(M, d_g)$ is a metric space, and its topology agrees with the original manifold topology. **Typical use:** this is the bridge that lets all of point-set topology run on a Riemannian manifold — completeness, Cauchy sequences, open balls, Heine–Borel, Arzelà–Ascoli — using the same language as in $\mathbb{R}^n$. The decisive move is the local comparability of $g$ with the Euclidean metric, which is reusable: any local geometric quantity that is continuous in coordinates is uniformly comparable to its Euclidean analogue on compact pieces.

- **[[Def - Musical Isomorphism (Flat and Sharp)|The musical isomorphism]]** $\flat : TM \to T^*M$: provides a canonical identification of vectors with covectors on a Riemannian manifold. **Typical use:** lets one define the gradient $\mathrm{grad}\, f = (df)^\sharp$, the divergence $\mathrm{div}\, X$, the Laplace–Beltrami operator $\Delta_g f = \mathrm{div}(\mathrm{grad}\, f)$. Every formula in vector calculus on a curved manifold passes through index-raising or index-lowering, and the musical isomorphism is the formal mechanism. The key reusable fact is *every component formula in coordinates is a $\flat$- or $\sharp$-version of a more invariant statement*, and once one recognises which, the coordinate formula is the mechanical consequence.

- **[[Def - Induced Metric on a Submanifold|The induced metric on a submanifold]]**: any submanifold of a Riemannian manifold is itself a Riemannian manifold. **Typical use:** every concrete Riemannian manifold one ever needs to compute with (sphere, torus, hyperboloid, surface of revolution, projective space) is constructed this way — pull back from an ambient Euclidean or hyperbolic space. The reusable pattern is "to put geometry on $S$, embed it somewhere and restrict". The same idea iterates: a submanifold of a submanifold is itself a submanifold, and the iterated induced metric is the same as the directly induced metric.

- **Sylvester's law of inertia for the signature** $(p, q)$: the signature of a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form is basis-independent. **Typical use:** any classification of indefinite metrics or, more broadly, of symmetric tensors of type $(0, 2)$. Reduces "what kind of metric is this?" to a finite invariant — for $n = 4$ the choices are $(4, 0)$ (Riemannian), $(0, 4)$ (negative-definite, equivalent), $(1, 3)$ and $(3, 1)$ (Lorentzian, equivalent), $(2, 2)$ (split signature, used in twistor theory), and that exhausts everything up to isomorphism. Recognise the applicability whenever signature must be checked or compared.

# Bridges

1. **Linear algebra — a Riemannian metric is an inner product per tangent space, varying smoothly.** Everything in this chapter is the linear algebra of [[Def - Inner Product Space|inner product spaces]] from [[Def - Inner Product Space|LA VI]] placed in every tangent space simultaneously and required to vary smoothly with the base point. The Gram–Schmidt process becomes the construction of [[Def - Orthonormal Basis|local orthonormal frames]]; the orthogonal complement becomes the normal bundle of a submanifold; the dual-space isomorphism induced by an inner product becomes the musical isomorphism. The linear algebra is unchanged from a single $T_pM$; what is new is the smoothness in $p$ and the resulting global structure. The signature classification — Sylvester's law of inertia — passes verbatim to the manifold case because non-degeneracy and signature are pointwise properties, and a continuous integer-valued function on a connected manifold is constant. So semi-Riemannian classification is literally the classification of indefinite symmetric forms on a single $T_pM$, lifted along the connected components of $M$.

2. **Special relativity — Minkowski space is the simplest Lorentzian manifold.** [[Special Relativity I — Postulates and Lorentz Transformations|Special relativity]] is the entire theory of $(\mathbb{R}^4, \eta)$ — a *flat* Lorentzian manifold, the case of §12.4 in which the metric is constant and the manifold is affine. The [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|Minkowski metric]] $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1)$ is the constant tensor field $\eta_{\mu\nu}(x) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ for all $x$, and the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]] $O(1, 3)$ is the isometry group of $(\mathbb{R}^4, \eta)$ fixing the origin. The causal classification of [[Def - Four-Vector|four-vectors]] into [[Def - Classification of Four-Vectors|timelike, spacelike, and null]] is exactly the causal classification of §12.4 applied at every point. From this perspective special relativity is "Riemannian geometry of an indefinite form on a flat vector space", and the move from SR to GR is the move from constant $\eta$ to variable $g(x)$ — the move from flat to curved within the same framework.

3. **General relativity — spacetime is a curved Lorentzian manifold.** The fundamental object of [[Def - Lorentzian Manifold|general relativity]] is a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold $(M, g)$ — a smooth manifold with a Lorentzian metric of signature $(1, 3)$. The metric $g$ is no longer the constant $\eta$ of special relativity but a varying tensor field, and its variation is the gravitational field. Free-falling particles travel along **timelike geodesics** of $g$ (massive particles) or **null geodesics** (light), and the Einstein field equations $R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac{1}{2}R\,g_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$ relate the Ricci curvature of the Levi-Civita connection to the energy–momentum tensor. The bridge is precisely Einstein's **equivalence principle**: at every event there is a *locally inertial frame* in which $g_{\mu\nu}$ reduces to $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ and its first derivatives vanish, so special relativity holds exactly at that event. Curvature is the obstruction to this reduction being global. Minkowski space is then the universal local model: every Lorentzian manifold looks like $\mathbb{M}$ in the tangent space at each point. This is the bridge that carries the indefinite linear algebra of §12.4 out into the field equations of gravitation.

4. **Topology — every smooth manifold is metrisable.** A formal consequence of the [[Thm - Existence of Riemannian Metrics via Partitions of Unity|existence of Riemannian metrics]] together with the [[Thm - The Riemannian Distance Makes M a Metric Space|metric-space theorem]] is that every smooth (Hausdorff, second-countable) manifold admits a distance function generating its topology — that is, every smooth manifold is metrisable as a topological space. Conversely, this gives a clean reason to take "metric space" topology and analysis seriously on a manifold: Cauchy sequences, completeness, total boundedness all make sense and tell you something. The bridge is that *the smooth structure carries metric structure as a free consequence* — once you commit to smoothness and to second countability, you cannot avoid being a metric space. The same is not true for Lorentzian manifolds: the indefinite metric does not give a distance function in the same way, and the relevant topology of "events" is the manifold topology, with $d_g$-type distances replaced by causal structure.

5. **Multivariate analysis — the induced metric on a parametrised surface in $\mathbb{R}^N$ is the first fundamental form.** In [[Multivariate Analysis I — Differentiation in Several Variables|multivariate analysis]], a parametrised surface $X : U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^N$ has its **first fundamental form** $I_{ij} = \langle \partial_i X, \partial_j X \rangle_{\bar g}$, which records arc-length, area, and angles on the surface as measured in the ambient Euclidean metric. This is *literally* the induced metric on the submanifold $X(U) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^N$, and the classical differential geometry of curves and surfaces (do Carmo) is the special case of Riemannian geometry where $M$ is a parametrised submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^3$. The Gauss equation, the Codazzi equation, the Theorema Egregium — these are all classical results expressed in the modern language of Riemannian metrics, induced from an ambient flat space.

6. **Lie groups — invariant metrics and bi-invariant structures.** For a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] $G$, one can construct a **left-invariant Riemannian metric** by choosing any inner product on $T_e G \cong \mathfrak{g}$ and translating it: $g_h(v, w) = \langle (dL_{h^{-1}})_h v, (dL_{h^{-1}})_h w \rangle$ for $h \in G$, $v, w \in T_h G$. The result is a Riemannian metric on $G$ invariant under all left translations. When the inner product is also $\mathrm{Ad}$-invariant (the case for compact groups, by averaging), the metric is **bi-invariant**, and the geometry simplifies dramatically: geodesics through the identity are one-parameter subgroups, the Lie-group exponential of [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|DG XI]] coincides with the Riemannian exponential map, and the sectional curvature has a closed form. This is the bridge between Lie theory and Riemannian geometry — compact Lie groups are particularly symmetric Riemannian manifolds, and homogeneous spaces $G/H$ inherit this symmetry.

7. **Differential forms — the Riemannian volume form integrates over Riemannian manifolds.** A Riemannian metric on an oriented manifold determines a canonical [[Def - Riemannian Volume Form|volume form]] $dV_g$ that locally equals $\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\,dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n$. This is the form one integrates a function against: $\int_M f\, dV_g$ is the manifold-intrinsic notion of "average value times volume", coinciding with the Lebesgue integral when $M = \mathbb{R}^n$ with the Euclidean metric. The bridge to [[Multivariate Analysis IV — Differential Forms and Stokes' Theorem|integration of forms on Euclidean space]] is that the Riemannian volume form is the case "I want the volume form induced from this particular inner product" — most often the Euclidean one, but on general Riemannian manifolds the metric is intrinsic and the volume form is forced upon you by the metric.

8. **Information geometry — statistical models as Riemannian manifolds.** A parametric family of probability distributions $\{p(x; \theta) : \theta \in \Theta\}$ on a sample space, with $\Theta$ an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^k$, carries a canonical Riemannian metric — the **Fisher information metric** $g_{ij}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\partial_i \log p \cdot \partial_j \log p]$. This is the metric for which the Cramér–Rao bound and the asymptotic distribution of the maximum likelihood estimator take their cleanest form. The bridge is that *families of distributions are Riemannian manifolds*, statistical inference is Riemannian geometry on $\Theta$, and the exponential families of statistics are dually flat Riemannian manifolds with both an "$e$-connection" and an "$m$-connection". This is the basis of **information geometry** — Amari's program — and connects the present chapter to applied probability and statistics.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a Riemannian metric is the data needed to do geometry on a manifold.** A smooth manifold by itself is a *calculus* object — you can differentiate, take Lie brackets, integrate forms — but it is not a *geometric* object: you cannot say how long a curve is or how far apart two points are. The Riemannian metric is exactly the data that promotes calculus to geometry. Once installed, lengths, angles, areas, volumes, gradients, divergences, Laplacians, and curvatures are all defined. The remarkable fact is that this data is universal: every smooth manifold admits a Riemannian metric. So in principle every smooth manifold is geometric — the only question is which metric to choose. The lesson is to view the Riemannian metric as an *additional structure* that the smooth structure does not determine but always admits, and to treat the entire subsequent development of Riemannian geometry as the unfolding of consequences of this single piece of additional data.

**The true name of the Riemannian metric is "smooth field of inner products on tangent spaces".** The formal definition — "smooth symmetric positive-definite $(0,2)$-tensor field" — is the right thing to compute with but the wrong thing to think. The operational picture, the one that explains why every formula looks the way it looks, is that at every point $p$ there is an honest inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_p$ on $T_pM$, and that this inner product depends smoothly on $p$. Then *every* construction of Riemannian geometry is a construction in linear algebra of inner product spaces, applied at each tangent space, glued together by the smooth dependence. The musical isomorphism is just the standard isomorphism between an inner product space and its dual, in every tangent space at once. The volume form is the standard one in every tangent space at once. Once one believes this picture, the constructions are no longer surprising — they are forced by what one already knows about a single inner product space.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: "want geometry on $S \subseteq M$" → "pull back the metric from $M$".** The single most-used construction in this chapter, and in all of differential geometry, is the induced metric on a submanifold. Whenever you see a manifold given as a subset of $\mathbb{R}^N$ — a sphere, a torus, a hyperboloid, a Lie group of matrices, a parametrised surface — the very first move is to give it the induced metric from the ambient Euclidean (or Minkowski, or other) structure. Every concrete example one ever computes with is constructed this way, and the abstract partition-of-unity existence theorem is only invoked when no such embedding is at hand. The reusable lesson: when geometry is needed but not given, *find a natural embedding and inherit*.

**Indefiniteness is not a degeneracy — it is the physics.** A mathematician meeting Lorentzian metrics for the first time can be tempted to view the loss of positive-definiteness as a defect — Cauchy–Schwarz fails, the triangle inequality reverses, "zero norm" does not mean "zero vector", the partition-of-unity argument breaks. None of this is a defect. The indefiniteness is exactly what produces the [[Def - Causal Classification of Tangent Vectors|causal trichotomy]] of timelike, spacelike, and null vectors, which is the geometric content of relativity. The light cone is real geometry, not a technical inconvenience. A positive-definite metric on $\mathbb{R}^4$ would give four-dimensional Euclidean space — no light cone, no causal structure, no time direction, nothing relativistic. The lesson: the difference between Riemannian and Lorentzian is not "easier" versus "harder" but "spatial geometry" versus "spacetime geometry", and each has its own logic. When working in the Lorentzian setting, never silently import a Riemannian intuition.

**Inheritance — where does the metric come from?** When confronted with a Riemannian manifold whose metric is given, ask: where does this metric come from? Is it the induced metric from an embedding? A pullback under a smooth map? A bi-invariant metric on a Lie group? A constant rescaling of the Euclidean metric? A symmetric quotient? The answer is almost always one of a small list of constructions, and recognising which it is gives you the entire structure of the manifold — its isometries, its geodesics, its curvature — for free. The metric on $S^n$ is induced from the embedding $S^n \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, which immediately tells you its isometry group is $O(n+1)$, its geodesics are great circles, and its curvature is constant. The metric on $\mathbb{H}^n$ is the upper-half-space metric $g = (dx_1^2 + \cdots + dx_n^2)/x_n^2$, induced via stereographic projection from one model of hyperbolic space to another; its isometries are Möbius transformations. The metric on a flat torus $\mathbb{R}^n / \mathbb{Z}^n$ is the quotient of the Euclidean metric, immediately flat. In every case, recognising the construction is the entire battle.
