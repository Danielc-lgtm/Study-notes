# Notation Registry

Throughout this topic, $M$, $N$, $P$ are smooth manifolds without boundary unless stated otherwise; $\dim M = m$ and $\dim N = n$. A smooth map between manifolds is denoted $F : M \to N$ or $\Phi : M \to N$. The full smooth-manifold notation registry lives on [[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases]]; the tangent-space machinery is set up on [[Differential Geometry III — Tangent Vectors and the Differential]].

- $F : M \to N$ — a smooth map between smooth manifolds.
- $dF_p : T_p M \to T_{F(p)} N$ — the **differential** of $F$ at $p \in M$, the linear map between tangent spaces; see [[Def - The Differential of a Smooth Map]].
- $\mathrm{rank}\, F$ at $p$, or $\mathrm{rank}\, dF_p$ — the **rank of $F$ at $p$**, the rank of $dF_p$ as a linear map; see [[Def - Rank of a Smooth Map]].
- $F$ is an **immersion** at $p$ if $dF_p$ is injective; a **submersion** if $dF_p$ is surjective; an **embedding** if it is an immersion and a topological embedding.
- $S \subseteq M$ — an **embedded submanifold** (subspace topology) or **immersed submanifold** (its own topology, only the inclusion need be a smooth immersion).
- $T_p S \subseteq T_p M$ — the tangent space of a submanifold at $p$, identified with a subspace of $T_p M$ via the differential of the inclusion.
- $\Phi^{-1}(c) = \{x \in M : \Phi(x) = c\}$ — the **level set** of $\Phi : M \to N$ at $c \in N$.
- A point $p$ is a **regular point** of $\Phi$ if $d\Phi_p$ is surjective, and a **critical point** otherwise. A value $c \in N$ is a **regular value** if every point of $\Phi^{-1}(c)$ is regular (vacuously so if $\Phi^{-1}(c) = \varnothing$), and a **critical value** otherwise.
- $\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{O}(n)$, $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ — the general linear, special linear, orthogonal, and special orthogonal groups; viewed as submanifolds of the matrix space $\mathrm{Mat}_n(\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$.
- $S^n = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1} : |x| = 1\}$ — the unit $n$-sphere.

**Standing convention.** Following [[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases|DG I]], all manifolds are Hausdorff, second-countable, and without boundary unless explicitly stated. "Submanifold" without qualification means a smooth embedded submanifold; when we mean the more general immersed kind we say so. "Smooth" means $C^\infty$.

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: **a smooth map's behaviour near a point is governed entirely by the rank of its differential there.**

This is the manifold-level upgrade of an analysis result we already know intimately. On $\mathbb{R}^n$ the [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]] says a smooth map is locally invertible exactly where its derivative is invertible, and the [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] says a system of equations can be locally solved exactly where its partial Jacobian is invertible. Together they convert local first-order linear information (a square block of the Jacobian) into local nonlinear structure (a [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]] or a graph). Everything in this topic is the same idea, transcribed to manifolds and pushed harder: when the differential $dF_p$ is *injective* you can locally embed; when it is *surjective* you can locally project; when its rank is *constant* you can locally do both and write $F$ as the world's simplest linear map.

The structural backbone of the chapter is a hierarchy of conditions on $dF_p$ that produce ever-stronger conclusions about $F$:

$$\text{constant rank} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{immersion or submersion} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{embedding (with topology)}$$

This is what makes the **[[Thm - The Rank Theorem|Rank Theorem]]** of §4.1 the central result of the chapter: it states that the *only* local information a constant-rank smooth map carries is its rank, because it can be put into the linear normal form $(x^1, \dots, x^m) \mapsto (x^1, \dots, x^r, 0, \dots, 0)$ by a smooth change of coordinates on both sides. Immersions are the $r = m$ case, submersions are the $r = n$ case, and constant rank in between is the constant-rank theorem.

The payoff of this normal-form theorem is the entire theory of **submanifolds** in §4.2. A submanifold of $M$ is what you get when a piece of $M$ "looks straight" in suitable coordinates — locally a slice $\{x^{k+1} = \dots = x^m = 0\}$. By the rank theorem this happens in two complementary ways: as the image of an embedding (the **parametric** picture: $S = F(N)$ for an embedding $F : N \to M$) and as a regular level set of a smooth map (the **implicit** picture: $S = \Phi^{-1}(c)$ for a submersion $\Phi$). The **[[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem|Regular Value Theorem]]** is the bridge between the two pictures: it says that whenever $c$ is a regular value of $\Phi$, the level set $\Phi^{-1}(c)$ is an embedded submanifold. This is the standard manufacturing device for submanifolds, and it is the manifold version of the analysis backbone — *the [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] gives local product structure; gluing these local pieces gives the manifold structure of the level set*.

The point of insisting on regularity at *every* point of the level set is structural rather than technical. Level sets at critical values can fail to be manifolds in spectacular ways — the cone $\{z^2 = x^2 + y^2\}$ has a vertex, $\{x^2 = y^2\}$ has a crossing, $\{x^2 - y^3 = 0\}$ has a cusp — and every one of these failures sits at a point where the defining map's derivative drops rank. So "regular value" is the manifold-side cousin of "transversality": it is the genericity hypothesis that excludes the pathologies.

§4.3 is the global half of the story. By itself the regular value theorem says nothing about whether regular values *exist*. **Sard's theorem** says they do, abundantly: the set of critical values of a smooth map has measure zero, so "almost every" value is regular. Combine this with the regular value theorem and you get a sweeping conclusion: a *generic* level set is automatically a submanifold. The same density-of-regular-values principle drives the **Whitney embedding theorem**, which says that every $n$-manifold can be smoothly embedded into $\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}$, justifying our habit of picturing manifolds as subsets of Euclidean space. The proof is to inject the manifold into some big $\mathbb{R}^N$ and then use Sard repeatedly to project down to a smaller and smaller Euclidean space until you hit $2n+1$.

The audience-assumption paragraph: the reader is assumed to be comfortable with smooth manifolds and smooth maps from [[Differential Geometry I — Smooth Manifolds and Atlases|DG I]] and [[Differential Geometry II — Smooth Maps and Partitions of Unity|DG II]], with the tangent space and the differential from [[Differential Geometry III — Tangent Vectors and the Differential|DG III]], and crucially with the [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]] and [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] for maps between open subsets of Euclidean space (refreshed in [[Multivariate Analysis II — Inverse and Implicit Function Theorems]]). Linear-algebra rank, kernel, and image (from [[Def - Rank of a Linear Map]] and [[Def - Null Space and Range]]) are used constantly. Familiarity with the [[Def - Submanifold of Euclidean Space|Euclidean submanifold]] theory is helpful but not required — this topic builds the analogous theory inside an abstract manifold.

# Concept Map

## §4.1 Maps of Constant Rank

- **[[Def - Rank of a Smooth Map]]**
	- The **rank** of a smooth map $F : M \to N$ at a point $p \in M$ is the rank of the linear map $dF_p : T_p M \to T_{F(p)} N$, equivalently the rank of the Jacobian matrix in any pair of local coordinates. It is bounded above by $\min(\dim M, \dim N)$ and is lower semicontinuous as a function of $p$: it can jump up in the limit but never down. A map has **constant rank** if the rank is the same at every point of $M$; in this case its local structure is rigid (see [[Thm - The Rank Theorem]]). Rank is an entirely local, linear-algebraic invariant — yet it controls the geometric type of $F$ near each point.

- **[[Def - Immersion, Submersion, and Embedding]]**
	- A smooth map $F : M \to N$ is an **immersion** at $p$ if $dF_p$ is injective ($\mathrm{rank}\, F = \dim M$, the maximum possible when $\dim M \leq \dim N$); a **submersion** if $dF_p$ is surjective ($\mathrm{rank}\, F = \dim N$); and a **smooth embedding** if it is an immersion that is also a [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphism]] onto its image in the [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] topology. Every [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]] is both an immersion and a submersion; the inclusion $S^n \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ is an embedding; the projection $\mathbb{R}^{n+1} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ is a submersion. The figure-eight curve (see [[Ex - The Figure-Eight Immersion]]) is an injective immersion that is *not* an embedding — the [[Def - Subspace|subspace]] topology disagrees with the domain's topology.

- **[[Thm - The Rank Theorem]]**
	- If $F : M \to N$ has **constant rank** $r$ in a neighbourhood of $p$, then there exist smooth coordinates centred at $p$ and at $F(p)$ in which $F$ has the linear coordinate representation $(x^1, \dots, x^m) \mapsto (x^1, \dots, x^r, 0, \dots, 0)$. So a constant-rank map *is* a linear projection in well-chosen coordinates — its only local invariant is the rank. The two special cases are the central tools of the topic: a submersion ($r = n$) looks locally like the coordinate projection $\mathbb{R}^m \to \mathbb{R}^n$, and an immersion ($r = m$) like the coordinate inclusion $\mathbb{R}^m \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$.

- **[[Thm - Local Immersion Theorem]]**
	- A smooth map is an immersion at $p$ if and only if there are smooth coordinates centred at $p$ and at $F(p)$ in which $F$ has the form $(x^1, \dots, x^m) \mapsto (x^1, \dots, x^m, 0, \dots, 0)$. This is the rank theorem specialised to maximal injective rank; the proof packages the [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]] for the "carrying along" auxiliary square map. Locally every immersion *is* the standard inclusion of $\mathbb{R}^m$ into $\mathbb{R}^n$ in suitable coordinates.

- **[[Thm - Local Submersion Theorem]]**
	- A smooth map is a submersion at $p$ if and only if there are smooth coordinates centred at $p$ and at $F(p)$ in which $F$ has the form $(x^1, \dots, x^n, x^{n+1}, \dots, x^m) \mapsto (x^1, \dots, x^n)$. This is the rank theorem specialised to maximal surjective rank, and it is the manifold version of the [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] — its content is "submersions admit local sections". Every submersion has a smooth local section through every point, and this is the property that drives all the structural results about quotient manifolds.

- **[[Thm - Submersions are Open Maps]]**
	- Every smooth submersion $F : M \to N$ is an open map: it sends open subsets of $M$ to open subsets of $N$. The proof is one line from the local submersion theorem — in coordinates $F$ is a coordinate projection, and projections are open. A surjective smooth submersion is therefore a quotient map; this is why submersions play, in the smooth category, the role quotient maps play in the topological category. The result fails for immersions: an injective immersion need not be open onto its image (the figure-eight is closed but not open in $\mathbb{R}^2$, and even an injective immersion into a higher-dimensional space cannot be open by [[Def - Dimension|dimension]]).

- **[[Ex - The Hopf Map is a Submersion]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Verify that the Hopf map $h : S^3 \to S^2$ defined via $\mathbb{C}^2 \to \mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2$ is a smooth submersion. Compute the rank of its differential and identify the fibres as great circles in $S^3$. This is the canonical nontrivial fibre bundle and a workhorse example in topology, gauge theory, and quantum information.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Transversality *(from Differential Topology)*
> Two smooth maps $F : M \to P$ and $G : N \to P$ are **transverse** at a common image point if their images of tangent spaces *together* span the tangent space of the target. Transversality is the natural several-map generalisation of "regular value" — it says the maps are independent to first order at the intersection — and it is the foundation of intersection theory, degree theory, and the proof that "most" maps are transverse (a Sard-style genericity result).

> [!tip] Unlocked: Smooth Quotient Manifold Theorem *(from Differential Geometry / Lie Theory)*
> A free, proper smooth action of a Lie group $G$ on a manifold $M$ produces a smooth manifold structure on the orbit space $M/G$ for which the quotient map $\pi : M \to M/G$ is a smooth submersion. This is the manifold-level upgrade of the topological quotient construction, and it is how **homogeneous spaces** $G/H$ are made into manifolds. The driving fact is precisely that submersions are quotient maps.

## §4.2 Submanifolds and the Regular Value Theorem

- **[[Def - Embedded Submanifold]]**
	- A subset $S \subseteq M$ is an **embedded submanifold** if it is a smooth manifold in its own right, equipped with the subspace topology, such that the inclusion $\iota : S \hookrightarrow M$ is a smooth embedding. Equivalently, $S$ satisfies the **local $k$-slice condition**: at every point $p \in S$ there is a smooth chart $(U, \varphi)$ of $M$ with $\varphi(U \cap S) = \varphi(U) \cap (\mathbb{R}^k \times \{0\})$, a flat slice of the coordinate cube. The smooth structure making $S$ an embedded submanifold is *unique*. The local-slice characterisation is what makes the implicit-function arguments work: at every point of $S$ one has a coordinate chart in which $S$ is straight.

- **[[Def - Immersed Submanifold]]**
	- An **immersed submanifold** of $M$ is a subset $S \subseteq M$ equipped with its *own* topology and smooth structure (not necessarily the subspace topology) such that the inclusion $\iota : S \hookrightarrow M$ is a smooth immersion (injective on tangent spaces). Every embedded submanifold is immersed; the converse fails — the figure-eight curve and the dense line on the torus are immersed but not embedded, because their domain topology disagrees with the subspace topology. Immersed submanifolds are exactly the images of injective smooth immersions, with the topology pulled back from the immersing map.

- **[[Def - Regular and Critical Points]]**
	- For a smooth map $\Phi : M \to N$, a point $p \in M$ is a **regular point** if $d\Phi_p$ is surjective and a **critical point** otherwise. A value $c \in N$ is a **regular value** if *every* point of $\Phi^{-1}(c)$ is regular — vacuously so when the preimage is empty — and a **critical value** otherwise. The set of regular points is open in $M$ (by lower semicontinuity of the rank); the set of regular *values* is open in $N$ when $M$ is compact, and is generic in any case by [[Thm - Sard's Theorem|Sard's theorem]]. Regular values are exactly the values at which the level set has the cleanest possible structure.

- **[[Thm - Regular Value Theorem on Manifolds]]**
	- Let $\Phi : M \to N$ be a smooth map and $c \in N$ a regular value. Then $\Phi^{-1}(c)$, if nonempty, is a properly embedded submanifold of $M$ of codimension $\dim N$, with tangent space $T_p \Phi^{-1}(c) = \ker d\Phi_p$ at every point. This is the manifold-level analogue of [[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem]] for Euclidean space, and the reasoning is identical: the [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] gives local graph structure at each point; assembling the local pieces produces the submanifold. It is the standard manufacturing device — apply it to $\Phi(x) = |x|^2 - 1$ and the sphere falls out; apply it to $\Phi(A) = A^T A - I$ and the orthogonal group falls out.

- **[[Def - Tangent Space of a Submanifold]]**
	- For an immersed or embedded submanifold $S \subseteq M$, the **tangent space** $T_p S$ at $p \in S$ is identified with the image of the differential $d\iota_p : T_p S \hookrightarrow T_p M$ of the inclusion. Equivalently, $T_p S$ is the set of velocities $\gamma'(0)$ of smooth curves $\gamma$ that lie in $S$ and pass through $p$ at time $0$. When $S = \Phi^{-1}(c)$ is a regular level set, $T_p S = \ker d\Phi_p$ — the directions in which the defining map does not change to first order. When $S = F(N)$ is the image of an embedding, $T_p S = \mathrm{im}\, dF_q$ where $F(q) = p$.

- **[[Ex - The Sphere as a Level Set]]** (⭐)
	- Show that the unit sphere $S^n \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ is an embedded $n$-dimensional submanifold by applying the regular value theorem to $f(x) = |x|^2$. Identify the tangent space $T_p S^n$ as $\{v \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1} : v \cdot p = 0\}$.

- **[[Ex - The Special Linear Group is a Submanifold of GL(n)|Ex - The Special Linear Group is a Submanifold of GL(n)]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that $\mathrm{SL}(n, \mathbb{R}) = \{A \in \mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R}) : \det A = 1\}$ is an embedded submanifold of $\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ of [[Def - Dimension|dimension]] $n^2 - 1$ by applying the regular value theorem to $\det$. Identify the tangent space at the identity as $\mathfrak{sl}(n) = \{X \in \mathrm{Mat}_n(\mathbb{R}) : \mathrm{tr}\, X = 0\}$ — the trace-zero matrices, which is the **Lie algebra** of $\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})$.

- **[[Ex - The Orthogonal Group as a Regular Level Set]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Show that $\mathrm{O}(n) = \{A : A^T A = I\}$ is an embedded submanifold of $\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$ of dimension $n(n-1)/2$ by applying the regular value theorem to $\Phi(A) = A^T A$ valued in the **symmetric** matrices. The choice of codomain is the non-obvious step: $\Phi(A)$ is automatically symmetric, so demanding surjectivity onto symmetric matrices (not all matrices) is what makes $I$ a regular value. The tangent space at the identity is $\mathfrak{o}(n) = \{X : X + X^T = 0\}$, the antisymmetric matrices.

- **[[Ex - An Injective Immersion is Not Always an Embedding]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Exhibit an injective smooth immersion $F : N \to M$ that is not an embedding because its image, with the subspace topology, is not homeomorphic to $N$. Use either the figure-eight parametrisation $\beta(t) = (\sin 2t, \sin t)$ on $(-\pi, \pi) \to \mathbb{R}^2$, or the irrational line on the torus. The figure-eight is compact-image but non-compact domain; the irrational line is dense in the torus.

- **[[Ex - The Figure-Eight Immersion]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Analyse the figure-eight $\beta(t) = (\sin 2t, \sin t)$ on $(-\pi, \pi)$: prove $\beta$ is an injective smooth immersion, show its image is *not* an embedded submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^2$, and exhibit the topology on the image that makes it an *immersed* submanifold diffeomorphic to $(-\pi, \pi) \cong \mathbb{R}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Matrix [[Def - Lie Group|Lie Groups]] as [[Def - Embedded Submanifold|Embedded Submanifolds]] *(from [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|Lie Theory]])*
> The exercises in this section are the foundation of **matrix Lie [[Def - Group|groups]]**: $\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{O}(n)$, $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, $\mathrm{U}(n)$, $\mathrm{SU}(n)$, $\mathrm{Sp}(2n,\mathbb{R})$ are all regular level sets of polynomial maps on $\mathrm{Mat}_n$, hence embedded submanifolds, hence smooth manifolds — and being also closed under matrix multiplication, they are **Lie [[Def - Group|groups]]**. The tangent space at the identity is the corresponding **Lie algebra** ($\mathfrak{sl}(n)$, $\mathfrak{o}(n)$, etc.), and its computation in these exercises is a direct preview of the general construction in [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|DG XI]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Cotangent Bundle and Phase Space *(from Hamiltonian Mechanics)*
> Once the tangent space of a submanifold is identified, the cotangent space $(T_p S)^*$ is the natural home for momenta on a constrained system, and the cotangent bundle $T^*S$ is the **phase space** of mechanics on the constraint surface. Constrained Hamiltonian systems live exactly on such cotangent bundles of regular level sets.

## §4.3 Sard's Theorem and Whitney Embedding

- **[[Def - Set of Measure Zero on a Manifold]]**
	- A subset $A \subseteq M$ of a smooth $n$-manifold has **measure zero** if for every smooth chart $(U, \varphi)$ of $M$, the Euclidean set $\varphi(A \cap U) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ has Lebesgue measure zero. The notion is *diffeomorphism-invariant*: smooth maps between manifolds of the same dimension send measure-zero sets to measure-zero sets, because a smooth map is locally Lipschitz on compact sets. Countable unions of measure-zero sets have measure zero; the complement of a measure-zero set is dense. Manifolds carry no canonical volume, so "measure zero" is the largest piece of measure theory that survives without a metric.

- **[[Thm - Sard's Theorem]]**
	- For any smooth map $F : M \to N$ between smooth manifolds, the set of **critical values** of $F$ has measure zero in $N$. Equivalently, almost every value is a regular value. The theorem is what makes the regular value theorem usable — it guarantees an abundant supply of regular values, so a generic level set is automatically an embedded submanifold. The proof is technical (induction on $\dim M$ plus a Lipschitz-bounded packing argument), but the *consequences* — Whitney embedding, mapping degree, Morse theory, transversality theorems — are everywhere.

- **[[Thm - Whitney Embedding Theorem]]**
	- Every smooth $n$-manifold admits a proper smooth embedding into $\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}$ (the **weak** Whitney theorem). For compact manifolds the proof reduces, via partitions of unity and bump functions, to assembling local coordinate maps into a single Euclidean embedding; for non-compact manifolds an exhaustion function controls the embedding at infinity. Once an embedding into *some* $\mathbb{R}^N$ exists, Sard's theorem is applied repeatedly to project down to $\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}$ — at each stage the directions of $\mathbb{R}^N$ that fail to preserve injectivity or immersion form a set of measure zero, so a "good" projection direction exists. The **strong** Whitney theorem improves the dimension to $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ for $n \geq 1$ using deeper algebraic-topological methods.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Morse Theory *(from Differential Topology)*
> A smooth function $f : M \to \mathbb{R}$ whose critical points are all *non-degenerate* (the Hessian at each critical point is non-singular) is a **Morse function**; by [[Thm - Sard's Theorem|Sard's theorem]] Morse functions are generic. **Morse theory** reconstructs the topology of $M$ from the Morse function's critical-point data — the number of critical points of each Morse index bounds the Betti numbers, and watching level sets cross critical values is how cobordism and surgery are organised.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Mapping Degree *(from Algebraic Topology)*
> For a smooth map $f : M \to N$ between *compact oriented* manifolds of the same dimension, the **mapping degree** $\deg(f)$ is the signed count of preimages of a regular value of $f$. Sard's theorem guarantees regular values exist; the signed count is independent of the regular value chosen, so degree is a smooth-[[Def - Homotopy|homotopy]] invariant. The fundamental theorem of algebra, the hairy ball theorem, and the Brouwer fixed-point theorem all fall out of degree theory.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Transversality Theorems *(from Differential Topology)*
> The same density-of-regular-values argument behind Sard upgrades to the **transversality density theorem**: in any reasonable space of smooth maps $M \to N$, the maps transverse to a given submanifold $S \subseteq N$ form an open dense set. This is the formal version of "perturb to general position", and it is the workhorse behind intersection theory, the Pontryagin–Thom construction, and Smale's $h$-cobordism.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of this topic fall into a small handful of recurring goals. By far the most common is **proving that a given subset is an embedded submanifold of a known manifold** — typically a sphere, an orthogonal group, an energy surface, a graph, an intersection. The route is almost always the regular value theorem: write the subset as $\Phi^{-1}(c)$ for some smooth map $\Phi$, check that $c$ is a regular value by computing $d\Phi_p$ at every point of the preimage, read off the dimension as $\dim M - \dim N$, and compute the tangent space as $\ker d\Phi_p$. A second target is **computing the tangent space at a specific point**, either as $\ker d\Phi_p$ (implicit picture), $\mathrm{im}\, dF_q$ (parametric picture), or as the velocity-vectors-of-curves-in-$S$ picture. The implicit picture is the most computationally efficient; the parametric picture is the one that recovers a basis; the curves picture is the one most useful for matrix groups, because matrix-valued curves are easy to write down.

A third recurring target is **distinguishing embedded from merely immersed submanifolds** — showing that an injective immersion is or is not an embedding, by checking whether the subspace topology coincides with the domain topology. This is where the figure-eight, the dense line on the torus, and Lie subgroup examples live. A fourth is **establishing the local normal form** of a constant-rank map — putting a given map into the standard projection-inclusion shape, usually as a step in a larger calculation. A fifth is **using Sard's theorem to assert genericity** — to claim that a perturbed map satisfies some condition (transversality, immersion, embedding) for almost every choice of parameter, even when no specific good choice is constructed.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The first source is **the rank of $dF$ at the points of interest**, which is universally the thing one computes. Once the rank is known one knows everything: a rank equal to $\dim M$ at a point means immersion there; a rank equal to $\dim N$ means submersion there; constant rank in a neighbourhood unlocks the rank theorem. The second source is **the level-set presentation** of a subset: $S = \Phi^{-1}(c)$ for a known smooth $\Phi$, after which the regular value theorem reduces the problem to computing $d\Phi_p$ at every $p \in S$. The third source is **an explicit parametrisation** $F : N \to M$ of the subset; if $F$ is an injective immersion then the image is at least an immersed submanifold (and often embedded if $N$ is compact). The fourth source is **compactness of the domain**: any injective immersion of a compact manifold is automatically an embedding ([[Def - Immersion, Submersion, and Embedding|Lee's Proposition 4.22]]), and compact submanifolds are automatically *properly* embedded. The fifth source is **a Lie-group structure on the candidate submanifold** — matrix multiplication on $\mathrm{O}(n)$, $\mathrm{SL}(n)$, etc. — which combines with the manifold structure to give a Lie group and produces the tangent-space-at-identity = Lie algebra dictionary.

The routes between them are stereotyped. *A level-set presentation routes through the regular value theorem to "$S$ is an embedded submanifold and $T_p S = \ker d\Phi_p$."* *A parametric presentation routes through the local immersion theorem to "$F$ is a local embedding at each point", and through compactness to "globally an embedding."* *Constant rank routes through the rank theorem to a coordinate normal form, and through that to whatever local question is being asked.* *Generic-existence questions route through Sard's theorem to "almost every value works".*

# Legal Operations

Each operation below is the move you reach for at a specific recognition signal. The list is self-contained: a reader with no differential-geometry background should be able to follow each item from the description and apply it on first encounter.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Compute the differential $dF_p$ in local coordinates.** Pick smooth charts $(U, \varphi)$ around $p$ and $(V, \psi)$ around $F(p)$; the coordinate representation $\hat F = \psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}$ is a smooth map between open subsets of Euclidean space, and $dF_p$ corresponds to the Jacobian matrix $D\hat F(\varphi(p))$ — see [[Def - The Differential of a Smooth Map]] and [[Def - Partial Derivatives and the Jacobian Matrix]]. This is the first thing to do whenever the rank of $F$ at $p$ is in question. *Trigger:* the problem asks about immersion/submersion/embedding/rank. *Pattern:* "in the chart $(U,\varphi)$, the Jacobian at $p$ is the matrix $[\partial \hat F^i / \partial x^j]$..."

2. **Apply the regular value theorem to a candidate defining map.** Whenever a subset is given by an equation $\Phi(x) = c$, write down the map $\Phi : M \to N$ explicitly, compute $d\Phi_p$ at every $p \in \Phi^{-1}(c)$, and check it is surjective there. If it is, [[Thm - Regular Value Theorem on Manifolds]] hands you the submanifold structure and the tangent space $T_p \Phi^{-1}(c) = \ker d\Phi_p$ for free. *Trigger:* the candidate submanifold is given by an equation. *Pattern:* "consider $\Phi(x) = \ldots$, check $d\Phi_p$ is surjective on $\Phi^{-1}(c)$, conclude codimension equals $\dim N$ and tangent space is the kernel."

3. **Choose the codomain to match the actual image.** When the natural defining map automatically lands in a lower-dimensional subspace (for instance $\Phi(A) = A^T A$ is automatically *symmetric*), the rank condition is checked into that smaller codomain rather than the full ambient space. Surjectivity onto the right codomain may hold even when surjectivity onto the wrong one fails. *Trigger:* the apparent codomain is "too big". *Pattern:* "$\Phi$ lands in $\mathrm{Sym}_n$, so check surjectivity $T_A \mathrm{GL}(n) \to T_I \mathrm{Sym}_n$, not $\to T_I \mathrm{Mat}_n$."

4. **Apply the rank theorem to put the map in normal form.** When the map's rank is constant in a neighbourhood (and *all* maps automatically have locally constant rank on the open set where rank achieves its maximum), one may pick smooth coordinates in which $F$ is the linear projection-inclusion $(x^1, \dots, x^m) \mapsto (x^1, \dots, x^r, 0, \dots, 0)$. This is the single sharpest computational tool for any local statement about $F$. *Trigger:* the map has constant rank and you need a local-structure conclusion. *Pattern:* "by the [[Thm - The Rank Theorem|rank theorem]] choose coordinates in which $F$ has the form $\ldots$, then $\ldots$."

5. **Construct a local section to test for or exploit submersion.** A submersion has a smooth section through every point of its image — the inverse of a coordinate projection in normal-form coordinates. Conversely, the existence of a local section at $p$ forces $dF_p$ to be surjective. Use sections to push problems "downward" through a submersion. *Trigger:* you need to lift a path/function from $N$ to $M$ across a submersion. *Pattern:* "let $\sigma : V \to M$ be a smooth section of $F$ with $\sigma(F(p)) = p$, then $\ldots$."

6. **Recognise compact-domain injective immersions as embeddings.** When the domain $N$ of an injective smooth immersion $F : N \to M$ is compact, $F$ is automatically a smooth embedding — its image is closed in $M$ (closed image of a compact set in a Hausdorff space) and the subspace topology agrees with the domain topology (a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphism]]). *Trigger:* you have an injective immersion of a compact manifold. *Pattern:* "$N$ is compact and $F$ is an injective immersion, so $F$ is a smooth embedding."

7. **Use Sard's theorem to assert a generic value works.** Whenever you need *some* regular value, or *some* value avoiding a critical set of measure zero, [[Thm - Sard's Theorem]] tells you almost every value works — even if you cannot exhibit one. The complement of a measure-zero set is dense, so you may "wiggle the value" to land in the regular set. *Trigger:* the problem reduces to "for some $c$" rather than "for all $c$", and you need to exhibit existence. *Pattern:* "by Sard, the critical values of $\Phi$ have measure zero, so there exists $c$ arbitrarily close to $c_0$ which is regular; perturb to this $c$."

8. **Identify tangent vectors as velocities of curves in $S$.** For an immersed or embedded submanifold $S \subseteq M$ and a point $p \in S$, the tangent space $T_p S$ is the set of velocities $\gamma'(0)$ of smooth curves $\gamma$ that lie *entirely in $S$* with $\gamma(0) = p$ (and are smooth as maps into $S$). This is the most useful tangent-space characterisation for matrix groups: write down a smooth one-parameter family of group elements through $I$ and differentiate to get a tangent vector in the Lie algebra. *Trigger:* asked to compute the tangent space to a Lie group at the identity, or any tangent space that admits a curve-based parametrisation. *Pattern:* "let $\gamma(t)$ be a smooth curve in $S$ with $\gamma(0) = p$; then $\gamma'(0) \in T_p S$, and $\ldots$."

9. **Pull back the implicit function theorem to a chart.** Every theorem in this topic is, ultimately, the [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] applied in coordinates. When stuck on a constant-rank or level-set argument on a manifold, go back to local coordinates, where the question becomes a question about smooth maps between open subsets of Euclidean space — and there the implicit/inverse function theorems are directly applicable. *Trigger:* you need a structural conclusion (local diffeomorphism, local graph, local product) at a point on a manifold. *Pattern:* "in a chart around $p$, the question is about a Euclidean-space map; apply the [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] there and transport the conclusion back to $M$."

10. **Use lower semicontinuity of rank.** The rank of $dF$ as a function on $M$ is lower semicontinuous: if $\mathrm{rank}\, dF_{p_0} = r$, then $\mathrm{rank}\, dF_p \geq r$ for $p$ near $p_0$. So the set $\{p : \mathrm{rank}\, dF_p \geq r\}$ is open. The set of maximal-rank points (immersion or submersion points) is therefore open. *Trigger:* you have rank information at a single point and want to extend it to a neighbourhood. *Pattern:* "rank is lower semicontinuous, so the set of submersion points contains an open neighbourhood of $p$."

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Concluding that an injective immersion is an embedding
> It is tempting to think injectivity plus immersion forces embedding, but this is false. The figure-eight curve $\beta : (-\pi, \pi) \to \mathbb{R}^2$, $\beta(t) = (\sin 2t, \sin t)$, is an injective smooth immersion whose image is *not* homeomorphic to $(-\pi, \pi)$ in the subspace topology — sequences $t_n \to \pm\pi$ converge in $\mathbb{R}^2$ to the crossing point at the origin, but they do not converge in $(-\pi, \pi)$. The dense line on the torus shows the same failure non-compactly. The operation becomes legal exactly when the *domain* is compact (then the map is automatically a topological embedding by the closed map lemma) — see [[Ex - The Figure-Eight Immersion]] for the canonical counterexample.

> [!warning] 2. Treating a critical level set as a submanifold
> The regular value theorem requires *every* point of the preimage to be a regular point of $\Phi$. It is tempting to apply it to a value $c$ where rank drops at some points, hoping the manifold structure still works. It does not, and the failures are dramatic: the cone $\{z^2 = x^2 + y^2\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$ is a critical level set of $\Phi(x,y,z) = z^2 - x^2 - y^2$, with $\nabla\Phi$ vanishing at the origin — and the cone has a singular vertex at exactly that point, with two tangent cones rather than one tangent plane. The operation becomes legal at a single value $c$ only when *all* points of $\Phi^{-1}(c)$ are regular; if just one is critical, you may at most apply it to the open subset of regular points.

> [!warning] 3. Choosing the wrong codomain for the defining map
> The naive defining map for $\mathrm{O}(n)$ inside $\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ is $\Phi(A) = A^T A$, viewed as $\mathrm{Mat}_n \to \mathrm{Mat}_n$ — but its differential at $I$ has rank $n(n+1)/2$, not $n^2$, so $I$ is *not* a regular value in this formulation. It is tempting to conclude $\mathrm{O}(n)$ is not a regular level set. The repair is to notice that $\Phi(A)$ is always symmetric, so $\Phi$ should be viewed as $\mathrm{Mat}_n \to \mathrm{Sym}_n$ where $\dim \mathrm{Sym}_n = n(n+1)/2$ — and then $d\Phi_I$ *is* surjective, $I$ *is* regular, and the dimension count $n^2 - n(n+1)/2 = n(n-1)/2$ is correct. The operation becomes legal when the codomain is chosen to match the actual image.

> [!warning] 4. Forgetting the topology when defining "submanifold"
> A subset $S \subseteq M$ does not have a canonical topology in general; an immersed submanifold structure is a *choice* of topology on $S$ along with a smooth structure. The figure-eight image set has a unique embedded-submanifold structure if and only if it has one at all, but it has *multiple* immersed-submanifold structures (one for each parametrisation; they need not agree). It is tempting to ask "is $S$ a submanifold of $M$" without specifying which kind, leading to ambiguity. The operation becomes precise when one says "embedded submanifold" (then the structure if it exists is unique) or "immersed submanifold *with a given topology*".

> [!warning] 5. Using "almost every value is regular" to conclude a specific value is regular
> Sard's theorem says the set of critical values has measure zero, hence the set of regular values is dense. It does *not* say a specifically named value (the origin, the value at a named point, an algebraically natural value) is regular — a measure-zero set can contain any specific point. The standard trap: "Sard says $0$ is a regular value of $\Phi(x) = |x|^4 - |x|^2$" — false, because $0$ is a critical value (the origin is a critical point). The operation becomes legal exactly when you do not commit to which value is regular: it gives existence of regular values in any neighbourhood, but not at a chosen one.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic come in five recognisable shapes, and identifying the shape is half the battle.

**If the problem asks you to show a set is a submanifold.** This is the most common type. The default strategy is the regular value theorem. Write the candidate set as $\Phi^{-1}(c)$ for an explicit smooth map $\Phi$; compute the differential $d\Phi_p$ at an arbitrary point $p$ of the candidate set (not just at one specific point — *every* point of the preimage must be regular); verify $d\Phi_p$ is surjective; conclude codimension $\dim N$ and tangent space $\ker d\Phi_p$. The two non-obvious decisions are choosing the *codomain* of $\Phi$ — make it the smallest space the image actually lies in, so that surjectivity is easier — and choosing the *defining equation* — sometimes a single scalar equation, sometimes a matrix equation, sometimes a multi-component one. For sphere-like examples, $\Phi(x) = |x|^2$ works because $\nabla\Phi = 2x \neq 0$ on the sphere; for matrix groups, the defining equation comes from the group's defining property (preserving an inner product, having determinant $1$, etc.) and the codomain is matrices of the appropriate symmetry type.

**If the problem asks you to compute a tangent space.** The three tools are $T_p S = \ker d\Phi_p$ for level-set descriptions (the most efficient — compute the kernel of the Jacobian), $T_p S = \mathrm{im}\, dF_q$ for parametric descriptions (the most explicit — write down a basis directly), and $T_p S = \{\gamma'(0) : \gamma \in S, \gamma(0) = p\}$ for everything else (the most flexible — write down a curve through $p$ and differentiate). For matrix Lie groups the curve approach is gold-standard: a curve $A(t)$ in $\mathrm{O}(n)$ with $A(0) = I$ satisfies $A(t)^T A(t) = I$, so $A'(0)^T + A'(0) = 0$, so $A'(0)$ is antisymmetric — and a dimension count shows every antisymmetric matrix is some $A'(0)$, so $T_I \mathrm{O}(n) = \mathfrak{o}(n)$, the antisymmetric matrices.

**If the problem asks you to put a constant-rank map in normal form.** This is the [[Thm - The Rank Theorem|rank theorem]]'s direct use case. The strategy is to verify the rank is constant (lower semicontinuity gives the open direction; the hypothesis must give the closed direction), then to apply the theorem to get smooth coordinates in which $F(x^1, \dots, x^m) = (x^1, \dots, x^r, 0, \dots, 0)$, then to read off whatever local fact is wanted. Use the immersion case ($r = m$) or submersion case ($r = n$) when applicable; they have cleaner statements ([[Thm - Local Immersion Theorem]], [[Thm - Local Submersion Theorem]]). Note that the *proof* of the rank theorem is the auxiliary-square-map device from the implicit function theorem; if asked to redo the proof, the strategy is to construct that auxiliary map and apply the inverse function theorem.

**If the problem asks you to distinguish embedded from immersed.** The technique is to compare two topologies: the topology of the domain (via the immersion) and the subspace topology on the image (from the ambient manifold). The map is an embedding iff these agree, iff the inclusion of the image (with subspace topology) admits a continuous inverse to the domain. For the figure-eight, the two topologies disagree at the crossing: a sequence $t_n \to \pi$ in the domain has $\beta(t_n) \to (0,0)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$, but $\pi$ is not in the domain, so the subspace-topology limit is realised by a non-convergent domain sequence. Standard repair tools: compact domain forces embedding ([[Def - Immersion, Submersion, and Embedding|Lee's Prop 4.22]]), and properness of the immersion forces embedding.

**If the problem asks for genericity or existence "for some regular value".** This is Sard's territory. The strategy is to identify the relevant smooth map $\Phi$, observe that the set of critical values has measure zero, conclude the set of regular values is dense, and pick a regular value satisfying whatever additional condition the problem requires (since the regular values are dense, this can be done for any open condition). Used this way, Sard's theorem is a *non-constructive existence tool*: it gives existence without constructing.

The meta-strategy threading through all of these: **every problem in this topic ultimately reduces, in suitable local coordinates, to the [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]] or [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem|implicit function theorem]] on Euclidean space.** The manifold setting just packages those Euclidean theorems intrinsically. When stuck, choose a chart and rewrite the question downstairs: there it becomes a problem about Jacobians of smooth maps between open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$, and the analytical machinery is all available.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - The Rank Theorem|The Rank Theorem]]**: a constant-rank smooth map has the linear normal form $(x^1, \dots, x^m) \mapsto (x^1, \dots, x^r, 0, \dots, 0)$ in suitable coordinates. **Typical use:** as the universal local-structure theorem behind every other result in this topic. Whenever a map has constant rank in a neighbourhood — and *every* maximal-rank point has constant rank in some neighbourhood, by lower semicontinuity — this theorem produces coordinates in which the map's local geometry is completely transparent. Its specialisations are the [[Thm - Local Immersion Theorem|local immersion theorem]] and [[Thm - Local Submersion Theorem|local submersion theorem]].

- **[[Thm - Regular Value Theorem on Manifolds|Regular Value Theorem]]**: a regular level set is an embedded submanifold of codimension $\dim N$, with tangent space $\ker d\Phi_p$. **Typical use:** the standard manufacturing device for embedded submanifolds. Recognise its applicability whenever a candidate submanifold is described by an equation. The combination "compute the differential, check regularity, read off dimension and tangent space" is the workhorse computation of the chapter, and it routes through this theorem every time. It is also the gateway to matrix Lie group theory: $\mathrm{O}(n)$, $\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{U}(n)$, $\mathrm{SU}(n)$, $\mathrm{Sp}(2n)$ are all regular level sets.

- **[[Thm - Sard's Theorem|Sard's Theorem]]**: critical values have measure zero. **Typical use:** as the genericity tool that converts "there exists *some* regular value" from a *desideratum* into a *theorem*. Whenever a construction requires picking a value avoiding a critical set, Sard says you can. This is what gives the Whitney embedding theorem its proof (almost every projection direction works), what justifies transversality arguments (almost every map is transverse), and what makes Morse theory generic (almost every smooth function is Morse).

- **The local-to-global submanifold principle**: being a submanifold is a local property, so a global structure is established by checking it at every point separately. **Typical use:** the implicit function theorem gives local graph structure at each point of a regular level set; the *global* manifold structure assembles automatically from the local pieces because the slice charts at different points agree on overlaps (the smooth structure is unique). This is the engine of the proof of the regular value theorem: do the work locally, then assemble. It generalises far beyond submanifolds — partitions of unity, sheaf-theoretic glueing, manifold structures via atlases all run on the same principle.

- **Tangent-space characterisations**: $T_p S = \ker d\Phi_p$ (level set), $T_p S = \mathrm{im}\, dF_q$ (parametric), $T_p S = \{\gamma'(0) : \gamma \subset S\}$ (curves). **Typical use:** these three descriptions are equivalent (the curve description is the canonical definition, and the other two are characterisations), but each is suited to a different computational setting. The kernel description is fastest when a defining function is available; the image description is most explicit when a parametrisation is known; the curve description is most flexible for matrix-valued objects (matrix Lie groups, especially) where curves are easy to write but defining functions or parametrisations are awkward.

# Bridges

1. **The analysis backbone — the manifold theorems are the Euclidean theorems applied chart-by-chart.** Every theorem in this topic is the corresponding Euclidean-space theorem ([[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem]], [[Thm - The Implicit Function Theorem]], [[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem]]) applied in local coordinates and then patched. The [[Thm - The Rank Theorem|rank theorem]] is the inverse function theorem applied twice — once to build coordinates on the source, once on the target. The [[Thm - Regular Value Theorem on Manifolds|manifold regular value theorem]] is the [[Thm - The Regular Value Theorem|Euclidean]] one applied chart-by-chart; the construction of the slice charts uses the implicit function theorem at every point of the level set. The relationship is symmetric: knowing the manifold theorems gives you the Euclidean theorems by restriction, and vice versa. This is the cleanest illustration of the polymath-notes principle that **manifold properties pull back to Euclidean space**.

2. **Linear algebra — rank, kernel, and image of $dF_p$ as a linear map.** The [[Def - Rank of a Smooth Map|rank of F at p]] is the [[Def - Rank of a Linear Map|linear rank]] of the differential $dF_p : T_p M \to T_{F(p)} N$. Surjectivity (submersion) means $\mathrm{rank}\, dF_p = \dim N$ — the kernel has dimension $\dim M - \dim N$ by rank–nullity, and this dimension is the dimension of the fibre of $F$ at $p$. Injectivity (immersion) means $\mathrm{rank}\, dF_p = \dim M$ — the kernel is trivial. The decomposition of $T_p M$ into $\ker dF_p$ (vertical at $p$) plus a complement (mapped isomorphically by $dF_p$ to its image) is the linear-algebra picture of the rank theorem's coordinate normal form. So every linear-algebra fact about [[Def - Null Space and Range|kernel and image]], [[Def - Rank of a Linear Map|rank]], and [[Def - Invertibility and Isomorphism|invertibility]] applies to $dF_p$ at each point, and constant rank means this linear picture is locally invariant.

3. **Submanifolds of $\mathbb{R}^n$ — the Euclidean prototype.** The notion of an [[Def - Submanifold of Euclidean Space|embedded submanifold of ℝⁿ]] (defined via local slicing by a diffeomorphism of an open set in $\mathbb{R}^n$) is exactly the special case of the manifold-level [[Def - Embedded Submanifold|embedded submanifold]] notion when the ambient manifold is $\mathbb{R}^n$. The four equivalent characterisations there — chart/straightening, implicit, parametric, graphical — generalise to the manifold setting verbatim; the local slice condition is the chart formulation, the regular value theorem produces the implicit formulation, an embedding produces the parametric formulation, and the graph formulation appears in the proof of the rank theorem. By Whitney's embedding theorem, every smooth manifold embeds as a submanifold of some $\mathbb{R}^n$, so the manifold theory and the Euclidean theory describe the same class of objects — the manifold theory just frees the geometry from a chosen embedding.

4. **Matrix Lie groups — the central source of nontrivial examples.** Every classical matrix Lie group — $\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{SL}(n,\mathbb{R})$, $\mathrm{O}(n)$, $\mathrm{SO}(n)$, $\mathrm{U}(n)$, $\mathrm{SU}(n)$, $\mathrm{Sp}(2n)$ — is an embedded submanifold of the matrix space $\mathrm{Mat}_n \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$, recognised by the regular value theorem applied to its defining equation (determinant equals one, $A^T A = I$, $A^*A = I$, etc.). The tangent space at the identity is its [[Def - Group|Lie algebra]], computed as the kernel of the differential of the defining map at $I$ — trace-zero matrices for $\mathfrak{sl}(n)$, antisymmetric matrices for $\mathfrak{o}(n)$, anti-Hermitian for $\mathfrak{u}(n)$. The full Lie group / Lie algebra correspondence (the exponential map, the bracket, the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula) is developed in [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|DG XI]], but the *manifold* half — that these groups are smooth manifolds of the predicted dimension with the predicted tangent space — is entirely the regular value theorem.

5. **Topology — embeddings, subspace topology, and proper maps.** The "embedding" half of the definition of [[Def - Immersion, Submersion, and Embedding|smooth embedding]] is purely topological: a smooth immersion is a smooth embedding iff it is a [[Def - Homeomorphism|homeomorphism onto its image]] in the [[Def - Subspace Topology|subspace topology]]. The failures of injective immersions to be embeddings — the figure-eight, the dense line on the torus — are entirely topological pathologies: the inclusion of the image into the ambient manifold is not a homeomorphism onto the domain's topology. [[Def - Compact Space|Compactness]] of the domain is the standard rescue: a continuous injective map from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism onto its image (closed map lemma). This is how the **compact** Whitney embedding theorem's proof goes through cleanly, while the non-compact case requires extra care via exhaustion functions.

6. **The forward bridge to vector fields and flows.** A submersion's smooth local sections are the building blocks of vector fields on its source: any smooth vector field on the base lifts (locally) to a vector field on the total space, and its integral curves are the lifts of the base curves. This is the geometric statement that submersions are "smooth fibrations" — a perspective developed in [[Differential Geometry V — Vector Fields, Flows, and the Lie Bracket|DG V]]. More specifically, the flow of a vector field on $M$ preserves a submanifold $S \subseteq M$ precisely when the vector field is tangent to $S$ at every point of $S$ — a tangent-space condition that connects directly to the tangent-space-of-submanifold characterisation here.

# Insights

**The unifying frame.** The single sentence that organises everything in this topic is: **first-order data ($dF_p$) controls local nonlinear structure of $F$ near $p$.** Every theorem here is a particular shape of this statement. The inverse function theorem says first-order invertibility implies local invertibility. The rank theorem says first-order rank determines local geometric type (when rank is constant). The regular value theorem says first-order surjectivity at every level-set point makes the level set a smooth submanifold with computable tangent space. Sard's theorem says first-order surjectivity holds at "almost every" value, so generic level sets are smooth. The shape is the same in each: a *linear-algebraic* condition on the differential is converted into a *nonlinear-geometric* conclusion about the map. The conversion mechanism is always the same — the implicit/inverse function theorem in coordinates — but the packaging is what makes manifold geometry possible.

**The true name of "regular value".** The textbook definition is "$d\Phi_p$ is surjective at every point of $\Phi^{-1}(c)$", but the operational meaning is sharper: **a regular value is a value at which the implicit function theorem applies, *uniformly* over the entire preimage.** This is why the regular value theorem is so powerful: it is the IFT enforced at every level-set point simultaneously, producing a global object from a local condition checked pointwise. The corresponding meaning of *critical value* is "value at which the IFT fails somewhere" — a single bad point on the level set suffices. This is why critical level sets can have spectacular singularities concentrated at isolated points (the cone vertex, the figure-eight crossing): the singularities sit exactly at the critical points of the defining map.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: "set described by an equation" ⟹ "regular value theorem".** This is the most-used routine in submanifold theory. The moment a problem says "consider the set $\{x \in M : \Phi(x) = c\}$", you compute $d\Phi_p$ at the level set's points, check surjectivity, and if yes, you have an embedded submanifold of codimension $\dim N$ and tangent space $\ker d\Phi_p$. This is so reliable that "the set is described by an equation" can be treated as a synonym for "is a submanifold, modulo checking regularity". The variant trigger "the set is described by an inequality" routes instead through *regular sublevel sets*, which are regular domains (manifolds with boundary) rather than submanifolds.

**Inheritance — manifold properties pull back to Euclidean space.** This is the meta-principle of the chapter, and it is worth stating explicitly: **every manifold-level theorem here is an assembly of Euclidean-level theorems applied chart-by-chart, with the manifold structure ensuring the local pieces agree on overlaps.** The rank theorem is the IFT applied twice; the regular value theorem is the IFT applied at every level-set point; even the Whitney embedding theorem ultimately reduces to bounding Lipschitz constants of smooth maps on compact subsets of Euclidean space. The complexity of differential geometry sits not in the local content (which is just multivariable analysis) but in the *glueing* — making sure the local pieces agree on chart overlaps, that smoothness is preserved by transition functions, that the resulting global object has a coherent structure. This is the deepest reason the theorems all "look like" their Euclidean counterparts: they *are* their Euclidean counterparts, with a glueing argument bolted on.

**Local-to-global as a strategic lever.** The pattern "local condition holding everywhere assembles to a global structure" appears in the rank theorem (constant rank yields global normal form on each connected component), the regular value theorem (regularity at every preimage point yields global manifold structure of the level set), and the Whitney embedding theorem (local coordinate embeddings assembled via partitions of unity yield a global embedding). Whenever you have a property of $F$ verified at every point of some set, ask whether that pointwise property assembles into a structural property of the whole set; it usually does, because the assembling work has been done by partitions of unity / paracompactness, and you just have to invoke it.

**Sard's theorem is the conversion of "for some" into "for almost every".** Without Sard, the regular value theorem is conditional — it says regular level sets are submanifolds, but it does not tell you that regular values exist. With Sard, regularity becomes generic: it holds for almost every value, so any construction that needs a regular value can pick one. This is the same content-flip that converts the existence of measure-zero sets in real analysis into a strategic tool: by knowing the *bad* set is small, one can constructively avoid it. Every transversality theorem, every density-of-good-maps result, every Whitney-style embedding argument is using Sard in this conversion-of-quantifiers role.
