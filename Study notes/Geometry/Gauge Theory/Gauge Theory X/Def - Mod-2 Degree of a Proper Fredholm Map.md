# Notation

Throughout, $X$ and $Y$ are **Banach manifolds** — Hausdorff, second countable topological spaces locally modelled on separable Banach spaces, with smooth ($C^\infty$) transition maps in the Fréchet sense — and $Y$ is **connected**. A map $F\colon X\to Y$ is written $F\in C^\infty(X;Y)$; its differential at $x\in X$ is the bounded linear map $d_xF\colon T_xX\to T_{F(x)}Y$. We write $F^{-1}(y)=\{x\in X:F(x)=y\}$ for the fibre over $y\in Y$, and $\#S$ for the cardinality of a finite set $S$. The additive group of two elements is $\mathbb Z/2=\{0,1\}$, and $n\bmod 2\in\mathbb Z/2$ is the parity of $n\in\mathbb Z_{\ge 0}$.

A map $F$ is a [[Def - Fredholm Map and Its Index|Fredholm map]] if $d_xF$ is a Fredholm operator for every $x\in X$; its **index** $\operatorname{index}F:=\dim\ker d_xF-\dim\operatorname{coker}d_xF\in\mathbb Z$ is independent of $x$ when $X$ is connected (and is defined component by component in general). A point $x$ is a **regular point** of $F$ if $d_xF$ is surjective, and $y\in Y$ is a [[Def - Regular Value and Transversality for Fredholm Maps|regular value]] if every $x\in F^{-1}(y)$ is a regular point; a value not in the image is regular vacuously. A continuous map is **proper** if the preimage of every compact set is compact (see [[Thm - Proper Maps are Closed and Fredholm Maps are Locally Proper]]).

This page depends on two standing conventions of the series, which we state before using them.

- **Fredholm and index conventions.** The index is computed with the sign $\dim\ker-\dim\operatorname{coker}$, and "index $0$" therefore means kernel and cokernel have equal (finite) dimension.
- **Manifold conventions.** All manifolds, finite- and infinite-dimensional, are second countable; the finite-dimensional Sard-type input is packaged in the [[Thm - Sard-Smale Theorem|Sard–Smale theorem]].

> [!warning] Convention: paracompact versus second countable
> Haydys states the definition and its accompanying theorem for **paracompact** Banach manifolds (Haydys, *Introduction to Gauge Theory*, §6.2, p. 53). The series works throughout with **second countable** Banach manifolds. A second countable manifold modelled on a metrisable (here Banach, hence metrisable) model space is itself metrisable, and every metrisable space is paracompact; so the second countable hypothesis is strictly stronger than paracompactness, and every statement Haydys proves under paracompactness holds verbatim here. Wherever a downstream page needs paracompactness — for instance to run a partition-of-unity argument or the Baire category step inside the Sard–Smale theorem — it may invoke it freely under our standing second countable convention. No conversion of formulas is required; only the ambient hypothesis is renamed.

> [!warning] Convention: a source typo
> On p. 53 Haydys prints the defining formula as $\deg_2 F:=\#f^{-1}(y)\bmod 2$, with a lower-case $f$ where the map is called $F$ throughout. This is a transcription slip; the object counted is the fibre $F^{-1}(y)$ of the very map $F$ being assigned a degree, and we write $\#F^{-1}(y)$ accordingly.

# Axiom Motivation

We want a single number attached to a map that answers the question every equation-solver eventually asks: *does $F(x)=y$ have a solution, and can it be made to have none by a small deformation of $F$?* The prototype is finite-dimensional and classical. For a smooth map $f\colon M\to N$ between closed oriented manifolds of the same dimension $n$, one picks a [[Def - Regular Value and Transversality for Fredholm Maps|regular value]] $y$ — one exists by Sard's theorem — observes that $f^{-1}(y)$ is a finite set (a compact $0$-manifold), attaches a sign $\pm 1$ to each preimage according to whether $d_xf$ preserves or reverses orientation, and sums the signs to get the **mapping degree** $\deg f$. This integer is a homotopy invariant, and it detects surjectivity: if $\deg f\neq 0$ then $f$ is onto, because a value missed by $f$ is a regular value with empty, hence zero-signed, fibre. The mapping degree is the reason $z\mapsto z^{k}$ wraps the circle $k$ times and the reason a vector field on an even sphere must vanish. Its content is entirely in the phrase *the finite-dimensional degree counts preimages of a regular value*.

Two features of that construction are worth isolating before we generalise, because each will become a hypothesis. First, the finiteness of $f^{-1}(y)$ came from two independent facts working together: the fibre over a regular value is a manifold of dimension $\dim M-\dim N=0$ (a discrete set), and it is compact because $M$ is compact. Second, the invariance of the count came from the fibre being able to move only in controlled ways as $y$ varies — no preimage can appear from or escape to infinity, again because $M$ is compact. In the infinite-dimensional setting neither compactness of the domain nor equality of dimensions is available, and we must find the exact substitutes. The theory of [[Def - Fredholm Map and Its Index|Fredholm maps]] supplies them: the Fredholm condition restores a finite "virtual dimension" $\operatorname{index}F$ for the fibre, and **properness** restores just enough compactness to pin the fibre down. The definition below is the shortest hypothesis list under which the counting construction survives, and we now check that each clause is necessary by examining what breaks when it is dropped.

**The map must be Fredholm.** Without the Fredholm hypothesis the differential $d_xF$ is an arbitrary bounded operator between Banach spaces: its kernel may be infinite-dimensional, its range need not be closed, and its cokernel need not be finite-dimensional. The [[Thm - Regular Value and Transversality Theorems for Fredholm Maps|regular value theorem]] that makes $F^{-1}(y)$ a manifold of dimension $\operatorname{index}F$ then has no content — indeed "index" is not even defined — and the fibre may be a set with no dimension at all. Concretely, on a separable Hilbert space $H$ with orthonormal basis $(e_n)_{n\ge 1}$, the diagonal operator $D(\sum_n a_ne_n)=\sum_n \tfrac1n a_ne_n$ is injective with dense but non-closed range (the vector $\sum_n \tfrac1n e_n$ lies in the closure of the range but not in the range), so its cokernel $H/\operatorname{im}D$ is not finite-dimensional and $D$ is not Fredholm; a "fibre count" for a map with such a differential is meaningless, since a linear equation $Dx=y$ has either a unique solution or none depending on $y$ in an uncontrolled way. The Fredholm condition is exactly what forbids these pathologies and equips the fibre with a finite dimension.

**The index must be zero.** Granting that $F$ is Fredholm, the [[Thm - Regular Value and Transversality Theorems for Fredholm Maps|regular value theorem]] gives $\dim F^{-1}(y)=\operatorname{index}F$ at a regular value. If $\operatorname{index}F>0$ the fibre is a positive-dimensional manifold — a curve, a surface, or worse — and "the number of points in it" is not a number: the model example is a submersion $\mathbb R^{n+1}\to\mathbb R^{n}$, whose fibres are curves. If $\operatorname{index}F<0$ the fibre over a regular value is a manifold of negative dimension, which forces it to be **empty**; a regular value then always has empty preimage (this is proved as the exercise [[Ex - Regular Values of a Fredholm Map of Negative Index]]), so the count is identically $0$ and carries no information. The single index at which the fibre is a genuine finite set of points — a $0$-dimensional manifold that can be non-empty — is $\operatorname{index}F=0$. This is not a convenience; it is the arithmetical statement that the "virtual dimension" of the solution set is exactly zero, which is the only case in which counting solutions is the right invariant. It is also the case that arises in gauge theory: the Seiberg–Witten and instanton equations are engineered so that, after gauge fixing, their linearisation is Fredholm of index equal to the expected dimension of the moduli space, and the point-counting invariants live where that dimension is zero.

**The map must be proper.** Properness is what replaces compactness of the domain, and it does two distinct jobs. First, it makes the fibre finite: the singleton $\{y\}$ is compact, so if $F$ is proper then $F^{-1}(y)$ is compact, and a compact $0$-manifold (a compact discrete space) is finite. Second — and this is the subtler job — it makes the count *locally constant*, which is what independence of the regular value ultimately rests on. Drop properness and both fail. Consider $F=\arctan\colon\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, a smooth map whose derivative $\arctan'(t)=1/(1+t^{2})$ is everywhere positive, so every $x$ is a regular point and $d_xF$ is a linear isomorphism of one-dimensional spaces, in particular Fredholm of index $0$; the target $\mathbb R$ is connected. Every real number is therefore a regular value. Yet $F$ is **not proper**: the preimage of the compact set $[0,\pi]\subset\mathbb R$ is $\arctan^{-1}([0,\pi])=[0,\infty)$ (because $\arctan$ maps $[0,\infty)$ onto $[0,\tfrac\pi2)\subseteq[0,\pi]$ and no negative real into $[0,\pi]$), which is not compact. The consequence is visible in the count. The image of $\arctan$ is the open interval $(-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2)$; a regular value $y$ with $|y|<\tfrac\pi2$ has exactly one preimage, while a regular value $y$ with $|y|>\tfrac\pi2$ has **empty** fibre. Thus $y\mapsto\#F^{-1}(y)\bmod 2$ jumps from $1$ to $0$ as $y$ crosses $\pm\tfrac\pi2$, even though every value on both sides is regular. There is no way to assign a single parity to $F$, because a preimage has escaped to infinity as $y$ left the image. Properness is precisely the hypothesis that forbids this escape.

> [!warning] The non-example, stated carefully
> The failure above needs the target to be all of $\mathbb R$, so that regular values lie on both sides of the image boundary. Viewed instead as a map $\arctan\colon\mathbb R\to(-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2)$ **onto its image**, $\arctan$ is a diffeomorphism and hence proper, with every fibre a single point and $\deg_2=1$; the pathology has been hidden by shrinking the codomain to the image. The lesson is that properness is a property of a map *together with its chosen codomain*, and the degree is only defined once the codomain is fixed. The instructive non-example is therefore $\arctan\colon\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$.

**The target must be connected.** The degree is meant to be one number for the whole map, and its independence of the chosen regular value is the heart of the theory. That independence is proved (on the page cited below) by moving any one regular value to any other by a diffeomorphism of $Y$ isotopic to the identity, which is possible exactly because $Y$ is connected. If $Y=Y_1\sqcup Y_2$ were disconnected, the two components could carry different counts — take $F$ to map onto $Y_1$ only, so that a regular value in $Y_1$ has non-empty fibre while a regular value in $Y_2$ has empty fibre — and no single degree would be well-defined. Connectedness is the hypothesis that makes "the" regular value legitimate. (One could, of course, define a degree on each component separately; the connectedness hypothesis simply records that we are treating one component at a time.)

**Why the count is taken modulo two.** The finite-dimensional mapping degree summed *signed* preimages, the sign recording an orientation comparison. In the Banach setting the analogue of that sign is a trivialisation of the [[Def - Fredholm Map and Its Index|determinant line]] of the family $x\mapsto d_xF$, which is extra data not always available and not part of the present hypotheses. With no signs to attach, the only invariant one can extract from a finite set is its cardinality, and the only feature of the cardinality that survives the deformations we allow — where two preimages can be created or destroyed in pairs, as a curve of solutions turns around — is its **parity**. Hence the mod-$2$ reduction. When the orientation data is supplied one recovers an integer-valued degree; that refinement is the subject of the companion definition [[Def - Integer Degree of an Oriented Proper Fredholm Map]] and the shared theorem below. A reader could invent the present definition from these desiderata alone: count the solutions of $F(x)=y$ for a value that makes the count finite and stable, and remember only the parity because that is all the hypotheses can protect.

# The Definition

Let $X$ and $Y$ be second countable Banach manifolds with $Y$ connected, and let
$$F\colon X\to Y \qquad\text{be a proper Fredholm map with }\operatorname{index}F=0 .$$
By the [[Thm - Sard-Smale Theorem|Sard–Smale theorem]] the set of regular values of $F$ is dense in $Y$, so a regular value exists. Fix any regular value $y\in Y$. Then, as established in the well-posedness discussion just below, the fibre $F^{-1}(y)$ is a finite set. The **$\mathbb Z/2$ degree** (or **mod-$2$ degree**) of $F$ is
$$\boxed{\ \deg_2 F:=\#F^{-1}(y)\ \bmod 2\ \in\ \mathbb Z/2 .\ }$$

That this number does not depend on the choice of regular value $y$, and that it is invariant under homotopy through proper Fredholm maps of index $0$, is the content of [[Thm - Well-Definedness and Homotopy Invariance of the Degree]] and is *not* proved on this page; the definition is only legitimate once those facts are in hand, and we state them precisely at the point where the definition would otherwise be ambiguous (the section *Relate to Other Fields* and the callout below).

**Well-posedness of the count (Step 0).** Before the formula can be written down we must know that $F^{-1}(y)$ is a finite set. This uses two of the standing hypotheses, each once.

> [!note]- Why $F^{-1}(y)$ is finite
> Fix a regular value $y$. We show $F^{-1}(y)$ is a compact $0$-dimensional manifold, hence finite, in two steps.
>
> **It is a $0$-dimensional manifold.** By the regular value theorem for Fredholm maps — *if $y$ is a regular value of a Fredholm map $F\colon X\to Y$, then $F^{-1}(y)$ is a smooth submanifold of $X$ with $\dim F^{-1}(y)=\operatorname{index}F$*, which is [[Thm - Regular Value and Transversality Theorems for Fredholm Maps|proved in the chapter]] — and the hypothesis $\operatorname{index}F=0$, the fibre $F^{-1}(y)$ is a smooth submanifold of dimension $0$ (since $\dim F^{-1}(y)=\operatorname{index}F=0$). A $0$-dimensional manifold is a discrete topological space: every point has a neighbourhood in $X$ meeting the fibre in that point alone.
>
> **It is compact.** The one-point set $\{y\}\subset Y$ is compact. Since $F$ is proper (preimages of compact sets are compact, [[Thm - Proper Maps are Closed and Fredholm Maps are Locally Proper|by definition of a proper map]]), the preimage $F^{-1}(\{y\})=F^{-1}(y)$ is compact.
>
> **Conclusion.** $F^{-1}(y)$ is a compact discrete space. A discrete space is compact if and only if it is finite: the cover by the singletons of its points is an open cover with no proper subcover, so a finite subcover exists only when the space itself is finite. Therefore $F^{-1}(y)$ is a finite set and $\#F^{-1}(y)$ is a well-defined non-negative integer. $\blacksquare$

The two hypotheses that enter here are exactly the two flagged in the motivation: $\operatorname{index}F=0$ forces the fibre to be $0$-dimensional, and properness forces it to be compact; together they force it to be finite.

For emphasis we record the statement whose proof legitimises the notation but lives elsewhere.

> [!note] Restatement of the well-definedness theorem (proved on its own page)
> **Theorem (well-definedness and homotopy invariance of $\deg_2$).** Let $F\colon X\to Y$ be a proper Fredholm map of index $0$ between second countable Banach manifolds with $Y$ connected.
> (i) The parity $\#F^{-1}(y)\bmod 2$ is the same for every regular value $y\in Y$; thus $\deg_2 F$ is well-defined.
> (ii) If $F_0$ and $F_1$ are homotopic through a homotopy $F_t$, $t\in[0,1]$, each $F_t$ a proper Fredholm map of index $0$, then $\deg_2 F_0=\deg_2 F_1$.
>
> The complete proof — the six-step argument through openness and density of regular values, local constancy of the count, the boundary-of-a-compact-$1$-manifold cobordism, and the homogeneity of $Y$ under diffeomorphisms isotopic to the identity — is on [[Thm - Well-Definedness and Homotopy Invariance of the Degree]]. Nothing on the present page reproves it; we use only its statement.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The mod-$2$ degree is the Banach-manifold member of a single family of *degree theories*, each a homomorphism from a monoid of maps, taken up to an appropriate homotopy, into a small abelian group. In finite dimensions and with orientations one has the integer-valued **Brouwer / Smooth mapping degree** $\deg\colon[M,N]\to\mathbb Z$ for closed oriented equidimensional $M,N$; forgetting orientations and reducing modulo two gives the mod-$2$ mapping degree, and the present definition is its extension to proper Fredholm maps of index $0$ between (possibly infinite-dimensional) manifolds. In the linear-analytic tradition the corresponding object is the **Leray–Schauder degree** for maps of the form identity-minus-compact on a Banach space, which are proper on bounded sets and Fredholm of index $0$; the mod-$2$ reduction of the Leray–Schauder degree is a special case of the construction here, the compact perturbation guaranteeing the Fredholm and properness hypotheses. Topologically, the parity $\deg_2 F$ is the **mod-$2$ intersection number** of the graph-type submanifold with a fibre, and it is the image of the oriented degree under $\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z/2$ whenever the oriented degree is defined.

**True name.** Operationally, $\deg_2 F$ is *the parity of the number of solutions of the equation $F(x)=y$ for a generic right-hand side $y$*. Everything about it is a statement about solvability: the invariant is designed so that a nonzero value is an obstruction to the equation being unsolvable. This is made exact by the corollary [[Thm - Nonzero Degree Implies Surjectivity]] — *if $\deg_2 F\neq 0$ then $F$ is surjective* — whose one-line reason is that a value outside the image is a regular value with empty fibre, so if some value is missed then some regular value has count $0$, whence $\deg_2 F=0$ by well-definedness. Reading the definition backwards: to prove that $F(x)=y$ has a solution for *every* $y$, it suffices to compute the parity of the solution set for *one* convenient $y$ and find it odd. This is exactly how existence theorems in gauge theory and nonlinear analysis are obtained from the degree, and it is the operational meaning that the official "count a fibre and reduce mod $2$" phrasing encodes.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — a smooth self-map of the $n$-sphere.** Let $f\colon S^{n}\to S^{n}$ be any smooth map, $n\ge 1$. We verify all four hypotheses clause by clause. *Banach manifolds, $Y$ connected:* $S^{n}$ is a finite-dimensional smooth manifold, in particular a second countable Banach manifold modelled on $\mathbb R^{n}$, and $S^{n}$ is connected for $n\ge 1$. *Fredholm of index $0$:* at each $x$, $d_xf\colon T_xS^{n}\to T_{f(x)}S^{n}$ is a linear map between $n$-dimensional spaces, so it is a Fredholm operator with $\dim\ker d_xf-\dim\operatorname{coker}d_xf=n-\dim\operatorname{im}d_xf-(n-\dim\operatorname{im}d_xf)$… more simply $\operatorname{index}d_xf=\dim T_xS^{n}-\dim T_{f(x)}S^{n}=n-n=0$ for a map between equidimensional spaces. *Proper:* $S^{n}$ is compact, and a continuous map with compact domain is proper, because the preimage of any set is a closed subset of the compact space $S^{n}$ and hence compact. A regular value $y$ exists by [[Thm - Sard-Smale Theorem|Sard–Smale]] (or classical Sard). Then $\deg_2 f=\#f^{-1}(y)\bmod 2$. This is the mod-$2$ reduction of the classical mapping degree: at a regular value the oriented degree is $\deg f=\sum_{x\in f^{-1}(y)}\varepsilon(x)$ with each sign $\varepsilon(x)=\pm 1$, and reducing modulo two collapses every sign to $1$, so $\#f^{-1}(y)\equiv\sum_x\varepsilon(x)=\deg f\pmod 2$. Thus $\deg_2 f=\deg f\bmod 2$; for instance the antipodal map on $S^{n}$ has $\deg=(-1)^{n+1}$ and hence $\deg_2=1$ for even $n$ and $0$ for odd $n$.

**Is an instance — the power map on the circle.** Take $n=1$ in the previous example: $f\colon S^{1}\to S^{1}$, $f(z)=z^{k}$ for a fixed integer $k$, where $S^{1}=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|=1\}$. The hypotheses hold as above (compact connected $1$-manifold, differential a linear isomorphism of $1$-dimensional spaces where $f'\neq 0$, hence index $0$; proper by compactness). We compute the fibre over a regular value. For $k\neq 0$ and any $w\in S^{1}$, the equation $z^{k}=w$ has exactly $|k|$ solutions $z=w^{1/k}\zeta^{j}$, $j=0,\dots,|k|-1$, where $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/|k|}$ is a primitive $|k|$-th root of unity; at each such $z$ the derivative in the angular coordinate is $k\neq 0$, so every point of the fibre is regular and $w$ is a regular value. Hence $\#f^{-1}(w)=|k|$ and
$$\deg_2 f=|k|\bmod 2=k\bmod 2\qquad(\text{since }|k|\equiv k\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2).$$
For $k=0$ the map is constant; any $w$ other than that constant value is a regular value with empty fibre, giving $\#f^{-1}(w)=0$ and $\deg_2 f=0=0\bmod 2$, consistent with the formula $k\bmod 2$. So the mod-$2$ degree of $z\mapsto z^{k}$ is the parity of $k$: odd powers have degree $1$ and even powers degree $0$.

**Is an instance — a linear Fredholm isomorphism.** Let $T\colon X\to Y$ be a bounded linear isomorphism between Banach spaces, regarded as Banach manifolds (each modelled on itself). *Fredholm of index $0$:* $T$ is its own differential at every point, $\ker T=0$ and $\operatorname{coker}T=0$, so $T$ is Fredholm with $\operatorname{index}T=0-0=0$. *$Y$ connected:* a Banach space is path-connected. *Proper:* $T$ is a homeomorphism, so for compact $K\subseteq Y$ the preimage $T^{-1}(K)$ is the image of $K$ under the continuous map $T^{-1}$, hence compact. Every $y\in Y$ is a regular value because $d_yT=T$ is surjective, and the fibre is the single point $T^{-1}(y)$. Therefore $\#T^{-1}(y)=1$ and $\deg_2 T=1$. More generally any diffeomorphism $F\colon X\to Y$ of Banach manifolds is a proper Fredholm map of index $0$ with $\deg_2 F=1$, since each fibre is a single point.

**Is NOT an instance — a non-proper Fredholm map.** Let $F=\arctan\colon\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$. *Fredholm of index $0$:* the differential is multiplication by $\arctan'(t)=(1+t^{2})^{-1}>0$, a linear isomorphism $\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, so $F$ is Fredholm of index $0$, and every point is regular. *$Y$ connected:* $\mathbb R$ is connected. *Proper: this fails.* The set $[0,\pi]\subset\mathbb R$ is compact, but $F^{-1}([0,\pi])=[0,\infty)$ (since $\arctan$ maps $[0,\infty)$ onto $[0,\tfrac\pi2)\subset[0,\pi]$ and takes no negative value into $[0,\pi]$ beyond $0$), which is not compact. Consequently the fibre count is not locally constant: the image is $(-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2)$, so a regular value $y_0$ with $|y_0|<\tfrac\pi2$ has $\#F^{-1}(y_0)=1$, while a regular value $y_1$ with $|y_1|>\tfrac\pi2$ has $\#F^{-1}(y_1)=0$. There is no consistent parity, and the definition does not apply. This is the precise way the properness clause fails and takes the degree with it; the mechanism is that as $y$ crosses $\pm\tfrac\pi2$ a single preimage escapes to $\pm\infty$, which properness forbids for a genuine proper map. The same phenomenon, examined as a drill, is [[Ex - Properness is Necessary for the Degree]].

**Corollary — nonzero degree forces solvability.** If $F\colon X\to Y$ is a proper Fredholm map of index $0$ between second countable Banach manifolds with $Y$ connected and $\deg_2 F\neq 0$, then $F$ is surjective. The reason, stated in *True name* above and proved in full on [[Thm - Nonzero Degree Implies Surjectivity]]: were some $y_0\in Y$ omitted by $F$, then $y_0$ would be a regular value with $F^{-1}(y_0)=\varnothing$, so $\deg_2 F=\#\varnothing\bmod 2=0$, contradicting $\deg_2 F\neq 0$. We do not reprove it here; we record it as the first payoff of the definition.

**Calibration check.** Confirm three things directly from the page. First, that the identity map of any Banach space has mod-$2$ degree $1$ (it is the case $T=\operatorname{id}$ of the linear-isomorphism example, fibre a single point). Second, that $z\mapsto z^{2}$ and $z\mapsto z^{4}$ on $S^{1}$ both have $\deg_2=0$ while $z\mapsto z^{3}$ has $\deg_2=1$, using only the count of $k$-th roots and the parity of $k$. Third — the conceptual check — explain why the constant map $S^{1}\to S^{1}$, which certainly has empty fibre over most values, is consistent with $\deg_2=0$, and why this is *not* a violation of well-definedness even though some values (the constant image) have infinite fibre: those exceptional values are not regular, and the degree is read only at regular values.

> When the family $x\mapsto d_xF$ is oriented by a trivialisation of its [[Def - Integer Degree of an Oriented Proper Fredholm Map|determinant line bundle]], each regular preimage acquires a sign and the signed count refines $\deg_2 F$ to an integer $\deg F\in\mathbb Z$ whose reduction modulo two is $\deg_2 F$. > The same parity can be computed by intersecting with a generic finite-dimensional family of maps rather than by finding a single regular value, which is often the only computable route in gauge theory; that this alternative count agrees with $\deg_2 F$ is [[Thm - Parametric Transversality|proved via parametric transversality]] and its companion.

> [!tip] Counting invariants of moduli spaces *(from Seiberg–Witten theory, Gauge Theory XI)*
> The **Seiberg–Witten invariant** in the case of a zero-dimensional moduli space is exactly a mod-$2$ (or, with orientations, integer) count of solutions of a proper Fredholm equation of index $0$, obtained from this degree applied to the gauge-fixed Seiberg–Witten map. The properness that this definition demands is supplied there by the compactness of the Seiberg–Witten moduli space, and the index-$0$ condition by the dimension formula.
