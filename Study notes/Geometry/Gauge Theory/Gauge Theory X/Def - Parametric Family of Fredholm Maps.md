# Notation

Throughout, $X$, $Y$, and $W$ are **Banach manifolds** in the sense of [[Def - Banach Manifold and Smooth Maps between Banach Spaces|the standing framework]]: Hausdorff, second countable, and modelled on separable Banach spaces, with smooth ($C^\infty$) transition maps. A map between Banach manifolds is **smooth** if it is $C^\infty$ in every pair of charts. We write $T_xX$ for the tangent space of $X$ at $x$ and $d_x\Phi\colon T_xX\to T_{\Phi(x)}Z$ for the differential at $x$ of a smooth map $\Phi\colon X\to Z$.

A smooth map $F\colon X\to Y$ is a **[[Def - Fredholm Map and Its Index|Fredholm map]]** if its differential $d_xF\colon T_xX\to T_{F(x)}Y$ is a [[Def - Fredholm Operator and Index|Fredholm operator]] — a bounded linear operator with finite-dimensional kernel and finite-dimensional cokernel (and hence closed range) — at every point $x\in X$. Its **index** is the locally constant integer $\operatorname{index}d_xF=\dim\ker d_xF-\dim\operatorname{coker}d_xF$; on a connected manifold this is a single integer, the **index** $\operatorname{index}F$. A point $y\in Y$ is a **[[Def - Regular Value and Transversality for Fredholm Maps|regular value]]** of a Fredholm map $F$ if $d_xF$ is surjective for every $x\in F^{-1}(y)$ (vacuously if $F^{-1}(y)=\varnothing$). A continuous map is **proper** if the preimage of every compact set is compact.

For the parametric setup we reserve script letters: $\mathcal F\colon X\times W\to Y$ is the total map, and for a fixed parameter $w\in W$ we write
$$\mathcal F_w\colon X\to Y,\qquad \mathcal F_w(x):=\mathcal F(x,w),$$
for the restriction to the slice $X\times\{w\}$. The letter $F$ (without a subscript) is reserved for the single map $F\colon X\to Y$ whose degree we ultimately wish to compute, and $w_0\in W$ is the parameter that recovers it. A **[[Def - Residual Set and Generic Property|residual]]** subset of a topological space is one containing a countable intersection of open dense sets; by the [[Thm - Baire Category Theorem|Baire category theorem]] a residual subset of a Banach manifold is dense. The set of "good" parameters is denoted $W_0\subset W$.

> [!warning] Convention: "second category" versus "residual"
> Haydys writes that the good parameters form a set "of second category." Following the series conventions we call this set **residual** — that is, comeagre: a countable intersection of open dense sets. Baire's own phrase "of second category" means only "not meagre," which is strictly weaker; Haydys's usage is the stronger comeagre notion, and we use the unambiguous word throughout. See [[Def - Residual Set and Generic Property]].

> [!warning] Convention: paracompact versus second countable
> Haydys states the parametric construction for maps between **paracompact** Banach manifolds. The series works with **second countable** Banach manifolds; since a second countable manifold modelled on a metrisable space is itself metrisable and second countable, it is Lindelöf and hence paracompact, so the two hypotheses coincide for our manifolds and every countable-cover argument below is legitimate. We record the reduction once here and do not repeat it.

This is a **compound page**: it fixes three interlocking notions at once — the four hypotheses (A)–(D) that make a family of Fredholm maps a legitimate vehicle for computing a degree; the *generic-parameter definition* of the mod-2 degree, which counts the preimages of a good parameter's slice map; and the fact that the *path spaces* $\Gamma(w_1,w_2)$ used to compare two good parameters are themselves Banach manifolds. They belong on one page because the definition of the degree is only intelligible once the hypotheses are in hand, and its well-definedness (proved on a later page) is carried out precisely over the path spaces constructed here.

# Axiom Motivation

The mod-2 degree of [[Def - Mod-2 Degree of a Proper Fredholm Map|the previous page]] was built by *moving the value*: given a proper index-zero Fredholm map $F\colon X\to Y$ and a target value $y$ that might be critical, one appeals to the [[Thm - Sard-Smale Theorem|Sard–Smale theorem]] to find a regular value $y'$ arbitrarily close to $y$, counts $\#F^{-1}(y')\bmod 2$, and proves this count independent of the nearby regular value. The whole construction leans on the freedom to slide $y$ a little. The question this page answers is: **what does one do when that freedom is not available?**

The situation in which it is not available is the one this entire series is built toward. Suppose $X$ and $Y$ carry an action of a Lie group $G$ and $F$ is $G$-equivariant, $F(g\cdot x)=g\cdot F(x)$. To keep the preimage $F^{-1}(y)$ inside the equivariant world — so that it inherits the $G$-action, and its quotient is a moduli space — one is forced to choose $y$ to be a *fixed point* of the $G$-action on $Y$. The fixed-point set is typically a thin, distinguished subset of $Y$ (often a single point, such as $0$), and there is no reason at all for it to contain a regular value. Sliding $y$ off the fixed-point set destroys equivariance; staying on it may leave $F^{-1}(y)$ singular. Sard–Smale, which promises regular values densely in $Y$, is useless because the one value we are permitted to use is pinned. This is not a contrived difficulty: it is exactly the geometry of the [[Def - Seiberg-Witten Equations|Seiberg–Witten equations]] (chapter XI), where the relevant value is the fixed point $0$ and the count of solutions is the invariant one wants.

The remedy is to perturb *the map* rather than *the value*. We embed our map $F$ into a family $\{\mathcal F_w\}_{w\in W}$ indexed by a connected Banach manifold $W$ of parameters, with $\mathcal F_{w_0}=F$ for one distinguished parameter, and we choose the parameter — not the value — generically. If we can arrange that a *good* parameter $w$ makes the pinned value $y$ regular for the slice map $\mathcal F_w$, then $\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ is a manifold we can count, and if the good parameters are dense we can always find one near $w_0$. In the equivariant application one perturbs the equation itself (in Seiberg–Witten theory, by a self-dual two-form $\eta$, which plays the role of $w$), keeping the value fixed at $0$; the count of solutions of the perturbed equation is the invariant. The four hypotheses below are exactly the conditions under which this scheme produces a well-defined number.

Let us see, clause by clause, why each of the four hypotheses is needed and what breaks if it is dropped.

**Hypothesis (A) — each slice map is Fredholm.** We require every $\mathcal F_w\colon X\to Y$ to be a Fredholm map. This is what gives each slice a finite index and lets the [[Thm - Regular Value and Transversality Theorems for Fredholm Maps|regular-value theorem]] present $\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ as a finite-dimensional manifold. If we drop it, the very object we mean to count has no dimension. The cleanest failure is the bounded inclusion $\iota\colon H_1(M;E)\hookrightarrow H_0(M;E)$ of one Sobolev space into the next, which is injective with *dense, non-closed* range and hence not Fredholm (it is the standard [[Def - Fredholm Map and Its Index|non-example]]); a family whose slices were maps of this kind would have $\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ an infinite-dimensional affine subspace, and "$\#\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$" would be meaningless. Note that (A) is genuinely a hypothesis on each slice, *not* a consequence of $\mathcal F$ being Fredholm on the product $X\times W$: the differential $d_{(x,w)}\mathcal F$ acts on the larger space $T_xX\oplus T_wW$, so $\mathcal F$ can be Fredholm on the product while individual restrictions $\mathcal F_w$ are not, and both facts must be assumed.

**Hypothesis (B) — the family contains $F$.** We require a distinguished parameter $w_0\in W$ with $\mathcal F_{w_0}=F$. Without it the construction would compute the mod-2 count of some anonymous member of the family, with no tie to the map $F$ we started from. Combined with the **connectedness of $W$** — which is part of the standing data, not an afterthought — hypothesis (B) is what forces every good slice to have the *same* count as $F$: a path in $W$ from $w_0$ to any $w$ produces a homotopy from $F=\mathcal F_{w_0}$ to $\mathcal F_w$ through proper index-zero Fredholm maps, along which [[Thm - Well-Definedness and Homotopy Invariance of the Degree|homotopy invariance of the degree]] holds. Drop connectedness and $W$ could split into components on which the count differs; drop (B) and there is nothing to anchor the number to. This is why the theorem identifying the parametric count with $\deg_2 F$ (below) needs both.

**Hypothesis (C) — the value is regular for the total map.** We require $y$ to be a regular value of the total map $\mathcal F\colon X\times W\to Y$, meaning $d_{(x,w)}\mathcal F$ is surjective for every $(x,w)\in\mathcal F^{-1}(y)$. This is subtle and is the engine of the whole construction, so it deserves emphasis: it is a condition on the *total* map, and it is *weaker* than asking $y$ to be regular for each individual $\mathcal F_w$ — indeed the point of the construction is that (C) can hold when no single slice makes $y$ regular. Surjectivity of $d_{(x,w)}\mathcal F$ may be achieved by the *parameter* directions $\partial_w\mathcal F$ filling in the cokernel of the slice differential $d_x\mathcal F_w$. Hypothesis (C) makes the **universal zero set** $\mathcal Z:=\mathcal F^{-1}(y)$ a Banach submanifold of $X\times W$ (by the regular-value theorem, [[Thm - Regular Value and Transversality Theorems for Fredholm Maps]]), and it is exactly what lets [[Thm - Parametric Transversality|parametric transversality]] conclude that the good parameters are residual. If (C) fails, $\mathcal Z$ need not be a manifold, the projection $\pi\colon\mathcal Z\to W$ has no smooth domain, and there may be *no* good parameter at all — the entire scheme collapses at its foundation. A concrete failure: with $X=Y=W=\mathbb R$ and $\mathcal F(x,w)=x^2+w^2$ at the value $y=0$, the only zero is $(0,0)$, at which $d_{(0,0)}\mathcal F=0$ is not surjective, so $0$ is not a regular value of $\mathcal F$; here no reparametrisation of $w$ can make the isolated singular zero regular, and there is no residual set of good parameters to fall back on.

**Hypothesis (D) — each slice map is proper.** We require every $\mathcal F_w\colon X\to Y$ to be proper. This is what makes the count *finite* and *stable*. Under (A), (C) and index zero, a good slice's preimage $\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ is a zero-dimensional manifold; properness upgrades "zero-dimensional manifold" to "compact zero-dimensional manifold," which is a finite set, so $\#\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ is a well-defined natural number. Properness is also what makes the cobordism between two good slices compact (used on the [[Thm - The Generic Parametric Count Defines the Mod-2 Degree|next-but-one page]]), so that a compact one-manifold with boundary appears and the parity argument closes. If (D) is dropped, the count can be infinite or can jump discontinuously between neighbouring regular values, and no invariant survives; the canonical witness is the diffeomorphism $\arctan\colon\mathbb R\to(-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2)$, which is a Fredholm map of index zero but is not proper, and whose preimage count over a regular value is $1$ inside the image and $0$ outside — a jump that no degree can tolerate. This is the same failure that made properness indispensable for the ordinary degree.

Could a reader invent hypotheses (A)–(D) unaided? The desiderata force them: we want a *finite* count [(A) gives it a dimension, index zero and (D) make it finite], *of a manifold* [(C) via the regular-value theorem], *attached to $F$* [(B) with connectedness], *that a dense set of parameters realises* [(C) again, through parametric transversality]. Each hypothesis is the minimal condition securing one of these, and dropping any one demolishes exactly one pillar.

# The Definition

**The parametric family.** Let $W$ be a connected Banach manifold (the *parameter manifold*), let $X$ and $Y$ be Banach manifolds, and let
$$\mathcal F\colon X\times W\longrightarrow Y$$
be a smooth map. Write $\mathcal F_w:=\mathcal F(\cdot,w)\colon X\to Y$ for the slice at $w\in W$. Fix a value $y\in Y$. The data $(\mathcal F,W,w_0,y)$ is a **parametric family of Fredholm maps** (adapted to $F:=\mathcal F_{w_0}$ at $y$) if the following hold:

- **(A)** for each $w\in W$, the slice map $\mathcal F_w\colon X\to Y$ is a Fredholm map;
- **(B)** there is a parameter $w_0\in W$ with $\mathcal F_{w_0}=F$;
- **(C)** $y$ is a regular value of the total map $\mathcal F\colon X\times W\to Y$.

A parametric family is **proper** if in addition

- **(D)** each slice map $\mathcal F_w\colon X\to Y$ is proper.

By (A) and the local constancy of the Fredholm index ([[Def - Fredholm Map and Its Index]]), the index $\operatorname{index}\mathcal F_w$ is independent of $w$ on the connected manifold $W$; we denote this common value $\operatorname{index}F$. For the degree we assume throughout that $\operatorname{index}F=0$.

**The good parameters.** Under (A)–(C), [[Thm - Parametric Transversality|parametric transversality]] — restated here as *the set*
$$W_0:=\{\,w\in W:\ y\text{ is a regular value of }\mathcal F_w\,\}$$
*is residual in $W$, hence dense* — guarantees that good parameters exist and are generic. We call $W_0$ the **residual set of good parameters**.

**The generic-parameter degree.** Suppose $(\mathcal F,W,w_0,y)$ is a proper parametric family with $\operatorname{index}F=0$. For any good parameter $w\in W_0$, the slice map $\mathcal F_w$ is a proper Fredholm map of index zero for which $y$ is a regular value, so $\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ is a compact zero-dimensional manifold — a finite set (proof in the corollary below). The **generic-parameter mod-2 degree** of $F$ is
$$\deg_2 F:=\#\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)\bmod 2\ \in\ \mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z,\qquad\text{for any }w\in W_0.$$
That this number is independent of the choice of $w\in W_0$, and that it agrees with the mod-2 degree $\deg_2 F$ of [[Def - Mod-2 Degree of a Proper Fredholm Map]], is the content of [[Thm - The Generic Parametric Count Defines the Mod-2 Degree]]; the two facts are the reason the same symbol $\deg_2 F$ is used for both. In the equivariant setting, where the value $y$ cannot be moved, one *takes this formula as the definition* of the degree, bypassing the value-perturbation construction of §10.2 entirely.

**The comparison path spaces.** To compare the counts of two good parameters $w_1,w_2\in W_0$ one joins them by a path in $W$ and studies the universal zero set along the path. Fix an order of differentiability $k\ge 0$. The **path space** with endpoints $w_1,w_2$ is
$$\Gamma(w_1,w_2):=\{\,\gamma\in C^k([1,2];W)\ :\ \gamma(1)=w_1,\ \gamma(2)=w_2\,\}.$$
The claim built into this definition — that $\Gamma(w_1,w_2)$ is itself a Banach manifold, so that Sard–Smale may be applied to a projection off it — is established in the corollary below.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The generic-parameter degree is the infinite-dimensional form of a principle that runs through all of differential topology: **a generic member of a family is transverse, and the count of solutions of a generic member is a deformation invariant.** In finite dimensions this is the parametric transversality theorem of Thom (Guillemin–Pollack, *Differential Topology*, §2.3): if $\mathcal F\colon X\times W\to Y$ is smooth and transverse to a submanifold $Z\subset Y$, then for almost every parameter $w$ the slice $\mathcal F_w$ is itself transverse to $Z$. Hypothesis (C) is precisely transversality of the total map to the point $\{y\}$, and $W_0$ is the almost-every set — except that "almost every," which relies on Lebesgue measure, must be replaced by "residual," because a Banach space carries no translation-invariant measure and the only workable notion of "generic" is the topological one (residual sets), reachable through Sard–Smale and Baire.

**True name.** Stripped of its analytic scaffolding, the object being defined is: *choose a generic member of the family, count its solutions of the pinned equation, reduce mod two*. The definition is already operational — it tells you exactly what to compute — with one proviso: the word "generic" is a promise ($W_0$ is residual, hence dense, hence non-empty and rich) that must be redeemed by [[Thm - Parametric Transversality|parametric transversality]], and the word "count" is well-posed only because properness makes the solution set finite. The construction is the template for every gauge-theoretic invariant that counts solutions of a nonlinear elliptic equation after a small generic perturbation: the Donaldson invariants count anti-self-dual connections, and the [[Def - Seiberg-Witten Invariant|Seiberg–Witten invariant]] counts solutions of the perturbed monopole equations, each obtained by exactly this device of perturbing the equation through a parameter and reading off a bordism-invariant count. The deformation-invariance of the count is the shadow of a *cobordism*: two generic slices are the two ends of a compact one-manifold cut out by a generic path, and the parity of a compact one-manifold's boundary is zero — this is the same mechanism that makes the finite-dimensional Brouwer degree a homotopy invariant ([[Def - Brouwer Degree of a Map]]).

# Examples / Corollaries

**Corollary — the count is finite.** *Under (A), (C), (D) and $\operatorname{index}F=0$, for every $w\in W_0$ the set $\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ is finite.* Since $w\in W_0$, the value $y$ is a regular value of the Fredholm map $\mathcal F_w$ (definition of $W_0$), so by the [[Thm - Regular Value and Transversality Theorems for Fredholm Maps|regular-value theorem for Fredholm maps]] — *if $y$ is a regular value of a Fredholm map, then $\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ is an embedded submanifold of dimension $\operatorname{index}\mathcal F_w$* — the preimage $\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ is a manifold of dimension $\operatorname{index}\mathcal F_w=\operatorname{index}F=0$, that is, a discrete set. By (D) the slice map $\mathcal F_w$ is proper, so the preimage $\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ of the compact set $\{y\}$ is compact. A discrete compact set is finite. Therefore $\#\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)<\infty$ and the generic-parameter degree is well-defined as an element of $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary — the path spaces are Banach manifolds.** This is the fact used to compare good parameters. We prove it completely in the case that carries every application in this series, and then say precisely what the general statement requires.

> [!note]- Full proof that $\Gamma(w_1,w_2)$ is a Banach manifold
> **What we must show.** We must show that $\Gamma(w_1,w_2)=\{\gamma\in C^k([1,2];W):\gamma(1)=w_1,\ \gamma(2)=w_2\}$ is a Banach manifold, with charts inherited from those of $W$.
>
> **Step 0 — the model space is a Banach space.** Let $E$ be a Banach space. The space $C^k([1,2];E)$ of $k$-times continuously differentiable maps $\gamma\colon[1,2]\to E$, with the norm
> $$\lVert\gamma\rVert_{C^k}:=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\ \sup_{t\in[1,2]}\ \lVert\gamma^{(j)}(t)\rVert_E,$$
> is a Banach space: it is a normed vector space, and it is complete because a $\lVert\cdot\rVert_{C^k}$-Cauchy sequence is, term by term in each derivative $\gamma^{(j)}$, uniformly Cauchy in $C^0([1,2];E)$, hence uniformly convergent to some continuous $g_j$ (uniform limits of continuous $E$-valued maps are continuous, and $C^0([1,2];E)$ is complete because $E$ is), and the standard theorem on differentiating uniform limits gives $g_j=g_0^{(j)}$, so the limit lies in $C^k$ (each of these classical facts is the $E$-valued form of the scalar statement, whose proof transfers verbatim since it uses only completeness of the codomain and the mean value inequality). We take $C^k([1,2];E)$ as the model space of the mapping manifold.
>
> **Step 1 — the case $W$ is an open subset of a Banach space.** Suppose first that $W=U$ is an open subset of a Banach space $E$ (this covers every use in the series; see the scope remark below). Then
> $$C^k([1,2];U)=\{\gamma\in C^k([1,2];E):\gamma([1,2])\subset U\}.$$
> We claim this is an *open* subset of the Banach space $C^k([1,2];E)$. Let $\gamma_0\in C^k([1,2];U)$. Its image $\gamma_0([1,2])$ is compact (continuous image of a compact set) and contained in the open set $U$, so the distance $\rho:=\operatorname{dist}(\gamma_0([1,2]),E\setminus U)$ is strictly positive (a continuous positive function on a compact set attains a positive minimum; if $U=E$ set $\rho:=1$). If $\lVert\gamma-\gamma_0\rVert_{C^k}<\rho$ then in particular $\sup_t\lVert\gamma(t)-\gamma_0(t)\rVert_E<\rho$, so $\gamma(t)\in U$ for every $t$ (definition of $\rho$), whence $\gamma\in C^k([1,2];U)$. Thus $C^k([1,2];U)$ contains the open $\lVert\cdot\rVert_{C^k}$-ball of radius $\rho$ about each of its points and is open. An open subset of a Banach space is a Banach manifold with the single identity chart, so $C^k([1,2];W)$ is a Banach manifold when $W=U$ is open in $E$.
>
> **Step 2 — the fixed-endpoint condition cuts out a closed complemented affine subspace.** Consider the *evaluation-at-endpoints* map
> $$\operatorname{ev}\colon C^k([1,2];E)\to E\times E,\qquad \operatorname{ev}(\gamma):=(\gamma(1),\gamma(2)).$$
> It is linear, and it is bounded because $\lVert\operatorname{ev}(\gamma)\rVert_{E\times E}=\lVert\gamma(1)\rVert_E+\lVert\gamma(2)\rVert_E\le 2\sup_t\lVert\gamma(t)\rVert_E\le 2\lVert\gamma\rVert_{C^k}$ (definition of the $C^k$-norm). It is surjective: given $(a,b)\in E\times E$ the affine map $\gamma(t):=a+(t-1)(b-a)$ is a $C^k$ (indeed polynomial) curve with $\gamma(1)=a$, $\gamma(2)=b$. Hence its kernel $K:=\ker\operatorname{ev}=\{\gamma:\gamma(1)=\gamma(2)=0\}$ is a closed subspace (kernel of a bounded operator, [[Def - Banach Manifold and Smooth Maps between Banach Spaces|continuity]]) and is complemented: writing $P(\gamma)(t):=\gamma(t)-\big[\gamma(1)+(t-1)(\gamma(2)-\gamma(1))\big]$ defines a bounded linear projection of $C^k([1,2];E)$ onto $K$ (it is bounded, being a composition of bounded evaluations and the affine-interpolation map, and $P^2=P$ because the interpolant of a curve already vanishing at both endpoints is $0$), whose complementary summand $\ker P$ is the two-dimensional space of affine curves. Now
> $$\Gamma(w_1,w_2)=\operatorname{ev}^{-1}(w_1,w_2)\cap C^k([1,2];U)$$
> is the intersection of the closed affine subspace $\operatorname{ev}^{-1}(w_1,w_2)=\gamma_*+K$ (where $\gamma_*(t):=w_1+(t-1)(w_2-w_1)$ is one solution) with the open set $C^k([1,2];U)$ of Step 1. An affine subspace $\gamma_*+K$ with $K$ closed and complemented is a Banach manifold modelled on $K$ (translation by $-\gamma_*$ is an affine chart onto the Banach space $K$), and intersecting with an open set keeps it a Banach manifold (an open subset of a manifold is a manifold). This is exactly what "the fixed-endpoint condition cuts out a closed affine subspace" means, made precise. Therefore $\Gamma(w_1,w_2)$ is a Banach manifold modelled on the Banach space $K=\{\gamma\in C^k([1,2];E):\gamma(1)=\gamma(2)=0\}$.
>
> **Conclusion.** When $W$ is (an open subset of) a Banach space, $C^k([1,2];W)$ is a Banach manifold and $\Gamma(w_1,w_2)$ is the Banach submanifold cut out by the two bounded, surjective endpoint evaluations, modelled on the closed complemented subspace of loops vanishing at both endpoints. This is all that any construction in the series requires. $\blacksquare$

> [!note] Scope remark — the general Banach-manifold target
> For a **general** connected Banach manifold $W$ modelled on a separable Banach space, $C^k([1,2];W)$ is still a Banach manifold, but its charts can no longer be a single identity map: a chart at a curve $\gamma_0$ is built from a chart of $W$ around a tubular neighbourhood of $\gamma_0([1,2])$ (equivalently, from a spray or connection on $W$), and the transition maps are shown smooth by the $\Omega$-lemma on composition of $C^k$ maps. This is Palais's mapping-manifold construction (R. Palais, *Foundations of Global Non-Linear Analysis*, Ch. IV). **It is not reproduced here and nothing in this series depends on it**, because in every application the parameter manifold $W$ is a Banach space or an open or affine subset of one — the target value in the ordinary degree ($W=Y$), or the affine space of perturbations in Seiberg–Witten theory ($W=\eta_0+L^2_k$-forms). The single case proved above is therefore the operative one.

**Is an instance — recovering the ordinary Sard–Smale construction by taking the value as the parameter.** Let $M$ be a closed surface and, on the Sobolev completions of chapter IX with $2k>\dim M$, consider the semilinear elliptic map $u\mapsto\Delta u+u^3$, which is a smooth proper Fredholm map of index zero ([[Ex - Semilinear Elliptic Maps are Fredholm|the semilinear elliptic map is Fredholm of index zero]]). Take the parameter manifold to be the *target itself*, $W:=Y$, and define
$$\mathcal F\colon X\times Y\to Y,\qquad \mathcal F(u,w):=\Delta u+u^3-w,$$
with the fixed value $y:=0$ and distinguished parameter $w_0:=0$, so that $F=\mathcal F_{w_0}\colon u\mapsto\Delta u+u^3$. We verify (A)–(C) clause by clause. **(A):** the slice $\mathcal F_w(u)=\Delta u+u^3-w$ differs from the Fredholm map $u\mapsto\Delta u+u^3$ by the constant $-w$, and translating the codomain does not change the differential $d_u\mathcal F_w=\Delta+3u^2$, so $\mathcal F_w$ is Fredholm of index $\operatorname{index}\Delta=0$ for every $w$. **(B):** $\mathcal F_0=F$ by construction. **(C):** the total differential is $d_{(u,w)}\mathcal F(\dot u,\dot w)=(\Delta+3u^2)\dot u-\dot w$; taking $\dot u=0$ and letting $\dot w$ range over $T_wY=Y$ shows the parameter directions alone already surject onto $Y$, so $d_{(u,w)}\mathcal F$ is surjective at every point, and in particular $0$ is a regular value of $\mathcal F$ — hypothesis (C) holds automatically, with the *value* directions doing all the work. Now the good parameters are, by definition, exactly those $w$ for which $0$ is a regular value of $\mathcal F_w$, that is, for which $w$ is a regular value of the map $u\mapsto\Delta u+u^3$ (since $\mathcal F_w(u)=0\iff\Delta u+u^3=w$, and $d_u\mathcal F_w=d_u(\Delta u+u^3)$); so $W_0$ *is* the set of regular values of $u\mapsto\Delta u+u^3$, and its residuality is exactly the [[Thm - Sard-Smale Theorem|Sard–Smale theorem]] applied to that one map. The parametric construction has reproduced the ordinary one: perturbing the map by an additive parameter is the same as perturbing the value, and the two definitions of $\deg_2$ coincide term for term.

**Is NOT an instance — a family failing (D).** Take $X=Y=W=\mathbb R$ (finite-dimensional Banach manifolds), the value $y:=0$, and
$$\mathcal F\colon\mathbb R\times\mathbb R\to\mathbb R,\qquad \mathcal F(x,w):=\arctan x-w,$$
with $w_0:=0$, so $F=\mathcal F_0=\arctan$. Hypotheses (A)–(C) all hold: each slice $\mathcal F_w=\arctan(\cdot)-w$ is a smooth map between one-dimensional manifolds, hence Fredholm of index $1-1=0$ (A); $\mathcal F_0=\arctan=F$ (B); and $\partial_w\mathcal F=-1\neq 0$ makes $d_{(x,w)}\mathcal F$ surjective everywhere, so $0$ is a regular value of the total map (C). But **(D) fails**: the slice $\mathcal F_w=\arctan(\cdot)-w$ maps $\mathbb R$ diffeomorphically onto the bounded open interval $(-\tfrac\pi2-w,\tfrac\pi2-w)$, and the preimage of a compact set that reaches an endpoint of this interval is unbounded, hence not compact — the map is not proper. The consequence is exactly the pathology the definition is designed to exclude. For $|w|<\tfrac\pi2$ the equation $\arctan x=w$ has the single solution $x=\tan w$, so $\#\mathcal F_w^{-1}(0)=1$; for $|w|\ge\tfrac\pi2$ it has none, so $\#\mathcal F_w^{-1}(0)=0$. Every $w$ is a good parameter (each slice is a diffeomorphism onto its image, so $0$ is regular whenever it is attained, and vacuously regular otherwise), yet the count jumps from $1$ to $0$ as $w$ crosses $\pm\tfrac\pi2$. There is no single number $\deg_2 F$: the generic-parameter degree is *not defined* for this family, precisely because properness is absent. This is the same mechanism that broke the ordinary degree for non-proper maps.

**Corollary — a nonzero degree forces solvability.** *If $(\mathcal F,W,w_0,y)$ is a proper parametric family with $\operatorname{index}F=0$ and $\deg_2 F\neq 0$ in $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$, then for every good parameter $w\in W_0$ the equation $\mathcal F_w(x)=y$ has a solution.* By the definition of the generic-parameter degree, $\deg_2 F=\#\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)\bmod 2$ for any $w\in W_0$; if this residue is nonzero then $\#\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)$ is odd, in particular at least $1$, so $\mathcal F_w^{-1}(y)\neq\varnothing$ and a solution exists. (This is the parametric form of the surjectivity corollary of the mod-2 degree; it is the abstract reason a nonvanishing Seiberg–Witten invariant forces the monopole equations to have solutions for every generic perturbation.) $\blacksquare$

**Calibration check.** First, re-examine the value-as-parameter example and confirm that $W_0$ is the set of regular values of $u\mapsto\Delta u+u^3$: the equivalence $\mathcal F_w(u)=0\iff\Delta u+u^3=w$ together with $d_u\mathcal F_w=d_u(\Delta u+u^3)$ says "$0$ regular for $\mathcal F_w$" is literally "$w$ regular for $u\mapsto\Delta u+u^3$," so the parametric $W_0$ and the ordinary regular-value set are the same object. Second, verify that hypothesis (C) is strictly weaker than "$y$ is a regular value of every $\mathcal F_w$": in the $\arctan$ non-example, (C) holds at every point through the $\partial_w$ direction, and it happens that each slice is also regular — but construct for yourself a family where $d_x\mathcal F_w$ has a one-dimensional cokernel at some $(x,w)\in\mathcal Z$ that is filled only by $\partial_w\mathcal F$, so that (C) holds while that particular slice is *not* regular at $y$; this is the generic situation and the whole reason the parameter is introduced. Third, check the role of connectedness of $W$ by asking what would go wrong if $W$ had two components, one containing $w_0$ and one not: the parametric count would still be locally constant on $W_0$ within each component, but there would be nothing forcing the two components' counts to agree, so "the" degree would be ill-posed — connectedness is what glues every good slice's count to $\deg_2 F$.
