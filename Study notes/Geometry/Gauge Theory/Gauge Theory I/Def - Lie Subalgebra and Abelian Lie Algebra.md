# Notation

Throughout, $\mathbb{K}$ denotes the base field, which is either $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$; every vector space on this page is a vector space over $\mathbb{K}$, and "linear" means $\mathbb{K}$-linear. A Lie algebra is written $(V, [\cdot,\cdot])$ or, when the bracket is understood, simply $V$; we also use the customary Fraktur letters $\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{h}$ for Lie algebras. Elements of $V$ are written $u, v, w$ (or $X, Y, Z$ when $V$ is a Lie algebra of a Lie group), and $[v, w] \in V$ is the bracket of $v$ and $w$. A vector subspace $W \subset V$ is a subset closed under addition and under multiplication by scalars in $\mathbb{K}$; the restricted bracket $[\cdot,\cdot]|_{W \times W}$ is the map $W \times W \to V$, $(w, w') \mapsto [w, w']$. For a smooth manifold $M$, $\mathfrak{X}(M)$ is the real vector space of [[Def - Smooth Vector Field|smooth vector fields]] on $M$, and $[X, Y] \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ is the [[Def - The Lie Bracket of Vector Fields|Lie bracket of vector fields]], the vector field acting on $f \in C^\infty(M)$ by $[X, Y]f = X(Yf) - Y(Xf)$. The symbol $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ is the vector space of $n \times n$ matrices with entries in $\mathbb{K}$; $E_{ij} \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ is the matrix whose $(i, j)$ entry is $1$ and whose other entries are $0$; $1_n$ is the identity matrix; $\operatorname{tr} A = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii}$ is the [[Def - Trace|trace]] of $A = (a_{ij})$; $A^t$ is the transpose. On $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the standard basis $e_1, e_2, e_3$, the cross product of $a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$ and $b = (b_1, b_2, b_3)$ is the vector $a \times b := (a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2,\; a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3,\; a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)$, and $a \cdot b := a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3$ is the dot product. The full registry for the chapter is on [[Gauge Theory I — Lie Groups, Representations, and Group Actions]].

This is a compound page: it defines three interlocking notions — the Lie algebra axioms (restated from [[Def - Lie Algebra]], now over $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$), the abelian Lie algebra, and the Lie subalgebra — because the second and third are defined by reference to the first, because the examples that certify the first (matrices under the commutator, $\mathbb{R}^3$ under the cross product, vector fields under the Lie bracket) are exactly the ambient algebras inside which the subalgebras of this chapter live, and because Bär introduces all three on one page (Definition 1.2.1, Example 1.2.2 and Definition 1.2.3, pp. 10–11).

> [!warning] Convention: the form of the Jacobi identity, the base field, and a misprint in the source
> Bär (Definition 1.2.1, p. 10) writes the Jacobi identity as $[\,[u, v], w] + [\,[v, w], u] + [\,[w, u], v] = 0$, with the nested bracket on the *left*; the vault's [[Def - Lie Algebra]] (following Lee) writes it as $[u, [v, w]] + [v, [w, u]] + [w, [u, v]] = 0$, nested on the *right*. In the presence of antisymmetry the two are the same identity up to an overall sign — this is proved in the Definition section below — and the series uses either freely. Bär allows $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$; [[Def - Lie Algebra]] fixes $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$; a complex Lie algebra is in particular a real one by restriction of scalars, so nothing proved for real Lie algebras is lost. Finally, Bär's antisymmetry axiom is misprinted as "$[v, w] = -[v, w]$" (which would force every bracket to vanish); the intended and correct statement, used on this page, is $[v, w] = -[w, v]$.

# Axiom Motivation

Where does the notion of a Lie algebra come from, and why these three axioms? The purpose of this chapter is to attach to every Lie group $G$ a linear object $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$ on which computations can be done by linear algebra, and to attach to every matrix group its algebra of "infinitesimal generators". Before we can say which linear object that is, we must say what kind of structure it carries, and the Lie algebra axioms are that structure. The present page has two aims: to record the axioms in the form the series uses, with the reasons behind each one, and to set up the two words — *abelian* and *subalgebra* — through which almost every Lie algebra of this chapter will be described ("$\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is the subalgebra of antisymmetric matrices in $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{R})$", "$\mathfrak{u}(1)$ is abelian").

**The model example and the remark that explains the axioms.** Bär's guiding example (Example 1.2.2.2) is $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ with the commutator $[A, B] := AB - BA$. Matrix multiplication is associative, but the commutator is not: $[\,[A, B], C]$ and $[A, [B, C]]$ are different matrices in general. What replaces associativity? Bär's remark on p. 10 is the key: *"the Jacobi identity can be thought of as a replacement for associativity."* We now make that remark precise. Expanding the cyclic sum of nested commutators produces twelve triple products of matrices, and every triple product appears exactly twice with opposite signs — but only if we are permitted to identify $(AB)C$ with $A(BC)$, which is precisely associativity. So the Jacobi identity is what associativity of the underlying product *becomes* when one sees only the commutator; it is the shadow that associativity casts on the bracket. Conversely, an abstract bracket that is bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the Jacobi identity behaves, for every purpose in this chapter, exactly as if it were the commutator of some associative product — and it is a theorem — the **Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt theorem**, not proved and not used in this series — that it always is one. The three axioms are therefore not an arbitrary list: they are exactly the properties of the commutator that survive forgetting the product.

**Desiderata.** We want a structure that (a) lives on a vector space, so that the tangent space $T_e G$ of a Lie group can carry it; (b) is compatible with the linear structure, so that it can be computed on a basis, restricted to subspaces, and transported by linear maps; (c) records only *non-commutativity* — the extent to which two infinitesimal motions fail to commute — and nothing else, since the bracket of left-invariant vector fields is a commutator of derivations; (d) is constrained tightly enough that the map $X \mapsto [X, \cdot]$ respects the bracket, since that is what makes the adjoint representation a representation. Axiom (i) delivers (a) and (b); axiom (ii) delivers (c); axiom (iii) delivers (d). We now show, clause by clause, what fails when each is dropped.

**Dropping bilinearity (clause (i)).** Without bilinearity the bracket cannot be computed on a basis and does not restrict to subspaces in any controlled way, and "the bracket of $2v$ with $w$" need bear no relation to "twice the bracket of $v$ with $w$". Clauses (ii) and (iii) do not force clause (i): on $\mathbb{R}^2$ define
$$[v, w] := v_1 w_1 (v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1)\, e_2 .$$
This bracket is antisymmetric, since exchanging $v$ and $w$ leaves the symmetric factor $v_1 w_1$ unchanged and changes the sign of the determinant factor $v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1$. It satisfies the Jacobi identity for a structural reason: every bracket is a multiple of $e_2$, and the bracket of any multiple $x = \lambda e_2$ of $e_2$ with any $w$ is $[x, w] = x_1 w_1 (x_1 w_2 - x_2 w_1) e_2 = 0$ because $x_1 = 0$; hence each of the three nested brackets $[\,[u, v], w]$, $[\,[v, w], u]$, $[\,[w, u], v]$ is the bracket of a multiple of $e_2$ with something, and all three vanish, so their sum is $0$. But it is not bilinear: $[e_1, e_1 + e_2] = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot (1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 1)\, e_2 = e_2$ (substituting $v = e_1$, $w = e_1 + e_2$), whereas $[e_1, e_1] = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot (0 - 0)\, e_2 = 0$ and $[e_1, e_2] = 1 \cdot 0 \cdot (\ldots)\, e_2 = 0$, so $[e_1, e_1 + e_2] \neq [e_1, e_1] + [e_1, e_2]$; and $[2v, w] = 4[v, w]$ (the formula is homogeneous of degree two in $v$), so homogeneity fails as well. Clause (i) is therefore an independent requirement, and it is what makes a Lie algebra an object of linear algebra at all.

**Dropping antisymmetry (clause (ii)).** Antisymmetry is what makes the bracket record only non-commutativity: for a commutator, $[A, B] = AB - BA$ vanishes when $A$ and $B$ commute and is odd under exchanging them, and $[A, A] = 0$ always. Bär's own example of what to exclude is the product itself: on $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ the bilinear bracket $[A, B] := AB$ has $[1_n, 1_n] = 1_n \neq -1_n$, so it is not antisymmetric — and the "bracket of an element with itself" is non-zero, which has no meaning as a measure of non-commutativity. This example also fails the Jacobi identity ($[\,[1_n, 1_n], 1_n] + [\,[1_n, 1_n], 1_n] + [\,[1_n, 1_n], 1_n] = 3 \cdot 1_n \neq 0$), so to see that antisymmetry is independent of the other two clauses we give a bracket that is bilinear and satisfies Jacobi but is symmetric: on $\mathbb{R}^2$ put $[v, w] := v_1 w_1 e_2$. It is bilinear (each coordinate is a product of one coordinate of $v$ and one of $w$). Every bracket lies in $\operatorname{span}(e_2)$, whose first coordinate is $0$, so every nested bracket $[\,[u, v], w] = ([u, v])_1 w_1 e_2 = 0 \cdot w_1 e_2 = 0$ and the Jacobi identity holds trivially. But $[e_1, e_1] = e_2 \neq -e_2 = -[e_1, e_1]$. What breaks without antisymmetry is the entire dictionary with groups: the bracket on $T_e G$ is defined through the commutator of vector fields (or, for matrix groups, of matrices), and commutators are antisymmetric by construction; a symmetric bilinear product such as $v_1 w_1 e_2$ is a Jordan-type product, which is a different algebraic species with no infinitesimal-group interpretation.

**Dropping the Jacobi identity (clause (iii)).** A bilinear antisymmetric bracket that fails Jacobi is easy to write down in dimension three. On $\mathbb{R}^3$ define the bracket on the basis by $[e_1, e_2] := e_3$, $[e_2, e_3] := e_1$, $[e_3, e_1] := e_1$, together with $[e_i, e_i] := 0$ and $[e_j, e_i] := -[e_i, e_j]$, and extend bilinearly (a bilinear map is determined by, and may be freely prescribed on, pairs of basis vectors). Antisymmetry holds on basis pairs by construction and hence, by bilinearity, everywhere. But
$$[\,[e_1, e_2], e_3] + [\,[e_2, e_3], e_1] + [\,[e_3, e_1], e_2] = [e_3, e_3] + [e_1, e_1] + [e_1, e_2] = 0 + 0 + e_3 = e_3 \neq 0 \qquad \text{(by the prescribed values)}.$$
(Replacing $[e_3, e_1] := e_1$ by $[e_3, e_1] := e_2$ gives the cross-product bracket, which does satisfy Jacobi, as we verify in the Examples section; the failure is a genuine restriction, not a triviality.) What breaks is desideratum (d): write $\operatorname{ad}_u : V \to V$, $\operatorname{ad}_u(v) := [u, v]$. The identity $\operatorname{ad}_u([v, w]) = [\operatorname{ad}_u v, w] + [v, \operatorname{ad}_u w]$ — which says that $\operatorname{ad}_u$ is a *derivation* of the bracket — is, as we prove in the Definition section, exactly the Jacobi identity rewritten. Without it, $u \mapsto \operatorname{ad}_u$ is not a Lie algebra homomorphism $V \to \operatorname{End}(V)$, the adjoint representation of the group does not differentiate to a representation of the algebra (this is the content of [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]], which would fail), and the fundamental-vector-field map of a group action is no longer a homomorphism into $\mathfrak{X}(M)$. Every structural use of Lie algebras in the later chapters passes through $\operatorname{ad}$, so Jacobi is the load-bearing axiom.

**The two derived notions.** Once the axioms are fixed, two questions arise immediately. First: which subspaces $W \subset V$ inherit the structure? A subspace inherits bilinearity, antisymmetry, and Jacobi from $V$ automatically, since these are identities among elements; the only thing that can fail is that $[w, w']$ might leave $W$. Closure under the bracket is therefore the one condition to impose, and a subspace satisfying it is a *Lie subalgebra*. The failure is concrete: in $\operatorname{Mat}(2 \times 2; \mathbb{R})$ the subspace $W = \operatorname{span}(E_{12}, E_{21})$ has $[E_{12}, E_{21}] = E_{12}E_{21} - E_{21}E_{12} = E_{11} - E_{22} \notin W$ (by the multiplication rule $E_{ij}E_{kl} = \delta_{jk} E_{il}$), so the restricted bracket does not map $W \times W$ into $W$ and $W$ carries no Lie algebra structure of its own. The natural strengthening of "subalgebra" is "ideal" — closure under bracketing with *all* of $V$, $[V, W] \subset W$ — which is the Lie-algebraic counterpart of a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] and is the condition under which the quotient $V/W$ is again a Lie algebra; we do not need ideals in this chapter and only record the name. Second: what is the simplest possible bracket? The zero bracket, $[v, w] = 0$ for all $v, w$, satisfies all three axioms (Example 1.2.2.1) and is the Lie algebra of every commutative Lie group; a Lie algebra with zero bracket is *abelian*. The word is chosen to match the group-theoretic word, and the match is exact in the sense the chapter later makes precise: the group $U(1) = SO(2)$ of [[Thm - SO(2) is Isomorphic to U(1)]] is abelian and its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{u}(1) = i\mathbb{R}$ is one-dimensional, hence abelian by the observation, proved below, that every one-dimensional Lie algebra is abelian.

Could a reader reconstruct the definitions from the above? The axioms are the three properties of the matrix commutator that survive forgetting the product; the subalgebra condition is the one thing a subspace does not inherit for free; the abelian case is the bracket that records no non-commutativity at all. Yes.

# The Definition

Let $V$ be a [[Def - Vector Space|vector space]] over $\mathbb{K} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$ together with a map $[\cdot,\cdot] : V \times V \to V$. The pair $(V, [\cdot,\cdot])$ is a **Lie algebra** (Bär, Definition 1.2.1) if and only if the following three axioms hold:

1. **Bilinearity.** For all $u, v, w \in V$ and $a, b \in \mathbb{K}$: $[au + bv, w] = a[u, w] + b[v, w]$ and $[w, au + bv] = a[w, u] + b[w, v]$.
2. **Antisymmetry.** For all $v, w \in V$: $[v, w] = -[w, v]$. (Bär's text misprints this as $[v, w] = -[v, w]$; the form given here is the intended one.)
3. **Jacobi identity.** For all $u, v, w \in V$:
$$[\,[u, v], w] + [\,[v, w], u] + [\,[w, u], v] = 0.$$

The map $[\cdot,\cdot]$ is the **Lie bracket** of $V$. This is [[Def - Lie Algebra]] with the base field enlarged to $\mathbb{K}$; the two agree verbatim for $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$.

A Lie algebra $(V, [\cdot,\cdot])$ is **abelian** (Bär, Example 1.2.2.1) if and only if its bracket is identically zero: $[v, w] = 0$ for all $v, w \in V$.

Let $(V, [\cdot,\cdot])$ be a Lie algebra and $W \subset V$ a vector [[Def - Subspace|subspace]]. Then $W$, together with the restricted map $[\cdot,\cdot]|_{W \times W}$, is a **Lie subalgebra** of $V$ (Bär, Definition 1.2.3) if and only if $W$ is closed under the bracket:
$$\text{for all } w, w' \in W: \quad [w, w'] \in W.$$

Bär states without proof that a Lie subalgebra is a Lie algebra in its own right; we prove it, together with the equivalent formulations that the rest of the chapter uses.

> [!note]- Lemma A: a Lie subalgebra is a Lie algebra
> **Statement.** Let $(V, [\cdot,\cdot])$ be a Lie algebra and $W \subset V$ a Lie subalgebra. Then $(W, [\cdot,\cdot]|_{W \times W})$ is a Lie algebra.
>
> **Proof.** We assume $W$ is a vector subspace of $V$ closed under the bracket, and we need to show that the restricted bracket is a map $W \times W \to W$ satisfying axioms 1–3.
>
> **Step 0 (the restricted bracket has the right codomain).** For $w, w' \in W$ the value $[w, w']$ is an element of $V$ by definition of the bracket on $V$, and it lies in $W$ by the closure hypothesis. Hence $[\cdot,\cdot]|_{W \times W}$ is a well-defined map $W \times W \to W$.
>
> **Axiom 1 for $W$.** Let $u, v, w \in W$ and $a, b \in \mathbb{K}$. Since $W$ is a subspace, $au + bv \in W$, so both sides of $[au + bv, w] = a[u, w] + b[v, w]$ are brackets of elements of $W$; the equality itself holds because it holds in $V$ (axiom 1 for $V$), and the restricted bracket takes the same values as the bracket of $V$. The identity in the second slot is verified by the same substitution with the roles of the two slots exchanged, each step surviving because axiom 1 for $V$ is stated for both slots.
>
> **Axiom 2 for $W$.** For $v, w \in W$, $[v, w] = -[w, v]$ holds in $V$ (axiom 2 for $V$), and both sides are elements of $W$ (by Step 0 and since $W$ is closed under scalar multiplication by $-1$).
>
> **Axiom 3 for $W$.** For $u, v, w \in W$, the three nested brackets $[\,[u, v], w]$, $[\,[v, w], u]$, $[\,[w, u], v]$ are brackets of elements of $W$ (by Step 0 applied twice), so they are computed by the restricted bracket, and their sum is $0$ because the Jacobi identity holds in $V$ (axiom 3 for $V$).
>
> Therefore $(W, [\cdot,\cdot]|_{W \times W})$ satisfies all three axioms, and a Lie subalgebra is a Lie algebra in its own right. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma B: the two forms of the Jacobi identity coincide, and Jacobi says that ad is a derivation
> **Statement.** Let $V$ be a vector space with a bilinear antisymmetric bracket. For $u \in V$ write $\operatorname{ad}_u : V \to V$, $\operatorname{ad}_u(v) := [u, v]$. Then the following three statements about $V$ are equivalent:
> (a) $[\,[u, v], w] + [\,[v, w], u] + [\,[w, u], v] = 0$ for all $u, v, w$ (Bär's form);
> (b) $[u, [v, w]] + [v, [w, u]] + [w, [u, v]] = 0$ for all $u, v, w$ (the form on [[Def - Lie Algebra]]);
> (c) $\operatorname{ad}_u([v, w]) = [\operatorname{ad}_u v, w] + [v, \operatorname{ad}_u w]$ for all $u, v, w$ ($\operatorname{ad}_u$ is a derivation of the bracket).
>
> **Proof.** We assume bilinearity and antisymmetry throughout and need to show (a) $\Leftrightarrow$ (b) and (b) $\Leftrightarrow$ (c).
>
> **(a) $\Leftrightarrow$ (b).** Fix $u, v, w \in V$. By antisymmetry applied to the outer bracket of each term,
> $$[\,[u, v], w] = -[w, [u, v]], \qquad [\,[v, w], u] = -[u, [v, w]], \qquad [\,[w, u], v] = -[v, [w, u]] \qquad \text{(axiom 2, outer bracket)}.$$
> Adding the three lines,
> $$[\,[u, v], w] + [\,[v, w], u] + [\,[w, u], v] = -\big([u, [v, w]] + [v, [w, u]] + [w, [u, v]]\big) \qquad \text{(sum of the three displayed identities, terms regrouped)}.$$
> The left side vanishes for all $u, v, w$ if and only if the right side does, and the right side vanishes if and only if the bracketed sum does. Hence (a) holds if and only if (b) holds.
>
> **(b) $\Rightarrow$ (c).** Fix $u, v, w$. Starting from (b),
> $$[u, [v, w]] = -[v, [w, u]] - [w, [u, v]] \qquad \text{(by (b), solving for the first term)}$$
> $$= [v, [u, w]] + [\,[u, v], w] \qquad \text{(axiom 2 applied to the inner bracket } [w, u] = -[u, w] \text{ and to the outer bracket } -[w, [u,v]] = [\,[u,v], w] \text{, using bilinearity to pull out the signs)}$$
> $$= [\,[u, v], w] + [v, [u, w]] \qquad \text{(reordering the two summands)}.$$
> Writing $[u, \cdot]$ as $\operatorname{ad}_u$, this reads $\operatorname{ad}_u([v, w]) = [\operatorname{ad}_u v, w] + [v, \operatorname{ad}_u w]$, which is (c).
>
> **(c) $\Rightarrow$ (b).** Fix $u, v, w$ and assume (c): $[u, [v, w]] = [\,[u, v], w] + [v, [u, w]]$. Then
> $$[u, [v, w]] + [v, [w, u]] + [w, [u, v]] = [\,[u, v], w] + [v, [u, w]] + [v, [w, u]] + [w, [u, v]] \qquad \text{(substituting (c) for the first term)}$$
> $$= [\,[u, v], w] + [w, [u, v]] + \big([v, [u, w]] + [v, [w, u]]\big) \qquad \text{(regrouping)}$$
> $$= [\,[u, v], w] + [w, [u, v]] + [v, [u, w] + [w, u]] \qquad \text{(bilinearity in the second slot)}$$
> $$= [\,[u, v], w] - [\,[u, v], w] + [v, 0] = 0 \qquad \text{(axiom 2: } [w, [u,v]] = -[\,[u,v], w] \text{ and } [w, u] = -[u, w] \text{; bilinearity gives } [v, 0] = 0).$$
> This is (b).
>
> Therefore (a), (b), and (c) are equivalent, and in particular the series may use either printed form of the Jacobi identity and may read it as the statement that every $\operatorname{ad}_u$ is a derivation of the bracket. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma C: antisymmetry is equivalent to the alternating property; a one-dimensional Lie algebra is abelian
> **Statement.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{K} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$ with a bilinear bracket. Then (i) $[v, w] = -[w, v]$ for all $v, w$ if and only if $[v, v] = 0$ for all $v$; (ii) if $V$ is a Lie algebra with $\dim V = 1$, then $V$ is abelian.
>
> **Proof.** **(i), direction 1 (antisymmetric $\Rightarrow$ alternating).** Assume antisymmetry and fix $v \in V$. Putting $w = v$ gives $[v, v] = -[v, v]$ (axiom 2 with $w = v$), hence $2[v, v] = 0$ (adding $[v, v]$ to both sides), hence $[v, v] = 0$ (since $2$ is invertible in $\mathbb{K}$, which is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$).
>
> **(i), direction 2 (alternating $\Rightarrow$ antisymmetric).** Assume $[x, x] = 0$ for every $x \in V$ and fix $v, w$. Then
> $$0 = [v + w, v + w] = [v, v] + [v, w] + [w, v] + [w, w] = [v, w] + [w, v] \qquad \text{(alternating property for } x = v + w \text{; bilinearity in both slots; alternating property for } x = v \text{ and } x = w).$$
> Hence $[v, w] = -[w, v]$.
>
> **(ii).** Let $V$ be a one-dimensional Lie algebra and choose a non-zero $x \in V$, so that every element is a scalar multiple of $x$. For $v = ax$, $w = bx$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{K}$,
> $$[v, w] = [ax, bx] = ab[x, x] = ab \cdot 0 = 0 \qquad \text{(bilinearity; part (i) direction 1)}.$$
> Therefore the bracket vanishes identically and $V$ is abelian. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma D: closure under the bracket may be checked on a basis
> **Statement.** Let $(V, [\cdot,\cdot])$ be a Lie algebra, $W \subset V$ a subspace, and $(w_\alpha)_{\alpha \in I}$ a basis of $W$ (any spanning set of $W$ suffices). Then $W$ is a Lie subalgebra of $V$ if and only if $[w_\alpha, w_\beta] \in W$ for all $\alpha, \beta \in I$.
>
> **Proof.** **Direction 1.** If $W$ is a subalgebra then $[w_\alpha, w_\beta] \in W$ because $w_\alpha, w_\beta \in W$ (the closure condition applied to these two elements).
>
> **Direction 2.** Assume $[w_\alpha, w_\beta] \in W$ for all $\alpha, \beta$ and let $w, w' \in W$. Since $(w_\alpha)$ spans $W$, there are finitely many indices and scalars with $w = \sum_\alpha a_\alpha w_\alpha$ and $w' = \sum_\beta b_\beta w_\beta$ (finite sums). Then
> $$[w, w'] = \Big[\sum_\alpha a_\alpha w_\alpha, \sum_\beta b_\beta w_\beta\Big] = \sum_{\alpha, \beta} a_\alpha b_\beta [w_\alpha, w_\beta] \qquad \text{(bilinearity, applied first in the left slot and then in the right slot)},$$
> and the right side is a finite linear combination of elements of $W$ (by hypothesis), hence lies in $W$ (since $W$ is a subspace). So $W$ is closed under the bracket, and by definition it is a Lie subalgebra. $\blacksquare$

**Unpacking in the smallest cases.** In dimension one, Lemma C says there is exactly one Lie algebra structure, the abelian one. In dimension two the Jacobi identity is automatic, and it is worth seeing why. Write $J(u, v, w) := [\,[u, v], w] + [\,[v, w], u] + [\,[w, u], v]$ for a bilinear antisymmetric bracket. Then $J$ is linear in each argument (each summand is a composition of bilinear maps), and $J$ vanishes whenever two of its arguments coincide:
$$J(u, u, w) = [\,[u, u], w] + [\,[u, w], u] + [\,[w, u], u] = 0 + [\,[u, w], u] - [\,[u, w], u] = 0 \qquad \text{(Lemma C(i) for } [u, u] = 0 \text{; antisymmetry } [w, u] = -[u, w] \text{ and bilinearity in the first slot)},$$
and the same computation with the repeated argument in positions $(1, 3)$ or $(2, 3)$ — namely $J(u, v, u) = [\,[u, v], u] + [\,[v, u], u] + [\,[u, u], v] = [\,[u, v], u] - [\,[u, v], u] + 0$ and $J(u, v, v) = [\,[u, v], v] + [\,[v, v], u] + [\,[v, u], v] = [\,[u, v], v] + 0 - [\,[u, v], v]$ — gives $0$ by the same two facts. Now let $V$ have basis $x, y$. By trilinearity, $J = 0$ on $V$ as soon as $J = 0$ on every triple of basis vectors; but every triple drawn from a two-element set $\{x, y\}$ has a repeated entry, so every such $J$ vanishes. Hence any bilinear antisymmetric bracket on a two-dimensional space is a Lie bracket, and it is determined by the single vector $[x, y] \in V$. The first genuine constraint appears in dimension three, which is why the counterexample in the Axiom Motivation section lives there and why the cross product on $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the first non-trivial example.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The definitions above can be restated in terms of maps, which is how they are used once homomorphisms enter in §1.3. A **Lie algebra homomorphism** $\varphi : V \to V'$ between Lie algebras is a linear map with $\varphi([v, w]) = [\varphi(v), \varphi(w)]$ for all $v, w \in V$. Two structural characterisations follow.

**A subspace is a Lie subalgebra if and only if its inclusion is a Lie algebra homomorphism for some bracket on it.** Let $W \subset V$ be a subspace and $\iota : W \to V$ the inclusion. If $W$ is a subalgebra, then with the restricted bracket $\iota([w, w']_W) = [w, w'] = [\iota w, \iota w']$ for all $w, w' \in W$ (since the restricted bracket takes the same values as the bracket of $V$), so $\iota$ is a homomorphism; and by Lemma A the restricted bracket is a Lie bracket on $W$. Conversely, suppose $W$ carries some Lie bracket $[\cdot,\cdot]_W$ for which $\iota$ is a homomorphism. Then for $w, w' \in W$, $[w, w'] = [\iota w, \iota w'] = \iota([w, w']_W) \in \iota(W) = W$ (homomorphism property of $\iota$), so $W$ is closed under the bracket of $V$, hence a subalgebra, and the given bracket $[\cdot,\cdot]_W$ is forced to equal the restricted one. So a Lie subalgebra is precisely a subspace whose inclusion is a morphism of Lie algebras, and the bracket making it so is unique.

**Abelian Lie algebras are the ones on which the adjoint maps vanish, equivalently the ones that are their own abelianisation.** One might guess that "abelian" could be characterised by "every linear subspace is a Lie subalgebra". That guess is wrong, and the counterexample is instructive. Let $V = \operatorname{span}(v, w)$ be two-dimensional with the bracket determined by $[v, w] := w$ (extended bilinearly and antisymmetrically; this is a Lie bracket by the dimension-two paragraph above). Its subspaces are $0$, the lines $\operatorname{span}(x)$, and $V$; the subspaces $0$ and $V$ are closed under the bracket trivially, and each line is closed because $[ax, bx] = ab[x, x] = 0 \in \operatorname{span}(x)$ (bilinearity; Lemma C(i)). So every subspace of $V$ is a Lie subalgebra, yet $V$ is not abelian, since $[v, w] = w \neq 0$. (Conversely, an abelian Lie algebra does have every subspace a subalgebra, since $[w, w'] = 0$ lies in every subspace — but the implication runs only one way.) This $V$ is the non-abelian two-dimensional Lie algebra; it appears inside $\mathfrak{gl}(2; \mathbb{R})$ as $\operatorname{span}(E_{11}, E_{12})$ with $[E_{11}, E_{12}] = E_{12}$, as the Calibration check below verifies. The correct structural characterisations of "abelian" are the two in Lemma E.

> [!note]- Lemma E: two structural characterisations of abelian Lie algebras
> **Statement.** For a Lie algebra $V$ over $\mathbb{K} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$ the following are equivalent: (a) $V$ is abelian; (b) $\operatorname{ad}_u = 0$ for every $u \in V$, where $\operatorname{ad}_u(v) = [u, v]$; (c) every linear map $\varphi : V \to V'$ into an abelian Lie algebra $V'$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism, and the identity map $\mathrm{id}_V : V \to V^{\mathrm{ab}}$, where $V^{\mathrm{ab}}$ denotes the same vector space with the zero bracket, is a Lie algebra homomorphism.
>
> **Proof.** **(a) $\Leftrightarrow$ (b).** $\operatorname{ad}_u = 0$ for all $u$ means $[u, v] = 0$ for all $u, v$ (definition of $\operatorname{ad}_u$, quantified over $v$), which is the definition of abelian; the two statements are the same statement.
>
> **(a) $\Rightarrow$ (c).** Let $V$ be abelian, $V'$ abelian, and $\varphi : V \to V'$ linear. For $v, w \in V$, $\varphi([v, w]) = \varphi(0) = 0$ (bracket of $V$ is zero; $\varphi$ linear) and $[\varphi v, \varphi w] = 0$ (bracket of $V'$ is zero); so $\varphi([v, w]) = [\varphi v, \varphi w]$ and $\varphi$ is a homomorphism. In particular $\mathrm{id}_V : V \to V^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is a homomorphism, since $V^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is abelian.
>
> **(c) $\Rightarrow$ (a).** Assume $\mathrm{id}_V : V \to V^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is a homomorphism. Then for all $v, w \in V$, $[v, w] = \mathrm{id}_V([v, w]) = [\mathrm{id}_V v, \mathrm{id}_V w]_{V^{\mathrm{ab}}} = 0$ (homomorphism property; the bracket of $V^{\mathrm{ab}}$ is zero). Hence $V$ is abelian.
>
> Therefore (a), (b), (c) are equivalent: an abelian Lie algebra is one whose adjoint maps all vanish, equivalently one on which the "forget the bracket" map to the same space with zero bracket is a morphism. $\blacksquare$

The upshot of this section is the two clean statements: a Lie subalgebra is a subspace whose inclusion is a morphism, with a uniquely determined bracket; an abelian Lie algebra is one all of whose adjoint maps vanish, equivalently one that is its own abelianisation.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**Associative algebras and the commutator functor.** Every associative algebra $A$ over $\mathbb{K}$ — matrices, the endomorphisms $\operatorname{End}(V)$ of a vector space, the linear differential operators on a manifold — becomes a Lie algebra under $[a, b] := ab - ba$; the verification for matrices in the Examples section uses only associativity and distributivity and so applies verbatim to any associative algebra. A subspace of $A$ that is closed under commutators is a Lie subalgebra of $A$ even when it is not closed under products: the antisymmetric matrices $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ are closed under $AB - BA$ but not under $AB$ (for antisymmetric $A, B$ one has $(AB)^t = B^t A^t = BA$, so $AB$ is antisymmetric exactly when $A$ and $B$ anticommute, and symmetric exactly when they commute), and the traceless matrices $\mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ are closed under commutators but not under products ($E_{12} E_{21} = E_{11}$ has trace $1$). This is the ordinary situation in this chapter: the Lie algebras $\mathfrak{o}(n)$, $\mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{K})$, $\mathfrak{u}(n)$, $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ of [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]] are all Lie subalgebras of $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ with the commutator, and none of them is a subalgebra for the associative product.

**Derivations and vector fields.** The Lie bracket of vector fields is the commutator of derivations: a vector field is a derivation $X : C^\infty(M) \to C^\infty(M)$, the composition $X \circ Y$ is not a derivation (it has second-order terms), but $X \circ Y - Y \circ X$ is, because the second-order terms are symmetric in $X$ and $Y$ and cancel. So $\mathfrak{X}(M)$ is a Lie subalgebra of the associative algebra of all linear operators on $C^\infty(M)$ with the commutator bracket — a subspace closed under commutators but not under composition, exactly as in the previous paragraph. The [[Def - Left-Invariant Vector Field|left-invariant vector fields]] on a Lie group $G$ form a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$ ([[Thm - Left-Invariant Vector Fields Form a Lie Algebra]]: the bracket of two left-invariant fields is left-invariant), and this subalgebra *is* the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ of $G$ ([[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group]]). Every Lie algebra of a Lie group in this series is therefore, at bottom, a Lie subalgebra of an algebra of vector fields.

**Poisson brackets.** The smooth functions on a symplectic manifold form a Lie algebra under the [[Def - Poisson Bracket|Poisson bracket]] ([[Thm - Poisson Bracket is a Lie Algebra Structure on Smooth Functions]]), an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra of the same kind as $\mathfrak{X}(M)$; and the standard fact that the map $f \mapsto X_f$ to the Hamiltonian vector field is, up to a convention-dependent sign, a Lie algebra homomorphism into $\mathfrak{X}(M)$ (proved in the geometric-mechanics notes, not here) exhibits its image as a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{X}(M)$. Conserved quantities in mechanics — functions Poisson-commuting with the Hamiltonian — form a Lie subalgebra, and a family of conserved quantities in involution (all Poisson brackets vanish) is an abelian Lie subalgebra; this is the algebraic form of Liouville integrability.

**True name.** The definition of a Lie algebra is already operational: to check that a bracket is a Lie bracket one checks bilinearity, antisymmetry (equivalently $[v, v] = 0$, Lemma C), and the Jacobi identity (equivalently, that each $\operatorname{ad}_u$ is a derivation, Lemma B). The true name of a Lie subalgebra is "a subspace on which the bracket can be computed without leaving the subspace" — by Lemma D, "a subspace whose basis brackets stay inside". The true name of an abelian Lie algebra is "a vector space regarded as a Lie algebra with nothing to compute": every abelian Lie algebra of dimension $n$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{K}^n$ with the zero bracket, since any linear isomorphism between spaces with zero brackets is a Lie algebra isomorphism (Lemma E(c)).

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — any vector space with the zero bracket (Bär, Example 1.2.2.1).** Let $V$ be any vector space over $\mathbb{K}$ and define $[v, w] := 0$ for all $v, w \in V$. Bilinearity: $[au + bv, w] = 0 = a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 0 = a[u, w] + b[v, w]$, and the same computation in the second slot. Antisymmetry: $[v, w] = 0 = -0 = -[w, v]$. Jacobi: $[\,[u, v], w] + [\,[v, w], u] + [\,[w, u], v] = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0$ (each bracket is $0$ by definition). Hence $(V, 0)$ is a Lie algebra, and it is abelian by definition. In particular $\mathbb{K}^n$ with the zero bracket is an abelian Lie algebra of dimension $n$; for $n = 1$ this is, by Lemma C(ii), the only Lie algebra structure on $\mathbb{K}$.

**Is an instance — $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ with the commutator (Bär, Example 1.2.2.2).** Define $[A, B] := AB - BA$ for $A, B \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$. We verify the three axioms.

*Bilinearity.* For $A, A', B \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ and $a, a' \in \mathbb{K}$,
$$[aA + a'A', B] = (aA + a'A')B - B(aA + a'A') = a(AB - BA) + a'(A'B - BA') = a[A, B] + a'[A', B] \qquad \text{(distributivity of matrix multiplication over addition, and compatibility with scalars)},$$
and the identity in the second slot follows by the same computation with the roles of the left and right factors exchanged, each step surviving because distributivity holds on both sides.

*Antisymmetry.* $[B, A] = BA - AB = -(AB - BA) = -[A, B]$ (definition of the commutator; additive inverse).

*Jacobi identity — the twelve-term expansion.* Fix $A, B, C$. Expanding each nested commutator by the definition,
$$[\,[A, B], C] = (AB - BA)C - C(AB - BA) = ABC - BAC - CAB + CBA \qquad \text{(definition; distributivity; associativity, which lets us write } (AB)C = A(BC) =: ABC \text{ without brackets)},$$
$$[\,[B, C], A] = (BC - CB)A - A(BC - CB) = BCA - CBA - ABC + ACB \qquad \text{(the same, with } (A, B, C) \text{ replaced by } (B, C, A)),$$
$$[\,[C, A], B] = (CA - AC)B - B(CA - AC) = CAB - ACB - BCA + BAC \qquad \text{(the same, with } (A, B, C) \text{ replaced by } (C, A, B)).$$
Adding the three lines, the twelve monomials cancel in six pairs: $ABC$ (line 1) with $-ABC$ (line 2); $-BAC$ (line 1) with $+BAC$ (line 3); $-CAB$ (line 1) with $+CAB$ (line 3); $+CBA$ (line 1) with $-CBA$ (line 2); $+BCA$ (line 2) with $-BCA$ (line 3); $+ACB$ (line 2) with $-ACB$ (line 3). Therefore
$$[\,[A, B], C] + [\,[B, C], A] + [\,[C, A], B] = 0 \qquad \text{(sum of the three expansions; the six cancellations just listed)}.$$
Exactly as Bär remarks, the computation uses associativity at one point and one point only: the monomial written $ABC$ arises in line 1 as $(AB)C$ and in line 2 as $A(BC)$, and the two cancel only because $(AB)C = A(BC)$. This is the precise sense in which the Jacobi identity is associativity seen through the commutator (Bär's remark, p. 10). Hence $(\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}), [\cdot,\cdot])$ is a Lie algebra, denoted $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$; the expansion is drilled in [[Ex - The Jacobi Identity for the Matrix Commutator Follows from Associativity]], and the fact that this commutator is the bracket of the Lie group $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is [[Ex - The Lie Bracket on a Matrix Lie Algebra is the Commutator]]. For $n \geq 2$ the algebra is not abelian: $[E_{12}, E_{21}] = E_{11} - E_{22} \neq 0$ (by $E_{ij}E_{kl} = \delta_{jk}E_{il}$).

**Is an instance — $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the cross product (Bär, Example 1.2.2.3).** Define $[a, b] := a \times b$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the coordinate formula of the Notation section. Bär says the Jacobi identity "is a simple computation"; we write it, via the triple-product identity, which we also prove.

*Bilinearity.* Each coordinate of $a \times b$ — for instance $(a \times b)_1 = a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2$ — is a sum of products of one coordinate of $a$ and one coordinate of $b$, hence is linear in $a$ for fixed $b$ and linear in $b$ for fixed $a$ (a sum of bilinear monomials is bilinear); so $a \times b$ is bilinear.

*Antisymmetry.* $(b \times a)_1 = b_2 a_3 - b_3 a_2 = -(a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2) = -(a \times b)_1$ (commutativity of multiplication in $\mathbb{R}$); the second and third coordinates are handled by the same computation with the index triple $(1, 2, 3)$ replaced by $(2, 3, 1)$ and by $(3, 1, 2)$ respectively — this substitution is legitimate because the coordinate formula for $a \times b$ is invariant under the cyclic relabelling $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$ of the indices (the formula for $(a \times b)_2$ is the formula for $(a \times b)_1$ with every index advanced by one, and likewise for $(a \times b)_3$), and each step of the first-coordinate computation used only commutativity of real multiplication, which is index-independent. Hence $b \times a = -(a \times b)$.

*Triple-product identity.* We claim that for all $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}^3$,
$$a \times (b \times c) = (a \cdot c)\, b - (a \cdot b)\, c.$$
Write $d := b \times c = (b_2 c_3 - b_3 c_2,\; b_3 c_1 - b_1 c_3,\; b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1)$. The first coordinate of $a \times d$ is
$$(a \times d)_1 = a_2 d_3 - a_3 d_2 = a_2(b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1) - a_3(b_3 c_1 - b_1 c_3) \qquad \text{(coordinate formula for the cross product; the values of } d_2, d_3)$$
$$= b_1(a_2 c_2 + a_3 c_3) - c_1(a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3) \qquad \text{(expanding and collecting the terms containing } b_1 \text{ and those containing } c_1)$$
$$= b_1\big((a \cdot c) - a_1 c_1\big) - c_1\big((a \cdot b) - a_1 b_1\big) \qquad \text{(since } a \cdot c = a_1 c_1 + a_2 c_2 + a_3 c_3 \text{ and } a \cdot b = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3)$$
$$= (a \cdot c)\, b_1 - (a \cdot b)\, c_1 \qquad \text{(the terms } -a_1 b_1 c_1 \text{ and } +a_1 b_1 c_1 \text{ cancel)},$$
which is the first coordinate of $(a \cdot c) b - (a \cdot b) c$. The second and third coordinates follow by the cyclic relabelling $1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 1$ of the indices, which is legitimate for the reason given under antisymmetry: both the cross-product formula and the dot product are invariant under it, and each line of the computation above used only the coordinate formula, distributivity, commutativity of real multiplication, and the definition of the dot product, all of which are index-independent. This proves the triple-product identity.

*Jacobi identity.* Fix $u, v, w \in \mathbb{R}^3$. By antisymmetry, $[\,[u, v], w] = (u \times v) \times w = -\,w \times (u \times v)$, and by the triple-product identity with $(a, b, c) = (w, u, v)$,
$$(u \times v) \times w = -\big((w \cdot v)\, u - (w \cdot u)\, v\big) = (w \cdot u)\, v - (v \cdot w)\, u \qquad \text{(antisymmetry; triple-product identity; symmetry of the dot product)}.$$
Applying the same identity to the cyclic permutations $(v, w, u)$ and $(w, u, v)$ of $(u, v, w)$ — a substitution under which the displayed identity remains valid, since it was proved for arbitrary $u, v, w$ —
$$(v \times w) \times u = (u \cdot v)\, w - (w \cdot u)\, v, \qquad (w \times u) \times v = (v \cdot w)\, u - (u \cdot v)\, w.$$
Adding the three displayed expressions,
$$[\,[u, v], w] + [\,[v, w], u] + [\,[w, u], v] = \big[(w \cdot u) v - (v \cdot w) u\big] + \big[(u \cdot v) w - (w \cdot u) v\big] + \big[(v \cdot w) u - (u \cdot v) w\big] = 0 \qquad \text{(each of } (w \cdot u) v,\ (v \cdot w) u,\ (u \cdot v) w \text{ appears once with each sign)}.$$
Hence $(\mathbb{R}^3, \times)$ is a Lie algebra. It is not abelian, since $e_1 \times e_2 = e_3 \neq 0$; it is three-dimensional, and [[Ex - R^3 with the Cross Product is the Lie Algebra so(3)]] shows that $a \mapsto (x \mapsto a \times x)$ identifies it with the Lie subalgebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ of antisymmetric $3 \times 3$ matrices inside $\mathfrak{gl}(3; \mathbb{R})$.

**Is an instance — the vector fields on a manifold (Bär, Example 1.2.2.4).** Let $M$ be a smooth manifold and $V := \mathfrak{X}(M)$ with the Lie bracket of vector fields, $[X, Y]f = X(Yf) - Y(Xf)$. Then $(\mathfrak{X}(M), [\cdot,\cdot])$ is a real Lie algebra. This is the content of [[Thm - The Space of Vector Fields is a Lie Algebra under the Lie Bracket]], which we restate: *for a smooth manifold $M$, the bracket $[X, Y]$ of two smooth vector fields is again a smooth vector field (the second-order terms of $X \circ Y - Y \circ X$ cancel, so the commutator is a derivation of $C^\infty(M)$), the map $(X, Y) \mapsto [X, Y]$ is $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear and antisymmetric, and it satisfies the Jacobi identity; moreover, for a diffeomorphism $F : M \to N$, $F_*[X, Y] = [F_*X, F_*Y]$.* The Jacobi identity is also worked in full, as an exercise, in [[Ex - The Jacobi Identity for Vector Fields]]. When $M$ has positive dimension this Lie algebra is infinite-dimensional: on $M = \mathbb{R}$ the fields $x^k \frac{d}{dx}$, $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$, are linearly independent (a vanishing linear combination $\sum_k c_k x^k \frac{d}{dx}$ applied to the function $x$ gives the vanishing polynomial $\sum_k c_k x^k$, forcing every $c_k = 0$), and their brackets are $[x^j \frac{d}{dx}, x^k \frac{d}{dx}] = (k - j)\, x^{j + k - 1} \frac{d}{dx}$ (by the coordinate formula $[X, Y] = (X Y^1 - Y X^1)\, \partial_1$ of [[Def - The Lie Bracket of Vector Fields]], with $X^1 = x^j$, $Y^1 = x^k$: $x^j \cdot k x^{k-1} - x^k \cdot j x^{j-1} = (k - j) x^{j+k-1}$), so it is not abelian either.

**Is NOT an instance — matrices with the product as bracket.** On $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ put $[A, B] := AB$. This is bilinear (distributivity), but it fails antisymmetry: $[1_n, 1_n] = 1_n$ while $-[1_n, 1_n] = -1_n$, and $1_n \neq -1_n$ since $2 \cdot 1_n \neq 0$ in characteristic zero. It also fails the Jacobi identity: $[\,[1_n, 1_n], 1_n] + [\,[1_n, 1_n], 1_n] + [\,[1_n, 1_n], 1_n] = 1_n + 1_n + 1_n = 3 \cdot 1_n \neq 0$. So the associative product itself is not a Lie bracket; only its antisymmetrisation $AB - BA$ is. (The symmetrisation $AB + BA$ is not a Lie bracket either — it is symmetric, not antisymmetric — and is the Jordan product mentioned in the Axiom Motivation.)

**Is NOT an instance of "subalgebra" — a subspace not closed under the bracket.** In $\mathfrak{gl}(2; \mathbb{R})$ let $W := \operatorname{span}(E_{12}, E_{21})$, the strictly-off-diagonal matrices. It is a two-dimensional subspace. But $[E_{12}, E_{21}] = E_{12}E_{21} - E_{21}E_{12} = E_{11} - E_{22}$ (by $E_{ij}E_{kl} = \delta_{jk}E_{il}$), a diagonal matrix with entries $(1, -1)$, which has zero off-diagonal part and is non-zero, hence is not in $W$. By Lemma D (Direction 1, contrapositive) $W$ is not a Lie subalgebra; the restricted bracket takes values outside $W$.

**Corollary — $\mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$.** Let $\mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{K}) := \{A \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) : \operatorname{tr} A = 0\}$. It is a subspace, being the kernel of the linear functional $\operatorname{tr}$ (linearity of the trace: $\operatorname{tr}(aA + bB) = \sum_i (a a_{ii} + b b_{ii}) = a \operatorname{tr} A + b \operatorname{tr} B$). It is closed under the commutator because the trace of every commutator vanishes: for any $A = (a_{ij})$, $B = (b_{ij})$,
$$\operatorname{tr}(AB) = \sum_{i=1}^n (AB)_{ii} = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} b_{ji} = \sum_{j=1}^n \sum_{i=1}^n b_{ji} a_{ij} = \sum_{j=1}^n (BA)_{jj} = \operatorname{tr}(BA) \qquad \text{(definition of the matrix product; interchange of two finite sums; commutativity of multiplication in } \mathbb{K}),$$
so $\operatorname{tr}[A, B] = \operatorname{tr}(AB) - \operatorname{tr}(BA) = 0$ (linearity of the trace). Hence $[A, B] \in \mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ for *all* $A, B \in \mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ — in particular for $A, B \in \mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ — and $\mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a Lie subalgebra (indeed an ideal) of $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$. That it is the Lie algebra of the group $SL(n; \mathbb{K})$ of [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]] is proved in [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]].

**Corollary — the antisymmetric matrices form a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{R})$.** Let $\mathfrak{so}(n) := \{A \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R}) : A^t = -A\}$, a subspace (the transpose is linear, so this is the kernel of $A \mapsto A^t + A$). For $A, B \in \mathfrak{so}(n)$,
$$[A, B]^t = (AB - BA)^t = B^t A^t - A^t B^t = (-B)(-A) - (-A)(-B) = BA - AB = -[A, B] \qquad \text{(transpose reverses products: } (XY)^t = Y^t X^t \text{; the hypotheses } A^t = -A,\ B^t = -B),$$
so $[A, B] \in \mathfrak{so}(n)$ and $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is a Lie subalgebra. Note that $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is *not* closed under the product: $E_{12} - E_{21} \in \mathfrak{so}(2)$ but $(E_{12} - E_{21})^2 = -E_{11} - E_{22} = -1_2$ is symmetric, not antisymmetric. The identification of $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ with the Lie algebra of $SO(n)$ and $O(n)$ is again on [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]].

**Corollary — the diagonal matrices form an abelian Lie subalgebra.** Let $\mathfrak{d}(n) := \{D \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) : d_{ij} = 0 \text{ for } i \neq j\}$, the diagonal matrices; it is a subspace with basis $E_{11}, \ldots, E_{nn}$. For $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_n)$ and $D' = \operatorname{diag}(d'_1, \ldots, d'_n)$, the product $DD'$ is diagonal with entries $d_i d'_i$ (the $(i, j)$ entry $\sum_k d_{ik} d'_{kj}$ is non-zero only for $i = k = j$), and likewise $D'D = \operatorname{diag}(d'_i d_i)$; so $[D, D'] = DD' - D'D = \operatorname{diag}(d_i d'_i - d'_i d_i) = 0$ (commutativity of $\mathbb{K}$). Hence $[D, D'] = 0 \in \mathfrak{d}(n)$, so $\mathfrak{d}(n)$ is a Lie subalgebra, and its restricted bracket is identically zero, so it is abelian. On a basis (Lemma D) this reads $[E_{ii}, E_{jj}] = \delta_{ij}E_{ij} - \delta_{ji}E_{ji} = 0$ for all $i, j$.

**Corollary — the left-invariant vector fields form a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$.** For a Lie group $G$, the [[Def - Left-Invariant Vector Field|left-invariant vector fields]] form a subspace of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$ closed under the Lie bracket; this is [[Thm - Left-Invariant Vector Fields Form a Lie Algebra]], which we restate: *if $X, Y \in \mathfrak{X}(G)$ satisfy $(L_g)_* X = X$ and $(L_g)_* Y = Y$ for all $g \in G$, then $(L_g)_*[X, Y] = [X, Y]$ for all $g$.* Together with the previous example and Lemma A, this makes the space of left-invariant fields a Lie algebra, namely a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$; its identification with $T_e G$ by evaluation at the identity is recorded on [[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group]], and carrying this out for the classical groups is exactly Bär's programme for the rest of §1.2.

**Calibration check.** First, verify that $\mathfrak{sl}(2; \mathbb{R})$ is a subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}(2; \mathbb{R})$ by Lemma D on the basis $H := E_{11} - E_{22}$, $E := E_{12}$, $F := E_{21}$: using $E_{ij}E_{kl} = \delta_{jk}E_{il}$, $[H, E] = HE - EH = E_{12} - (-E_{12}) = 2E$, $[H, F] = HF - FH = -E_{21} - E_{21} = -2F$, $[E, F] = E_{11} - E_{22} = H$; all three brackets are traceless, as they must be by the trace corollary, and all three lie in $\operatorname{span}(H, E, F)$. Second, verify that the diagonal matrices in $\mathfrak{gl}(2; \mathbb{R})$ form an abelian subalgebra by computing $[E_{11}, E_{22}] = E_{11}E_{22} - E_{22}E_{11} = 0 - 0 = 0$. Third, confirm that the strictly upper-triangular $2 \times 2$ matrices $\operatorname{span}(E_{12})$ form a subalgebra (one-dimensional, hence abelian by Lemma C(ii)), while the upper-triangular matrices $\operatorname{span}(E_{11}, E_{12}, E_{22})$ form a subalgebra that is *not* abelian ($[E_{11}, E_{12}] = E_{12} \neq 0$) — the two-dimensional subalgebra $\operatorname{span}(E_{11}, E_{12})$ inside it, with $[E_{11}, E_{12}] = E_{12}$, is the non-abelian two-dimensional Lie algebra discussed in the Categorical section. If you can also say, without looking, which single axiom the bracket $[A, B] := AB$ on matrices violates first and why, you have understood the definition.

> Every Lie algebra $\mathfrak{o}(n), \mathfrak{so}(n), \mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{K}), \mathfrak{u}(n), \mathfrak{su}(n)$ is described in [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]] as a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ cut out by a linear condition ($A^t = -A$, $\operatorname{tr} A = 0$, $A^* = -A$); the closure computations on this page ($\operatorname{tr}[A, B] = 0$ and $[A, B]^t = -[A, B]$) are the templates for all of them, and $\mathfrak{u}(1) = i\mathbb{R}$ is the abelian one.

> [!tip] The adjoint representation *(from §1.3–§1.4)*
> Lemma B identifies the Jacobi identity with the statement that $\operatorname{ad}_u = [u, \cdot]$ is a derivation; [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]] shows that $\operatorname{ad}$ is the differential of the adjoint representation $\operatorname{Ad}$ of the group, and the Jacobi identity is exactly what makes $\operatorname{ad} : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})$ a Lie algebra homomorphism.

> [!tip] Fundamental vector fields and $\mathfrak{g}$-valued forms *(from §1.5 and chapter IV)*
> The map $\xi \mapsto \xi_M$ from $\mathfrak{g}$ into $\mathfrak{X}(M)$ attached to a right action is a Lie algebra homomorphism ([[Thm - The Fundamental Vector Field Map is a Lie Algebra Homomorphism for Right Actions]]), so its image is a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{X}(M)$; and the bracket $[\alpha \wedge \beta]$ of $\mathfrak{g}$-valued forms in the structure equation $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac12[\omega \wedge \omega]$ of chapter IV is built from the Lie bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$ — for an abelian structure group such as $U(1)$ the quadratic term vanishes and the curvature is simply $d\omega$, which is why electromagnetism is linear and Yang–Mills theory is not.
