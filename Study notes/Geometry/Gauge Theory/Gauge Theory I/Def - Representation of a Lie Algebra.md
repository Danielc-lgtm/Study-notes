# Notation

This page depends on the following standing conventions of the series. Lie groups are smooth manifolds — smooth means $C^\infty$, Hausdorff, second countable — carrying a group structure whose multiplication and inversion are smooth. The Lie algebra of a Lie group $G$ is $\mathfrak{g} = T_eG$ with the bracket transported from the Lie bracket of left-invariant vector fields; for a matrix group this bracket is the commutator $[X, Y] = XY - YX$. The adjoint representation of $G$ is $\operatorname{Ad}_g = d_e \alpha_g$, where $\alpha_g(h) = ghg^{-1}$ is conjugation, and for matrix groups $\operatorname{Ad}_g X = gXg^{-1}$; the adjoint representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is $\operatorname{ad}(X)(Y) = [X, Y]$. Representations, like all group actions on manifolds in this series, are **left** actions.

This is a compound page: it defines three interlocking notions — a **representation** of a Lie algebra, **equivalence** of two such representations, and the **induced homomorphism** $\varphi_* = d_e\varphi$ of a Lie group homomorphism (in particular the **differential** $\varrho_*$ of a Lie group representation) — because the third notion is the only source of examples of the first that the series ever uses, and the second is what makes the classification theorems of the chapter statements about isomorphism classes rather than about formulas.

Throughout, $\mathfrak{g}$ denotes a finite-dimensional real [[Def - Lie Algebra|Lie algebra]] with bracket $[\cdot, \cdot] : \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ (bilinear, antisymmetric, satisfying the Jacobi identity; the axioms are restated on [[Def - Lie Subalgebra and Abelian Lie Algebra]]), and $X, Y, Z$ denote its elements. When a Lie group $G$ with neutral element $e$ is present, $\mathfrak{g} = T_eG$ is its Lie algebra in the sense of [[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group]], and $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is its [[Def - Exponential Map of a Lie Group|exponential map]]; we use only the two facts that $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ is a smooth curve with $\exp(0) = e$ and $\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} \exp(tX) = X$ (proved on [[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]]). The field $\mathbb{K}$ is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and $V$ denotes a [[Def - Finite-Dimensional Vector Space|finite-dimensional]] $\mathbb{K}$-[[Def - Vector Space|vector space]] of dimension $n = \dim_{\mathbb{K}} V$. We write $\operatorname{End}(V)$ for the $\mathbb{K}$-vector space of $\mathbb{K}$-[[Def - Linear Map|linear maps]] $V \to V$, with composition written as juxtaposition $SR := S \circ R$ and identity $\operatorname{id}_V$, and $\operatorname{Aut}(V) = GL(V) \subset \operatorname{End}(V)$ for the group of [[Def - Invertibility and Isomorphism|invertible]] linear maps, which is a Lie group by Lemma A of [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]]. The **commutator** of $S, R \in \operatorname{End}(V)$ is $[S, R] := SR - RS$; Lemma 0 below shows that $(\operatorname{End}(V), [\cdot, \cdot])$ is a Lie algebra, which the literature calls $\mathfrak{gl}(V)$. The symbol $\lambda$ (and $\tilde\lambda$, $\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$) always denotes a representation of a Lie algebra, $\varrho$ (and $\tilde\varrho$, $\varrho_k$, $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}$) a representation of a Lie group as on [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]], and $T$ a linear isomorphism between two representation spaces. For a Lie group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$ we write $\varphi_* := d_e\varphi : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ for its differential at the neutral element; for a representation $\varrho$ the same symbol $\varrho_* = d_e\varrho$ is read as a map into $\operatorname{End}(V)$ through the identification of Lemma 2 below.

For an ordered [[Def - Basis|basis]] $b = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ of $V$ we write, exactly as on [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]], $F_b : V \to \mathbb{K}^n$ for the coordinate isomorphism and $\mathcal{M}_b : \operatorname{End}(V) \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$, $\mathcal{M}_b(S) = F_b \circ S \circ F_b^{-1}$, for the map sending a linear map to its [[Def - Matrix of a Linear Map|matrix]] in the basis $b$; $\mathcal{M}_b$ is a linear isomorphism which is multiplicative and unital ($\mathcal{M}_b(SR) = \mathcal{M}_b(S)\mathcal{M}_b(R)$, $\mathcal{M}_b(\operatorname{id}_V) = 1_n$) and restricts to a Lie group isomorphism $\operatorname{Aut}(V) \to GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ — all of this is Lemma A, Step 1, of [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]]. The space $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ with the commutator is written $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$, and for a closed subgroup $G \subset GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_1 G \subset \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ carries the commutator bracket by [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]]. The trace of a square matrix is $\operatorname{tr}$ ([[Def - Trace]]), and $A^t$ is the transpose. $E_{ij}$ denotes the matrix unit with a single entry $1$ in row $i$, column $j$; the Pauli matrices are $\sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\sigma_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\sigma_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $(-i\sigma_1, -i\sigma_2, -i\sigma_3)$ is the basis of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ of [[Ex - su(2) in the Basis of Anti-Hermitian Pauli Matrices]], with $[-i\sigma_a, -i\sigma_b] = 2\epsilon_{abc}(-i\sigma_c)$.

> [!warning] Convention: the base field of a representation, and two misprints in Bär
> A representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on a *complex* space $V$ is, for Bär (Definition 1.3.16) and for the series, a Lie algebra homomorphism $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ where $\mathfrak{g}$ is a **real** Lie algebra and $\operatorname{End}(V) = \operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)$ is the space of *complex-linear* maps regarded as a real Lie algebra; so $\lambda$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and each $\lambda(X)$ is $\mathbb{C}$-linear. This matches the group side, where a complex representation is a homomorphism of *real* Lie groups $G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ whose values are complex-linear ([[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]], Notation). Two misprints in the source are corrected on this page: Bär's Definition 1.3.16 defines an equivalence between $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ and $\tilde\lambda : \tilde{\mathfrak{g}} \to \operatorname{End}(\tilde V)$, but the commuting square he draws only makes sense for two representations of the *same* Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, which is what is meant and what we write (Appendix of the Bär content map, item 4); and Bär's Remark 1.4.8 begins "for any Lie group $G$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{h}$" where $\mathfrak{g}$ is meant. Bär writes $\varrho_*$ and $\varphi_*$ for $d_e\varrho$ and $d_e\varphi$; Haydys does not treat representations of Lie algebras abstractly and writes the same objects directly as matrices. The series follows Bär's notation.

> [!warning] Convention: the route to "the differential of a representation is a representation"
> Bär proves that $\varphi_* = d_e\varphi$ preserves brackets (his Lemma 1.4.9) by computing $\varphi_*([X, Y]) = \varphi_*(\operatorname{Ad}_*(X)Y)$ and moving $\varphi$ through $\operatorname{Ad}$ and $\exp$; that computation uses $\operatorname{Ad}_* = \operatorname{ad}$ for the *general* Lie group $G$, which Bär verifies only for matrix groups (his Remark 1.4.8). The series proves $\operatorname{Ad}_* = \operatorname{ad}$ for every Lie group on [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]], and proves that $\varphi_*$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism independently, by the argument that left-invariant vector fields are $\varphi$-related, on [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]]. Corollary C below uses the latter page as its primary proof and records Bär's computation, with every line justified, as a second proof.

The full registry for the chapter is on [[Gauge Theory I — Lie Groups, Representations, and Group Actions]].

# Axiom Motivation

A representation of a Lie group, as defined on [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]], lets a symmetry group act linearly on the values of a field. But the objects that gauge theory actually computes with are not the group elements; they are the *infinitesimal* symmetries — the Lie algebra. The connection form of chapter IV takes values in $\mathfrak{g}$, not in $G$; the curvature $F_A$ is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 2-form; and the covariant derivative on an associated bundle, the object that couples a matter field to the gauge field, is built by letting these $\mathfrak{g}$-valued forms act on the fibre $V$. So we need to know how the Lie algebra acts on $V$, and we need the action to be determined by the group action from which it comes. The question this page answers is therefore: what is the infinitesimal shadow of a linear group action, and what algebraic structure does it carry?

Let us see what the shadow must be by computing it in the smallest case. Let $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ be a representation. For $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ the curve $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ passes through $e$ with velocity $X$, so the curve $t \mapsto \varrho(\exp(tX))$ passes through $\operatorname{id}_V$ inside $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$, and its velocity is a tangent vector to $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ at the identity. Since $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is an open subset of the vector space $\operatorname{End}(V)$ (Lemma 2), that tangent vector *is* an element of $\operatorname{End}(V)$: a linear map $\varrho_*(X) : V \to V$, not necessarily invertible. For $G = U(1)$ acting on $V = \mathbb{C}$ by $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}(z) = z$, and $X = i\theta \in \mathfrak{u}(1) = i\mathbb{R}$, the curve is $t \mapsto e^{it\theta}$ and its velocity at $t = 0$ is multiplication by $i\theta$; the infinitesimal shadow of "rotate the plane" is "multiply by an imaginary number". The assignment $X \mapsto \varrho_*(X)$ is linear, because it is the differential of a smooth map at a point. The first two clauses of the definition — a linear map $\mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ — are thus forced by the origin of the object.

What is not forced by that computation, and is the substance of the definition, is the third clause: that brackets in $\mathfrak{g}$ go to commutators in $\operatorname{End}(V)$. The group law $\varrho(gh) = \varrho(g)\varrho(h)$ is a statement about *products*; its infinitesimal version cannot be a statement about products of elements of $\mathfrak{g}$, because $\mathfrak{g}$ has no product, only a bracket. What does survive is that the bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$ measures the failure of $G$ to be commutative to second order — for a matrix group, $e^{tX}e^{tY}e^{-tX}e^{-tY} = 1 + t^2[X, Y] + O(t^3)$, as computed on [[Ex - Tangent Space of the General Linear Group at the Identity]] — and a homomorphism carries this second-order failure in $G$ to the second-order failure in $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$, which is the commutator of the matrices. So the differential of a homomorphism must carry $[X, Y]$ to $\varrho_*(X)\varrho_*(Y) - \varrho_*(Y)\varrho_*(X)$. This is proved in general on [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]] and specialised to representations in Corollary C below. The definition of a Lie algebra representation abstracts exactly this: a linear map that carries brackets to commutators, whether or not it arises from a group.

Now the per-clause failure analysis. **Drop linearity** and the object is no longer the shadow of anything: the map $\mathfrak{u}(1) \to \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C})$, $i\theta \mapsto \theta^2$, is smooth and carries the (zero) bracket of the abelian Lie algebra $\mathfrak{u}(1)$ to the (zero) commutator of $\mathbb{C}$, but it is not additive, so it cannot be the differential of any map at any point, and no group representation of $U(1)$ has it as its infinitesimal version. **Drop bracket preservation** and the map no longer encodes the non-commutativity of the group; the transpose $X \mapsto X^t$ on $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ is linear but reverses every commutator, $[X, Y]^t = -[X^t, Y^t]$, and we exhibit in the Examples two $2 \times 2$ matrices for which the two sides differ — the map is an *anti*-homomorphism, the same defect that afflicts the naive dual $g \mapsto \varrho(g)^*$ on [[Def - Constructions on Representations]], and the repair is the same: a sign, $X \mapsto -X^t$, which is exactly the differential of the correct dual representation $g \mapsto (g^{-1})^t$. A second, more insidious failure is rescaling: $X \mapsto \tfrac12 X$ on $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ is linear, but $[\tfrac12 X, \tfrac12 Y] = \tfrac14 [X, Y] \neq \tfrac12 [X, Y]$ whenever $[X, Y] \neq 0$; the Examples show that no scalar multiple $cX$ other than $c \in \{0, 1\}$ works on a non-abelian Lie algebra, so bracket preservation fixes the *normalisation* of the action. Physically this is the statement that the angular momentum operators cannot be rescaled independently of one another without destroying their commutation relations. **Drop the requirement that the target be $\operatorname{End}(V)$ with the commutator**, and ask instead for a map into $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$: no linear map $\mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ other than the zero map on a one-dimensional space can have all its values invertible, since $\lambda(0) = 0$ is never invertible for $V \neq 0$, and a Lie algebra is a vector space, not a group — its natural target is the *Lie algebra* of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$, which Lemma 3 identifies with $\operatorname{End}(V)$ under the commutator. **Drop finite-dimensionality of $V$** and the identification $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V)$ has no meaning, because $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is no longer a Lie group; the purely algebraic definition still makes sense (and is used for the infinite-dimensional representations of quantum mechanics), but the bridge to group representations, which is the only reason the series needs the notion, is gone.

The second notion, equivalence, is a comparison: two representations are equivalent if they differ by a relabelling $T : V \to \tilde V$ of the representation space that carries every $\lambda(X)$ to $\tilde\lambda(X)$. Why must $T$ be an isomorphism? Because the zero map $V \to \tilde V$ intertwines any two representations, so "there is an intertwining linear map" would make every pair equivalent, and because a non-invertible $T$ would collapse rather than relabel. Why for every $X$? Because the relabelling must be one and the same for the whole Lie algebra; a $T$ that intertwined $\lambda(X)$ and $\tilde\lambda(X)$ for a single $X$ would say nothing about the others. Why the same $\mathfrak{g}$ on both sides (the correction of Bär's misprint)? Because the square $T\lambda(X) = \tilde\lambda(X)T$ has to be read with the *same* $X$ on both sides, which requires $X$ to lie in one Lie algebra. Equivalence is an equivalence relation (Lemma 4), and it is compatible with differentiation: equivalent group representations have equivalent differentials (Corollary D). A forward reference explains why the notion is indispensable: [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] proves that a subspace of $V$ is invariant under a representation $\varrho$ of a *connected* group if and only if it is invariant under $\varrho_*$, and then classifies representations of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ by highest weights; the classification is a statement up to equivalence, and it is transported back to $SU(2)$ through Corollary D.

The third notion is the induced homomorphism $\varphi_* = d_e\varphi$ of a Lie group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$; the differential $\varrho_*$ of a representation is the case $H = \operatorname{Aut}(V)$. The one point that has to be settled before $\varrho_*$ can be called a map into $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is the identification of the tangent space $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ with $\operatorname{End}(V)$ — Bär's unproved identification $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) \cong \operatorname{End}(V)$ in his Remark 1.3.19 — and the further point that the Lie bracket of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$, defined through left-invariant vector fields, is the commutator. Both are proved below (Lemmas 2 and 3), and they are the reason the definition of a Lie algebra representation uses the commutator and not some other bracket on $\operatorname{End}(V)$.

Could a reader invent the definition from these desiderata? We claim so: "the infinitesimal version of a linear action" forces a map $\mathfrak{g} \to T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V)$; "the differential of a smooth map" forces linearity; "the group law to second order" forces brackets to go to commutators; and "the same up to relabelling" forces the definition of equivalence. The definition is the conjunction of these forced choices and nothing else.

# The Definition

We first establish that the target of a representation is a Lie algebra, so that the phrase "Lie algebra homomorphism into $\operatorname{End}(V)$" has a meaning.

> [!note]- Lemma 0: $\operatorname{End}(V)$ with the commutator is a Lie algebra
> **Statement.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$-vector space. The commutator $[S, R] := SR - RS$ makes $\operatorname{End}(V)$ a Lie algebra over $\mathbb{K}$, hence also over $\mathbb{R}$. The same holds for $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ with $[A, B] = AB - BA$, and for every subspace of either that is closed under the commutator.
>
> **Proof.** We must verify the three axioms of [[Def - Lie Algebra]] — bilinearity, antisymmetry, and the Jacobi identity — for the map $(S, R) \mapsto SR - RS$ on $\operatorname{End}(V)$. Let $S, R, Q \in \operatorname{End}(V)$ and $a, b \in \mathbb{K}$.
>
> **Bilinearity.** Composition of linear maps distributes over addition and commutes with scalars: $(aS + bS')R = aSR + bS'R$ and $R(aS + bS') = aRS + bRS'$ (both because the maps are linear and composition is evaluated pointwise). Hence
> $$[aS + bS', R] = (aS + bS')R - R(aS + bS') = a(SR - RS) + b(S'R - RS') = a[S, R] + b[S', R] \qquad \text{(distributivity, then regrouping),}$$
> and the identity in the second slot, $[R, aS + bS'] = a[R, S] + b[R, S']$, follows by the same two distributive laws with the roles of the factors exchanged.
>
> **Antisymmetry.** $[S, R] = SR - RS = -(RS - SR) = -[R, S]$ (by the definition of the commutator and the vector space axioms).
>
> **Jacobi identity.** Expanding by the definition of the commutator and using associativity of composition to drop parentheses,
> $$[S, [R, Q]] = S(RQ - QR) - (RQ - QR)S = SRQ - SQR - RQS + QRS \qquad \text{(distributivity and associativity),}$$
> and, applying the same expansion to the cyclic permutations $(S, R, Q) \to (R, Q, S) \to (Q, S, R)$,
> $$[R, [Q, S]] = RQS - RSQ - QSR + SQR, \qquad [Q, [S, R]] = QSR - QRS - SRQ + RSQ.$$
> Adding the three lines, each of the six words $SRQ, SQR, RQS, QRS, RSQ, QSR$ occurs exactly once with the sign $+$ and once with the sign $-$: $SRQ$ appears with $+$ in the first line and $-$ in the third; $SQR$ with $-$ in the first and $+$ in the second; $RQS$ with $-$ in the first and $+$ in the second; $QRS$ with $+$ in the first and $-$ in the third; $RSQ$ with $-$ in the second and $+$ in the third; $QSR$ with $-$ in the second and $+$ in the third. Therefore
> $$[S, [R, Q]] + [R, [Q, S]] + [Q, [S, R]] = 0 \qquad \text{(all twelve terms cancel in pairs),}$$
> which is the Jacobi identity in the form $[X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]] = 0$ of [[Def - Lie Algebra]]; by antisymmetry it is the same as the form $[[X, Y], Z] + [[Y, Z], X] + [[Z, X], Y] = 0$ used on [[Def - Lie Subalgebra and Abelian Lie Algebra]].
>
> **The matrix case and subspaces.** The computation used only that composition is associative and bilinear, which holds for matrix multiplication as well; so $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ is a Lie algebra under the commutator (this is also [[Ex - The Jacobi Identity for the Matrix Commutator Follows from Associativity]]). A subspace closed under the commutator is a Lie subalgebra and hence a Lie algebra by Lemma A of [[Def - Lie Subalgebra and Abelian Lie Algebra]]. A complex vector space is a real vector space, and $\mathbb{C}$-bilinearity implies $\mathbb{R}$-bilinearity, so $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is a real Lie algebra when $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$. $\blacksquare$

> **Definition (representation of a Lie algebra; Bär Definition 1.3.16).** Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a finite-dimensional real Lie algebra and $V$ a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{K} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$. A **representation** of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism
> $$\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \longrightarrow \operatorname{End}(V),$$
> that is, a map satisfying
> 1. **(linearity)** $\lambda(aX + bY) = a\lambda(X) + b\lambda(Y)$ for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, and
> 2. **(bracket preservation)** $\lambda([X, Y]) = [\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] = \lambda(X)\lambda(Y) - \lambda(Y)\lambda(X)$ for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$,
>
> where $\operatorname{End}(V)$ carries the commutator bracket of Lemma 0. The representation is **real** if $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ and **complex** if $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$; its **dimension** is $\dim_{\mathbb{K}} V$. We say that $\mathfrak{g}$ **acts on $V$ through $\lambda$** and write $X \cdot v := \lambda(X)v$ when $\lambda$ is understood.

> **Definition (equivalence of Lie algebra representations; Bär Definition 1.3.16, corrected).** Let $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ and $\tilde\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(\tilde V)$ be two representations of the *same* Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ over the same field $\mathbb{K}$. A linear isomorphism $T : V \to \tilde V$ is an **equivalence** of $\lambda$ and $\tilde\lambda$ if
> $$T \circ \lambda(X) = \tilde\lambda(X) \circ T \qquad \text{for every } X \in \mathfrak{g},$$
> that is, if for every $X$ the square with $\lambda(X)$ on the left, $\tilde\lambda(X)$ on the right, and $T$ along the top and bottom commutes. Two representations are **equivalent** if an equivalence between them exists.

> **Definition (induced homomorphism; Bär Lemma 1.4.9 and Remark 1.3.19).** Let $\varphi : G \to H$ be a [[Def - Lie Group Homomorphism|Lie group homomorphism]]. The **induced Lie algebra homomorphism** is its differential at the neutral element,
> $$\varphi_* := d_e\varphi : \mathfrak{g} = T_eG \longrightarrow T_{e}H = \mathfrak{h};$$
> it is a Lie algebra homomorphism by [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]], restated in Corollary C. For a representation $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ the **differential of $\varrho$** is
> $$\varrho_* := d_e\varrho : \mathfrak{g} \longrightarrow T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V),$$
> where the last identification is Lemma 2; explicitly, $\varrho_*(X) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} \varrho(\exp(tX))$, the derivative of a curve in the vector space $\operatorname{End}(V)$. Corollary C proves that $\varrho_*$ is a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$.

The smallest concrete case is $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{u}(1) = i\mathbb{R}$ (the Lie algebra of $U(1)$, computed on [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]] as $\{a \in \mathbb{C} : \bar a = -a\}$) acting on $V = \mathbb{C}$, where $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}$ through $S \mapsto S(1)$ and the commutator is zero because $\mathbb{C}$ is commutative. A representation is then an $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\lambda : i\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}$, and every such map is of the form $\lambda(i\theta) = i\theta \cdot w$ for the fixed complex number $w = \lambda(i)/i$; bracket preservation is the identity $0 = 0$. The differential of the representation $\varrho_k(z) = z^k$ is the case $w = k$, as the calibration check at the end of the page verifies: the integer that a physicist calls the charge of the field is the number by which the generator of $\mathfrak{u}(1)$ acts. On a non-abelian Lie algebra the bracket clause is not vacuous; for $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, with $[-i\sigma_a, -i\sigma_b] = 2\epsilon_{abc}(-i\sigma_c)$, a representation must send the three basis elements to three operators $J_1, J_2, J_3$ satisfying $[J_a, J_b] = 2\epsilon_{abc}J_c$, and the Examples show that the identity map $\mathfrak{su}(2) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^2)$ does this while $X \mapsto \tfrac12 X$ does not.

**Equivalent formulation as a bilinear action.** The definition packs the family of operators $\{\lambda(X)\}_{X \in \mathfrak{g}}$ into one linear map. It is often more convenient to think of a bilinear map $\mathfrak{g} \times V \to V$ satisfying one identity, and since the series uses both pictures we prove that they are the same.

> [!note]- Proposition: representations are bilinear actions with the commutator identity
> **Statement.** Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a Lie algebra and $V$ a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$-vector space. The formulas $X \cdot v := \lambda(X)v$ and $\lambda(X) := (v \mapsto X \cdot v)$ set up a bijection between representations $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ and maps $\mathfrak{g} \times V \to V$, $(X, v) \mapsto X \cdot v$, that are $\mathbb{R}$-linear in $X$, $\mathbb{K}$-linear in $v$, and satisfy
> $$[X, Y] \cdot v = X \cdot (Y \cdot v) - Y \cdot (X \cdot v) \qquad \text{for all } X, Y \in \mathfrak{g},\ v \in V.$$
>
> **Proof.** We need to show that each formula produces an object of the other kind and that the two formulas are inverse to each other.
>
> **Direction 1 (from $\lambda$ to an action).** Let $\lambda$ be a representation and put $X \cdot v := \lambda(X)v$. The map is $\mathbb{K}$-linear in $v$ because $\lambda(X) \in \operatorname{End}(V)$ is a $\mathbb{K}$-linear map. It is $\mathbb{R}$-linear in $X$ because $(aX + bY) \cdot v = \lambda(aX + bY)v = (a\lambda(X) + b\lambda(Y))v = a(X \cdot v) + b(Y \cdot v)$ (by clause 1 of the definition and the definition of the vector space operations on $\operatorname{End}(V)$). The identity holds because
> $$[X, Y] \cdot v = \lambda([X, Y])v = \big(\lambda(X)\lambda(Y) - \lambda(Y)\lambda(X)\big)v = X \cdot (Y \cdot v) - Y \cdot (X \cdot v) \qquad \text{(by clause 2, then evaluating the composite maps at } v\text{).}$$
>
> **Direction 2 (from an action to $\lambda$).** Let $(X, v) \mapsto X \cdot v$ be a map with the three stated properties and define $\lambda(X) : V \to V$ by $\lambda(X)v := X \cdot v$. Then $\lambda(X)$ is $\mathbb{K}$-linear by the linearity in $v$, so $\lambda(X) \in \operatorname{End}(V)$. The map $X \mapsto \lambda(X)$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear because for every $v$, $\lambda(aX + bY)v = (aX + bY) \cdot v = a(X \cdot v) + b(Y \cdot v) = (a\lambda(X) + b\lambda(Y))v$ (by the linearity in $X$), and two linear maps that agree on every $v$ are equal. Clause 2 holds because for every $v$,
> $$\lambda([X, Y])v = [X, Y] \cdot v = X \cdot (Y \cdot v) - Y \cdot (X \cdot v) = \lambda(X)\lambda(Y)v - \lambda(Y)\lambda(X)v = [\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)]v \qquad \text{(by the commutator identity of the action),}$$
> and again maps agreeing on every $v$ are equal.
>
> **The two directions are inverse.** Starting from $\lambda$, forming the action $X \cdot v = \lambda(X)v$, and forming the operator $v \mapsto X \cdot v$ returns $\lambda(X)$; starting from an action, forming $\lambda$, and forming $\lambda(X)v$ returns $X \cdot v$. Both compositions are the identity, so the correspondence is a bijection. $\blacksquare$

**Equivalent formulation in matrices (Bär Remark 1.3.17).** Up to equivalence, a representation is a Lie algebra homomorphism $\mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$. This is Corollary F in the Examples section, once equivalence has been shown to be an equivalence relation, which we do now.

> [!note]- Lemma 4: equivalence of representations is an equivalence relation
> **Statement.** On the class of representations of a fixed Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ over a fixed field $\mathbb{K}$, the relation "$\lambda$ and $\tilde\lambda$ are equivalent" is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
>
> **Proof.** We verify the three properties clause by clause.
>
> **Reflexivity.** For a representation $\lambda$ on $V$, the identity $\operatorname{id}_V$ is a linear isomorphism and $\operatorname{id}_V \circ \lambda(X) = \lambda(X) = \lambda(X) \circ \operatorname{id}_V$ for every $X$ (since composition with the identity does nothing). So $\operatorname{id}_V$ is an equivalence of $\lambda$ with itself.
>
> **Symmetry.** Let $T : V \to \tilde V$ be an equivalence of $\lambda$ and $\tilde\lambda$, so $T\lambda(X) = \tilde\lambda(X)T$ for all $X$. Its inverse $T^{-1} : \tilde V \to V$ is a linear isomorphism (the inverse of a linear bijection is linear, [[Def - Invertibility and Isomorphism]]). Composing the intertwining identity with $T^{-1}$ on the left and on the right,
> $$T^{-1}\big(T\lambda(X)\big)T^{-1} = T^{-1}\big(\tilde\lambda(X)T\big)T^{-1}, \quad\text{that is,}\quad \lambda(X)T^{-1} = T^{-1}\tilde\lambda(X) \qquad \text{(by } T^{-1}T = \operatorname{id}_V \text{ and } TT^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_{\tilde V}\text{),}$$
> for every $X$. So $T^{-1}$ is an equivalence of $\tilde\lambda$ and $\lambda$.
>
> **Transitivity.** Let $T : V \to \tilde V$ be an equivalence of $\lambda$ and $\tilde\lambda$, and $\tilde T : \tilde V \to \hat V$ an equivalence of $\tilde\lambda$ and $\hat\lambda$. The composite $\tilde T T : V \to \hat V$ is a linear isomorphism (a composite of linear bijections, with inverse $T^{-1}\tilde T^{-1}$), and for every $X$,
> $$(\tilde T T)\lambda(X) = \tilde T\big(T\lambda(X)\big) = \tilde T\big(\tilde\lambda(X)T\big) = \big(\tilde T\tilde\lambda(X)\big)T = \big(\hat\lambda(X)\tilde T\big)T = \hat\lambda(X)(\tilde T T) \qquad \text{(associativity; the identity for } T\text{; associativity; the identity for } \tilde T\text{; associativity).}$$
> So $\tilde T T$ is an equivalence of $\lambda$ and $\hat\lambda$.
>
> Therefore equivalence of representations is an equivalence relation. $\blacksquare$

The induced homomorphism $\varphi_*$ requires two identifications before it can be used with representations, and we now prove both: the tangent space of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ at the identity is $\operatorname{End}(V)$ (Lemma 2, Bär's identification $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) \cong \operatorname{End}(V)$), and the Lie bracket that $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ carries as a Lie group is the commutator (Lemma 3). The first rests on a general fact about open subsets of vector spaces, which we prove first.

> [!note]- Lemma 1: the tangent space of an open subset of a vector space
> **Statement.** Let $W$ be a real vector space of finite dimension $N$, given the smooth structure in which every linear isomorphism $\phi : W \to \mathbb{R}^N$ is a global chart; let $U \subseteq W$ be open and $p \in U$. Then the map
> $$\iota_p : W \to T_pU, \qquad \iota_p(w) := \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}(p + tw),$$
> the velocity at $t = 0$ of the straight line through $p$ in the direction $w$, is a linear isomorphism. For every smooth curve $c : (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to U$ with $c(0) = p$ the velocity $\dot c(0) \in T_pU$ equals $\iota_p(c'(0))$, where $c'(0) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{1}{t}(c(t) - p) \in W$ is the ordinary derivative. If $U' \subseteq W'$ is an open subset of a second such vector space and $f : U \to U'$ is smooth, then under these identifications the differential $d_pf : T_pU \to T_{f(p)}U'$ is the ordinary (Fréchet) derivative $Df(p) : W \to W'$; in particular, if $f$ is the restriction of a linear map $L : W \to W'$, then $d_pf = L$ for every $p$.
>
> **Proof.** We are given the vector space $W$, its open subset $U$, the point $p$, and we must show, first, that the smooth structure on $W$ is well defined; second, that $\iota_p$ is a linear isomorphism; third, the formula for velocities; and fourth, the formula for differentials.
>
> **Step 0 (the smooth structure on $W$).** Any two linear isomorphisms $\phi, \phi' : W \to \mathbb{R}^N$ differ by the linear map $\phi' \circ \phi^{-1} : \mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}^N$, which is smooth (its components are linear polynomials) with smooth inverse $\phi \circ \phi'^{-1}$; so all linear charts are smoothly compatible and determine one smooth structure on $W$, of dimension $N$. By [[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold]] the open subset $U$ is a smooth manifold with the restricted charts $\phi|_U$.
>
> **Step 1 ($\iota_p$ is a linear isomorphism).** Fix a linear isomorphism $\phi : W \to \mathbb{R}^N$. The chart $\phi|_U$ induces the linear isomorphism $\Phi_p : T_pU \to \mathbb{R}^N$ that sends the velocity $\dot c(0)$ of a smooth curve $c$ through $p$ to the derivative $(\phi \circ c)'(0)$ of its coordinate expression ([[Def - The Tangent Space]] and [[Thm - Dimension of the Tangent Space]]: in a chart, tangent vectors are identified with coordinate velocity vectors, and this identification is a linear isomorphism onto $\mathbb{R}^N$). For the line $c_w(t) = p + tw$ the coordinate expression is $\phi(p + tw) = \phi(p) + t\phi(w)$ (by linearity of $\phi$), whose derivative at $t = 0$ is the constant $\phi(w)$. Hence
> $$\Phi_p \circ \iota_p = \phi \qquad \text{(evaluating both sides on } w\text{: } \Phi_p(\dot c_w(0)) = (\phi \circ c_w)'(0) = \phi(w)\text{),}$$
> so $\iota_p = \Phi_p^{-1} \circ \phi$ is a composite of two linear isomorphisms and is therefore a linear isomorphism. (It does not depend on $\phi$: it was defined without reference to a chart.)
>
> **Step 2 (velocities of curves).** Let $c$ be a smooth curve in $U$ with $c(0) = p$. Its coordinate expression $\phi \circ c$ is a smooth curve in $\mathbb{R}^N$, and since $\phi$ is linear and continuous,
> $$(\phi \circ c)'(0) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\phi(c(t)) - \phi(p)}{t} = \lim_{t \to 0} \phi\Big(\frac{c(t) - p}{t}\Big) = \phi(c'(0)) \qquad \text{(linearity of } \phi \text{, then continuity of } \phi\text{).}$$
> Therefore $\Phi_p(\dot c(0)) = \phi(c'(0)) = \Phi_p(\iota_p(c'(0)))$ (by Step 1), and injectivity of $\Phi_p$ gives $\dot c(0) = \iota_p(c'(0))$.
>
> **Step 3 (differentials).** Let $f : U \to U'$ be smooth and $w \in W$. By [[Thm - Chain Rule for the Differential]], the differential sends the velocity of a curve to the velocity of its image: $d_pf(\dot c(0)) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} f(c(t))$. Take $c = c_w$, the line through $p$ in direction $w$. Then
> $$d_pf(\iota_p(w)) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} f(p + tw) = \iota_{f(p)}\big((f \circ c_w)'(0)\big) = \iota_{f(p)}\big(Df(p)\,w\big) \qquad \text{(chain rule for the differential; Step 2 applied in } U'\text{; the ordinary chain rule [[Thm - The Chain Rule]] for } f \circ c_w \text{ with } c_w'(0) = w\text{).}$$
> So $\iota_{f(p)}^{-1} \circ d_pf \circ \iota_p = Df(p)$, which is the claim. If $f = L|_U$ for a linear map $L$, then $f(p + tw) = L(p) + tL(w)$ and its ordinary derivative at $t = 0$ is $L(w)$, so $Df(p) = L$ for every $p$.
>
> **Conclusion.** The tangent space of an open subset of a finite-dimensional vector space is canonically the vector space itself, velocities of curves are ordinary derivatives, and differentials are ordinary derivatives; a linear map is its own differential at every point. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2 (Bär's identification in Remark 1.3.19): $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is open in $\operatorname{End}(V)$ and $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V)$
> **Statement.** Let $V$ be a $\mathbb{K}$-vector space of dimension $n \geq 1$. Then $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is a real vector space of dimension $n^2$ ($\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$) or $2n^2$ ($\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$), $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is an open subset of it, and the Lie group structure of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ from Lemma A of [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]] is the open-submanifold structure. Consequently, for every $S_0 \in \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, the map $\iota_{S_0} : \operatorname{End}(V) \to T_{S_0}\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ of Lemma 1 is a linear isomorphism, and in particular $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V)$ canonically: the velocity at $t = 0$ of a smooth curve $S(t)$ in $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ with $S(0) = \operatorname{id}_V$ is the ordinary derivative $S'(0) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{1}{t}(S(t) - \operatorname{id}_V) \in \operatorname{End}(V)$.
>
> **Proof.** We must show, first, that $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is a finite-dimensional real vector space and identify its dimension; second, that $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is open in it; third, that the two smooth structures on $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ agree; and fourth, deduce the tangent-space statement.
>
> **Step 1 (the vector space $\operatorname{End}(V)$).** Fix an ordered basis $b$ of $V$. The map $\mathcal{M}_b : \operatorname{End}(V) \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ is a $\mathbb{K}$-linear isomorphism (Lemma A, Step 1, of [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]]), so $\dim_{\mathbb{K}} \operatorname{End}(V) = n^2$; as a real vector space, $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ and $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}^{n^2} = \mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$ (a complex number is a pair of real numbers), which gives the two real dimensions. We give $\operatorname{End}(V)$ the smooth structure of Lemma 1, Step 0; $\mathcal{M}_b$ composed with any linear identification $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) \cong \mathbb{R}^N$ is one of its global charts, so $\mathcal{M}_b : \operatorname{End}(V) \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ is a diffeomorphism.
>
> **Step 2 ($\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is open).** By Lemma A, Step 1, of [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]], $S \in \operatorname{End}(V)$ is invertible if and only if $\mathcal{M}_b(S) \in GL(n; \mathbb{K})$; hence $\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \mathcal{M}_b^{-1}(GL(n; \mathbb{K}))$. The set $GL(n; \mathbb{K}) = \{A : \det A \neq 0\}$ is open in $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$, because $\det$ is a polynomial in the entries, hence continuous, and $\mathbb{K} \setminus \{0\}$ is open ([[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]]). The preimage of an open set under the homeomorphism $\mathcal{M}_b$ is open, so $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is open in $\operatorname{End}(V)$.
>
> **Step 3 (the two smooth structures on $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ agree).** Lemma A of [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]] gives $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ the unique structure for which $\mathcal{M}_b|_{\operatorname{Aut}(V)} : \operatorname{Aut}(V) \to GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a diffeomorphism, where $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is an open submanifold of $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$. The open-submanifold structure on $\operatorname{Aut}(V) \subseteq \operatorname{End}(V)$ has the same property, because the diffeomorphism $\mathcal{M}_b$ of Step 1 restricts to a diffeomorphism between the open subsets $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ and $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ (a diffeomorphism restricted to an open subset, with the open image, is a diffeomorphism in the restricted charts, since the charts of an open submanifold are the restrictions of the ambient charts by [[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold]]). Two smooth structures on the same set for which the same bijection to $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a diffeomorphism coincide (their atlases are both the pull-back of the atlas of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ along that bijection). So the Lie group $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is the open submanifold $\operatorname{Aut}(V) \subseteq \operatorname{End}(V)$.
>
> **Step 4 (the tangent space).** By Steps 2 and 3, $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is an open subset of the finite-dimensional real vector space $\operatorname{End}(V)$ with the open-submanifold structure, so Lemma 1 applies with $W = \operatorname{End}(V)$, $U = \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, and $p = S_0$: the map $\iota_{S_0}$ is a linear isomorphism $\operatorname{End}(V) \to T_{S_0}\operatorname{Aut}(V)$, and the velocity of a curve $S(t)$ through $S_0$ is $\iota_{S_0}(S'(0))$ with $S'(0)$ the ordinary derivative. Taking $S_0 = \operatorname{id}_V$ gives the statement. $\blacksquare$

The DG III exercise [[Ex - Tangent Space of the General Linear Group at the Identity]] is the matrix case of Lemma 2, $T_1 GL(n; \mathbb{R}) = \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R})$; Lemma 2 is that argument transported along $\mathcal{M}_b$ and made independent of $b$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: the Lie algebra of the Lie group $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is $\operatorname{End}(V)$ with the commutator
> **Statement.** Let $V$ be a $\mathbb{K}$-vector space of dimension $n \geq 1$. Under the identification $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V)$ of Lemma 2, the Lie bracket of the Lie algebra $\operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{Aut}(V))$ — the bracket transported from left-invariant vector fields as in [[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group]] — is the commutator: $[S, R] = SR - RS$ for all $S, R \in \operatorname{End}(V)$.
>
> **Proof.** We are given the Lie group $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ and must show that its Lie algebra bracket, computed through left-invariant vector fields, agrees with the commutator of Lemma 0 under $\iota_{\operatorname{id}}$.
>
> **Step 1 (the matrix case).** For $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, the Lie algebra is $T_1 GL(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ and its bracket is the matrix commutator: this is Lemma 3 of [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]] (for a closed subgroup $G \subseteq GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, the bracket of the left-invariant fields $A^L|_g = gA$ and $B^L|_g = gB$, evaluated at $1$, is $AB - BA$; the real case is also [[Ex - The Lie Bracket on a Matrix Lie Algebra is the Commutator]] and [[Ex - The Lie Algebra of GL(n,R) is the Space of n by n Matrices]]), applied with $G = GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ itself.
>
> **Step 2 (transport along $\mathcal{M}_b$).** Fix a basis $b$ of $V$. The map $\mathcal{M}_b|_{\operatorname{Aut}(V)} : \operatorname{Aut}(V) \to GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a Lie group isomorphism (Lemma A of [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]]). By [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]] — for a Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$, the differential $F_* = d_eF : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is linear and satisfies $F_*[X, Y]_{\mathfrak{g}} = [F_*X, F_*Y]_{\mathfrak{h}}$ — the differential $d_{\operatorname{id}}\mathcal{M}_b : \operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{Aut}(V)) \to \operatorname{Lie}(GL(n; \mathbb{K}))$ preserves brackets. Since $\mathcal{M}_b$ is the restriction to the open subset $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ of the *linear* map $\mathcal{M}_b : \operatorname{End}(V) \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$, Lemma 1, Step 3, gives $d_{\operatorname{id}}\mathcal{M}_b = \mathcal{M}_b$ under the identifications $\iota_{\operatorname{id}}$ and $\iota_{1_n}$ of Lemma 2. Therefore, for $S, R \in \operatorname{End}(V)$,
> $$\mathcal{M}_b\big([S, R]_{\operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{Aut}(V))}\big) = \big[\mathcal{M}_b(S), \mathcal{M}_b(R)\big]_{\operatorname{Lie}(GL(n; \mathbb{K}))} = \mathcal{M}_b(S)\mathcal{M}_b(R) - \mathcal{M}_b(R)\mathcal{M}_b(S) = \mathcal{M}_b(SR - RS) \qquad \text{(bracket preservation; Step 1; multiplicativity and linearity of } \mathcal{M}_b\text{).}$$
>
> **Step 3 (conclusion).** Since $\mathcal{M}_b$ is injective, $[S, R]_{\operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{Aut}(V))} = SR - RS$. The right-hand side does not involve $b$, so the identification is canonical. Therefore the Lie algebra of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is $\operatorname{End}(V)$ with the commutator. $\blacksquare$

With Lemmas 2 and 3, the phrase "$\varrho_* : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism" has a single meaning, and the definition of the differential of a representation above is complete. Its two most useful consequences — that $\varrho_*$ is a representation, and that $\varrho_*(X) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\varrho(\exp(tX))$ — are Corollary C in the Examples section.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

A representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$ is the same thing as a **$\mathfrak{g}$-module** structure on $V$: by the Proposition above, it is a bilinear map $\mathfrak{g} \times V \to V$ in which the bracket acts as the commutator of the actions. The analogy with a [[Def - Module|module]] over a ring is exact once one replaces $\mathfrak{g}$ by its universal enveloping algebra $U(\mathfrak{g})$, the associative algebra generated by $\mathfrak{g}$ subject to $XY - YX = [X, Y]$; a $\mathfrak{g}$-module is then precisely a $U(\mathfrak{g})$-module, and equivalences of representations are precisely $U(\mathfrak{g})$-module isomorphisms. The series does not construct $U(\mathfrak{g})$ and does not use this reformulation; it is recorded because it explains why the representation theory of Lie algebras behaves like module theory (submodules, quotients, direct sums, homomorphism spaces) rather than like group theory.

In the categorical language of [[Def - Category]] and [[Def - Functor]], a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism $\mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$, that is, a morphism in the category of real Lie algebras with target the Lie algebra of endomorphisms of an object of $\mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb{K}}$. The representations of $\mathfrak{g}$ form a category $\mathbf{Rep}(\mathfrak{g})$ whose objects are pairs $(V, \lambda)$ and whose morphisms $(V, \lambda) \to (\tilde V, \tilde\lambda)$ are the **intertwiners**, the linear maps $T$ with $T\lambda(X) = \tilde\lambda(X)T$ for all $X$; the equivalences of this page are exactly the isomorphisms of $\mathbf{Rep}(\mathfrak{g})$, since an intertwiner that is a linear isomorphism has an inverse that is again an intertwiner (Lemma 4, Symmetry). The structural content of the induced homomorphism is that differentiation is a functor
$$\mathbf{Rep}(G) \longrightarrow \mathbf{Rep}(\mathfrak{g}), \qquad (V, \varrho) \mapsto (V, \varrho_*), \qquad T \mapsto T,$$
from the category of representations of the Lie group $G$ (objects $(V, \varrho)$, morphisms the linear maps intertwining $\varrho$ and $\tilde\varrho$) to $\mathbf{Rep}(\mathfrak{g})$: an intertwiner of group representations is an intertwiner of their differentials. That the assignment is well defined on objects is Corollary C; that it is well defined on morphisms is Corollary D; that it preserves identities and composition is immediate because it does nothing to the morphisms. This functor is the restriction to $H = \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ of the Lie functor $\mathbf{LieGrp} \to \mathbf{LieAlg}$, $G \mapsto \mathfrak{g}$, $\varphi \mapsto \varphi_*$, recorded as the Corollary on [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]]. In the other direction, the statement that for a *simply connected* $G$ every representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is $\varrho_*$ for a unique representation $\varrho$ of $G$ — the integration half of the Lie correspondence — is **not proved and not used in this series**; the classification on [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] avoids it by producing the group representations $\odot^k \varrho_{\mathrm{st}}$ directly and using the Lie algebra only to compare them.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name: a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is a family of infinitesimal generators satisfying the commutation relations of $\mathfrak{g}$.** The official definition is already operational, but the physicist's reading of it deserves to be spelled out because it is the reading under which the notion appears in gauge theory. Choose a basis $(X_1, \dots, X_m)$ of $\mathfrak{g}$, and write the bracket in this basis as $[X_a, X_b] = \sum_c f_{ab}^{\ c} X_c$ with real **structure constants** $f_{ab}^{\ c}$. By linearity, a representation $\lambda$ is determined by the $m$ operators $J_a := \lambda(X_a) \in \operatorname{End}(V)$, and by bracket preservation these operators satisfy the commutation relations $[J_a, J_b] = \sum_c f_{ab}^{\ c} J_c$; conversely, any $m$ operators satisfying these relations define a representation by $\lambda(\sum_a x_a X_a) := \sum_a x_a J_a$ (linearity is built in, and bracket preservation for basis elements extends to all of $\mathfrak{g}$ by bilinearity of both brackets). For $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ in the basis $(-i\sigma_a)$ the relations are $[J_a, J_b] = 2\epsilon_{abc}J_c$, and with $J_a = -2i L_a$ they become the angular momentum relations $[L_a, L_b] = i\epsilon_{abc}L_c$ of quantum mechanics (with $\hbar = 1$); a complex representation of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ is exactly a triple of angular momentum operators on a finite-dimensional state space, and the classification on [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] is the classification of spins.

**Gauge theory.** In chapter IV the curvature of a connection on a principal $G$-bundle is a 2-form with values in the adjoint bundle, built from $\mathfrak{g}$ through $\operatorname{Ad}$; the covariant derivative it induces on an associated bundle $P \times_\varrho V$ has curvature $\varrho_*(F_A)$, the $\mathfrak{g}$-valued form $F_A$ pushed into $\operatorname{End}(V)$ through the differential of the representation. Every formula of the series in which a Lie-algebra-valued form acts on a vector-valued form passes through a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$, and every such representation is the differential $\varrho_*$ of the group representation that defines the bundle. The adjoint representation $\operatorname{ad} = (\operatorname{Ad})_*$ is the case $V = \mathfrak{g}$ (Corollary A and [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]]), and it is the representation under which the curvature itself transforms; the Bianchi identity $dF_A + [A \wedge F_A] = 0$ is the statement that $F_A$ is covariantly closed for the covariant derivative built from $\operatorname{ad}$.

**Group actions.** The fundamental vector field construction of [[Def - Fundamental Vector Field of a Group Action]] is the non-linear cousin of the differential of a representation: a smooth action of $G$ on a manifold $M$ differentiates to a linear map $\mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{X}(M)$ into the (infinite-dimensional) Lie algebra of vector fields, which preserves brackets for right actions and reverses them for left actions ([[Thm - The Fundamental Vector Field Map is a Lie Algebra Homomorphism for Right Actions]]). When $M = V$ is a vector space and the action is linear, the fundamental vector field of $X$ is the linear vector field $v \mapsto \varrho_*(X)v$, and the sign difference between the two settings comes from the convention that representations act on the left while the bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$ is defined through *left*-invariant fields, whose flows are *right* translations.

**Compression.** Everything on this page is one idea applied three times: *differentiate at the identity*. A Lie group becomes a Lie algebra; a homomorphism becomes a linear bracket-preserving map; a representation becomes a family of generators with the right commutation relations. The one genuinely new fact is that the bracket of the *target* — the Lie algebra of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ — is the commutator (Lemma 3), and it is new only because the Lie bracket of a general Lie group is defined through vector fields; for matrix groups it is the statement that the second-order term of $e^{tX}e^{tY}e^{-tX}e^{-tY}$ is $t^2[X, Y]$.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the trivial representation (Bär Example 1.3.18.1).** For any Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ and any $V$, the zero map $\lambda(X) := 0 \in \operatorname{End}(V)$ is a representation. Clause 1: $\lambda(aX + bY) = 0 = a \cdot 0 + b \cdot 0 = a\lambda(X) + b\lambda(Y)$. Clause 2: $\lambda([X, Y]) = 0$ and $[\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] = [0, 0] = 0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 0 = 0$. Both clauses hold, so $\lambda = 0$ is a representation; it is the differential of the trivial group representation $\varrho(g) = \operatorname{id}_V$, because the curve $t \mapsto \varrho(\exp(tX)) = \operatorname{id}_V$ is constant and its derivative is $0$ (Lemma 2).

**Is an instance — the adjoint representation (Bär Example 1.3.18.2).** This is Corollary A.

> [!note]- Corollary A (Bär Example 1.3.18.2): $\operatorname{ad}$ is a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $\mathfrak{g}$
> **Statement.** Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a Lie algebra. For $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ define $\operatorname{ad}(X) : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ by $\operatorname{ad}(X)(Y) := [X, Y]$. Then $\operatorname{ad}(X) \in \operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})$, the map $\operatorname{ad} : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})$ is linear, and $\operatorname{ad}([X, Y]) = [\operatorname{ad}(X), \operatorname{ad}(Y)]$ for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$. Hence $\operatorname{ad}$ is a real representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V = \mathfrak{g}$, the **adjoint representation** of $\mathfrak{g}$ ([[Def - Adjoint Representation]]).
>
> **Proof.** We are given the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ and must verify three things: that each $\operatorname{ad}(X)$ is linear, that $X \mapsto \operatorname{ad}(X)$ is linear, and that brackets go to commutators.
>
> **$\operatorname{ad}(X)$ is an endomorphism.** For $Y, Z \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, $\operatorname{ad}(X)(aY + bZ) = [X, aY + bZ] = a[X, Y] + b[X, Z] = a\operatorname{ad}(X)(Y) + b\operatorname{ad}(X)(Z)$ (by bilinearity of the bracket in its second slot). So $\operatorname{ad}(X) \in \operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})$.
>
> **$\operatorname{ad}$ is linear.** For $X, X' \in \mathfrak{g}$, $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, and every $Y$, $\operatorname{ad}(aX + bX')(Y) = [aX + bX', Y] = a[X, Y] + b[X', Y] = (a\operatorname{ad}(X) + b\operatorname{ad}(X'))(Y)$ (by bilinearity of the bracket in its first slot); two endomorphisms agreeing on every $Y$ are equal.
>
> **Brackets go to commutators.** Fix $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$ and let $Z \in \mathfrak{g}$ be arbitrary. We compute, using the Jacobi identity in the form $[[X, Y], Z] + [[Y, Z], X] + [[Z, X], Y] = 0$ and the antisymmetry of the bracket:
> $$\operatorname{ad}([X, Y])(Z) = [[X, Y], Z] \qquad \text{(definition of } \operatorname{ad}\text{)}$$
> $$= -[[Y, Z], X] - [[Z, X], Y] \qquad \text{(Jacobi identity, solved for the first term)}$$
> $$= [X, [Y, Z]] - [Y, [X, Z]] \qquad \text{(antisymmetry: } -[[Y, Z], X] = [X, [Y, Z]] \text{ and } -[[Z, X], Y] = [[X, Z], Y] = -[Y, [X, Z]]\text{)}$$
> $$= \operatorname{ad}(X)\big(\operatorname{ad}(Y)(Z)\big) - \operatorname{ad}(Y)\big(\operatorname{ad}(X)(Z)\big) \qquad \text{(definition of } \operatorname{ad} \text{, twice in each term)}$$
> $$= \big(\operatorname{ad}(X)\operatorname{ad}(Y) - \operatorname{ad}(Y)\operatorname{ad}(X)\big)(Z) = [\operatorname{ad}(X), \operatorname{ad}(Y)](Z) \qquad \text{(definition of composition and of the commutator).}$$
> Since $Z$ was arbitrary, $\operatorname{ad}([X, Y]) = [\operatorname{ad}(X), \operatorname{ad}(Y)]$.
>
> **Conclusion.** Both clauses of the definition hold, so $\operatorname{ad}$ is a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on itself. The bracket-preservation identity, read as $\operatorname{ad}(X)[Y, Z] = [\operatorname{ad}(X)Y, Z] + [Y, \operatorname{ad}(X)Z]$, says that each $\operatorname{ad}(X)$ is a derivation of the bracket; this is the Jacobi identity rewritten, and it is the reason the Jacobi identity is an axiom. $\blacksquare$

In the smallest non-abelian case, $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{su}(2)$ with basis $(-i\sigma_1, -i\sigma_2, -i\sigma_3)$ and $[-i\sigma_a, -i\sigma_b] = 2\epsilon_{abc}(-i\sigma_c)$, the operator $\operatorname{ad}(-i\sigma_3)$ sends $-i\sigma_1 \mapsto [-i\sigma_3, -i\sigma_1] = 2\epsilon_{312}(-i\sigma_2) = 2(-i\sigma_2)$, $-i\sigma_2 \mapsto 2\epsilon_{321}(-i\sigma_1) = -2(-i\sigma_1)$, and $-i\sigma_3 \mapsto 0$; its matrix in this basis is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, twice the generator of rotations in the $(-i\sigma_1, -i\sigma_2)$-plane. This is the infinitesimal form of the fact, drilled on [[Ex - The Adjoint Representation of SU(2) in the Pauli Basis]], that $\operatorname{Ad}_g$ for $g = \operatorname{diag}(e^{i\varphi}, e^{-i\varphi})$ is a rotation by the angle $2\varphi$ — the doubling of the angle is the factor $2$ in the structure constants. For an abelian Lie algebra, $\operatorname{ad} = 0$ is the trivial representation.

**Is an instance — $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V)$ and the differential of a representation.** Corollary B is Lemma 2 restated for the record, since it is the item Bär uses without proof; Corollary C is the main theorem of the page.

> [!note]- Corollary B (Bär Remark 1.3.19, the identification $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) \cong \operatorname{End}(V)$)
> **Statement.** For a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$-vector space $V$, the group $GL(V) = \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is an open subset of $\operatorname{End}(V)$, and the tangent space at the identity is $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V)$, the velocity of a curve being its ordinary derivative.
>
> **Proof.** This is Lemma 2 above, Steps 2 and 4. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Corollary C (Bär Lemma 1.4.9, Corollary 1.4.10, Remark 1.3.19): the differential of a Lie group homomorphism is a Lie algebra homomorphism, and the differential of a representation is a representation
> **Statement.** (i) If $\varphi : G \to H$ is a Lie group homomorphism, then $\varphi_* = d_e\varphi : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism: it is linear and $\varphi_*([X, Y]) = [\varphi_*X, \varphi_*Y]$ for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$. (ii) If $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is a representation of the Lie group $G$, then $\varrho_* = d_e\varrho : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ is a representation of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$, real or complex according as $\varrho$ is. (iii) For every $X \in \mathfrak{g}$,
> $$\varrho_*(X) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} \varrho(\exp(tX)) \in \operatorname{End}(V),$$
> the ordinary derivative of a curve in $\operatorname{End}(V)$; for a closed subgroup $G \subseteq GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ with $X \in \mathfrak{g} \subseteq \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$, this reads $\varrho_*(X) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\varrho(e^{tX})$ with $e^{tX}$ the matrix exponential.
>
> **Proof.** We are given a Lie group homomorphism $\varphi$, respectively a representation $\varrho$, and we must show that the differential at $e$ preserves brackets, identify the target bracket for $H = \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, and derive the curve formula.
>
> **Part (i).** This is exactly the statement of [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]]: for a Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$, the differential $F_* = dF_{e_G} : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is linear and $F_*[X, Y]_{\mathfrak{g}} = [F_*X, F_*Y]_{\mathfrak{h}}$ for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$, where both brackets are the transported brackets of left-invariant vector fields. Its proof there shows that the left-invariant field of $X$ on $G$ is $F$-related to the left-invariant field of $F_*X$ on $H$ (because $F \circ L_g = L_{F(g)} \circ F$), that $F$-related fields have $F$-related brackets, and evaluates at $e$. Apply it with $F = \varphi$.
>
> **Part (ii).** Apply Part (i) with $H = \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, which is a Lie group by Lemma A of [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]], and $\varphi = \varrho$, a Lie group homomorphism by the definition of a representation: $d_e\varrho : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{Aut}(V))$ is linear and preserves the brackets, where the bracket on $\operatorname{Lie}(\operatorname{Aut}(V)) = T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is the one transported from left-invariant vector fields. By Lemma 2, $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V)$; by Lemma 3, the transported bracket is the commutator $SR - RS$. Hence $\varrho_* = d_e\varrho$, read as a map $\mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$, is $\mathbb{R}$-linear (clause 1) and satisfies $\varrho_*([X, Y]) = \varrho_*(X)\varrho_*(Y) - \varrho_*(Y)\varrho_*(X)$ (clause 2). Each $\varrho_*(X)$ lies in $\operatorname{End}(V) = \operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{K}}(V)$, so it is $\mathbb{K}$-linear, and the representation is real or complex according as $V$ is. Therefore $\varrho_*$ is a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$.
>
> **Part (iii).** Let $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and let $c(t) := \exp(tX)$, a smooth curve in $G$ with $c(0) = e$ and $\dot c(0) = X$ ([[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]]: $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ is the integral curve through $e$ of the left-invariant field of $X$). By [[Thm - Chain Rule for the Differential]], $d_e\varrho(\dot c(0)) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\varrho(c(t))$, the velocity in $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ of the curve $\varrho \circ c$. By Lemma 2 this velocity is the ordinary derivative of $t \mapsto \varrho(\exp(tX))$ in the vector space $\operatorname{End}(V)$, which is the displayed formula. For a closed subgroup $G \subseteq GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, $\exp(tX) = e^{tX}$ by [[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential]] (for $X \in \mathfrak{g} \subseteq \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ the matrix exponential $e^{tX} = \sum_{k \geq 0} t^kX^k/k!$ lies in $G$ and equals $\exp_G(tX)$), which gives the matrix form. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Second proof of Corollary C(i), following Bär's Lemma 1.4.9, with every step justified
> This proof uses three results proved elsewhere in the series and DG XI, restated here: (a) for every Lie group, $\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(tX)}Y = [X, Y]$, that is, $(\operatorname{Ad})_* = \operatorname{ad}$ (part (iii) of [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]]); (b) for a Lie group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$ and $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, $\varphi(\exp_G(tX)) = \exp_H(t\varphi_*X)$ (the [[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map|naturality of the exponential map]], Bär's Corollary 1.4.7, applied to $tX$ and using linearity of $\varphi_*$); (c) $\frac{d}{ds}\big|_{s=0}\exp(sY) = Y$ ([[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]]). Here $\alpha_g(h) = ghg^{-1}$ is conjugation ([[Def - Left and Right Translations and Conjugation on a Lie Group]]) and $\operatorname{Ad}_g = d_e\alpha_g$ ([[Def - Adjoint Representation]]), so that, by [[Thm - Chain Rule for the Differential]] and (c), $\operatorname{Ad}_g Y = \frac{d}{ds}\big|_{s=0}\alpha_g(\exp(sY))$ for every $g$ and $Y$. We also use that $\varphi$ commutes with conjugation: for $g, h \in G$, $\varphi(\alpha_g(h)) = \varphi(ghg^{-1}) = \varphi(g)\varphi(h)\varphi(g)^{-1} = \alpha_{\varphi(g)}(\varphi(h))$ (by the homomorphism property, including $\varphi(g^{-1}) = \varphi(g)^{-1}$; this is Bär's footnote).
>
> **Goal.** Fix $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$; we need to show $\varphi_*([X, Y]) = [\varphi_*X, \varphi_*Y]$.
>
> **Step 1 (express the bracket through $\operatorname{Ad}$).** Define the curve $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathfrak{g}$, $f(t) := \operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(tX)}Y$; it is smooth because $(g, Z) \mapsto \operatorname{Ad}_g Z$ is smooth (part (i) of the $\operatorname{Ad}$ page) and $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ is smooth. By (a), $f'(0) = [X, Y]$.
>
> **Step 2 (push the derivative through the linear map $\varphi_*$).** Since $\varphi_* : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is a linear map between finite-dimensional vector spaces, Lemma 1, Step 3, gives $\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\varphi_*(f(t)) = \varphi_*(f'(0))$ (the differential of a linear map is the map itself). Hence
> $$\varphi_*([X, Y]) = \varphi_*(f'(0)) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\varphi_*\big(\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(tX)}Y\big) \qquad \text{(Step 1; linearity of } \varphi_*\text{).}$$
>
> **Step 3 (compute $\varphi_*(\operatorname{Ad}_g Y)$ for fixed $g = \exp(tX)$).** For each fixed $t$,
> $$\varphi_*\big(\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(tX)}Y\big) = d_e\varphi\Big(\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\alpha_{\exp(tX)}(\exp(sY))\Big) \qquad \text{(} \operatorname{Ad}_g Y \text{ as the velocity of } s \mapsto \alpha_g(\exp(sY)) \text{, a curve through } \alpha_g(e) = e\text{)}$$
> $$= \frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\varphi\big(\alpha_{\exp(tX)}(\exp(sY))\big) \qquad \text{(chain rule for the differential, [[Thm - Chain Rule for the Differential]])}$$
> $$= \frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\alpha_{\varphi(\exp(tX))}\big(\varphi(\exp(sY))\big) \qquad \text{(} \varphi \text{ commutes with conjugation)}$$
> $$= \frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\alpha_{\exp(t\varphi_*X)}\big(\exp(s\varphi_*Y)\big) \qquad \text{(naturality of } \exp \text{, (b), applied twice)}$$
> $$= \operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(t\varphi_*X)}(\varphi_*Y) \qquad \text{(} \operatorname{Ad}_{g'}Y' \text{ as the velocity of } s \mapsto \alpha_{g'}(\exp(sY')) \text{, in } H \text{, with } g' = \exp(t\varphi_*X) \text{ and } Y' = \varphi_*Y\text{).}$$
>
> **Step 4 (differentiate in $t$ and conclude).** Combining Steps 2 and 3,
> $$\varphi_*([X, Y]) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(t\varphi_*X)}(\varphi_*Y) = [\varphi_*X, \varphi_*Y] \qquad \text{(by (a) applied in the Lie group } H \text{ to } \varphi_*X, \varphi_*Y \in \mathfrak{h}\text{).}$$
> Therefore $\varphi_*$ preserves brackets; it is linear as the differential of a smooth map at a point, so it is a Lie algebra homomorphism. No interchange of the two derivatives $\partial_t$ and $\partial_s$ was needed: the $s$-derivative was evaluated for each fixed $t$ before the $t$-derivative was taken. $\blacksquare$

**Is an instance — the differential of the standard representation is the inclusion.** Let $G \subseteq GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ be a closed subgroup with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_1G \subseteq \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$, and let $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}} : G \hookrightarrow GL(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{K}^n)$ be the standard representation, the inclusion map, which is a representation by [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]]. We verify that $(\varrho_{\mathrm{st}})_*(X) = X$ for every $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, that is, $(\varrho_{\mathrm{st}})_*$ is the inclusion $\mathfrak{g} \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{K}^n)$. By Corollary C(iii), $(\varrho_{\mathrm{st}})_*(X) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}(e^{tX}) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}e^{tX}$, the ordinary derivative in $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ of the matrix exponential curve, which is $Xe^{0} = X$ by [[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential]] ($\frac{d}{dt}e^{tX} = Xe^{tX}$). That the inclusion $\mathfrak{g} \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism is the statement that the bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$ is the restriction of the commutator, [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]], Lemma 3 — consistent with Corollary C(ii), as it must be. Concretely, for $G = SU(2)$ the standard representation on $\mathbb{C}^2$ differentiates to the inclusion $\mathfrak{su}(2) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^2)$, so the generators $J_a = -i\sigma_a$ act on $\mathbb{C}^2$ as the anti-Hermitian Pauli matrices themselves, and the relations $[J_a, J_b] = 2\epsilon_{abc}J_c$ are the Pauli commutation relations of [[Ex - su(2) in the Basis of Anti-Hermitian Pauli Matrices]].

**Is an instance — the differential of the dual of the standard representation is $X \mapsto -X^t$.** On [[Def - Constructions on Representations]] the dual of the standard representation of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}^*(g) = (g^{-1})^t$ in the dual basis (also [[Ex - The Dual Representation Requires the Inverse]]). We verify directly that $\lambda(X) := -X^t$ is a representation of $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ on $\mathbb{K}^n$. Clause 1: transposition is linear, so $-(aX + bY)^t = a(-X^t) + b(-Y^t)$. Clause 2: using $(AB)^t = B^tA^t$,
$$\lambda([X, Y]) = -(XY - YX)^t = -(Y^tX^t - X^tY^t) = X^tY^t - Y^tX^t = (-X^t)(-Y^t) - (-Y^t)(-X^t) = [\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] \qquad \text{(transpose reverses products; two minus signs cancel in each product).}$$
Both clauses hold. And it is indeed the differential of $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}^*$: by Corollary C(iii), $(\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}^*)_*(X) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\big((e^{tX})^{-1}\big)^t = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}(e^{-tX})^t = (-X)^t = -X^t$, using $(e^{tX})^{-1} = e^{-tX}$ (the matrix exponential page: $e^{sX}e^{tX} = e^{(s+t)X}$ and $e^{0} = 1$), the linearity and continuity of transposition (so that it commutes with the derivative), and $\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}e^{-tX} = -X$.

**Is NOT an instance — the transpose $X \mapsto X^t$ on $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$, $n \geq 2$.** The map $\tau(X) := X^t$ is linear, so clause 1 holds, but clause 2 fails: for all $X, Y$,
$$\tau([X, Y]) = (XY - YX)^t = Y^tX^t - X^tY^t = [Y^t, X^t] = -[\tau(X), \tau(Y)] \qquad \text{(transpose reverses products; antisymmetry of the commutator),}$$
so $\tau$ is an *anti*-homomorphism, and it is a homomorphism only where $[\tau(X), \tau(Y)] = 0$. A concrete witness: $X = E_{12}$, $Y = E_{21}$. Then $XY = E_{12}E_{21} = E_{11}$ and $YX = E_{21}E_{12} = E_{22}$ (matrix units multiply by $E_{ij}E_{kl} = \delta_{jk}E_{il}$), so $[X, Y] = E_{11} - E_{22}$, which is diagonal and equals its own transpose: $\tau([X, Y]) = E_{11} - E_{22}$. On the other hand $\tau(X) = E_{21}$ and $\tau(Y) = E_{12}$, so $[\tau(X), \tau(Y)] = E_{21}E_{12} - E_{12}E_{21} = E_{22} - E_{11}$. Since $E_{11} - E_{22} \neq E_{22} - E_{11}$ (the $(1,1)$ entries are $1$ and $-1$), clause 2 fails and $\tau$ is not a representation. The failure is repaired by a sign, as verified in the previous example; the pair (transpose fails, minus transpose works) is the Lie algebra shadow of the pair (naive dual $g \mapsto g^t$ fails, correct dual $g \mapsto (g^{-1})^t$ works) on [[Def - Constructions on Representations]], because the differential of $g \mapsto g^{-1}$ at $e$ is $-\operatorname{id}$ ([[Thm - The Exponential Map is a Local Diffeomorphism at the Origin]]).

**Is NOT an instance — a rescaling of a non-abelian representation.** Let $\lambda$ be any representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$, and put $\lambda_c(X) := c\lambda(X)$. Clause 1 holds because $\lambda_c$ is a scalar multiple of a linear map. For clause 2,
$$\lambda_c([X, Y]) = c\lambda([X, Y]) = c[\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)], \qquad [\lambda_c(X), \lambda_c(Y)] = [c\lambda(X), c\lambda(Y)] = c^2[\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] \qquad \text{(bracket preservation for } \lambda\text{; bilinearity of the commutator),}$$
so $\lambda_c$ is a representation if and only if $(c^2 - c)[\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] = 0$ for all $X, Y$, that is, if and only if $c \in \{0, 1\}$ or $\lambda(\mathfrak{g})$ is commutative. For the inclusion $\lambda = (\varrho_{\mathrm{st}})_* : \mathfrak{su}(2) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}^2)$ and $c = \tfrac12$: $\lambda_{1/2}([-i\sigma_1, -i\sigma_2]) = \tfrac12 \cdot 2(-i\sigma_3) = -i\sigma_3$, while $[\lambda_{1/2}(-i\sigma_1), \lambda_{1/2}(-i\sigma_2)] = \tfrac14[-i\sigma_1, -i\sigma_2] = \tfrac14 \cdot 2(-i\sigma_3) = -\tfrac{i}{2}\sigma_3$, and $-i\sigma_3 \neq -\tfrac{i}{2}\sigma_3$. So $X \mapsto \tfrac12 X$ is not a representation of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$: the normalisation of the generators is fixed by the structure constants, and "spin $\tfrac12$" is obtained not by halving the generators but by the representation $\varrho_1 = \varrho_{\mathrm{st}}$ itself, whose $H$-eigenvalues are $\pm 1$ in the normalisation of [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]].

**Corollary D — equivalent group representations have equivalent differentials.**

> [!note]- Corollary D: differentiation preserves equivalence
> **Statement.** Let $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ and $\tilde\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(\tilde V)$ be representations of the Lie group $G$, and let $T : V \to \tilde V$ be a linear map with $T \circ \varrho(g) = \tilde\varrho(g) \circ T$ for all $g \in G$. Then $T \circ \varrho_*(X) = \tilde\varrho_*(X) \circ T$ for all $X \in \mathfrak{g}$. In particular, if $\varrho$ and $\tilde\varrho$ are equivalent through $T$, then $\varrho_*$ and $\tilde\varrho_*$ are equivalent through the same $T$.
>
> **Proof.** We are given the intertwining identity for the group representations and must show it for the differentials. Fix $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and consider the two curves $t \mapsto T \circ \varrho(\exp(tX))$ and $t \mapsto \tilde\varrho(\exp(tX)) \circ T$ in the finite-dimensional vector space $\mathcal{L}(V, \tilde V)$ of linear maps $V \to \tilde V$. They are equal for every $t$ (by the hypothesis with $g = \exp(tX)$). Hence their ordinary derivatives at $t = 0$ are equal. The maps $S \mapsto T \circ S$ on $\operatorname{End}(V)$ and $\tilde S \mapsto \tilde S \circ T$ on $\operatorname{End}(\tilde V)$ are linear, so by Lemma 1, Step 3, they commute with differentiation of curves:
> $$T \circ \varrho_*(X) = T \circ \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\varrho(\exp(tX)) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\big(T \circ \varrho(\exp(tX))\big) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\big(\tilde\varrho(\exp(tX)) \circ T\big) = \tilde\varrho_*(X) \circ T \qquad \text{(Corollary C(iii); linearity of } S \mapsto TS \text{; the hypothesis; linearity of } \tilde S \mapsto \tilde ST \text{; Corollary C(iii)).}$$
> If $T$ is moreover a linear isomorphism, this is precisely the statement that $T$ is an equivalence of $\varrho_*$ and $\tilde\varrho_*$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary E — kernel and image.**

> [!note]- Corollary E: the kernel of a representation is an ideal and the image is a Lie subalgebra
> **Statement.** Let $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ be a representation. Then $\ker\lambda = \{X : \lambda(X) = 0\}$ is an ideal of $\mathfrak{g}$ — a subspace with $[X, Y] \in \ker\lambda$ whenever $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $Y \in \ker\lambda$ — and $\lambda(\mathfrak{g})$ is a Lie subalgebra of $\operatorname{End}(V)$ in the sense of [[Def - Lie Subalgebra and Abelian Lie Algebra]]. The representation is **faithful** if $\ker\lambda = 0$.
>
> **Proof.** $\ker\lambda$ and $\lambda(\mathfrak{g})$ are subspaces because $\lambda$ is linear (kernel and image of a linear map, [[Def - Kernel and Image]] in its linear form). If $Y \in \ker\lambda$ and $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, then $\lambda([X, Y]) = [\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] = [\lambda(X), 0] = 0$ (bracket preservation; the commutator with $0$ is $0$), so $[X, Y] \in \ker\lambda$. If $S = \lambda(X)$ and $R = \lambda(Y)$ lie in the image, then $[S, R] = [\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] = \lambda([X, Y])$ lies in the image. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary F (Bär Remark 1.3.17) — matrix form.** Every representation $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ is equivalent to a representation $\mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ on $\mathbb{K}^n$, and two choices of basis give equivalent matrix representations. Fix a basis $b$ of $V$ and put $\lambda_b := \mathcal{M}_b \circ \lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$. This is a representation: it is linear as a composite of linear maps, and
$$\lambda_b([X, Y]) = \mathcal{M}_b\big(\lambda(X)\lambda(Y) - \lambda(Y)\lambda(X)\big) = \mathcal{M}_b(\lambda(X))\mathcal{M}_b(\lambda(Y)) - \mathcal{M}_b(\lambda(Y))\mathcal{M}_b(\lambda(X)) = [\lambda_b(X), \lambda_b(Y)] \qquad \text{(bracket preservation for } \lambda\text{; multiplicativity and linearity of } \mathcal{M}_b\text{).}$$
The coordinate isomorphism $F_b : V \to \mathbb{K}^n$ is an equivalence of $\lambda$ and $\lambda_b$, because $F_b \circ \lambda(X) = F_b\lambda(X)F_b^{-1}F_b = \mathcal{M}_b(\lambda(X)) \circ F_b = \lambda_b(X) \circ F_b$ (inserting $F_b^{-1}F_b = \operatorname{id}_V$; definition of $\mathcal{M}_b$). For a second basis $b'$, transitivity and symmetry (Lemma 4) give the equivalence $F_{b'}F_b^{-1} = Q$ of $\lambda_b$ and $\lambda_{b'}$, and explicitly $\lambda_{b'}(X) = Q\lambda_b(X)Q^{-1}$, the change of basis formula. So, as Bär says, up to equivalence a Lie algebra representation is a Lie algebra homomorphism into a matrix algebra.

**Calibration check.** Three verifications the reader should carry out from what is on the page. First, compute the differential of the representations $\varrho_k : U(1) \to GL(1; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}^\times$, $\varrho_k(z) = z^k$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$), which are the building blocks of [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]]. Here $\mathfrak{u}(1) = i\mathbb{R}$, $\exp(t \cdot i\theta) = e^{it\theta}$ (the $1 \times 1$ matrix exponential), and $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}$; write $c(t) := e^{it\theta}$, so $c(0) = 1$ and $c'(0) = i\theta$ (the velocity of the one-parameter subgroup is its generator). For $k \geq 1$ the product rule gives $\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}c(t)^k = k\,c(0)^{k-1}c'(0) = ik\theta$; for $k = 0$ the curve is constant and the derivative is $0 = i \cdot 0 \cdot \theta$; for $k < 0$, differentiating the identity $c(t)^kc(t)^{-k} = 1$ at $t = 0$ (product rule, $c(0) = 1$) gives $\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}c(t)^k = -\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}c(t)^{-k} = -i(-k)\theta = ik\theta$. Therefore, by Corollary C(iii),
$$(\varrho_k)_*(i\theta) = ik\theta \qquad \text{for all } k \in \mathbb{Z},\ \theta \in \mathbb{R}:$$
the generator $i\theta$ of $\mathfrak{u}(1)$ acts on the charge-$k$ representation as multiplication by $ik\theta$, and the charge is read off as the eigenvalue of the generator $i$. Bracket preservation is the identity $0 = 0$, since both $\mathfrak{u}(1)$ and $\mathbb{C}$ are abelian, and $(\varrho_k)_*$ is faithful if and only if $k \neq 0$. Second, verify that the one-dimensional representation $\det : GL(n; \mathbb{K}) \to \mathbb{K}^\times$ (the calibration check of [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]]) differentiates to the trace: $\det_*(X) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\det(e^{tX}) = \operatorname{tr}X$ by Lemma 1 of [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]] (the derivative of $\det$ at $1$ in the direction $X$ is $\operatorname{tr}X$) and the chain rule; then check clause 2 by hand: $\operatorname{tr}[X, Y] = \operatorname{tr}(XY) - \operatorname{tr}(YX) = 0$, and $[\operatorname{tr}X, \operatorname{tr}Y] = 0$ in the abelian Lie algebra $\mathbb{K} = \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{K})$, so $\operatorname{tr} : \mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K}) \to \mathbb{K}$ is a representation, with kernel the ideal $\mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ of traceless matrices (Corollary E). Third, check that on the Lie algebra side the two adjoint representations agree: $(\operatorname{Ad})_* = \operatorname{ad}$ by [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]], so Corollary C(ii) applied to $\varrho = \operatorname{Ad}$ recovers Corollary A, and for $SU(2)$ the matrix of $\operatorname{ad}(-i\sigma_3)$ computed after Corollary A is the derivative at $\varphi = 0$ of the rotation matrix by $2\varphi$ of [[Ex - The Adjoint Representation of SU(2) in the Pauli Basis]], once the sign convention $\exp(\varphi(-i\sigma_3)) = \operatorname{diag}(e^{-i\varphi}, e^{i\varphi})$ is taken into account. If you can also explain why the transpose fails but minus the transpose works, you have understood the definition.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Invariant subspaces and the classification of representations *(from this chapter)*
> For a *connected* Lie group $G$, a subspace $W \subseteq V$ is invariant under $\varrho(G)$ if and only if it is invariant under $\varrho_*(\mathfrak{g})$; this is Lemma 5 of [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]], and it converts the classification of representations of the compact groups $U(1)$ and $SU(2)$ into linear algebra on $\mathfrak{u}(1) = i\mathbb{R}$ and on $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ — the highest-weight argument with $H, E, F$. Corollary D is what transports the resulting equivalences back to the group.

> [!tip] The adjoint bundle and the action of curvature on associated bundles *(from Gauge Theory III–IV)*
> A principal $G$-bundle $P$ and a representation $\varrho$ produce the associated bundle $P \times_\varrho V$; the curvature $F_A \in \Omega^2(M; \operatorname{ad}P)$ of a connection acts on sections of the associated bundle through $\varrho_*$, and the covariant derivative it induces has curvature $\varrho_*(F_A)$. **The theorem that principal connections induce covariant derivatives on associated bundles**, in chapter IV, is the point where the differential of a representation enters gauge theory for good; the case $\varrho = \operatorname{Ad}$, $\varrho_* = \operatorname{ad}$, gives the adjoint bundle $\operatorname{ad}P$ in which $F_A$ itself lives.

> [!tip] Infinitesimal generators of group actions *(from this chapter)*
> The fundamental vector field of [[Def - Fundamental Vector Field of a Group Action]] is the differential of a group action on a manifold, and [[Thm - The Fundamental Vector Field Map is a Lie Algebra Homomorphism for Right Actions]] is the analogue of Corollary C for actions that are not linear; for a linear action the fundamental vector field of $X$ is the linear field $v \mapsto \varrho_*(X)v$.

> [!tip] Angular momentum and spin *(from Quantum Mechanics)*
> A complex representation of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ is a triple of operators $L_1, L_2, L_3$ with $[L_a, L_b] = i\epsilon_{abc}L_c$ (after the rescaling $J_a = -2iL_a$ of the generators $J_a = \lambda(-i\sigma_a)$), and the irreducible ones are the spin-$\tfrac{k}{2}$ representations of dimension $k + 1$; the **Casimir operator** $L_1^2 + L_2^2 + L_3^2$, which commutes with every $\lambda(X)$, is not used in this series but is the standard tool for recognising which irreducible representation one is looking at.
