# Notation

Throughout, $\mathbb{K}$ denotes either $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{K}$; we write $n = \dim_{\mathbb{K}} V$. A **Lie algebra** $\mathfrak{g}$ over $\mathbb{K}$ is a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$-vector space equipped with a bilinear, antisymmetric bracket $[\cdot,\cdot] : \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ satisfying the Jacobi identity; the standing example is $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$, the tangent space at the identity $e$ of a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] $G$, with the bracket of [[Def - Left-Invariant Vector Field|left-invariant vector fields]] (for a matrix group this is the commutator $[X,Y] = XY - YX$). The full symbol registry for the chapter is on the topic page **Gauge Theory I — Lie Groups, Representations, and Group Actions**.

We write $\operatorname{End}(V)$ for the associative algebra of $\mathbb{K}$-linear maps $V \to V$, and $\operatorname{Aut}(V) = GL(V) = \{A \in \operatorname{End}(V) : A \text{ is invertible}\}$ for the group of linear automorphisms of $V$. The space $\operatorname{End}(V)$ carries a canonical Lie algebra structure, denoted $\mathfrak{gl}(V)$, whose bracket is the **commutator**
$$[A, B] := A \circ B - B \circ A \qquad (A, B \in \operatorname{End}(V));$$
this bracket is bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the Jacobi identity precisely because composition in $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is associative (the same computation that makes the matrix commutator a Lie bracket, on [[Def - Lie Subalgebra and Abelian Lie Algebra|Def - Lie Subalgebra and Abelian Lie Algebra]]). When a basis of $V$ is fixed, $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is identified with $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ and $\mathfrak{gl}(V)$ with $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$.

A **Lie algebra homomorphism** $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ between Lie algebras over the same field is a $\mathbb{K}$-linear map with $\lambda([X, Y]_{\mathfrak{g}}) = [\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)]_{\mathfrak{h}}$ for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$; when $\lambda$ reverses the bracket, $\lambda([X,Y]) = -[\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)]$, it is called a **Lie algebra anti-homomorphism**. For a smooth map $\varphi$ between manifolds, $d_e \varphi$ (equivalently $\varphi_{*,e}$, or simply $\varphi_*$ when the base point is the identity) denotes its differential at $e$; we reserve $\varrho$ for representations of a Lie group and $\lambda$ for representations of a Lie algebra.

> [!warning] Convention: source typo in the definition of equivalence
> Bär (Definition 1.3.16) writes the second representation as $\tilde{\lambda} : \tilde{\mathfrak{g}} \to \operatorname{End}(\tilde V)$, with a tilde on the Lie algebra. This is a misprint: equivalence is a relation between two representations of **one fixed** Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ (an intertwiner cannot connect representations of different algebras, since the intertwining condition below quantifies over a single $X \in \mathfrak{g}$). We use the corrected form $\tilde{\lambda} : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(\tilde V)$ throughout.

# Axiom Motivation

We already have, on [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group|Def - Representation of a Lie Group]], the notion of a linear action of a Lie group $G$: a Lie group homomorphism $\varrho : G \to GL(V)$. The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$ is the infinitesimal shadow of $G$ near the identity, and the entire strategy of the theory is to trade hard questions about the group for linear-algebraic questions about the algebra. So the object we must now isolate is the infinitesimal shadow of a representation: the datum that a representation of $G$ leaves behind when we differentiate it at the identity. Naming that datum abstractly, so that it can exist and be studied even when no group is in sight, is the purpose of this definition.

What must such an object be? A group representation attaches to each $g \in G$ an invertible operator $\varrho(g) \in GL(V)$, and it does so multiplicatively: $\varrho(gh) = \varrho(g)\varrho(h)$. Differentiating a curve $t \mapsto \varrho(\exp(tX))$ at $t = 0$ replaces the group element $g$ by an algebra element $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and replaces the invertible operator $\varrho(g)$ by an operator $\lambda(X) \in \operatorname{End}(V)$ that need no longer be invertible — the tangent space to $GL(V)$ at the identity is all of $\operatorname{End}(V)$ (this is proved below, and it is exactly why the algebra-level operators are unrestricted endomorphisms rather than automorphisms). The multiplicativity of $\varrho$ becomes, after differentiation, the requirement that $\lambda$ carry the bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$ to the commutator bracket of $\operatorname{End}(V)$. So the desiderata write themselves: $\lambda$ should be linear (a differential always is), it should land in $\operatorname{End}(V)$ (not merely $GL(V)$), and it should be a Lie algebra homomorphism into $\mathfrak{gl}(V)$.

Consider dropping the **bracket-preservation** clause and asking only that $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ be linear. Then $\lambda$ retains no trace of the one structure that distinguishes a Lie algebra from a bare vector space, and the notion collapses: every linear map would be a "representation", the adjoint representation below would not be singled out, and the correspondence with group representations would break, because the differential of a genuine group representation always does preserve the bracket (this is the content of the corollary in the Examples section, resting on [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism|Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]]). The failure is concrete: the transpose map $\lambda(X) = X^{t}$ on $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ is linear and even bijective, yet it *reverses* the bracket, so it is an anti-homomorphism and not a representation; a definition that admitted it would fail to be closed under the operation "differentiate a group representation", because no group representation differentiates to a bracket-reverser. The transpose example is verified clause by clause in the Examples section and shows that bracket-preservation is not automatic and must be imposed.

Consider instead *strengthening* the codomain, demanding that $\lambda$ take values in $\operatorname{Aut}(V) = GL(V)$ rather than $\operatorname{End}(V)$. This is worse than restrictive — it is almost always impossible. The operator $\lambda(X)$ is an infinitesimal generator; for the trivial representation $\lambda(X) = 0$ for every $X$, and $0 \notin GL(V)$ whenever $V \neq 0$. The point of passing to the algebra is precisely that the invertibility constraint of the group disappears in the tangent space, leaving the affine-linear ambient space $\operatorname{End}(V)$ in which brackets and sums make unconstrained sense. Requiring invertibility would forbid the trivial representation, forbid $\operatorname{ad}(X)$ (which is singular for every nonzero $X$, since $\operatorname{ad}(X)(X) = [X, X] = 0$ by antisymmetry puts the nonzero vector $X$ in its kernel, and which is the zero operator outright whenever $X$ lies in the centre of $\mathfrak{g}$), and generally forbid every representation but the rare ones by invertible operators. So the codomain must be $\operatorname{End}(V)$, and this is not a technical convenience but the essential simplification the infinitesimal picture buys us.

A reader given only these desiderata — "the infinitesimal form of a linear group action, carrying the bracket to the commutator, unconstrained by invertibility" — would arrive at exactly the definition below. The equivalence relation attached to it is forced by the same logic that forces the equivalence of group representations: two representations should count as the same when they differ only by a linear change of coordinates on the representation space, that is, by conjugation with a fixed invertible $T$, uniform in $X$.

# The Definition

This is a compound page: it fixes three interlocking notions — a representation of a Lie algebra, equivalence of such representations, and the representation $\varphi_* = d_e\varphi$ induced by a Lie group homomorphism — because the third is the mechanism by which the first arises from the group theory of the previous pages, and the second is what makes "the same representation" precise.

**Representation of a Lie algebra.** Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a Lie algebra over $\mathbb{K}$ and $V$ a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$-vector space. A **representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$** is a Lie algebra homomorphism
$$\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \longrightarrow \mathfrak{gl}(V) = \big(\operatorname{End}(V), [\cdot,\cdot]\big),$$
that is, a $\mathbb{K}$-linear map $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ satisfying
$$\lambda([X, Y]) = [\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] = \lambda(X)\lambda(Y) - \lambda(Y)\lambda(X) \qquad \text{for all } X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}.$$
If $V$ is a real vector space the representation is called **real**; if $V$ is a complex vector space it is called **complex** — the adjective names the scalar field of the representation space $V$. The vector space $V$ is the **representation space**, and $n = \dim_{\mathbb{K}} V$ is the **dimension** of the representation. One case deserves an explicit word, because it is the one the calibration example just below falls under: when $\mathfrak{g}$ is a *real* Lie algebra one still speaks of its **complex representations**, meaning that $V$ is a complex vector space, that $\operatorname{End}(V)$ is read as the complex-linear endomorphism algebra $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)$, and that $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)$ is required only to be $\mathbb{R}$-linear and bracket-preserving (equivalently, $\lambda$ extends uniquely to a $\mathbb{C}$-linear representation of the complexification $\mathfrak{g} \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$). The homomorphism condition is unchanged; only the scalar field over which $\lambda$ is asked to be linear is relaxed from that of $V$ to that of $\mathfrak{g}$.

In the smallest nontrivial concrete case, take $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{u}(1) = i\mathbb{R}$, the Lie algebra of the circle group $U(1)$, which is abelian so that $[X, Y] = 0$ for all $X, Y$, and take $V = \mathbb{C}$, so that $\operatorname{End}(V) = \mathbb{C}$ acting by multiplication. A representation is then a $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\lambda : i\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}$ with $\lambda(0) = [\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] = 0$ automatically (the target is commutative, so every commutator vanishes and the homomorphism condition is empty). Thus for an abelian $\mathfrak{g}$ and a one-dimensional $V$ a representation is nothing but a linear functional; the maps $\lambda(i\theta) = ik\theta$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ (multiplication by the scalar $ik\theta$) are the representations that arise from the group representations $z \mapsto z^{k}$, as the calibration check below verifies.

**Equivalence.** Two representations $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ and $\tilde{\lambda} : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(\tilde V)$ of the *same* Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ are **equivalent**, written $\lambda \cong \tilde{\lambda}$, if there is a linear isomorphism (an **intertwiner**) $T : V \to \tilde V$ such that
$$T \circ \lambda(X) = \tilde{\lambda}(X) \circ T \qquad \text{for all } X \in \mathfrak{g},$$
equivalently $\tilde{\lambda}(X) = T \lambda(X) T^{-1}$ for all $X$. In words: after transporting $V$ to $\tilde V$ by the single fixed isomorphism $T$, the two families of operators coincide.

**The induced representation.** Let $\varphi : G \to H$ be a [[Def - Lie Group Homomorphism|Lie group homomorphism]] between Lie groups with Lie algebras $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$ and $\mathfrak{h} = T_e H$. Its **induced map** is the differential at the identity,
$$\varphi_* := d_e \varphi : \mathfrak{g} \longrightarrow \mathfrak{h}.$$
By [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism|Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]] — restated and applied in the Examples section — $\varphi_*$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism. When $H = \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ and $\varphi = \varrho$ is a [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group|representation of the Lie group $G$]], the induced map $\varrho_* = d_e \varrho : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ is, by the corollary below, a representation of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$: it is *the* representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ associated to $\varrho$.

**Matrix form (Remark 1.3.17).** Choosing a basis of $V$ identifies $\operatorname{End}(V)$ with $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ and hence exhibits every representation, up to equivalence, as a Lie algebra homomorphism $\lambda : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$; a change of basis of $V$ replaces $\lambda$ by the equivalent representation $X \mapsto T \lambda(X) T^{-1}$ with $T$ the change-of-basis matrix. This is the concrete picture one computes with.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

There is a clean structural reading. The commutator makes $\operatorname{End}(V)$ into the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{gl}(V)$, and a representation is by definition simply a morphism in the category of Lie algebras with target $\mathfrak{gl}(V)$. Equivalently, a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$ is exactly the structure of a **$\mathfrak{g}$-module** on $V$: a $\mathbb{K}$-bilinear action map
$$\mathfrak{g} \times V \longrightarrow V, \qquad (X, v) \longmapsto X \cdot v := \lambda(X) v,$$
subject to the single compatibility axiom
$$[X, Y] \cdot v = X \cdot (Y \cdot v) - Y \cdot (X \cdot v) \qquad \text{for all } X, Y \in \mathfrak{g},\ v \in V.$$
The passage between the two descriptions is a tautology: given $\lambda$, set $X \cdot v = \lambda(X)v$, and the module axiom is the homomorphism condition evaluated on $v$; conversely a bilinear action satisfying the axiom defines $\lambda(X) = (v \mapsto X \cdot v) \in \operatorname{End}(V)$, linear in $X$ by bilinearity and bracket-preserving by the axiom. Representations of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$ and $\tilde V$ are equivalent exactly when the corresponding modules are isomorphic as $\mathfrak{g}$-modules, the isomorphism being the intertwiner $T$. The morphisms of the category of $\mathfrak{g}$-modules are the **intertwining maps** — linear maps $S : V \to \tilde V$ (not required invertible) with $S \circ \lambda(X) = \tilde{\lambda}(X) \circ S$ for all $X$ — and an equivalence is an invertible intertwiner. This is the precise sense in which a representation is "a linear action of the Lie algebra": the module viewpoint is the one under which direct sums, tensor products, duals, and subrepresentations (invariant subspaces) are the module-theoretic constructions, developed on [[Def - Constructions on Representations|Def - Constructions on Representations]].

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** The official definition — a bracket-preserving linear map into $\operatorname{End}(V)$ — is already the operational one: to *use* a representation is to compute with the operators $\lambda(X)$ and their commutators, and the module reformulation above is a relabelling, not a new idea. The single sentence that compresses the concept is: **a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is the infinitesimal, invertibility-freed form of a linear group action, obtained by differentiating at the identity, and it remembers precisely the bracket.** Everywhere a Lie group acts linearly, its Lie algebra acts by the derivative of that action; conversely the representation theory of $\mathfrak{g}$ controls that of the connected group $G$, because a subspace of $V$ is invariant under $\varrho(G)$ if and only if it is invariant under $\varrho_*(\mathfrak{g})$ (proved, for connected $G$, on [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)|Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]]).

The concept recurs verbatim across fields under different names. In physics, a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is a collection of operators — angular momentum operators for $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, say — closing under commutation into the same structure constants as the algebra; the "raising and lowering operators" of quantum mechanics are the images $\lambda(E)$, $\lambda(F)$ of a standard basis of $\mathfrak{sl}(2; \mathbb{C})$, and the eigenvalues of $\lambda(H)$ are the weights. In associative-algebra terms, a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is the same datum as a left module over the **universal enveloping algebra** $U(\mathfrak{g})$, the associative algebra generated by $\mathfrak{g}$ with $XY - YX$ identified with $[X, Y]$; this is the sense in which Lie representation theory is a special case of associative-algebra module theory, and it is why tensor and dual constructions behave functorially. In gauge theory, which is the destination of this chapter, a representation $\lambda$ of the structure algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is what turns a principal connection into a covariant derivative on the associated vector bundle $P \times_{\varrho} V$: the connection's $\mathfrak{g}$-valued curvature acts on sections through $\lambda$, so the physically observable field strength lives in $\operatorname{End}(V)$ via exactly this map.

# Examples / Corollaries

Each example is checked against the two clauses of the definition — linearity of $\lambda$ and preservation of the bracket — and the non-example is checked to fail the second clause on an explicit pair. The three corollaries are the results the definition was built to yield; each is proved in full on the page.

**Is an instance — the trivial representation (Example 1.3.18.1).** Fix any Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ and any $V$, and set $\lambda(X) := 0 \in \operatorname{End}(V)$ for every $X \in \mathfrak{g}$. Linearity holds because the zero map is linear. Bracket-preservation holds because
$$\lambda([X, Y]) = 0 \qquad \text{and} \qquad [\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] = [0, 0] = 0 \quad (\text{commutator of zero operators}),$$
so both sides vanish and agree for all $X, Y$. Hence $\lambda = 0$ is a representation, of dimension $n = \dim V$; it is the infinitesimal form of the trivial group representation $\varrho(g) = \operatorname{id}_V$.

**Is an instance — the adjoint representation $\operatorname{ad}$ (Example 1.3.18.2, Theorem 1.3.8).** Define $\operatorname{ad} : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})$ by $\operatorname{ad}(X)(Y) := [X, Y]$, taking the representation space to be $\mathfrak{g}$ itself. This is the **adjoint representation of the Lie algebra**, and the assertion that it is a representation is a corollary proved here in full.

> [!note]- Corollary 1: $\operatorname{ad}$ is a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $\mathfrak{g}$
> **Goal.** We must show three things: (a) for each fixed $X$, the map $\operatorname{ad}(X) : Y \mapsto [X, Y]$ lies in $\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})$; (b) the map $X \mapsto \operatorname{ad}(X)$ is $\mathbb{K}$-linear; (c) $\operatorname{ad}([X, Y]) = [\operatorname{ad}(X), \operatorname{ad}(Y)]$ in $\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})$.
>
> **(a) Each $\operatorname{ad}(X)$ is an endomorphism.** The bracket $[\cdot,\cdot]$ is bilinear (an axiom of a Lie algebra, [[Def - Lie Algebra|Def - Lie Algebra]]), so in particular it is linear in its second argument. Therefore $\operatorname{ad}(X) : Y \mapsto [X, Y]$ is a $\mathbb{K}$-linear map $\mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$, that is, $\operatorname{ad}(X) \in \operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})$ (by linearity of the bracket in the second slot).
>
> **(b) $X \mapsto \operatorname{ad}(X)$ is linear.** For $a, b \in \mathbb{K}$ and $X_1, X_2, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$,
> $$\operatorname{ad}(aX_1 + bX_2)(Y) = [aX_1 + bX_2, Y] = a[X_1, Y] + b[X_2, Y] \qquad \text{(bilinearity of the bracket in the first slot)}$$
> $$= a\,\operatorname{ad}(X_1)(Y) + b\,\operatorname{ad}(X_2)(Y) = \big(a\,\operatorname{ad}(X_1) + b\,\operatorname{ad}(X_2)\big)(Y) \qquad \text{(definition of } \operatorname{ad}\text{, addition in } \operatorname{End}(\mathfrak g)).$$
> Since this holds for every $Y$, the operators agree: $\operatorname{ad}(aX_1 + bX_2) = a\,\operatorname{ad}(X_1) + b\,\operatorname{ad}(X_2)$. So $\operatorname{ad}$ is linear.
>
> **(c) $\operatorname{ad}$ preserves the bracket.** Fix $X, Y, Z \in \mathfrak{g}$; we evaluate both sides on $Z$. The **Jacobi identity** of $\mathfrak{g}$ ([[Def - Lie Algebra|Def - Lie Algebra]]), in its cyclic form, reads
> $$[[X, Y], Z] + [[Y, Z], X] + [[Z, X], Y] = 0,$$
> hence
> $$\operatorname{ad}([X, Y])(Z) = [[X, Y], Z] = -[[Y, Z], X] - [[Z, X], Y] \qquad \text{(Jacobi identity, rearranged)}.$$
> We rewrite each term using **antisymmetry** $[U, W] = -[W, U]$ ([[Def - Lie Algebra|Def - Lie Algebra]]). First,
> $$-[[Y, Z], X] = [X, [Y, Z]] = \operatorname{ad}(X)\big([Y, Z]\big) = \operatorname{ad}(X)\big(\operatorname{ad}(Y)(Z)\big) \qquad \text{(antisymmetry; then definition of } \operatorname{ad} \text{ twice)}.$$
> Second, writing $[Z, X] = -[X, Z] = -\operatorname{ad}(X)(Z)$,
> $$-[[Z, X], Y] = [Y, [Z, X]] = \big[Y,\, -\operatorname{ad}(X)(Z)\big] = -\big[Y, \operatorname{ad}(X)(Z)\big] = -\operatorname{ad}(Y)\big(\operatorname{ad}(X)(Z)\big) \qquad \text{(antisymmetry; bilinearity; definition of } \operatorname{ad}).$$
> Combining the two displayed lines,
> $$\operatorname{ad}([X, Y])(Z) = \operatorname{ad}(X)\operatorname{ad}(Y)(Z) - \operatorname{ad}(Y)\operatorname{ad}(X)(Z) = \big[\operatorname{ad}(X), \operatorname{ad}(Y)\big](Z) \qquad \text{(definition of the commutator in } \operatorname{End}(\mathfrak g)).$$
> As $Z$ was arbitrary, $\operatorname{ad}([X, Y]) = [\operatorname{ad}(X), \operatorname{ad}(Y)]$.
>
> **Conclusion.** By (a), (b), (c), $\operatorname{ad} : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(\mathfrak{g})$ is a $\mathbb{K}$-linear bracket-preserving map, hence a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on itself. $\blacksquare$

The adjoint representation is the differential of the [[Def - Adjoint Representation|Lie group adjoint representation]] $\operatorname{Ad} : G \to GL(\mathfrak{g})$, that is $\operatorname{ad} = \operatorname{Ad}_* = d_e\operatorname{Ad}$; this identification, together with the matrix-group formula $\operatorname{Ad}_g X = gXg^{-1}$, is established on [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad|Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]]. The corollary just proved shows independently — from the Jacobi identity alone, with no group in sight — that $\operatorname{ad}$ deserves the name "representation".

**Is an instance — the differential of the standard representation is the inclusion.** Let $G \subseteq GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ be a matrix Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} \subseteq \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{K}^n)$, and let $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}} : G \hookrightarrow GL(\mathbb{K}^n)$ be the [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group|standard representation]], namely the inclusion. Its differential at the identity is the inclusion of tangent spaces $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}*} = d_e\varrho_{\mathrm{st}} : \mathfrak{g} \hookrightarrow \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{K}^n)$, $X \mapsto X$. We check directly that $X \mapsto X$ is a representation: it is linear (the identity inclusion of a subspace), and for $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$, using that the bracket on $\mathfrak{g}$ is the commutator for a matrix group ([[Ex - The Lie Bracket on a Matrix Lie Algebra is the Commutator|Ex - The Lie Bracket on a Matrix Lie Algebra is the Commutator]]) and that the bracket on $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{K}^n)$ is that same commutator,
$$\varrho_{\mathrm{st}*}([X, Y]) = [X, Y] = XY - YX = [\varrho_{\mathrm{st}*}(X), \varrho_{\mathrm{st}*}(Y)] \qquad \text{(commutator bracket on both sides).}$$ So the standard representation of a matrix group differentiates to the tautological inclusion $\mathfrak{g} \hookrightarrow \mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$.

**Is NOT an instance — transpose on $\mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$ (per-clause failure of bracket-preservation).** Let $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{K}^n)$ with the commutator bracket, take $V = \mathbb{K}^n$ so that $\operatorname{End}(V) = \mathfrak{gl}(n; \mathbb{K})$, and define $\lambda(X) := X^{t}$, the transpose. The **linearity** clause holds: $(aX_1 + bX_2)^{t} = aX_1^{t} + bX_2^{t}$. The **bracket** clause fails. Using $(AB)^{t} = B^{t}A^{t}$,
$$\lambda([X, Y]) = (XY - YX)^{t} = Y^{t}X^{t} - X^{t}Y^{t} = [Y^{t}, X^{t}] = -[X^{t}, Y^{t}] = -[\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)],$$
so $\lambda$ is a Lie algebra **anti**-homomorphism, not a homomorphism. To see that the two sides genuinely differ (that this is not a degenerate coincidence), take $n = 2$ and the matrix units $X = E_{12}$, $Y = E_{21}$. Then $[X, Y] = E_{12}E_{21} - E_{21}E_{12} = E_{11} - E_{22}$, so
$$\lambda([X, Y]) = (E_{11} - E_{22})^{t} = E_{11} - E_{22},$$
whereas
$$[\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)] = [E_{12}^{t}, E_{21}^{t}] = [E_{21}, E_{12}] = E_{21}E_{12} - E_{12}E_{21} = E_{22} - E_{11} = -(E_{11} - E_{22}).$$
These are negatives of each other and both nonzero, so $\lambda([X, Y]) \neq [\lambda(X), \lambda(Y)]$. Transpose is therefore not a representation. (It becomes one after composing with negation, $X \mapsto -X^{t}$, which is the differential of the group anti-automorphism repair $g \mapsto (g^{-1})^{t}$ underlying the dual representation on [[Def - Constructions on Representations|Def - Constructions on Representations]]; the sign is exactly what fixes the bracket.)

**Corollary 2 — the tangent space of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ at the identity is $\operatorname{End}(V)$ (I1.3.3).** This is the fact, used silently above, that the algebra-level operators range over all of $\operatorname{End}(V)$.

> [!note]- Corollary 2: $T_{\operatorname{id}}\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V)$
> **Goal.** We show that $\operatorname{Aut}(V) = GL(V)$ is an open submanifold of the vector space $\operatorname{End}(V)$ and that its tangent space at the identity is canonically $\operatorname{End}(V)$ itself.
>
> **Step 1 — $GL(V)$ is open in $\operatorname{End}(V)$.** Fix a basis of $V$, identifying $\operatorname{End}(V) \cong \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) \cong \mathbb{K}^{n^2}$, a finite-dimensional vector space. The determinant $\det : \operatorname{End}(V) \to \mathbb{K}$ is a polynomial in the matrix entries, hence continuous, and $GL(V) = \{A : \det A \neq 0\} = \det^{-1}(\mathbb{K} \setminus \{0\})$ is the preimage of the open set $\mathbb{K} \setminus \{0\}$ under a continuous map (since $\det A \neq 0$ if and only if $A$ is invertible). Therefore $GL(V)$ is open in $\operatorname{End}(V)$.
>
> **Step 2 — tangent space of an open subset of a vector space.** An open subset $U$ of a finite-dimensional vector space $W$ is a smooth manifold whose tangent space at each point $p \in U$ is canonically identified with $W$: the identification sends $w \in W$ to the velocity $\dot{c}(0)$ of the straight curve $c(t) = p + tw$, which lies in $U$ for small $t$ because $U$ is open. This is the tangent space of an open submanifold ([[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold|Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold]]), and the identification is the standard one for a vector space regarded as a manifold via a single global chart.
>
> **Step 3 — conclusion.** Applying Step 2 with $W = \operatorname{End}(V)$, $U = GL(V)$ (open by Step 1), and $p = \operatorname{id}_V$ gives
> $$T_{\operatorname{id}} \operatorname{Aut}(V) = T_{\operatorname{id}} GL(V) \cong \operatorname{End}(V),$$
> the velocity of $t \mapsto \operatorname{id} + tA$ being $A$. In particular the Lie algebra of $GL(V)$ is $\operatorname{End}(V)$, and — by the commutator computation for matrix groups, [[Ex - The Lie Bracket on a Matrix Lie Algebra is the Commutator|Ex - The Lie Bracket on a Matrix Lie Algebra is the Commutator]] — its bracket is the commutator, so $\operatorname{Lie}(GL(V)) = \mathfrak{gl}(V)$. The concrete case $V = \mathbb{K}^n$ is [[Ex - The Lie Algebra of GL(n,R) is the Space of n by n Matrices|Ex - The Lie Algebra of GL(n,R) is the Space of n by n Matrices]]. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 3 — the differential of a group representation is an algebra representation (Theorem 1.3.9 / Theorem 1.4.9).** This is the corollary the whole page is aimed at: it says the "differentiate at the identity" construction lands inside the class just defined.

> [!note]- Corollary 3: if $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is a Lie group representation, then $\varrho_* : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ is a Lie algebra representation
> **Goal.** Given a [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group|Lie group representation]] $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ — a Lie group homomorphism into $GL(V)$ — we must show that $\varrho_* = d_e\varrho : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism into $\mathfrak{gl}(V)$, i.e. a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$.
>
> **Step 0 — the target is a Lie algebra we understand.** By Corollary 2, $\operatorname{Aut}(V) = GL(V)$ is a Lie group with Lie algebra $\operatorname{Lie}(GL(V)) = \operatorname{End}(V)$, the bracket being the commutator, so $\operatorname{Lie}(GL(V)) = \mathfrak{gl}(V)$. Thus $\varrho : G \to GL(V)$ is a Lie group homomorphism between Lie groups whose Lie algebras are $\mathfrak{g}$ and $\mathfrak{gl}(V)$, and $\varrho_* = d_e\varrho$ is a linear map $\mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$.
>
> **Step 1 — invoke the induced-homomorphism theorem.** We use the following result, proved in full elsewhere in the vault and restated here at its point of use.
> > **Theorem (a Lie group homomorphism induces a Lie algebra homomorphism, [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]]).** For any Lie group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to H$ between Lie groups with Lie algebras $\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{h}$, the differential $\varphi_* = d_e\varphi : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism: it is linear and $\varphi_*[X, Y] = [\varphi_* X, \varphi_* Y]$ for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$.
> Apply this theorem with $\varphi = \varrho$ and $H = GL(V)$, whose Lie algebra $\mathfrak{h} = \operatorname{End}(V)$ with the commutator bracket was identified in Step 0.
>
> **Step 2 — conclusion.** The theorem yields that $\varrho_* : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ is linear and satisfies $\varrho_*[X, Y] = [\varrho_* X, \varrho_* Y]$, where the bracket on the right is the commutator in $\operatorname{End}(V)$ (Step 0). This is exactly the definition of a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$. Therefore $\varrho_*$ is a Lie algebra representation, the one associated to $\varrho$. $\blacksquare$

Bär defers this corollary from Remark 1.3.19 to Corollary 1.4.10; the proof is complete once the induced-homomorphism theorem and Corollary 2 are in hand, which is why it is recorded here rather than left as a forward reference.

**Calibration check.** Two verifications the reader can carry out from the page alone. First, compute the algebra representations underlying the circle-group representations $\varrho_k : U(1) \to GL(1; \mathbb{C})$, $z \mapsto z^{k}$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$), the subject of [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)|Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]]. The Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{u}(1) = T_1 U(1) = i\mathbb{R}$, and the exponential map is $\exp(i\theta) = e^{i\theta}$ (the [[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential|matrix exponential]] for the $1 \times 1$ group). By the induced-map definition and the chain rule,
$$(\varrho_k)_*(i\theta) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} \varrho_k\big(\exp(t \cdot i\theta)\big) = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} \big(e^{it\theta}\big)^{k} = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} e^{ikt\theta} = ik\theta \in \mathbb{C} = \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{C}),$$
that is, $(\varrho_k)_*$ is multiplication by the scalar $ik\theta$; symbolically $i\theta \mapsto ik\theta$. For $k = 0$ this recovers the trivial representation $\lambda = 0$, consistent with $\varrho_0(z) = 1$ being the trivial group representation. Second, confirm that equivalence is an equivalence relation on representations: reflexivity via $T = \operatorname{id}$; symmetry because $T\lambda(X) = \tilde\lambda(X)T$ gives $T^{-1}\tilde\lambda(X) = \lambda(X)T^{-1}$; transitivity because if additionally $S\tilde\lambda(X) = \hat\lambda(X)S$ then $(ST)\lambda(X) = S\tilde\lambda(X)T = \hat\lambda(X)(ST)$, with $ST$ again an isomorphism.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Constructions on representations *(from this section)*
> With the module viewpoint fixed, the [[Def - Constructions on Representations|direct sum, tensor product, dual, and exterior and symmetric powers]] of Lie algebra representations are defined by differentiating the corresponding group constructions, or equivalently by the Leibniz-type formulas $(\lambda \otimes \mu)(X) = \lambda(X) \otimes \operatorname{id} + \operatorname{id} \otimes \mu(X)$ and $\lambda^*(X) = -\lambda(X)^{t}$ — the additive, derivation-like behaviour that replaces the multiplicative behaviour of group representations, with the dual's minus sign explained by the transpose non-example above.

> [!tip] Complex representations of $U(1)$ and $SU(2)$ *(from this section)*
> The classification of the irreducible complex representations of $U(1)$ and $SU(2)$ on [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)|Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] is carried out at the Lie-algebra level and transported back to the group, using that for a connected group invariant subspaces coincide at the two levels; the integer weights there are precisely the eigenvalues of the operators $\lambda(H)$ produced by this definition.

> [!tip] Associated bundles and covariant derivatives *(from Gauge Theory III–IV)*
> A representation $\lambda$ of the structure algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is the ingredient that lets a principal connection act on the **associated vector bundle** $P \times_{\varrho} V$: the connection one-form's $\mathfrak{g}$-values are pushed into $\operatorname{End}(V)$ by $\lambda$, producing the covariant derivative and, through the curvature, the field strength of gauge theory.
