# Notation

This page depends on two of the series' standing conventions. Lie groups are smooth manifolds — smooth means $C^\infty$, Hausdorff, second countable — carrying a group structure whose multiplication and inversion are smooth, and a Lie group homomorphism is a smooth group homomorphism. Representations, like all group actions on manifolds in this series, are **left** actions: the element $g$ acts first and $h$ acts afterwards in the product $hg$, so that $\varrho(hg) = \varrho(h) \circ \varrho(g)$ is the order in which the maps are composed. (Right actions are reserved for principal bundles, which do not appear on this page.)

This is a compound page: it defines five interlocking notions — a **representation** of a Lie group, a **faithful** representation, the **trivial** representation, the **standard** representations of the classical matrix groups, and **equivalence** of representations — because each of the last four is a one-line specialisation or comparison of the first, and none of them is used in the series without the others.

Throughout, $G$ denotes a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] with neutral element $e$, and $\mathbb{K}$ denotes one of the two fields $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. The letter $V$ denotes a [[Def - Finite-Dimensional Vector Space|finite-dimensional]] $\mathbb{K}$-[[Def - Vector Space|vector space]], and $n = \dim_{\mathbb{K}} V$ is its dimension over $\mathbb{K}$. We write $\operatorname{End}(V)$ for the $\mathbb{K}$-vector space of all $\mathbb{K}$-[[Def - Linear Map|linear maps]] $V \to V$ and $\operatorname{Aut}(V) \subset \operatorname{End}(V)$ for the subset of [[Def - Invertibility and Isomorphism|invertible]] linear maps; $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is a group under composition, with neutral element the identity map $\operatorname{id}_V$. The same group is written $GL(V)$ in much of the literature, and we use the two names interchangeably; Bär writes $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ throughout §1.3. For the coordinate space $V = \mathbb{K}^n$ we identify $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{K}^n)$ with the space $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ of $n \times n$ matrices with entries in $\mathbb{K}$ — a linear map corresponds to its [[Def - Matrix of a Linear Map|matrix]] with respect to the standard basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$, and composition of maps corresponds to the product of matrices by [[Thm - Composition Corresponds to Matrix Multiplication]] — so that $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{K}^n) = GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, the general linear group of invertible matrices. The [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups|classical matrix groups]] $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, $O(n)$, $SO(n)$, $SL(n; \mathbb{K})$, $U(n)$, $SU(n)$ are Lie groups: $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is the open subset $\{A : \det A \neq 0\}$ of $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) \cong \mathbb{K}^{n^2}$, its multiplication is polynomial and its inversion rational in the entries, and each of the others is a closed subgroup of some $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, hence an embedded Lie subgroup by [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem|the closed subgroup theorem]]. When $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$ we regard $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C}) \cong \mathbb{C}^{n^2} \cong \mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$ as a *real* manifold of dimension $2n^2$, so that $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$ and $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ for complex $V$ are real Lie groups; a complex representation is a homomorphism of real Lie groups whose values happen to be complex-linear maps. (Bär's §1.1 prints the real dimension of $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C})$ as $(2n)^2$; the correct value $2n^2$ is used here.)

The symbol $\varrho$ (and $\tilde\varrho$, $\varrho_1$, $\varrho_2$, $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}$) always denotes a representation; $T$ denotes a linear isomorphism between the underlying spaces of two representations; $\ker \varrho = \{g \in G : \varrho(g) = \operatorname{id}_V\}$ is the [[Def - Kernel and Image|kernel]] of $\varrho$ as a group homomorphism. For an ordered [[Def - Basis|basis]] $b = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ of $V$ we write $F_b : V \to \mathbb{K}^n$ for the coordinate isomorphism $F_b\big(\sum_{j=1}^n x_j b_j\big) = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, and $\mathcal{M}_b : \operatorname{End}(V) \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$, $\mathcal{M}_b(S) = F_b \circ S \circ F_b^{-1}$, for the map sending a linear map to its matrix in the basis $b$ (that this is the same as the matrix of [[Def - Matrix of a Linear Map]] is the "central identity" $\mathcal{M}(Sv) = \mathcal{M}(S)\mathcal{M}(v)$ on that page). The unit circle group is $U(1) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$ under multiplication, and $\mathbb{K}^\times = GL(1; \mathbb{K}) = \mathbb{K} \setminus \{0\}$.

> [!warning] Convention: the smooth structure on $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$
> Bär's Definition 1.3.1 asks that $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ be a Lie group homomorphism, and his Remark 1.3.2 supplies the Lie group structure on $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ by choosing a basis, $\operatorname{Aut}(V) \cong GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. The choice is harmless, but that it is harmless is a statement requiring proof: two bases give two identifications, and the smooth structures they transport must coincide. Lemma A below proves this, so that "smooth map into $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$" has one meaning. Haydys does not treat representations abstractly and works with matrix groups directly; for him a representation is a smooth homomorphism into $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, which is the matrix form of the same notion (Lemma E below shows every representation is equivalent to one of this form).

# Axiom Motivation

The whole of gauge theory rests on one idea: a symmetry group $G$ that acts on the *fields* of a physical theory, and the fields take values in a vector space. The electromagnetic potential is acted on by $U(1)$, a Dirac spinor by $SU(2)$ or by the spin group, a quark triplet by $SU(3)$; and in every case the action of $g \in G$ on the field value $v$ is *linear* — it preserves superposition, because the field equations are linear in the field values at a point and the symmetry must carry solutions to solutions. So the object we need is a way of letting a Lie group act on a finite-dimensional vector space by linear maps, in a manner that respects both the group law and the smooth structure of the group. That object is a representation. Later in the series it reappears as the input to the associated bundle construction — a principal $G$-bundle together with a representation $\varrho$ of $G$ on $V$ produces a vector bundle with fibre $V$ (the **associated bundle**, chapter III), and every matter field of a gauge theory is a section of such a bundle. The definition below is therefore not a piece of algebra we pass through on the way to geometry; it is the dictionary that translates "the field has charge $k$" or "the field is a spinor" into geometry.

Let us collect what the definition must capture. First, every group element $g$ must act by a linear map $\varrho(g) : V \to V$, and the action must be invertible, since $g^{-1}$ must undo what $g$ does. Second, acting by $g$ and then by $h$ must be the same as acting by the product $hg$ — otherwise the group law of $G$ is not reflected in the action and the word "symmetry group" is unearned. Third, the action must depend smoothly on $g$, so that the infinitesimal generators of the symmetry — the Lie algebra — can act too; this is the clause that lets us differentiate $\varrho$ at $e$ and obtain a Lie algebra representation ([[Def - Representation of a Lie Algebra]]), and without it none of the Lie-algebraic machinery of the series (adjoint bundles, curvature as a Lie-algebra-valued form, the Chern–Weil homomorphism) could be attached to the group. Fourth, the vector space must be finite-dimensional, so that "smooth map into $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$" has a meaning: $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ must itself be a Lie group, and it is one exactly when $V$ is finite-dimensional, where a choice of basis identifies it with the open subset $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ of the matrix space $\mathbb{K}^{n^2}$.

Now the per-clause failure analysis. **Drop linearity** and what remains is a smooth action of $G$ on the manifold $V$, which need not respect the vector space structure at all. The circle $U(1)$ acts smoothly on $\mathbb{C}$ by $z \cdot v := z(v - 1) + 1$, rotation about the point $1$ rather than about the origin; this is an action (we verify it in the Examples), but $z \cdot 0 = 1 - z \neq 0$ for $z \neq 1$, so no map $v \mapsto z \cdot v$ is linear and the action is not a representation. Physically, an action that moves the zero field to a non-zero field is not a symmetry of a linear field theory. **Drop the homomorphism property** and the assignment $g \mapsto \varrho(g)$ no longer encodes the group: the map $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{K}^\times$, $t \mapsto 1 + t$, is smooth and linear in $t$ but $\varrho(s + t) = 1 + s + t$ is not $\varrho(s)\varrho(t) = 1 + s + t + st$, and worse, $\varrho(-1) = 0$ is not invertible at all. The homomorphism clause also carries a subtlety: it forces $\varrho(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$ and $\varrho(g^{-1}) = \varrho(g)^{-1}$ (Corollary (i) below), so invertibility of each $\varrho(g)$ would follow automatically from the homomorphism property into the *monoid* $\operatorname{End}(V)$ together with $\varrho(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$; we ask for values in $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ directly because that is the Lie group in which smoothness is measured. **Drop smoothness** and one loses the derivative $d_e \varrho$: there are group homomorphisms $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^\times_{>0}$ that are not continuous (they are built from a $\mathbb{Q}$-basis of $\mathbb{R}$, whose existence rests on the axiom of choice; such homomorphisms are not used anywhere in this series), and for these the "infinitesimal generator" does not exist. For the compact groups of gauge theory it is a theorem — not proved and not used in this series — that every *continuous* homomorphism between Lie groups is smooth, so the clause could be weakened to continuity; we keep smoothness because it is what every argument actually uses. **Drop finite-dimensionality** and the phrase "Lie group homomorphism into $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$" has no meaning, because $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ has no canonical manifold structure; the regular representation of $U(1)$ on the infinite-dimensional space $C^\infty(U(1))$ by translation, $(\varrho(z) f)(w) = f(z^{-1} w)$, is a perfectly good linear action but is outside the scope of this page, and the theory of such representations needs the analytic notions of chapter IX.

The remaining notions are comparisons. A representation is **faithful** if $\varrho$ is injective, so that the group is seen in full inside $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$. The failure of faithfulness is measured by the kernel $\ker \varrho$, a closed normal subgroup of $G$ (Lemma B): a representation is exactly as unfaithful as its kernel is large. The adjoint representation of $SU(2)$ has kernel $\{\pm 1_2\}$ and is therefore not faithful, a fact with geometric consequences — it is why $SU(2)$ is a double cover of $SO(3)$, and why spinors exist ([[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]] and chapter VIII). Two representations are **equivalent** if they are the same up to a relabelling of the vector space: an isomorphism $T : V \to \tilde V$ with $T \circ \varrho(g) = \tilde\varrho(g) \circ T$ for every $g$. Why an isomorphism and not merely a linear map? The zero map $V \to \tilde V$ intertwines any two representations, so "there is an intertwining linear map" would make every pair equivalent; invertibility is what makes $T$ a relabelling rather than a collapse. Why for *every* $g$? Because the relabelling must be one and the same for the whole group; a $T$ that intertwined $\varrho(g)$ and $\tilde\varrho(g)$ for a single $g$ would say nothing about the group. Equivalent representations have the same dimension and the same kernel (Lemma D), so faithfulness and dimension are invariants of the equivalence class, and the representation theory of $G$ — the classification of chapter I's [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] — is a classification up to equivalence.

Could a reader invent the definition from these desiderata? We claim so: "$G$ acts on $V$" forces a map $G \times V \to V$; "linearly" forces each $\varrho(g)$ to lie in $\operatorname{End}(V)$; "as a group" forces $\varrho(gh) = \varrho(g)\varrho(h)$, hence invertibility and $\varrho(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$; "smoothly" forces $\varrho$ to be a smooth map into the Lie group $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$; and the last phrase forces $V$ to be finite-dimensional. The definition is the conjunction of these forced choices and nothing else.

# The Definition

> **Definition (representation of a Lie group; Bär Definition 1.3.1).** Let $G$ be a Lie group and let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{K} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$. A **representation** of $G$ on $V$ is a [[Def - Lie Group Homomorphism|Lie group homomorphism]]
> $$\varrho : G \longrightarrow \operatorname{Aut}(V) = GL(V),$$
> that is, a map satisfying
> 1. **(homomorphism)** $\varrho(gh) = \varrho(g) \circ \varrho(h)$ for all $g, h \in G$, and
> 2. **(smoothness)** $\varrho$ is a smooth map of manifolds, where $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ carries the Lie group structure of Lemma A below.
>
> The representation is called **real** if $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ and **complex** if $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$. The number $\dim_{\mathbb{K}} V$ is the **dimension** of the representation. The vector space $V$ is called the **representation space**, and we say that $G$ **acts on $V$ through $\varrho$**, writing $g \cdot v := \varrho(g)(v)$ when $\varrho$ is understood.

The definition packs a family of maps $\{\varrho(g)\}_{g \in G}$ into a single map $\varrho$ with values in a group, so that both the group law (clause 1) and the manifold structure (clause 2) of $G$ can be compared with those of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ at once. Before anything can be said about clause 2, the Lie group structure of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ must be established, and — since it is constructed by choosing a basis — shown to be independent of the choice. This is the content of Bär's Remark 1.3.2, proved here in full.

> [!note]- Lemma A (Bär Remark 1.3.2): $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is a Lie group, canonically
> **Statement.** Let $V$ be a $\mathbb{K}$-vector space of dimension $n \geq 1$. For every ordered basis $b = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ of $V$ the map $\mathcal{M}_b : \operatorname{Aut}(V) \to GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, $\mathcal{M}_b(S) = F_b \circ S \circ F_b^{-1}$, is a group isomorphism. The topology and smooth structure on $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ obtained by declaring $\mathcal{M}_b$ to be a diffeomorphism make $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ a Lie group, and they do not depend on the basis $b$. With this structure $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is a Lie group of real dimension $n^2$ if $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ and $2n^2$ if $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$. (For $n = 0$, $V = \{0\}$ and $\operatorname{Aut}(V) = \{\operatorname{id}_V\}$ is the trivial group, a zero-dimensional Lie group; nothing below needs this case.)
>
> **Proof.** We are given a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$-vector space $V$ with $n = \dim V \geq 1$ and two ordered bases $b$ and $b'$; we must show, first, that $\mathcal{M}_b$ is a group isomorphism onto $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$; second, that transporting the Lie group structure of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ along $\mathcal{M}_b$ makes $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ a Lie group; and third, that the structures transported along $\mathcal{M}_b$ and along $\mathcal{M}_{b'}$ coincide.
>
> **Step 0 (the coordinate map is an isomorphism).** The map $F_b : V \to \mathbb{K}^n$, $F_b\big(\sum_j x_j b_j\big) = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, is well defined because every $v \in V$ has a unique expression $v = \sum_j x_j b_j$ (this is the definition of a [[Def - Basis|basis]]: spanning gives existence and linear independence gives uniqueness); it is linear because $\sum_j x_j b_j + \lambda \sum_j y_j b_j = \sum_j (x_j + \lambda y_j) b_j$ (by the vector space axioms); it is injective because $F_b(v) = 0$ means all coordinates vanish, so $v = 0$; and it is surjective because $(x_1, \dots, x_n) = F_b\big(\sum_j x_j b_j\big)$. Hence $F_b$ is a linear isomorphism with inverse $F_b^{-1}(x) = \sum_j x_j b_j$.
>
> **Step 1 ($\mathcal{M}_b$ is a group isomorphism $\operatorname{Aut}(V) \to GL(n; \mathbb{K})$).** For $S \in \operatorname{End}(V)$ the composite $F_b \circ S \circ F_b^{-1}$ is a linear map $\mathbb{K}^n \to \mathbb{K}^n$, which we identify with its matrix in the standard basis (Notation); this is the meaning of $\mathcal{M}_b(S) \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$. The map $\mathcal{M}_b : \operatorname{End}(V) \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ is linear in $S$ (composition with fixed maps is linear), and it is bijective with inverse $A \mapsto F_b^{-1} \circ A \circ F_b$, since
> $$F_b^{-1} \circ (F_b \circ S \circ F_b^{-1}) \circ F_b = S \quad\text{and}\quad F_b \circ (F_b^{-1} \circ A \circ F_b) \circ F_b^{-1} = A \qquad \text{(by } F_b^{-1} F_b = \operatorname{id}_V \text{ and } F_b F_b^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{K}^n}\text{).}$$
> It is multiplicative and unital:
> $$\mathcal{M}_b(S_1 \circ S_2) = F_b S_1 F_b^{-1} F_b S_2 F_b^{-1} = \mathcal{M}_b(S_1)\,\mathcal{M}_b(S_2) \qquad \text{(inserting } F_b^{-1} F_b = \operatorname{id}_V \text{; composition of linear maps of } \mathbb{K}^n \text{ is matrix multiplication by [[Thm - Composition Corresponds to Matrix Multiplication]]),}$$
> $$\mathcal{M}_b(\operatorname{id}_V) = F_b F_b^{-1} = 1_n \qquad \text{(the identity of } \mathbb{K}^n \text{ has matrix } 1_n\text{).}$$
> Now let $S \in \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ with inverse $S^{-1}$. Then $\mathcal{M}_b(S)\mathcal{M}_b(S^{-1}) = \mathcal{M}_b(SS^{-1}) = \mathcal{M}_b(\operatorname{id}_V) = 1_n$ and $\mathcal{M}_b(S^{-1})\mathcal{M}_b(S) = \mathcal{M}_b(S^{-1}S) = 1_n$ (both by multiplicativity and unitality), so $\mathcal{M}_b(S)$ is invertible: $\mathcal{M}_b(\operatorname{Aut}(V)) \subseteq GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. Conversely let $A \in GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ with inverse $B$, and put $S := \mathcal{M}_b^{-1}(A)$, $R := \mathcal{M}_b^{-1}(B)$. Then $\mathcal{M}_b(SR) = AB = 1_n = \mathcal{M}_b(\operatorname{id}_V)$ and $\mathcal{M}_b(RS) = BA = 1_n = \mathcal{M}_b(\operatorname{id}_V)$ (multiplicativity), so $SR = \operatorname{id}_V = RS$ by injectivity of $\mathcal{M}_b$, and $S \in \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ with $\mathcal{M}_b(S) = A$. Hence $\mathcal{M}_b$ restricts to a bijection $\operatorname{Aut}(V) \to GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ which is multiplicative, that is, a group isomorphism.
>
> **Step 2 (transport of structure).** The group $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a Lie group: it is the open subset $\{A : \det A \neq 0\}$ of $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) \cong \mathbb{K}^{n^2}$ (open because $\det$ is a polynomial in the entries, hence continuous, and $\mathbb{K} \setminus \{0\}$ is open), it is an open submanifold by [[Thm - Open Subset of a Smooth Manifold]], its multiplication $(A, B) \mapsto AB$ has entries that are polynomials in the entries of $A$ and $B$, and its inversion $A \mapsto A^{-1}$ has entries that are rational functions with denominator $\det A \neq 0$ by Cramer's rule ([[Thm - Cofactor Expansion and Cramer's Rule]]); all of this is verified on [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]], whose statement we have just restated. Declare a subset $U \subseteq \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ open if and only if $\mathcal{M}_b(U)$ is open in $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, and take as charts on $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ the maps $\varphi \circ \mathcal{M}_b$ for charts $(W, \varphi)$ of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, on the domains $\mathcal{M}_b^{-1}(W)$. Since $\mathcal{M}_b$ is a bijection, this is a topology (preimages under a bijection preserve unions and finite intersections), it is Hausdorff and second countable because $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is, the chart domains cover $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$, and the transition maps are $(\varphi \circ \mathcal{M}_b) \circ (\psi \circ \mathcal{M}_b)^{-1} = \varphi \circ \psi^{-1}$, smooth because they are transition maps of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. So $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is a smooth manifold of the same dimension as $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ — namely $n^2$ over $\mathbb{R}$ and $2n^2$ over $\mathbb{C}$ — and by construction $\mathcal{M}_b$ is a diffeomorphism (in the charts it reads $\varphi \circ \mathcal{M}_b \circ \mathcal{M}_b^{-1} \circ \varphi^{-1} = \operatorname{id}$). The multiplication $m_V$ of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is
> $$m_V = \mathcal{M}_b^{-1} \circ m_{GL} \circ (\mathcal{M}_b \times \mathcal{M}_b) \qquad \text{(since } \mathcal{M}_b \text{ is a group isomorphism, Step 1),}$$
> a composition of the smooth maps $\mathcal{M}_b \times \mathcal{M}_b$ (a product of diffeomorphisms is a diffeomorphism of the product manifolds, by [[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]]: in product charts it is the product of the two coordinate expressions), $m_{GL}$, and $\mathcal{M}_b^{-1}$; hence smooth by [[Ex - Composition of Smooth Maps is Smooth]]. In the same way the inversion $i_V = \mathcal{M}_b^{-1} \circ i_{GL} \circ \mathcal{M}_b$ is smooth. Therefore $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$, with the structure transported along $\mathcal{M}_b$, is a Lie group.
>
> **Step 3 (independence of the basis).** Let $b'$ be a second ordered basis and put $Q := F_{b'} \circ F_b^{-1} : \mathbb{K}^n \to \mathbb{K}^n$, a linear isomorphism (composition of two, Step 0), identified with a matrix $Q \in GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. For $S \in \operatorname{Aut}(V)$,
> $$\mathcal{M}_{b'}(S) = F_{b'} S F_{b'}^{-1} = (F_{b'} F_b^{-1})(F_b S F_b^{-1})(F_b F_{b'}^{-1}) = Q\,\mathcal{M}_b(S)\,Q^{-1} \qquad \text{(inserting } F_b^{-1} F_b = \operatorname{id}_V \text{ twice; } (F_{b'} F_b^{-1})^{-1} = F_b F_{b'}^{-1}\text{).}$$
> Thus $\mathcal{M}_{b'} = c_Q \circ \mathcal{M}_b$, where $c_Q : GL(n; \mathbb{K}) \to GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, $c_Q(A) = QAQ^{-1}$, is conjugation by $Q$. (This is the [[Thm - Change of Basis Formula|change of basis formula]]: matrices of one operator in two bases are similar.) The map $A \mapsto QAQ^{-1}$ on $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ is linear in $A$, hence smooth, and it carries $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ into itself because $QAQ^{-1}$ has the inverse $QA^{-1}Q^{-1}$; its restriction $c_Q$ to the open subset $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is therefore smooth ([[Ex - The Inclusion of an Open Submanifold is Smooth]]: a smooth map restricted to an open submanifold, with values in an open submanifold, is smooth), with smooth inverse $c_{Q^{-1}}$, since $c_{Q^{-1}}(c_Q(A)) = Q^{-1}QAQ^{-1}Q = A$. So $c_Q$ is a diffeomorphism of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. Now a subset $U \subseteq \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is $b'$-open if and only if $\mathcal{M}_{b'}(U) = c_Q(\mathcal{M}_b(U))$ is open, if and only if $\mathcal{M}_b(U)$ is open (because $c_Q$ is a homeomorphism), if and only if $U$ is $b$-open: the two topologies agree. And a $b'$-chart $\varphi \circ \mathcal{M}_{b'} = \varphi \circ c_Q \circ \mathcal{M}_b$ is smoothly compatible with every $b$-chart $\psi \circ \mathcal{M}_b$, because the transition map is $(\varphi \circ c_Q \circ \mathcal{M}_b) \circ (\psi \circ \mathcal{M}_b)^{-1} = \varphi \circ c_Q \circ \psi^{-1}$, smooth since $c_Q$ is smooth, and likewise in the other direction using $c_{Q^{-1}}$. Two smoothly compatible atlases determine the same maximal atlas, so the two smooth structures coincide.
>
> **Conclusion.** For every basis $b$, $\mathcal{M}_b$ is a group isomorphism onto the Lie group $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, transporting its Lie group structure to $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$; the transported topology and smooth structure are the same for all bases. Therefore $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is a Lie group in a canonical way, and every $\mathcal{M}_b$ is a Lie group isomorphism $\operatorname{Aut}(V) \to GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. $\blacksquare$

With Lemma A in hand, clause 2 of the definition has an unambiguous meaning, and we can give the definition its most concrete form: a representation of $G$ on $V$ is, after a choice of basis, a smooth homomorphism into a matrix group. We record this precisely as Lemma E at the end of this section, once equivalence has been defined. First, the smallest concrete case. Take $G = U(1)$ and $V = \mathbb{C}$, so that $\operatorname{Aut}(V) = GL(1; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}^\times$ and a representation is a smooth homomorphism $U(1) \to \mathbb{C}^\times$. The inclusion $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}(z) = z$ is one: it is a homomorphism because $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}(zw) = zw = \varrho_{\mathrm{st}}(z)\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}(w)$, and it is smooth because it is the restriction to the embedded submanifold $U(1) \subset \mathbb{C}$ of the identity map of $\mathbb{C}$ (see the Examples for the details of this smoothness argument). The action is $z \cdot v = zv$: the circle rotates the plane $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^2$ about the origin. Every other one-dimensional complex representation of $U(1)$ is a power $z \mapsto z^k$ — that is the content of [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] — and the integer $k$ is what a physicist calls the charge.

**An equivalent formulation: smooth linear actions.** The definition says "a homomorphism into a group of linear maps"; it is often more convenient to think "a smooth action by linear maps". The two are the same, and since the series uses both pictures constantly we prove the equivalence in full. Here $V$ carries the manifold structure transported from $\mathbb{K}^n$ along any coordinate map $F_b$; it is independent of $b$ because $F_{b'} \circ F_b^{-1} = Q$ is a linear, hence smooth, bijection of $\mathbb{K}^n$ with linear inverse, so the same argument as Step 3 of Lemma A applies.

> [!note]- Proposition (representations are smooth linear actions)
> **Statement.** Let $G$ be a Lie group, $V$ a finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$-vector space, and $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ any map. Define $\Phi : G \times V \to V$ by $\Phi(g, v) := \varrho(g)(v)$. Then $\varrho$ is a representation if and only if $\Phi$ is a [[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group|smooth left action]] of $G$ on the manifold $V$ — that is, $\Phi$ is smooth, $\Phi(e, v) = v$ for all $v$, and $\Phi(g, \Phi(h, v)) = \Phi(gh, v)$ for all $g, h, v$ — and each map $\Phi(g, \cdot) : V \to V$ is $\mathbb{K}$-linear. (Conversely, every smooth left action of $G$ on $V$ by $\mathbb{K}$-linear maps arises in this way from the unique $\varrho$ with $\varrho(g) = \Phi(g, \cdot)$.)
>
> **Proof.** Fix a basis $b$ of $V$ and write $\varrho_b := \mathcal{M}_b \circ \varrho : G \to GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. We prove the two directions separately.
>
> **Direction 1 ($\Rightarrow$).** Assume $\varrho$ is a representation. We need to show that $\Phi$ is smooth, satisfies the two action axioms, and is linear in $v$.
>
> *Linearity in $v$:* $\Phi(g, \cdot) = \varrho(g)$ is linear by the definition of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$.
>
> *The unit axiom:* by the homomorphism property, $\varrho(e) = \varrho(ee) = \varrho(e) \circ \varrho(e)$; composing on the left with $\varrho(e)^{-1}$, which exists because $\varrho(e) \in \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, gives $\operatorname{id}_V = \varrho(e)$. Hence $\Phi(e, v) = \varrho(e)v = v$.
>
> *The compatibility axiom:* $\Phi(g, \Phi(h, v)) = \varrho(g)(\varrho(h)v) = (\varrho(g) \circ \varrho(h))(v) = \varrho(gh)(v) = \Phi(gh, v)$ (by the definition of $\Phi$, the definition of composition, and clause 1 of the definition of a representation).
>
> *Smoothness:* Let $\operatorname{ev} : GL(n; \mathbb{K}) \times \mathbb{K}^n \to \mathbb{K}^n$, $\operatorname{ev}(A, x) = Ax$, be matrix–vector multiplication. Its components $(Ax)_j = \sum_k A_{jk} x_k$ are polynomials in the entries of $A$ and $x$, so $\operatorname{ev}$ is smooth on $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) \times \mathbb{K}^n$ and hence on the open subset $GL(n; \mathbb{K}) \times \mathbb{K}^n$. For $(g, v) \in G \times V$,
> $$\Phi(g, v) = \varrho(g)v = F_b^{-1}\big(F_b \varrho(g) F_b^{-1}(F_b v)\big) = F_b^{-1}\Big(\operatorname{ev}\big(\varrho_b(g), F_b(v)\big)\Big) \qquad \text{(inserting } F_b^{-1} F_b = \operatorname{id}_V \text{ twice; definition of } \mathcal{M}_b\text{).}$$
> The map $(g, v) \mapsto (\varrho_b(g), F_b(v))$ from $G \times V$ to $GL(n; \mathbb{K}) \times \mathbb{K}^n$ is smooth because a map into a product manifold is smooth exactly when its two components are (in product charts, which are products of charts by [[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]], the coordinate expression is the pair of coordinate expressions), and its components $\varrho_b = \mathcal{M}_b \circ \varrho$ (composition of the smooth $\varrho$ with the diffeomorphism $\mathcal{M}_b$ of Lemma A) and $F_b$ (a chart of $V$, hence smooth) are smooth. Then $\Phi = F_b^{-1} \circ \operatorname{ev} \circ (\varrho_b, F_b)$ is a composition of smooth maps, hence smooth by [[Ex - Composition of Smooth Maps is Smooth]].
>
> **Direction 2 ($\Leftarrow$).** Assume $\Phi$ is a smooth left action with each $\Phi(g, \cdot)$ linear. We need to show that $\varrho(g) := \Phi(g, \cdot)$ lies in $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$, that $\varrho$ is a group homomorphism, and that $\varrho$ is smooth.
>
> *Values in $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$:* $\varrho(g)$ is linear by hypothesis, and for every $v$,
> $$\varrho(g)\big(\varrho(g^{-1}) v\big) = \Phi(g, \Phi(g^{-1}, v)) = \Phi(gg^{-1}, v) = \Phi(e, v) = v \qquad \text{(compatibility axiom, then } gg^{-1} = e\text{, then the unit axiom),}$$
> and in the same way $\varrho(g^{-1})(\varrho(g) v) = \Phi(g^{-1}g, v) = v$. So $\varrho(g)$ is invertible with inverse $\varrho(g^{-1})$.
>
> *Homomorphism:* for all $v$, $\varrho(gh)v = \Phi(gh, v) = \Phi(g, \Phi(h, v)) = \varrho(g)(\varrho(h)v)$ (compatibility axiom), so $\varrho(gh) = \varrho(g) \circ \varrho(h)$.
>
> *Smoothness:* Since $\mathcal{M}_b$ is a diffeomorphism (Lemma A), $\varrho = \mathcal{M}_b^{-1} \circ \varrho_b$ is smooth as soon as $\varrho_b : G \to GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is. As $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is an open subset of $\mathbb{K}^{n^2}$ with the identity as chart, $\varrho_b$ is smooth if and only if each matrix entry $g \mapsto \varrho_b(g)_{jk}$ is a smooth function on $G$. By the definition of the matrix of a linear map in the basis $b$, the $k$-th column of $\varrho_b(g)$ is the coordinate vector of $\varrho(g) b_k$, so
> $$\varrho_b(g)_{jk} = \operatorname{pr}_j\big(F_b(\Phi(g, b_k))\big) \qquad \text{(definition of } \mathcal{M}_b \text{ and of } \Phi\text{),}$$
> where $\operatorname{pr}_j : \mathbb{K}^n \to \mathbb{K}$ is the $j$-th coordinate projection, a linear map. The map $g \mapsto (g, b_k)$, $G \to G \times V$, is smooth because its components $\operatorname{id}_G$ and the constant map are smooth; $\Phi$ is smooth by hypothesis; $F_b$ and $\operatorname{pr}_j$ are linear, hence smooth. Therefore each entry is a composition of smooth maps, hence smooth, so $\varrho_b$ and therefore $\varrho$ are smooth.
>
> **Conclusion.** Under the correspondence $\varrho \leftrightarrow \Phi$, the homomorphism clause of a representation is the pair of action axioms, and the smoothness clause of a representation is the smoothness of the action map; the linearity of the action is built into both sides. Therefore representations of $G$ on $V$ are exactly the smooth left actions of $G$ on $V$ by linear maps. $\blacksquare$

The remaining four notions are now short to state.

> **Definition (faithful representation; Bär Definition 1.3.3).** A representation $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is **faithful** if and only if $\varrho$ is injective.

> [!note]- Lemma B (the kernel): faithfulness is triviality of the kernel
> **Statement.** Let $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ be a representation. Then $\ker \varrho = \{g \in G : \varrho(g) = \operatorname{id}_V\}$ is a closed normal subgroup of $G$, hence an embedded Lie subgroup, and $\varrho$ is faithful if and only if $\ker \varrho = \{e\}$.
>
> **Proof.** We are given a representation $\varrho$; we must show that $\ker \varrho$ is a normal subgroup, that it is closed, and that injectivity of $\varrho$ is equivalent to $\ker \varrho = \{e\}$.
>
> **Normal subgroup.** By Direction 1 of the Proposition, $\varrho(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$, so $\varrho$ is a group homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ in the sense of [[Def - Homomorphism]], and the kernel of any group homomorphism is a normal subgroup — this is proved clause by clause on [[Def - Kernel and Image]]; we restate the argument. It contains $e$ since $\varrho(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$. If $g, h \in \ker \varrho$ then $\varrho(gh^{-1}) = \varrho(g) \circ \varrho(h)^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_V \circ \operatorname{id}_V^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_V$ (homomorphism property and $\varrho(h^{-1}) = \varrho(h)^{-1}$, which follows from $\varrho(h)\varrho(h^{-1}) = \varrho(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$), so $gh^{-1} \in \ker \varrho$: a subgroup. If $g \in \ker \varrho$ and $x \in G$ then $\varrho(x g x^{-1}) = \varrho(x) \circ \operatorname{id}_V \circ \varrho(x)^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_V$, so $xgx^{-1} \in \ker \varrho$: [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal]].
>
> **Closed.** $\varrho$ is smooth, hence continuous by [[Thm - Smooth Maps are Continuous]], and $\ker \varrho = \varrho^{-1}(\{\operatorname{id}_V\})$ is the preimage of a point. Points are closed in the Hausdorff space $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ (Lemma A, Step 2), so $\ker \varrho$ is closed in $G$. By [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]] — a closed subgroup of a Lie group is an embedded Lie subgroup — $\ker \varrho$ is an embedded Lie subgroup of $G$.
>
> **Faithful if and only if trivial kernel.** Suppose $\varrho$ is injective and $g \in \ker \varrho$. Then $\varrho(g) = \operatorname{id}_V = \varrho(e)$, so $g = e$ by injectivity; hence $\ker \varrho = \{e\}$. Conversely suppose $\ker \varrho = \{e\}$ and $\varrho(g) = \varrho(h)$. Then $\varrho(g^{-1}h) = \varrho(g)^{-1} \circ \varrho(h) = \operatorname{id}_V$ (homomorphism property), so $g^{-1}h \in \ker \varrho = \{e\}$, that is, $g = h$; hence $\varrho$ is injective.
>
> **Conclusion.** The kernel of a representation is a closed normal subgroup, and a representation is faithful exactly when its kernel is trivial. Therefore the kernel is the complete measure of the failure of faithfulness. $\blacksquare$

> **Definition (trivial representation; Bär Example 1.3.4.1).** For any Lie group $G$ and any finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$-vector space $V$, the **trivial representation** of $G$ on $V$ is $\varrho(g) := \operatorname{id}_V$ for every $g \in G$. It is a representation, and it is faithful only for the trivial group $G = \{e\}$; both claims are verified in the Examples.

> **Definition (standard representations of the classical groups; Bär Example 1.3.10).** The **standard representation** $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}$ of a [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups|classical matrix group]] is its defining action on the coordinate space:
> 1. for $G = GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}} := \operatorname{id} : G \to GL(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{K}^n)$;
> 2. for $G \in \{O(n), SL(n; \mathbb{R}), SO(n)\}$, the inclusion $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}} : G \hookrightarrow GL(n; \mathbb{R}) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, a real $n$-dimensional representation;
> 3. for $G \in \{U(n), SL(n; \mathbb{C}), SU(n)\}$, the inclusion $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}} : G \hookrightarrow GL(n; \mathbb{C}) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^n)$, a complex $n$-dimensional representation.
>
> Each is a faithful representation; this is verified in the Examples.

> **Definition (equivalent representations; Bär Definition 1.3.12).** Let $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ and $\tilde\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(\tilde V)$ be two representations of the same Lie group $G$ over the same field $\mathbb{K}$. A linear isomorphism $T : V \to \tilde V$ is an **equivalence** (or **intertwining isomorphism**) from $\varrho$ to $\tilde\varrho$ if
> $$T \circ \varrho(g) = \tilde\varrho(g) \circ T \qquad \text{for every } g \in G,$$
> that is, if for every $g$ the square with $\varrho(g)$ on the left edge, $\tilde\varrho(g)$ on the right edge, and $T$ on the top and bottom edges commutes. The representations are **equivalent**, written $\varrho \cong \tilde\varrho$, if an equivalence exists. Equivalently, $\tilde\varrho(g) = T \circ \varrho(g) \circ T^{-1}$ for all $g$: the representation $\tilde\varrho$ is $\varrho$ conjugated by the relabelling $T$.

In the smallest case, $V = \tilde V = \mathbb{C}$ and $G = U(1)$, a linear isomorphism $T$ is multiplication by a non-zero scalar $c$, and since scalars commute, $T \circ \varrho(g) = \tilde\varrho(g) \circ T$ reads $c\,\varrho(g) = \tilde\varrho(g)\,c$, that is, $\varrho = \tilde\varrho$: two one-dimensional representations are equivalent if and only if they are equal. This is why the one-dimensional representations $z \mapsto z^k$ of $U(1)$ are pairwise inequivalent for distinct $k$, a fact recorded in the Examples and used in [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]].

> [!note]- Lemma C: equivalence of representations is an equivalence relation
> **Statement.** On the class of representations of a fixed Lie group $G$ over a fixed field $\mathbb{K}$, the relation $\varrho \cong \tilde\varrho$ is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
>
> **Proof.** We must verify the three clauses.
>
> **Reflexivity.** $T = \operatorname{id}_V$ is a linear isomorphism $V \to V$ and $\operatorname{id}_V \circ \varrho(g) = \varrho(g) = \varrho(g) \circ \operatorname{id}_V$ for every $g$. So $\varrho \cong \varrho$.
>
> **Symmetry.** Let $T : V \to \tilde V$ be an equivalence from $\varrho$ to $\tilde\varrho$. Then $T^{-1} : \tilde V \to V$ is a linear isomorphism, and for every $g$,
> $$T^{-1} \circ \tilde\varrho(g) = T^{-1} \circ \tilde\varrho(g) \circ T \circ T^{-1} = T^{-1} \circ T \circ \varrho(g) \circ T^{-1} = \varrho(g) \circ T^{-1} \qquad \text{(insert } TT^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_{\tilde V}\text{; use } \tilde\varrho(g) T = T \varrho(g)\text{; use } T^{-1}T = \operatorname{id}_V\text{).}$$
> So $T^{-1}$ is an equivalence from $\tilde\varrho$ to $\varrho$.
>
> **Transitivity.** Let $T : V \to \tilde V$ be an equivalence from $\varrho$ to $\tilde\varrho$ and $T' : \tilde V \to \hat V$ an equivalence from $\tilde\varrho$ to $\hat\varrho$. Then $T' \circ T : V \to \hat V$ is a linear isomorphism (composition of two, with inverse $T^{-1} \circ T'^{-1}$), and for every $g$,
> $$(T' \circ T) \circ \varrho(g) = T' \circ \tilde\varrho(g) \circ T = \hat\varrho(g) \circ (T' \circ T) \qquad \text{(first } T\varrho(g) = \tilde\varrho(g)T\text{, then } T'\tilde\varrho(g) = \hat\varrho(g)T'\text{).}$$
> So $T' \circ T$ is an equivalence from $\varrho$ to $\hat\varrho$.
>
> **Conclusion.** All three clauses hold; therefore $\cong$ is an equivalence relation, and "the representations of $G$ up to equivalence" is a well-defined set of equivalence classes. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma D: dimension and kernel are invariants of the equivalence class
> **Statement.** If $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ and $\tilde\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(\tilde V)$ are equivalent, then $\dim_{\mathbb{K}} V = \dim_{\mathbb{K}} \tilde V$ and $\ker \varrho = \ker \tilde\varrho$. In particular $\varrho$ is faithful if and only if $\tilde\varrho$ is.
>
> **Proof.** Let $T : V \to \tilde V$ be an equivalence. **Dimension.** $T$ is a linear isomorphism, and isomorphic finite-dimensional vector spaces have the same dimension by [[Thm - Two Vector Spaces Isomorphic iff Same Dimension]] (an isomorphism carries a basis of $V$ to a basis of $\tilde V$ of the same length). **Kernel.** For $g \in G$, $\tilde\varrho(g) = T \circ \varrho(g) \circ T^{-1}$. If $\varrho(g) = \operatorname{id}_V$ then $\tilde\varrho(g) = T \circ T^{-1} = \operatorname{id}_{\tilde V}$; conversely if $\tilde\varrho(g) = \operatorname{id}_{\tilde V}$ then $\varrho(g) = T^{-1} \circ \tilde\varrho(g) \circ T = T^{-1} \circ T = \operatorname{id}_V$. Hence $g \in \ker \varrho$ if and only if $g \in \ker \tilde\varrho$, that is, $\ker \varrho = \ker \tilde\varrho$. **Faithfulness.** By Lemma B, faithfulness is the condition $\ker = \{e\}$, which is the same condition for both. **Conclusion.** Dimension, kernel, and faithfulness are unchanged by passing to an equivalent representation. $\blacksquare$

Finally, the matrix form, which is Bär's Example 1.3.13 and the bridge between the coordinate-free definition and the way representations are actually computed.

> [!note]- Lemma E (Bär Example 1.3.13): every representation is equivalent to a matrix representation, and any two bases give equivalent matrix representations
> **Statement.** Let $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ be a representation and $b$ an ordered basis of $V$, with coordinate isomorphism $F_b : V \to \mathbb{K}^n$. Then $\varrho_b := \mathcal{M}_b \circ \varrho : G \to GL(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{K}^n)$, $\varrho_b(g) = F_b \circ \varrho(g) \circ F_b^{-1}$, is a representation of $G$ on $\mathbb{K}^n$ — a **matrix representation** — and $F_b$ is an equivalence from $\varrho$ to $\varrho_b$. If $b'$ is a second ordered basis, then $T := F_{b'} \circ F_b^{-1} \in GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is an equivalence from $\varrho_b$ to $\varrho_{b'}$.
>
> **Proof.** We are given $\varrho$, $b$, $b'$; we must show that $\varrho_b$ is a representation, that $F_b$ intertwines $\varrho$ and $\varrho_b$, and that $T$ intertwines $\varrho_b$ and $\varrho_{b'}$.
>
> **$\varrho_b$ is a representation.** It is a group homomorphism because it is the composition of the homomorphism $\varrho$ with the group isomorphism $\mathcal{M}_b$ (Lemma A, Step 1): $\varrho_b(gh) = \mathcal{M}_b(\varrho(g)\varrho(h)) = \mathcal{M}_b(\varrho(g))\mathcal{M}_b(\varrho(h)) = \varrho_b(g)\varrho_b(h)$. It is smooth because it is the composition of the smooth map $\varrho$ with the diffeomorphism $\mathcal{M}_b$ (Lemma A, Step 2), by [[Ex - Composition of Smooth Maps is Smooth]]. Its values lie in $GL(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{K}^n)$ by Lemma A, Step 1.
>
> **$F_b$ is an equivalence.** $F_b : V \to \mathbb{K}^n$ is a linear isomorphism (Lemma A, Step 0), and for every $g$,
> $$\varrho_b(g) \circ F_b = F_b \circ \varrho(g) \circ F_b^{-1} \circ F_b = F_b \circ \varrho(g) \qquad \text{(definition of } \varrho_b\text{; } F_b^{-1} F_b = \operatorname{id}_V\text{).}$$
> This is the intertwining identity $F_b \circ \varrho(g) = \varrho_b(g) \circ F_b$.
>
> **$T$ is an equivalence.** $T = F_{b'} \circ F_b^{-1}$ is a linear isomorphism $\mathbb{K}^n \to \mathbb{K}^n$ (composition of two), that is, an element of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, and for every $g$,
> $$T \circ \varrho_b(g) = F_{b'} F_b^{-1} \cdot F_b \varrho(g) F_b^{-1} = F_{b'} \varrho(g) F_b^{-1} = F_{b'} \varrho(g) F_{b'}^{-1} \cdot F_{b'} F_b^{-1} = \varrho_{b'}(g) \circ T \qquad \text{(cancel } F_b^{-1}F_b\text{; insert } F_{b'}^{-1}F_{b'} = \operatorname{id}_V\text{; definitions of } \varrho_{b'} \text{ and } T\text{).}$$
> Equivalently, $\varrho_{b'}(g) = T \varrho_b(g) T^{-1}$: the matrices of $\varrho(g)$ in the two bases are similar, by one and the same matrix $T$ for all $g$.
>
> **Conclusion.** Every representation is equivalent, through a coordinate map, to a smooth homomorphism into a matrix group, and changing the basis changes the matrix representation only within its equivalence class. Therefore, for questions invariant under equivalence — dimension, kernel, faithfulness, and the classification of chapter I — nothing is lost by working with matrix representations, which is what Haydys does throughout. $\blacksquare$

# Categorical Definition

A representation is a functor, an equivalence is a natural isomorphism, and the smoothness clause is the one thing the bare categorical picture does not see. We make each of these statements precise and verify it.

**The one-object category of a group.** Let $\mathbf{B}G$ be the [[Def - Category|category]] with a single object $*$, with morphism set $\operatorname{Hom}(*, *) := G$, with composition $h \circ g := hg$ (the group product), and with identity morphism $1_* := e$. This is a category: composition is associative because the group product is, and $e \circ g = eg = g = ge = g \circ e$ because $e$ is the neutral element. Every morphism $g$ of $\mathbf{B}G$ is an [[Def - Isomorphism, Monomorphism, Epimorphism|isomorphism]], with inverse $g^{-1}$, since $g \circ g^{-1} = e = 1_*$ and $g^{-1} \circ g = e = 1_*$; so $\mathbf{B}G$ is a [[Def - Groupoid|groupoid]] with one object. Let $\mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb{K}}$ be the category of finite-dimensional $\mathbb{K}$-vector spaces and $\mathbb{K}$-linear maps.

**Representations as functors.** A [[Def - Functor|functor]] $R : \mathbf{B}G \to \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb{K}}$ consists of an object $V := R(*)$ and, for each morphism $g \in G$, a linear map $R(g) : V \to V$, subject to $R(hg) = R(h) \circ R(g)$ (functors preserve composition) and $R(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$ (functors preserve identities). We claim that such functors are exactly the group homomorphisms $G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$. Given a functor $R$, each $R(g)$ is a linear isomorphism: since $g$ is an isomorphism in $\mathbf{B}G$ and functors preserve isomorphisms ([[Thm - Functors Preserve Isomorphisms]], restated: if $g$ has inverse $g^{-1}$ then $R(g)$ has inverse $R(g^{-1})$, because $R(g) \circ R(g^{-1}) = R(gg^{-1}) = R(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$ and likewise in the other order), we have $R(g) \in \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, and $g \mapsto R(g)$ is multiplicative, hence a group homomorphism. Conversely a group homomorphism $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ defines a functor by $R(*) := V$, $R(g) := \varrho(g)$: composition is preserved by the homomorphism property and the identity is preserved because $\varrho(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$ (Direction 1 of the Proposition). The two constructions are inverse to one another, so **the algebraic content of a representation of $G$ on $V$ is a functor $\mathbf{B}G \to \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb{K}}$ with value $V$ on the unique object.**

**Equivalences as natural isomorphisms.** Let $R, \tilde R : \mathbf{B}G \to \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb{K}}$ be the functors of $\varrho$ and $\tilde\varrho$. A [[Def - Natural Transformation|natural transformation]] $\eta : R \Rightarrow \tilde R$ has one component, $\eta_* =: T : V \to \tilde V$, and its naturality condition — for every morphism $g : * \to *$, the square $\tilde R(g) \circ \eta_* = \eta_* \circ R(g)$ commutes — reads $\tilde\varrho(g) \circ T = T \circ \varrho(g)$ for every $g \in G$. This is exactly the intertwining condition in the definition of equivalence. The natural transformation is a natural isomorphism if and only if its single component $T$ is an isomorphism. Therefore **an equivalence of representations is a natural isomorphism between the corresponding functors**, and the category whose objects are representations of $G$ over $\mathbb{K}$ and whose morphisms are intertwining linear maps (not necessarily invertible) is the [[Def - Functor Category|functor category]] $[\mathbf{B}G, \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb{K}}]$. Lemma C, that equivalence is an equivalence relation, is then the general fact that isomorphism in any category is an equivalence relation.

**Representations as modules.** The same structure can be phrased as a [[Def - Module|module]]. Let $\mathbb{K}[G]$ be the group algebra: the $\mathbb{K}$-vector space of finite formal linear combinations $\sum_{g} a_g\, g$ (finitely many $a_g \neq 0$), with the product extended bilinearly from the group product. A representation $\varrho$ makes $V$ a left $\mathbb{K}[G]$-module by $\big(\sum_g a_g g\big) \cdot v := \sum_g a_g\, \varrho(g) v$; this is well defined because the sum is finite, additive in the module element and in the ring element because each $\varrho(g)$ is linear and the formula is linear in the coefficients, unital because $e \cdot v = \varrho(e)v = v$, and associative because $(g \cdot (h \cdot v)) = \varrho(g)\varrho(h)v = \varrho(gh)v = (gh) \cdot v$ on group elements, which extends bilinearly. Conversely a $\mathbb{K}[G]$-module structure on $V$ restricts to a homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$. An equivalence of representations is then a $\mathbb{K}[G]$-[[Def - Module Homomorphism|module isomorphism]]. This is why representations are called **$G$-modules** in the algebraic literature.

**What the categorical picture omits.** Neither $\mathbf{B}G$ nor $\mathbb{K}[G]$ sees the manifold structure of $G$; the functor picture captures clause 1 of the definition and says nothing about clause 2. A representation of a *Lie* group is a functor $\mathbf{B}G \to \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb{K}}$ whose action on morphisms, the map $G = \operatorname{Hom}(*, *) \to \operatorname{Hom}(V, V) \supset \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, is smooth; in the language of enriched categories, $\mathbf{B}G$ is a category enriched in smooth manifolds and the functor is required to be a smooth functor. We do not develop that language; the honest summary is that the categorical definition is the algebraic half of the notion, and smoothness is an additional condition imposed on the single map $\varrho$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name: a linear $G$-action.** The official definition, "a Lie group homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$", and the operational one, "a smooth action of $G$ on $V$ by linear maps", are proved equal in the Proposition above; the second is the one to keep in mind. It places representations inside the theory of [[Def - Group Action|group actions]]: a representation is a [[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group|smooth action]] on a manifold that happens to be a vector space, with the extra requirement that every group element act linearly. The group-theoretic fact that actions of $G$ on a set $X$ are the same as homomorphisms $G \to \operatorname{Sym}(X)$ ([[Thm - Actions Correspond to Homomorphisms]]) is the discrete shadow of the Proposition: replace $\operatorname{Sym}(X)$ by the subgroup $\operatorname{Aut}(V) \subset \operatorname{Sym}(V)$ of linear bijections and add smoothness. Faithfulness of the representation is the same as effectiveness of the action — no group element other than $e$ acts trivially — in the vocabulary of [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions]].

**Physics: charge and particle content.** In a gauge theory with structure group $G$, each species of matter field is specified by a representation of $G$; the representation is what a physicist means by the "quantum numbers" or the "charge" of the field. For $G = U(1)$ the representations $z \mapsto z^k$ are labelled by an integer $k$, the electric charge in units of the elementary charge, and the classification theorem [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] says that every field coupled to electromagnetism decomposes into components of definite charge. For $G = SU(2)$ the irreducible representations $\varrho_k$ of dimension $k + 1$ are the spin-$\frac{k}{2}$ representations; the standard representation $\varrho_1$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ is the spinor, and the adjoint representation on $\mathfrak{su}(2) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$, complexified, is $\varrho_2$, the vector — the statement that "a vector is a pair of spinors" is the equivalence $\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_1 \cong \varrho_2 \oplus \varrho_0$ of [[Ex - Decomposing the Tensor Square of the Standard Representation of SU(2)]].

**Geometry: associated bundles and the adjoint bundle.** The representation is the input that turns a principal bundle into a vector bundle. Given a principal $G$-bundle $P \to M$ and a representation $\varrho$ on $V$, the [[Def - Associated Bundle|associated bundle]] $P \times_\varrho V$ is the quotient of $P \times V$ by the relation $(p \cdot g, v) \sim (p, \varrho(g) v)$, a vector bundle with fibre $V$; connections on $P$ induce covariant derivatives on it ([[Thm - Principal Connections Induce Covariant Derivatives on Associated Bundles]]). The most important instance uses the [[Def - Adjoint Representation|adjoint representation]] $\operatorname{Ad} : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{g})$, $\operatorname{Ad}_g = d_e(h \mapsto ghg^{-1})$, which is a representation by [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]]; the associated bundle $\operatorname{ad} P = P \times_{\operatorname{Ad}} \mathfrak{g}$ is where the curvature of a connection lives. So the two representations that dominate the series are the standard representation (matter fields) and the adjoint representation (gauge fields).

**Lie algebras: the infinitesimal version.** Because $\varrho$ is smooth, it has a differential $d_e \varrho : \mathfrak{g} \to T_{\operatorname{id}} \operatorname{Aut}(V) = \operatorname{End}(V)$ at the neutral element, and by [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]] — the differential at $e$ of a Lie group homomorphism is a Lie algebra homomorphism — this is a representation of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ on $V$ in the sense of [[Def - Representation of a Lie Algebra]]. The passage $\varrho \mapsto d_e \varrho$ is how the $\operatorname{Ad}$ representation becomes $\operatorname{ad}_X Y = [X, Y]$, and how a charge-$k$ representation of $U(1)$ becomes multiplication by $ik$ on $\mathfrak{u}(1) = i\mathbb{R}$. This is the single most used consequence of the smoothness clause.

**Compression.** All five notions on this page are instances of one pattern: a representation is an object in the functor category $[\mathbf{B}G, \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb{K}}]$ with a smoothness condition; faithfulness is injectivity on morphisms; the trivial representation is the constant functor; the standard representation of a matrix group is the inclusion functor; and equivalence is isomorphism in the functor category. Everything else — dimension, kernel, matrix form — is an invariant or a coordinate description of that object.

# Examples / Corollaries

Every example below is verified clause by clause against the definition: values in $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$, the homomorphism property, and smoothness. Where faithfulness is claimed, the kernel is computed.

**Is an instance — the trivial representation (Bär Example 1.3.4.1).** Let $\varrho(g) := \operatorname{id}_V$ for all $g \in G$. *Values:* $\operatorname{id}_V \in \operatorname{Aut}(V)$. *Homomorphism:* $\varrho(gh) = \operatorname{id}_V = \operatorname{id}_V \circ \operatorname{id}_V = \varrho(g) \circ \varrho(h)$. *Smoothness:* a constant map between manifolds is smooth, since in any charts its coordinate expression is a constant function. So $\varrho$ is a representation. *Kernel:* $\ker \varrho = \{g : \varrho(g) = \operatorname{id}_V\} = G$. By Lemma B, $\varrho$ is faithful if and only if $G = \{e\}$: the trivial representation is faithful only for the trivial group, as Bär states. In the action picture, every $g$ fixes every vector; the action forgets the group entirely.

**Is an instance — the standard representations of the classical groups (Bär Example 1.3.10).** Let $G$ be one of $O(n)$, $SO(n)$, $SL(n; \mathbb{R})$ inside $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$, or one of $U(n)$, $SU(n)$, $SL(n; \mathbb{C})$ inside $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$, and let $\iota : G \hookrightarrow GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ be the inclusion. *Values:* $\iota(A) = A \in GL(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{K}^n)$ because $G$ is a subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. *Homomorphism:* $\iota(AB) = AB = \iota(A)\iota(B)$, since the group operation of $G$ is the restriction of the matrix product. *Smoothness:* $G$ is a closed subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ — it is the preimage of a closed set under a continuous map, namely $O(n) = \{A : A^t A = 1_n\}$, $SL(n; \mathbb{K}) = \{A : \det A = 1\}$, $U(n) = \{A : A^* A = 1_n\}$, and the intersections $SO(n) = O(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{R})$, $SU(n) = U(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{C})$, with $A \mapsto A^t A$, $A \mapsto \det A$, $A \mapsto A^* A$ polynomial in the real and imaginary parts of the entries; this is written out on [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]] and drilled in [[Ex - The Classical Groups are Closed Subgroups of GL(n)]]. By [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]] (a closed subgroup of a Lie group is an embedded Lie subgroup), $G$ is an [[Def - Embedded Submanifold|embedded submanifold]] of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, and by clause 2 of the definition of an embedded submanifold the inclusion $\iota$ is a smooth embedding, in particular a smooth map. So $\iota$ is a Lie group homomorphism, hence a representation: real of dimension $n$ in the first three cases, complex of dimension $n$ in the last three. For $G = GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ itself, $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}} = \operatorname{id}$ is smooth (the identity map of a manifold is smooth) and a homomorphism. *Kernel:* $\iota(A) = 1_n$ if and only if $A = 1_n$, so $\ker \iota = \{1_n\}$ and every standard representation is faithful by Lemma B. The contrast with the adjoint representation is instructive: the standard representation of $SU(2)$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ is faithful, but the adjoint representation of $SU(2)$ on $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ has kernel $\{\pm 1_2\}$ ([[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]] and [[Ex - The Adjoint Representation of SU(2) in the Pauli Basis]]); two inequivalent representations of the same group, of dimensions $2$ and $3$, one faithful and one not.

**Is an instance — a representation of $U(1)$ by conjugation.** Define $\bar\varrho : U(1) \to GL(1; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}^\times$, $\bar\varrho(z) := \bar z$. *Values:* $|\bar z| = |z| = 1 \neq 0$, so $\bar z \in \mathbb{C}^\times$. *Homomorphism:* $\overline{zw} = \bar z\,\bar w$ (complex conjugation is a ring homomorphism of $\mathbb{C}$). *Smoothness:* $U(1) = \{z : |z|^2 = 1\}$ is a closed subgroup of $GL(1; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}^\times$, hence an embedded submanifold with smooth inclusion $\iota$ (closed subgroup theorem, as above); the map $\kappa : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$, $x + iy \mapsto x - iy$, is $\mathbb{R}$-linear, hence smooth; and $\bar\varrho = \kappa \circ \iota$ with values in the open subset $\mathbb{C}^\times$, so $\bar\varrho$ is smooth as a composition. *Kernel:* $\bar z = 1$ if and only if $z = 1$, so $\bar\varrho$ is faithful. Observe that on $U(1)$ we have $\bar z = z^{-1}$ (because $z \bar z = |z|^2 = 1$), so $\bar\varrho$ is the representation $\varrho_{-1} : z \mapsto z^{-1}$ of charge $-1$, the dual of the standard representation ([[Ex - Tensor Products and Duals of the Representations of U(1)]]). *Inequivalent to the standard representation:* by the remark after the definition of equivalence, two one-dimensional representations are equivalent only if equal, and $\bar\varrho(i) = -i \neq i = \varrho_{\mathrm{st}}(i)$. So a representation and its complex conjugate need not be equivalent, even though the underlying real actions on $\mathbb{R}^2$ (rotation by $\varphi$ and rotation by $-\varphi$) are conjugate by the reflection $y \mapsto -y$ — that reflection is $\mathbb{R}$-linear but not $\mathbb{C}$-linear, which is exactly why it is not an equivalence of complex representations.

**Is an instance, but not faithful — the charge-two representation of $U(1)$.** Define $\varrho_2(z) := z^2 \in \mathbb{C}^\times$. *Homomorphism:* $(zw)^2 = z^2 w^2$. *Smoothness:* $z \mapsto z^2$ is polynomial on $\mathbb{C}$, restricted to the embedded submanifold $U(1)$ and landing in $\mathbb{C}^\times$. *Kernel:* $z^2 = 1$ with $|z| = 1$ has exactly the two solutions $z = \pm 1$, so $\ker \varrho_2 = \{1, -1\} \neq \{1\}$ and $\varrho_2$ is not faithful: the action $v \mapsto z^2 v$ does not distinguish $z$ from $-z$. Geometrically, $\varrho_2$ factors through the double cover $U(1) \to U(1)$, $z \mapsto z^2$, and the induced representation of the quotient $U(1)/\{\pm 1\}$ is faithful.

**Is NOT an instance — a "square root" of the standard representation.** One might hope to define a representation $\sigma : U(1) \to \mathbb{C}^\times$ with $\sigma(z)^2 = z$, a "charge one-half". No such map exists, well-defined or not. First, the formula $e^{i\theta} \mapsto e^{i\theta/2}$ is not well defined as a function on $U(1)$: the angles $\theta$ and $\theta + 2\pi$ name the same point $z$ but give the values $e^{i\theta/2}$ and $e^{i\theta/2 + i\pi} = -e^{i\theta/2}$. Second, and more decisively, no group homomorphism $\sigma : U(1) \to \mathbb{C}^\times$ satisfies $\sigma(z)^2 = z$ for all $z$, continuous or not: suppose one did. Then $\sigma(-1)^2 = -1$ by the square-root property, while
$$\sigma(-1)^2 = \sigma(-1)\,\sigma(-1) = \sigma\big((-1)(-1)\big) = \sigma(1) = 1 \qquad \text{(homomorphism property; } (-1)^2 = 1\text{; } \sigma(1) = 1 \text{ since } \sigma \text{ is a homomorphism, by Direction 1 of the Proposition applied to the group homomorphism property alone).}$$
The two computations give $-1 = 1$, a contradiction. So the homomorphism clause, not merely smoothness, excludes half-integer charges for $U(1)$; this is the algebraic reason electric charge is quantised in integer multiples once the gauge group is taken to be $U(1)$ rather than its universal cover $\mathbb{R}$. (For $G = \mathbb{R}$ the map $t \mapsto e^{it/2}$ *is* a representation; it is the passage to the quotient $\mathbb{R} \to U(1)$ that kills it.)

**Is NOT an instance — a smooth action that is not linear.** Let $U(1)$ act on $V = \mathbb{C}$ by $\Phi(z, v) := z(v - 1) + 1$. *Smoothness:* $\Phi$ is polynomial in the real and imaginary parts of $z$ and $v$, restricted to $U(1) \times \mathbb{C}$. *Unit axiom:* $\Phi(1, v) = (v - 1) + 1 = v$. *Compatibility:* $\Phi(z, \Phi(w, v)) = z\big(w(v - 1) + 1 - 1\big) + 1 = zw(v - 1) + 1 = \Phi(zw, v)$. So $\Phi$ is a smooth left action of $U(1)$ on the manifold $\mathbb{C}$. *Failure of linearity:* $\Phi(z, 0) = 1 - z$, which is non-zero for $z \neq 1$, whereas a linear map sends $0$ to $0$. Hence the maps $\Phi(z, \cdot)$ do not lie in $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C})$ and $\Phi$ is not a representation; by the Proposition, it fails precisely the linearity requirement and nothing else. It is the rotation of the plane about the point $1$; conjugating by the translation $v \mapsto v - 1$ turns it into the standard representation, but a translation is not linear, so this conjugation is not an equivalence of representations, and indeed there is nothing to be equivalent to.

**Is NOT an instance — neither a homomorphism nor valued in the automorphisms.** The smooth map $\varrho : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $\varrho(t) = 1 + t$, sends $t = -1$ to $0 \notin \mathbb{R}^\times$, so it does not even take values in $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}^\times$; and where it does, it is not a homomorphism, since $\varrho(1 + 1) = 3 \neq 4 = \varrho(1)\varrho(1)$. The correct one-dimensional real representations of the additive group $\mathbb{R}$ are $t \mapsto e^{at}$ for $a \in \mathbb{R}$; these satisfy $e^{a(s+t)} = e^{as}e^{at}$ and are smooth, and $t \mapsto 1 + t$ is the first-order Taylor polynomial of the one with $a = 1$, which is exactly why it fails at second order.

**Is an instance — change of basis gives equivalent matrix representations (Bär Example 1.3.13), worked for $SO(2)$.** Let $\varrho = \varrho_{\mathrm{st}}$ be the standard representation of $SO(2)$ on $V = \mathbb{R}^2$, $\varrho(R_\varphi) = R_\varphi = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix}$ (every element of $SO(2)$ has this form by [[Thm - SO(2) is Isomorphic to U(1)]]). Take $b = (e_1, e_2)$, the standard basis, and $b' = (u_1, u_2)$ with $u_1 = e_1$, $u_2 = e_1 + e_2$. Then $F_b = \operatorname{id}$ and $\varrho_b(R_\varphi) = R_\varphi$. The coordinate map $F_{b'}$ sends $v = y_1 u_1 + y_2 u_2 = (y_1 + y_2) e_1 + y_2 e_2$ to $(y_1, y_2)$, so $F_{b'}^{-1}(y) = Py$ with $P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (columns the $u_j$), and $T := F_{b'} \circ F_b^{-1} = F_{b'} = P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (check: $P P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1_2$). By Lemma E, $\varrho_{b'}(R_\varphi) = T R_\varphi T^{-1} = P^{-1} R_\varphi P$. We compute, writing $c = \cos\varphi$, $s = \sin\varphi$:
$$R_\varphi P = \begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c & c - s \\ s & s + c \end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{(matrix product),}$$
$$P^{-1} R_\varphi P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} c & c - s \\ s & s + c \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} c - s & -2s \\ s & c + s \end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{(matrix product: } (1,1)\text{ entry } c - s\text{, } (1,2) \text{ entry } (c - s) - (s + c) = -2s\text{).}$$
So the same rotation has matrix $R_\varphi$ in the basis $b$ and matrix $\begin{pmatrix} c - s & -2s \\ s & c + s \end{pmatrix}$ in the basis $b'$; the two matrix representations $\varrho_b, \varrho_{b'} : SO(2) \to GL(2; \mathbb{R})$ are different maps but are equivalent through the fixed matrix $T = P^{-1}$. *Sanity checks:* the trace is $2c$ in both bases and the determinant is $(c - s)(c + s) + 2s^2 = c^2 - s^2 + 2s^2 = c^2 + s^2 = 1$ in both, as similarity invariance requires ([[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative]], corollary: $\det(S^{-1}AS) = \det A$). And $\varrho_{b'}$ is again a representation, as Lemma E guarantees — one can see it directly, since $P^{-1} R_\varphi P \cdot P^{-1} R_\psi P = P^{-1} R_\varphi R_\psi P = P^{-1} R_{\varphi + \psi} P$.

**Is an instance — the standard representation of $SO(2)$ and the real form of the standard representation of $U(1)$ are equivalent.** Regard $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^2$ via $x + iy \leftrightarrow (x, y)$ and let $U(1)$ act on $\mathbb{R}^2$ through $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}$ read as a *real* two-dimensional representation. Multiplication by $z = e^{i\varphi} = c + is$ sends $x + iy$ to $(cx - sy) + i(sx + cy)$, whose matrix in the basis $(1, i)$ is $\begin{pmatrix} c & -s \\ s & c \end{pmatrix} = R_\varphi$. Pulling the standard representation of $SO(2)$ back along the Lie group isomorphism $\Psi : U(1) \to SO(2)$, $e^{i\varphi} \mapsto R_\varphi$, of [[Thm - SO(2) is Isomorphic to U(1)]] therefore gives exactly the real form of $\varrho_{\mathrm{st}}$; the two are equal, hence equivalent via $T = \operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{R}^2}$. This is the first instance of a general operation, pulling back a representation along a homomorphism, recorded as Corollary (ii).

**Corollary (i) — the values at $e$ and at inverses.** For every representation, $\varrho(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$ and $\varrho(g^{-1}) = \varrho(g)^{-1}$. *Proof.* The first identity was proved in Direction 1 of the Proposition: $\varrho(e) = \varrho(e)\varrho(e)$ and $\varrho(e)$ is invertible. For the second, $\varrho(g)\varrho(g^{-1}) = \varrho(gg^{-1}) = \varrho(e) = \operatorname{id}_V$ and $\varrho(g^{-1})\varrho(g) = \varrho(g^{-1}g) = \operatorname{id}_V$ (homomorphism property and the first identity), so $\varrho(g^{-1})$ is the two-sided inverse of $\varrho(g)$; inverses in a group are unique, so $\varrho(g^{-1}) = \varrho(g)^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary (ii) — pull-back along a homomorphism.** If $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is a representation and $\phi : H \to G$ is a Lie group homomorphism, then $\phi^* \varrho := \varrho \circ \phi : H \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is a representation of $H$, with $\ker(\phi^* \varrho) = \phi^{-1}(\ker \varrho)$. *Proof.* $\varrho \circ \phi$ is a group homomorphism, since $(\varrho \circ \phi)(hh') = \varrho(\phi(h)\phi(h')) = \varrho(\phi(h))\varrho(\phi(h'))$ (homomorphism property of $\phi$, then of $\varrho$), and it is smooth as a composition of smooth maps ([[Ex - Composition of Smooth Maps is Smooth]]). Its kernel is $\{h : \varrho(\phi(h)) = \operatorname{id}_V\} = \{h : \phi(h) \in \ker \varrho\} = \phi^{-1}(\ker \varrho)$. $\blacksquare$ In particular, restricting a representation of $G$ to a closed subgroup $H \subset G$ (taking $\phi$ the inclusion, smooth by the closed subgroup theorem as above) gives a representation of $H$, with kernel $H \cap \ker \varrho$; this is how the standard representations of $O(n)$, $SO(n)$, and $SL(n; \mathbb{K})$ arise from that of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$.

**Corollary (iii) — the differential is a Lie algebra representation.** For a representation $\varrho$, the differential $d_e \varrho : \mathfrak{g} \to \operatorname{End}(V)$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism for the commutator bracket on $\operatorname{End}(V)$. *Proof.* $\varrho$ is a Lie group homomorphism, so by [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]] — for a Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$, the differential $dF_e : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism — $d_e \varrho$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism into the Lie algebra of $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$. That this Lie algebra is $\operatorname{End}(V)$ with the commutator bracket, and the full statement that $d_e \varrho$ is a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$, are proved on [[Def - Representation of a Lie Algebra]], which restates this corollary at its point of use. $\blacksquare$

**Calibration check — the determinant.** Verify from the definition that $\det : GL(n; \mathbb{K}) \to GL(1; \mathbb{K}) = \mathbb{K}^\times$ is a one-dimensional representation, and compute its kernel. *Values:* $\det A \neq 0$ for $A \in GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ ([[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative]], corollary: an operator is invertible if and only if its determinant is non-zero), so $\det A \in \mathbb{K}^\times = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{K})$, where $\det A$ acts on $\mathbb{K}$ by multiplication. *Homomorphism:* $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$, which is [[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative]] itself. *Smoothness:* by the Leibniz formula on [[Def - Determinant]], $\det A = \sum_{\sigma} \operatorname{sign}(\sigma) A_{\sigma(1)1} \cdots A_{\sigma(n)n}$ is a polynomial in the entries of $A$, hence smooth on the open subset $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ of $\mathbb{K}^{n^2}$, with values in the open subset $\mathbb{K}^\times$. So $\det$ is a real (if $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$) or complex (if $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$) one-dimensional representation of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. *Kernel:* $\ker \det = \{A : \det A = 1\} = SL(n; \mathbb{K})$, the special linear group; so Lemma B recovers the fact that $SL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a closed normal subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. *Faithfulness:* for $n = 1$, $\det A = A$ and the representation is the identity, which is faithful. For $n \geq 2$, the diagonal matrix $A = \operatorname{diag}(2, \tfrac12, 1, \dots, 1)$ satisfies $\det A = 2 \cdot \tfrac12 = 1$ and $A \neq 1_n$, so $\ker \det \neq \{1_n\}$ and $\det$ is not faithful. The same computation restricted along the inclusions of Corollary (ii) gives two further representations worth knowing. On $O(n)$: from $A^t A = 1_n$ and multiplicativity, $\det(A^t)\det(A) = 1$; and $\det A^t = \det A$, because in the Leibniz formula for $\det A^t$ the summand for $\sigma$ is $\operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\prod_i A_{i\sigma(i)} = \operatorname{sign}(\sigma^{-1})\prod_j A_{\sigma^{-1}(j) j}$ (substituting $j = \sigma(i)$ and using $\operatorname{sign}(\sigma^{-1}) = \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)$), and $\sigma \mapsto \sigma^{-1}$ is a bijection of the permutations; hence $(\det A)^2 = 1$ and $\det|_{O(n)}$ takes values in $\{\pm 1\}$, with kernel $O(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{R}) = SO(n)$. On $U(n)$: from $A^* A = 1_n$, $\det(A^*)\det A = 1$, and $\det A^* = \det(\bar A^t) = \det \bar A = \overline{\det A}$ (transpose invariance just shown, then the fact that complex conjugation is a ring homomorphism applied to the Leibniz polynomial); hence $|\det A|^2 = 1$, so $\det|_{U(n)}$ is a representation $U(n) \to U(1)$ with kernel $U(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{C}) = SU(n)$. Two further checks the reader can carry out from this page alone: that the pull-back of the trivial representation along any homomorphism is trivial and the pull-back of a faithful representation along an injective homomorphism is faithful (both from Corollary (ii) and Lemma B); and that if $T$ is an equivalence from $\varrho$ to $\tilde\varrho$ then so is $\lambda T$ for every $\lambda \in \mathbb{K}^\times$, so that an equivalence, when it exists, is never unique.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Constructions on representations *(from Gauge Theory I)*
> With representations defined, the direct sum, tensor product, exterior and symmetric powers, dual, and complexification of representations can be built and verified — [[Def - Constructions on Representations]]. The dual representation is the first place where the homomorphism clause bites: the naive $g \mapsto \varrho(g)^*$ reverses the order of composition and one must use $\varrho(g^{-1})^*$ ([[Ex - The Dual Representation Requires the Inverse]]).

> [!tip] Representations of Lie algebras and the differential $d_e \varrho$ *(from Gauge Theory I)*
> Corollary (iii) is the door to [[Def - Representation of a Lie Algebra]]: every representation of $G$ differentiates to a representation of $\mathfrak{g}$, and for connected $G$ the latter determines the former up to equivalence. The adjoint representation and its differential $\operatorname{ad}$ are the central instance, [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]].

> [!tip] Classification for $U(1)$ and $SU(2)$ *(from Gauge Theory I)*
> The notions of equivalence and faithfulness are exactly what [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] classifies: every complex representation of $U(1)$ is equivalent to a direct sum of the charges $z \mapsto z^k$, and every complex representation of $SU(2)$ to a direct sum of the symmetric powers $\varrho_k$ of the standard representation.

> [!tip] Associated bundles *(from Gauge Theory III)*
> A representation $\varrho$ of $G$ on $V$ and a principal $G$-bundle $P$ produce the vector bundle $P \times_\varrho V$ — [[Def - Associated Bundle]]. Matter fields are sections of associated bundles for the standard representations; the curvature of a connection is a section of the adjoint bundle, associated to $\operatorname{Ad}$.
