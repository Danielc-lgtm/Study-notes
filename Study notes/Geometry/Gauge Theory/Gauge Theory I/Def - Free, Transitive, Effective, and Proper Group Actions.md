# Notation

Throughout, manifolds are smooth, Hausdorff, and second countable, and "smooth" means $C^\infty$; $G$ is a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] with identity $e$, multiplication $(g, h) \mapsto gh$, and inversion $g \mapsto g^{-1}$, and $M$ is a smooth manifold. Following the series conventions, Lie groups act on manifolds on the **left** unless the manifold is the total space of a principal bundle, on which they act on the **right**; both kinds of action are defined on this page.

A **left action** is a smooth map $\theta : G \times M \to M$, written $\theta(g, x) =: g \cdot x$; for fixed $g \in G$ we write $\theta_g : M \to M$, $\theta_g(x) := g \cdot x$, and for fixed $x \in M$ we write $\theta^{(x)} : G \to M$, $\theta^{(x)}(g) := g \cdot x$, the **orbit map** of $x$. A **right action** is a smooth map $M \times G \to M$, written $(x, g) \mapsto x \cdot g$, with $\theta^g(x) := x \cdot g$. The **orbit** of $x$ is $G \cdot x := \{g \cdot x : g \in G\} \subseteq M$ (for a right action, $x \cdot G := \{x \cdot g : g \in G\}$), the **stabiliser** (also called the isotropy group) of $x$ is $G_x := \{g \in G : g \cdot x = x\} \subseteq G$, and the **orbit space** is the set of orbits, written $G \backslash M$ for a left action and $M / G$ for a right action, with the [[Def - Quotient Topology and Identification Map|quotient topology]] induced by the projection $\pi : M \to G \backslash M$, $\pi(x) := G \cdot x$. We write $\operatorname{Diff}(M)$ for the group of [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphisms]] $M \to M$ under composition, $Z(G) := \{g \in G : gh = hg \text{ for all } h \in G\}$ for the **centre** of $G$, and $\Theta : G \times M \to M \times M$, $\Theta(g, x) := (g \cdot x, x)$, for the **shear map** of a left action (the name is descriptive, not standard). A continuous map $f : X \to Y$ between topological spaces is **proper** if $f^{-1}(K)$ is [[Def - Compact Space|compact]] for every compact $K \subseteq Y$. For matrix groups (see [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]]) $\mathbf{1}$ or $\mathbf{1}_n$ denotes the identity matrix, $e_1, \dots, e_n$ the standard basis, and $S^{k} := \{x \in \mathbb{R}^{k+1} : |x| = 1\}$ the unit sphere.

This is a compound page: it defines six interlocking notions — left and right actions, orbits and stabilisers, the orbit space with its quotient topology, the four adjectives *effective*, *free*, *transitive*, and *proper*, and the centre $Z(G)$ — because every one of them is a single sentence long and none of them is used anywhere in the series without at least two of the others; the theorems that use them (the quotient manifold theorem, the principal-bundle construction, the fundamental vector field) each need the whole package at once.

> [!warning] Convention: orbit-space notation and the letter for $\theta_g$
> Bär writes the orbit space as $G \backslash M$ for **every** action, including the right actions of a structure group on the total space of a principal bundle in his §2.2. This series writes $G \backslash M$ for a left action and $M / G$ (on bundles, $P / G$) for a right action, so that the side on which the group is written is the side on which it acts. The two symbols name the same set whenever an action has been converted from one side to the other by the recipe of Corollary 3 below (orbits are unchanged by the conversion). Bär also writes $L_g$ for the map $x \mapsto g \cdot x$; this series reserves $L_g$ for left translation on the group itself ([[Def - Left and Right Translations and Conjugation on a Lie Group]]) and writes $\theta_g$, as [[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group]] does. Finally, Bär's Example 1.5.9 names the group of rotations about the $z$-axis "$U(1)$" where his Example 1.5.7 (the same action) says $SO(2)$; the two groups are isomorphic as Lie groups by [[Thm - SO(2) is Isomorphic to U(1)]], and this page writes $SO(2)$ throughout.

# Axiom Motivation

A group acting on a manifold is the mathematical form of the statement "this space has these symmetries". The notion of a [[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group|smooth action]] itself — a smooth map $G \times M \to M$ compatible with the multiplication and the identity of $G$ — is deliberately minimal, so that every symmetry that occurs in nature qualifies: the rotations of a sphere, the translations of a torus, a one-parameter flow, the gauge group acting on connections. The price of that minimality is that the bare notion says very little. The trivial action, in which every element of $G$ does nothing, is a smooth action; so is the action of $G$ on a single point. What we want to know about a given action is *how much* of $G$ is visible in $M$, *how much* of $M$ is reached by $G$, and *how well-behaved* the resulting decomposition of $M$ into orbits is. The four adjectives defined on this page are the four answers, and each is exactly the hypothesis of a theorem that fails without it.

**The first question — how much of $G$ acts? — has two answers, and they are different.** Suppose we ask which group elements act as the identity on $M$. The set of such elements is a subgroup of $G$ (it is the kernel of the homomorphism $g \mapsto \theta_g$ established in Proposition 1 below), and the action is *effective* when that kernel is trivial: distinct elements of $G$ act as distinct diffeomorphisms, so $G$ is faithfully represented inside $\operatorname{Diff}(M)$. Now suppose we ask the sharper question of which group elements fix *some* point. An element $g \ne e$ may move most of $M$ while fixing a few points — a rotation of the sphere fixes its two poles — and such an element is invisible at those points even though the action is effective. The action is *free* when no $g \ne e$ fixes any point at all: every stabiliser $G_x$ is trivial, every orbit is a bijective image of $G$. Freeness implies effectiveness (Corollary 2), and the rotation of $S^2$ about an axis is the standard witness that the converse fails. What breaks if freeness is dropped is the structure of the orbit space: the orbit space of the rotation action on $S^2$ is the closed interval $[-1, 1]$ (Example 5), which has boundary points precisely at the images of the two poles, the two points with non-trivial stabiliser, so the orbit space is not a manifold. The quotient manifold theorem ([[Thm - Quotient Manifold Theorem for Free Proper Actions]]) asks for freeness for exactly this reason, and the principal bundles of chapter III are the actions that are free by fiat.

**The second question — how much of $M$ is reached? — is answered by transitivity.** The orbits of an action partition $M$ (Proposition 3), and the action is *transitive* when there is only one orbit, that is, when any point can be carried to any other. A transitive action makes $M$ look the same at every point, since $\theta_g$ carries a neighbourhood of $x$ to a neighbourhood of $g \cdot x$ diffeomorphically; such an $M$ is a [[Def - Homogeneous Space|homogeneous space]], and the theorem [[Thm - Orbit-Stabilizer for Lie Group Actions]] identifies it with the coset space $G / G_x$. Without transitivity that identification is false — the coset space $G / G_x$ is only the orbit of $x$, not all of $M$ — and $M$ decomposes into a family of homogeneous pieces of possibly different dimensions, as $S^2$ under the $z$-axis rotations decomposes into circles and two points. Transitivity and freeness together say that the orbit map $g \mapsto g \cdot x$ is a bijection $G \to M$ for every $x$; the action of $G$ on itself by left multiplication is the model, and an action that is both free and transitive is a copy of that model in which one has forgotten which point is the identity — the torsor picture used for the fibres of principal bundles.

**The third question — is the orbit decomposition well-behaved? — is answered by properness, and it is the only one of the four that is topological rather than algebraic.** Even a free action can have an orbit space that is a bad topological space. The action of $\mathbb{R}$ on the punctured plane $\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ by $t \cdot (x, y) := (e^t x, e^{-t} y)$ is free, but the orbits of $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ — the two half-axes — cannot be separated in the orbit space, because the orbit of $(s, 1)$ passes through $(1, s)$ and so accumulates on both half-axes as $s \to 0$ (Non-example 8). The condition that rules this out is that the shear map $\Theta(g, x) = (g \cdot x, x)$ be a proper map: the preimage of a compact set is compact. Read in words — in the sequential language that is available on manifolds, a reformulation we neither prove nor use on this page — properness says that if a sequence $x_k$ converges and the translated points $g_k \cdot x_k$ also converge, then the group elements $g_k$ cannot escape to infinity: some subsequence converges. In the hyperbolic example, $x_k = (1/k, 1)$ converges to $(0, 1)$, the translated points $g_k \cdot x_k = (1, 1/k)$ with $g_k = \ln k$ converge to $(1, 0)$, and $g_k \to \infty$. It is properness that makes the orbit space Hausdorff and, with freeness, a manifold; and it is automatic for compact groups (Corollary 5), which is why Bär's version of the quotient theorem assumes $G$ compact and the series' version assumes only properness.

**Why these four and not others?** One could ask for strengthenings — for instance that the action be *simply transitive* (free and transitive), or that stabilisers be finite rather than trivial, or that all orbits have the same dimension — and each of these names a genuinely useful class. But the four adjectives on this page are the minimal ones that separate the four independent failure modes just described: a non-trivial kernel (fixed by effectiveness), a non-trivial stabiliser (fixed by freeness), several orbits (fixed by transitivity), and a non-closed orbit relation (fixed by properness). Every other useful property is a conjunction or a weakening of these. A reader who has understood the four failure modes can reconstruct the four definitions by writing down, for each failure, the shortest statement that forbids it.

# The Definition

We first restate the notion of an action from [[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group]], in both the left and the right form, since everything on this page refers to it.

**Definition 1 (left and right actions; Bär Definitions 1.5.1 and 1.5.26).** Let $G$ be a Lie group and $M$ a smooth manifold. A **left action** of $G$ on $M$ is a smooth map $\theta : G \times M \to M$, $(g, x) \mapsto g \cdot x$, such that

1. **Compatibility.** $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$ for all $g, h \in G$ and all $x \in M$;
2. **Identity.** $e \cdot x = x$ for all $x \in M$.

A **right action** of $G$ on $M$ is a smooth map $M \times G \to M$, $(x, g) \mapsto x \cdot g$, such that

1. **Compatibility.** $x \cdot (gh) = (x \cdot g) \cdot h$ for all $g, h \in G$ and all $x \in M$;
2. **Identity.** $x \cdot e = x$ for all $x \in M$.

The word "action" without qualification means a left action. Every definition below is stated for left actions; the right-action version is obtained by writing $x \cdot g$ for $g \cdot x$ throughout, and Corollary 3 shows that nothing is lost by this, since every right action is a left action in disguise.

The definition of effectiveness refers to the homomorphism $g \mapsto \theta_g$, so we establish that map first.

> **Proposition 1 (each $\theta_g$ is a diffeomorphism, and $g \mapsto \theta_g$ is a homomorphism; Bär Remark 1.5.2).** Let $\theta$ be a left action of $G$ on $M$. Then for every $g \in G$ the map $\theta_g : M \to M$, $\theta_g(x) = g \cdot x$, is a diffeomorphism with inverse $\theta_{g^{-1}}$, and the map
> $$\Phi : G \to \operatorname{Diff}(M), \qquad \Phi(g) := \theta_g,$$
> is a group homomorphism. For a right action the corresponding map $g \mapsto \theta^g$ satisfies $\theta^{gh} = \theta^h \circ \theta^g$, so it is an anti-homomorphism, and $g \mapsto \theta^{g^{-1}}$ is a homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Diff}(M)$.

> [!note]- Proof of Proposition 1
> We are given a left action $\theta$ and must show three things: that each $\theta_g$ is smooth, that $\theta_g \circ \theta_h = \theta_{gh}$ and $\theta_e = \mathrm{id}_M$, and that these identities make $\theta_g$ invertible with smooth inverse and $\Phi$ a homomorphism. We first record that $\operatorname{Diff}(M)$ is a group under composition: the composition of two diffeomorphisms is smooth with smooth inverse (the composition of the inverses in the reverse order), the identity $\mathrm{id}_M$ is a diffeomorphism, and the inverse of a diffeomorphism is a diffeomorphism by definition; associativity is associativity of composition of maps.
>
> **Smoothness of $\theta_g$.** Fix $g \in G$. Let $\iota_g : M \to G \times M$, $\iota_g(x) := (g, x)$. Its two components are the constant map $x \mapsto g$ and the identity of $M$, both smooth, so $\iota_g$ is smooth (a map into a product manifold is smooth if and only if its components are). Then $\theta_g = \theta \circ \iota_g$ (since $\theta(\iota_g(x)) = \theta(g, x) = g \cdot x = \theta_g(x)$), a composition of smooth maps, hence smooth.
>
> **The composition law.** For $g, h \in G$ and $x \in M$,
> $$(\theta_g \circ \theta_h)(x) = g \cdot (h \cdot x) = (gh) \cdot x = \theta_{gh}(x) \qquad \text{(by the definition of } \theta_g, \theta_h \text{; then by Compatibility)},$$
> so $\theta_g \circ \theta_h = \theta_{gh}$ as maps $M \to M$. Also $\theta_e(x) = e \cdot x = x$ for all $x$ (by Identity), so $\theta_e = \mathrm{id}_M$.
>
> **Invertibility.** Applying the composition law with $h = g^{-1}$ and then with the roles exchanged,
> $$\theta_g \circ \theta_{g^{-1}} = \theta_{g g^{-1}} = \theta_e = \mathrm{id}_M, \qquad \theta_{g^{-1}} \circ \theta_g = \theta_{g^{-1} g} = \theta_e = \mathrm{id}_M \qquad \text{(composition law; } gg^{-1} = e = g^{-1}g \text{; } \theta_e = \mathrm{id}_M).$$
> Hence $\theta_g$ is a bijection with inverse $\theta_{g^{-1}}$, and this inverse is smooth by the first block applied to $g^{-1}$. Therefore $\theta_g$ is a diffeomorphism of $M$.
>
> **Homomorphism.** For $g, h \in G$, $\Phi(gh) = \theta_{gh} = \theta_g \circ \theta_h = \Phi(g) \circ \Phi(h)$ (by the composition law), which is the statement that $\Phi$ is a homomorphism into the group $(\operatorname{Diff}(M), \circ)$.
>
> **The right-action version.** For a right action, put $\iota^g(x) := (x, g)$; its two components are the identity of $M$ and the constant map $x \mapsto g$, both smooth, so $\iota^g$ is smooth, and $\theta^g = \theta \circ \iota^g$ is smooth as a composition. For $g, h \in G$ and $x \in M$,
> $$(\theta^h \circ \theta^g)(x) = (x \cdot g) \cdot h = x \cdot (gh) = \theta^{gh}(x) \qquad \text{(definition of } \theta^g, \theta^h \text{; Compatibility for right actions)},$$
> so $\theta^{gh} = \theta^h \circ \theta^g$, and $\theta^e(x) = x \cdot e = x$ (Identity for right actions) gives $\theta^e = \mathrm{id}_M$. Applying the composition law with $h = g^{-1}$ and then with the roles exchanged, $\theta^g \circ \theta^{g^{-1}} = \theta^{g^{-1} g} = \theta^e = \mathrm{id}_M$ and $\theta^{g^{-1}} \circ \theta^g = \theta^{g g^{-1}} = \theta^e = \mathrm{id}_M$ (composition law $\theta^{ab} = \theta^b \circ \theta^a$; $g^{-1} g = e = g g^{-1}$; $\theta^e = \mathrm{id}_M$), so $\theta^g$ is a diffeomorphism with inverse $\theta^{g^{-1}}$, the inverse smooth by the smoothness of $\theta^{g^{-1}}$ just shown. Setting $\Psi(g) := \theta^{g^{-1}}$ we get $\Psi(gh) = \theta^{(gh)^{-1}} = \theta^{h^{-1} g^{-1}} = \theta^{g^{-1}} \circ \theta^{h^{-1}} = \Psi(g) \circ \Psi(h)$ (using $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1} g^{-1}$, which holds because $(gh)(h^{-1} g^{-1}) = g (h h^{-1}) g^{-1} = g g^{-1} = e$, and the anti-homomorphism law). Therefore $\Psi$ is a homomorphism. $\blacksquare$

**Definition 2 (orbits, stabilisers, orbit space; Bär Definition 1.5.8).** Let $\theta$ be a left action of $G$ on $M$. For $x \in M$ the **orbit** of $x$ is the subset
$$G \cdot x := \{g \cdot x : g \in G\} = \theta^{(x)}(G) \subseteq M,$$
and the **stabiliser** of $x$ is the subset
$$G_x := \{g \in G : g \cdot x = x\} = (\theta^{(x)})^{-1}(\{x\}) \subseteq G.$$
The **orbit space** is the set of all orbits,
$$G \backslash M := \{G \cdot x : x \in M\},$$
equipped with the quotient topology induced by the surjection $\pi : M \to G \backslash M$, $\pi(x) := G \cdot x$; that is, a subset $W \subseteq G \backslash M$ is open if and only if $\pi^{-1}(W)$ is open in $M$. For a right action the orbit is $x \cdot G := \{x \cdot g : g \in G\}$, the stabiliser is $G_x := \{g : x \cdot g = x\}$, and the orbit space is written $M / G$.

**Definition 3 (effective, free, transitive, proper; Bär Definition 1.5.4 and the series' properness convention).** Let $\theta$ be a left action of $G$ on $M$. The action is called

- **effective** (some authors say *faithful*) if for every $g \in G$: $\big(g \cdot x = x \text{ for all } x \in M\big) \Longrightarrow g = e$;
- **free** if for every $g \in G$: $\big(g \cdot x = x \text{ for some } x \in M\big) \Longrightarrow g = e$;
- **transitive** if for all $x, y \in M$ there exists $g \in G$ with $g \cdot x = y$;
- **proper** if the shear map $\Theta : G \times M \to M \times M$, $\Theta(g, x) := (g \cdot x, x)$, is a proper map, that is, if $\Theta^{-1}(K)$ is compact for every compact $K \subseteq M \times M$.

For a right action the shear map is $M \times G \to M \times M$, $(x, g) \mapsto (x \cdot g, x)$, and the other three adjectives are defined by the same sentences with $x \cdot g$ in place of $g \cdot x$.

**Definition 4 (the centre; Bär Example 1.5.6.3).** The **centre** of the group $G$ is
$$Z(G) := \{g \in G : gh = hg \text{ for all } h \in G\}.$$

**The smallest case.** Take $G = U(1) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$ and $M = \mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^2$ with $z \cdot w := zw$. Compatibility is associativity of complex multiplication and Identity is $1 \cdot w = w$. The orbit of $w \ne 0$ is the circle $\{u \in \mathbb{C} : |u| = |w|\}$ (since $|zw| = |w|$, and every $u$ with $|u| = |w|$ equals $(u/w) w$ with $|u/w| = 1$), and the orbit of $0$ is $\{0\}$; the stabiliser of $w \ne 0$ is $\{1\}$ (from $zw = w$ and $w \ne 0$ we get $z = 1$), while the stabiliser of $0$ is all of $U(1)$. The action is therefore effective (an element fixing every point fixes $1 \in \mathbb{C}$, hence is $1$), not free (the origin), and not transitive (two orbits already, $\{0\}$ and the unit circle). The orbit space is the set of circles about the origin together with the origin, identified with $[0, \infty)$ by the radius, and the shear map $(z, w) \mapsto (zw, w)$ is proper because $U(1)$ is compact (Corollary 5 below). Every notion on this page is visible in this one picture.

## Equivalent formulations

> **Proposition 2 (effectiveness as injectivity; Bär Definition 1.5.4).** For a left action the following are equivalent: (a) the action is effective; (b) for every $g \in G$, $\theta_g = \mathrm{id}_M$ implies $g = e$; (c) the homomorphism $\Phi : G \to \operatorname{Diff}(M)$ of Proposition 1 is injective; (d) the intersection of all stabilisers, $\bigcap_{x \in M} G_x$, is $\{e\}$. In every case the subgroup $\ker \Phi = \{g : \theta_g = \mathrm{id}_M\}$ equals $\bigcap_{x \in M} G_x$.

> [!note]- Proof of Proposition 2
> We must show four equivalences; we show (a)$\Leftrightarrow$(b), (b)$\Leftrightarrow$(c), and (a)$\Leftrightarrow$(d), after first identifying the kernel.
>
> **The kernel is the intersection of the stabilisers.** For $g \in G$: $g \in \ker \Phi$ if and only if $\theta_g = \mathrm{id}_M$ (definition of the kernel, the identity of $\operatorname{Diff}(M)$ being $\mathrm{id}_M$), if and only if $\theta_g(x) = x$ for every $x \in M$ (two maps are equal if and only if they agree at every point), if and only if $g \cdot x = x$ for every $x$ (definition of $\theta_g$), if and only if $g \in G_x$ for every $x$ (definition of $G_x$), if and only if $g \in \bigcap_{x \in M} G_x$. Hence $\ker \Phi = \bigcap_x G_x$.
>
> **(a)$\Leftrightarrow$(b).** By the chain just written, the hypothesis "$g \cdot x = x$ for all $x \in M$" in (a) is the same statement as "$\theta_g = \mathrm{id}_M$" in (b); the conclusions "$g = e$" coincide. So (a) and (b) are the same assertion.
>
> **(c)$\Rightarrow$(b).** Assume $\Phi$ injective and let $\theta_g = \mathrm{id}_M$. Since $\theta_e = \mathrm{id}_M$ (Proposition 1), $\Phi(g) = \Phi(e)$, so $g = e$ by injectivity.
>
> **(b)$\Rightarrow$(c).** Assume (b) and let $\Phi(g) = \Phi(h)$, that is, $\theta_g = \theta_h$. Then
> $$\theta_{h^{-1} g} = \theta_{h^{-1}} \circ \theta_g = \theta_{h^{-1}} \circ \theta_h = \theta_{h^{-1} h} = \theta_e = \mathrm{id}_M \qquad \text{(composition law of Proposition 1 twice; } \theta_g = \theta_h \text{; } h^{-1}h = e),$$
> so $h^{-1} g = e$ by (b), that is, $g = h$. Hence $\Phi$ is injective.
>
> **(a)$\Leftrightarrow$(d).** By the first block, the set of $g$ with $g \cdot x = x$ for all $x$ is exactly $\bigcap_x G_x$, and $e$ belongs to it (Identity). Statement (a) says that this set contains no element other than $e$, that is, that it equals $\{e\}$; this is (d). $\blacksquare$

> **Proposition 3 (orbits partition $M$; freeness and transitivity in terms of orbits and stabilisers).** Let $\theta$ be a left action of $G$ on $M$.
> (i) The relation $x \sim y :\Leftrightarrow$ there exists $g \in G$ with $g \cdot x = y$ is an equivalence relation on $M$ whose equivalence class of $x$ is the orbit $G \cdot x$. Consequently the orbits partition $M$: distinct orbits are disjoint and their union is $M$, and $G \backslash M$ is the quotient set $M / {\sim}$.
> (ii) Each stabiliser $G_x$ is a closed subgroup of $G$; hence, by [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem|the closed subgroup theorem]] (a closed subgroup of a Lie group is an embedded Lie subgroup), an embedded Lie subgroup.
> (iii) The action is free if and only if $G_x = \{e\}$ for every $x \in M$.
> (iv) Suppose $M \ne \emptyset$. The action is transitive if and only if $G \cdot x = M$ for some $x \in M$, if and only if $G \cdot x = M$ for every $x \in M$, if and only if $G \backslash M$ consists of a single point.

> [!note]- Proof of Proposition 3
> **(i) Reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity.** For every $x$, $e \cdot x = x$ (Identity), so $x \sim x$. If $x \sim y$ via $g$, so that $g \cdot x = y$, then
> $$g^{-1} \cdot y = g^{-1} \cdot (g \cdot x) = (g^{-1} g) \cdot x = e \cdot x = x \qquad \text{(substitute } y = g \cdot x \text{; Compatibility; } g^{-1}g = e \text{; Identity)},$$
> so $y \sim x$ via $g^{-1}$. If $x \sim y$ via $g$ and $y \sim z$ via $h$, then $(hg) \cdot x = h \cdot (g \cdot x) = h \cdot y = z$ (Compatibility, then the two hypotheses), so $x \sim z$ via $hg$. Hence $\sim$ is an equivalence relation. The class of $x$ is $\{y \in M : \exists g,\ g \cdot x = y\} = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\} = G \cdot x$ by definition of the orbit. The classes of an equivalence relation partition the underlying set, so the orbits partition $M$, and the set of classes $M / {\sim}$ is by definition the set of orbits $G \backslash M$.
>
> **(ii) $G_x$ is a subgroup.** $e \in G_x$ by Identity. If $g, h \in G_x$ then $(gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x) = g \cdot x = x$ (Compatibility; $h \in G_x$; $g \in G_x$), so $gh \in G_x$. If $g \in G_x$ then $g^{-1} \cdot x = g^{-1} \cdot (g \cdot x) = (g^{-1} g) \cdot x = x$ ($g \in G_x$; Compatibility; Identity), so $g^{-1} \in G_x$. Hence $G_x$ is a [[Def - Subgroup|subgroup]].
>
> **(ii) $G_x$ is closed.** The orbit map $\theta^{(x)} = \theta \circ \jmath_x$ with $\jmath_x : G \to G \times M$, $\jmath_x(g) := (g, x)$, is smooth (as in Proposition 1: the components of $\jmath_x$ are the identity of $G$ and a constant map), hence continuous. The singleton $\{x\}$ is closed in $M$, because $M$ is Hausdorff: for every $y \ne x$ there are disjoint open sets $U \ni x$, $V \ni y$, so $V \subseteq M \setminus \{x\}$ is an open neighbourhood of $y$ inside the complement, which is therefore open. Hence $G_x = (\theta^{(x)})^{-1}(\{x\})$ is the preimage of a closed set under a continuous map, and is closed in $G$. The closed subgroup theorem — a subgroup of a Lie group that is closed as a subset is an embedded Lie subgroup — now applies.
>
> **(iii)** The action is free if and only if for every $g \ne e$ there is no $x$ with $g \cdot x = x$ (this is the contrapositive of the defining implication), if and only if for every $x$ the set $G_x$ contains no $g \ne e$, if and only if $G_x \subseteq \{e\}$ for every $x$; and $e \in G_x$ always, so this says $G_x = \{e\}$ for every $x$.
>
> **(iv)** Assume $M \ne \emptyset$ and write (T) for transitivity, (S) for "$G \cdot x_0 = M$ for some $x_0$", (A) for "$G \cdot x = M$ for all $x$", and (P) for "$G \backslash M$ is a single point". We show (T)$\Rightarrow$(A)$\Rightarrow$(S)$\Rightarrow$(T) and (A)$\Leftrightarrow$(P).
>
> *(T)$\Rightarrow$(A).* Fix $x \in M$. For any $y \in M$ transitivity gives $g$ with $g \cdot x = y$, so $y \in G \cdot x$; thus $M \subseteq G \cdot x$, and $G \cdot x \subseteq M$ by definition. Hence $G \cdot x = M$.
>
> *(A)$\Rightarrow$(S).* Since $M \ne \emptyset$ there is some $x_0 \in M$, and (A) gives $G \cdot x_0 = M$.
>
> *(S)$\Rightarrow$(T).* Let $x, y \in M$. By (S) both lie in $G \cdot x_0$, so there are $g, h \in G$ with $g \cdot x_0 = x$ and $h \cdot x_0 = y$. Then
> $$(h g^{-1}) \cdot x = h \cdot \big(g^{-1} \cdot (g \cdot x_0)\big) = h \cdot \big((g^{-1} g) \cdot x_0\big) = h \cdot x_0 = y \qquad \text{(Compatibility twice; } g^{-1} g = e \text{ and Identity; the choice of } h),$$
> so $hg^{-1}$ carries $x$ to $y$, which is transitivity.
>
> *(A)$\Leftrightarrow$(P).* If (A) holds then every orbit equals $M$, so the set of orbits has the single element $M$; conversely if $G \backslash M$ is a single point then all orbits coincide with a single orbit $O$, and since the orbits cover $M$ by (i), $O = M$, so every orbit is $M$. $\blacksquare$

# Categorical Definition

Let $\mathbf{B}G$ denote the [[Def - Groupoid|groupoid]] with a single object $*$, with $\operatorname{Hom}(*, *) := G$, with composition given by the group multiplication, and with identity morphism $e$; the groupoid axioms are the group axioms (associativity, the neutral element, inverses). Let $\mathbf{Man}$ be the [[Def - Category|category]] of smooth manifolds and smooth maps. A [[Def - Functor|functor]] $F : \mathbf{B}G \to \mathbf{Man}$ consists of a manifold $M := F(*)$ and, for each $g \in G$, a smooth map $F(g) : M \to M$, subject to $F(gh) = F(g) \circ F(h)$ and $F(e) = \mathrm{id}_M$. Given a left action $\theta$, setting $F(g) := \theta_g$ produces such a functor: the two functor axioms are exactly the composition law $\theta_{gh} = \theta_g \circ \theta_h$ and $\theta_e = \mathrm{id}_M$ of Proposition 1, which in turn are exactly Compatibility and Identity of Definition 1. Conversely a functor $F$ gives a family of maps $\theta_g := F(g)$ satisfying Compatibility and Identity, and one recovers a left action provided the assembled map $\theta(g, x) := F(g)(x)$ is *jointly* smooth in $(g, x)$ — a condition that a bare functor does not impose, since it only sees each $F(g)$ separately. So, precisely: a left action of $G$ on $M$ is a functor $\mathbf{B}G \to \mathbf{Man}$ whose assembled action map is smooth. (In the language of internal categories, it is a functor from $G$, viewed as a Lie groupoid over a point, into manifolds, with smoothness internal to $\mathbf{Man}$.) A right action is a functor $\mathbf{B}G^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathbf{Man}$ with the same proviso: contravariance $F(gh) = F(h) \circ F(g)$ is the anti-homomorphism law $\theta^{gh} = \theta^h \circ \theta^g$ of Proposition 1.

The four adjectives are then properties of the functor. *Effective* means that $F$ is faithful — injective on morphisms — by Proposition 2(c). For the remaining three it is more natural to pass to the **action groupoid** $G \ltimes M$: its objects are the points of $M$, a morphism from $x$ to $y$ is a pair $(g, x)$ with $g \cdot x = y$, composition is $(h, g \cdot x) \circ (g, x) := (hg, x)$, and the identity at $x$ is $(e, x)$; the groupoid axioms are again Compatibility and Identity, and the inverse of $(g, x)$ is $(g^{-1}, g \cdot x)$ by the symmetry computation in Proposition 3(i). In this groupoid the automorphism group of the object $x$ is the stabiliser $G_x$, the set of objects isomorphic to $x$ is the orbit $G \cdot x$, and the set of isomorphism classes of objects is the orbit space $G \backslash M$. Hence: *free* means that every automorphism group of $G \ltimes M$ is trivial (Proposition 3(iii)); *transitive* means that $G \ltimes M$ is connected, that is, has a single isomorphism class (Proposition 3(iv)). *Proper* is the statement that the source-target map $(g, x) \mapsto (g \cdot x, x)$ from the morphisms of $G \ltimes M$ to pairs of objects — which is the shear map $\Theta$ — is a proper map; this is the definition of a proper Lie groupoid, of which action groupoids of proper actions are the basic examples.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The group-theoretic ancestor of everything here is the [[Def - Group Action|action of an abstract group on a set]], with its [[Def - Orbit and Stabiliser|orbits and stabilisers]]; the vocabulary *free*, *transitive*, *faithful* is the same there, and the orbit-stabiliser counting formula $|G \cdot x| \cdot |G_x| = |G|$ for finite groups is the finite shadow of the dimension formula $\dim (G \cdot x) = \dim G - \dim G_x$ that the smooth theory proves. What the smooth setting adds is topology, and properness is the one adjective that has no counterpart for abstract groups: it is a statement about the behaviour of the action at infinity in $G$, and for a finite group it is automatic. In the theory of dynamical systems an action of the additive group $\mathbb{R}$ is a flow, an action of $\mathbb{Z}$ is the iteration of a single diffeomorphism, a free action is a flow without fixed points, and a non-proper free action is a flow with recurrent orbits — the irrational line on the torus is the standard example — so properness is the dynamical condition "no recurrence", which is why the orbit space of a proper action can be a reasonable space while that of a recurrent flow is not. In geometry, Klein's Erlangen programme describes a geometry as a manifold with a transitive group of symmetries — a homogeneous space — and the adjectives on this page are the dictionary between the algebra of $G$ and the geometry of $M$.

**True name.** The official definitions are already operational, but the way they are used in practice is through Proposition 2 and Proposition 3: an action is a homomorphism $\Phi : G \to \operatorname{Diff}(M)$ that happens to be jointly smooth; *effective* means $\Phi$ is injective; *free* means every stabiliser is trivial, equivalently every orbit map $\theta^{(x)} : G \to M$ is injective; *transitive* means every orbit map is surjective; and *proper* means that the orbit maps are, uniformly in $x$, proper in the sense that group elements carrying a convergent sequence of points to a convergent sequence of points must themselves accumulate. The reader who keeps these four rephrasings in mind — injective on $G$, injective on orbit maps, surjective on orbit maps, proper on the shear — will find every verification on this page mechanical.

# Examples / Corollaries

We first prove the corollaries the spec assigns to this page and then verify the examples, since the examples use the corollaries.

> **Corollary 1 (Bär Remark 1.5.2).** For every left action, each $\theta_g$ is a diffeomorphism of $M$ with inverse $\theta_{g^{-1}}$, and $g \mapsto \theta_g$ is a group homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Diff}(M)$.

This is Proposition 1, proved above.

> **Corollary 2 (free implies effective; Bär Remark 1.5.5).** If $M \ne \emptyset$ and the action is free, then it is effective. The converse fails: the rotation action of $SO(2)$ on $S^2$ (Example 5) is effective but not free. The hypothesis $M \ne \emptyset$ cannot be dropped: every action on the empty manifold is free (there is no $x$ to fix), but it is effective only when $G = \{e\}$.

> [!note]- Proof of Corollary 2
> Assume $M \ne \emptyset$ and the action is free; we must show that any $g \in G$ with $g \cdot x = x$ for all $x \in M$ equals $e$. Let such a $g$ be given. **Produce a fixed point:** since $M \ne \emptyset$, choose $x_0 \in M$; then $g \cdot x_0 = x_0$ by the hypothesis on $g$. **Apply freeness:** the implication defining freeness, "$g \cdot x = x$ for some $x$ implies $g = e$", applies with $x = x_0$ and yields $g = e$. Therefore the action is effective.
>
> For the failure of the converse see the verification in Example 5. For the remark on $M = \emptyset$: the defining implication of freeness has a false hypothesis for every $g$ (there is no $x \in \emptyset$), so it holds; the defining implication of effectiveness has, for every $g$, the vacuously true hypothesis "$g \cdot x = x$ for all $x \in \emptyset$", so effectiveness asserts $g = e$ for every $g \in G$, that is, $G = \{e\}$. $\blacksquare$

> **Corollary 3 (left and right actions correspond; Bär Remark 1.5.27).** (a) If $(g, x) \mapsto g \cdot x$ is a left action of $G$ on $M$, then $x * g := g^{-1} \cdot x$ defines a right action of $G$ on $M$. (b) If $(x, g) \mapsto x \cdot g$ is a right action, then $g * x := x \cdot g^{-1}$ defines a left action. (c) The two constructions are inverse to each other, and they preserve orbits, stabilisers, and each of the four adjectives. (d) Without the inverse the recipe fails: for a left action, $x \star g := g \cdot x$ satisfies $x \star (gh) = (x \star h) \star g$, the compatibility law with the order of $g$ and $h$ reversed, so it is a right action if and only if $\theta_{gh} = \theta_{hg}$ for all $g, h$, which holds for instance when $G$ is abelian but not in general.

> [!note]- Proof of Corollary 3
> **(a) Direction 1: from left to right.** Let $\theta$ be a left action and define $\rho : M \times G \to M$, $\rho(x, g) := g^{-1} \cdot x$. *Smoothness:* $\rho = \theta \circ \sigma$ with $\sigma : M \times G \to G \times M$, $\sigma(x, g) := (g^{-1}, x)$; the components of $\sigma$ are $(x, g) \mapsto g^{-1}$, which is the composition of the projection to $G$ with inversion, smooth because inversion is smooth in a Lie group ([[Def - Lie Group]]), and the projection to $M$; hence $\sigma$ is smooth and so is $\rho$. *Identity:* $x * e = e^{-1} \cdot x = e \cdot x = x$ (since $e^{-1} = e$; Identity for $\theta$). *Compatibility:* for $g, h \in G$, $x \in M$,
> $$x * (gh) = (gh)^{-1} \cdot x = (h^{-1} g^{-1}) \cdot x = h^{-1} \cdot (g^{-1} \cdot x) = h^{-1} \cdot (x * g) = (x * g) * h \qquad \text{(definition of } * \text{; } (gh)^{-1} = h^{-1} g^{-1} \text{; Compatibility for } \theta \text{; definition of } * \text{ twice)},$$
> where $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1} g^{-1}$ was verified in the proof of Proposition 1. Hence $*$ is a right action.
>
> **(b) Direction 2: from right to left.** Let $(x, g) \mapsto x \cdot g$ be a right action and define $\lambda : G \times M \to M$, $\lambda(g, x) := x \cdot g^{-1}$. *Smoothness:* $\lambda$ is the composition of $(g, x) \mapsto (x, g^{-1})$, smooth by the same component argument, with the right action map. *Identity:* $e * x = x \cdot e^{-1} = x \cdot e = x$. *Compatibility:*
> $$(gh) * x = x \cdot (gh)^{-1} = x \cdot (h^{-1} g^{-1}) = (x \cdot h^{-1}) \cdot g^{-1} = (h * x) \cdot g^{-1} = g * (h * x) \qquad \text{(definition of } * \text{; } (gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1} \text{; Compatibility for the right action; definition of } * \text{ twice)}.$$
> Hence $*$ is a left action.
>
> **(c) The constructions are mutually inverse.** Starting from a left action $\theta$, applying (a) and then (b) gives $g \cdot' x = x * g^{-1} = (g^{-1})^{-1} \cdot x = g \cdot x$ (definition of $*$ from (a); $(g^{-1})^{-1} = g$), the original action; starting from a right action and applying (b) then (a) gives $x \cdot' g = g^{-1} * x = x \cdot (g^{-1})^{-1} = x \cdot g$. *Orbits:* for the right action of (a), $x * G = \{g^{-1} \cdot x : g \in G\} = \{h \cdot x : h \in G\} = G \cdot x$, because $g \mapsto g^{-1}$ is a bijection of $G$ onto itself (it is its own inverse). *Stabilisers:* $x * g = x$ if and only if $g^{-1} \cdot x = x$, if and only if $x = g \cdot x$ (apply $\theta_g$ to both sides, respectively $\theta_{g^{-1}}$, using Proposition 1), so the stabiliser of $x$ is the same subgroup $G_x$. *Effective, free, transitive:* effectiveness is the condition $\bigcap_x G_x = \{e\}$ (Proposition 2(d)), freeness is $G_x = \{e\}$ for all $x$ (Proposition 3(iii)), and transitivity is the condition that there is a single orbit (Proposition 3(iv), whose proof did not use the side of the action); each is stated purely in terms of orbits and stabilisers, which are unchanged. *Proper:* the shear map of the right action is $\Theta'(x, g) = (x * g, x) = (g^{-1} \cdot x, x) = \Theta(\sigma(x, g))$ with $\sigma$ the map from (a); $\sigma$ is a homeomorphism $M \times G \to G \times M$ (its inverse is $(g, x) \mapsto (x, g^{-1})$, continuous by the same argument). For compact $K \subseteq M \times M$, $\Theta'^{-1}(K) = \sigma^{-1}(\Theta^{-1}(K))$, which is the image of the compact set $\Theta^{-1}(K)$ under the continuous map $\sigma^{-1}$, hence compact by Lemma B below (the continuous image of a compact set is compact). So if $\Theta$ is proper then so is $\Theta'$; conversely, since $\Theta = \Theta' \circ \sigma^{-1}$ and $\sigma^{-1}$ is continuous, $\Theta^{-1}(K) = \sigma(\Theta'^{-1}(K))$ is the image of a compact set under the continuous map $\sigma$, hence compact if $\Theta'$ is proper, so $\Theta'$ proper implies $\Theta$ proper. For direction (b), the left action is $\lambda(g, x) = x \cdot g^{-1}$ with shear map $\Theta''(g, x) = (x \cdot g^{-1}, x)$; writing $\tau : G \times M \to M \times G$, $\tau(g, x) := (x, g^{-1})$ (a homeomorphism, its inverse $(x, g) \mapsto (g^{-1}, x)$ continuous by the smoothness of inversion and the projections), one has $\Theta''(g, x) = (x \cdot g^{-1}, x) = \Theta'_{\mathrm{r}}(\tau(g, x))$, where $\Theta'_{\mathrm{r}}(x, g) := (x \cdot g, x)$ is the shear map of the given right action; the same preimage-of-a-compact-set computation with $\tau$ in place of $\sigma$ shows $\Theta''$ proper if and only if $\Theta'_{\mathrm{r}}$ proper, so properness is preserved in this direction as well.
>
> **(d) The recipe without the inverse.** For a left action put $x \star g := g \cdot x$. Then $x \star (gh) = (gh) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x) = (x \star h) \star g$ (Compatibility for $\theta$), which is the right-action compatibility law $x \star (gh) = (x \star g) \star h$ if and only if $g \cdot (h \cdot x) = h \cdot (g \cdot x)$ for all $x$, that is, $\theta_{gh} = \theta_{hg}$. When $G$ is abelian, $gh = hg$ and this holds. When it fails — for instance for the left multiplication action of a non-abelian $G$ on itself, where $\theta_{gh} = \theta_{hg}$ would mean $gh = hg$ — the map $\star$ is not a right action. $\blacksquare$

> **Corollary 4 (the centre is a closed normal subgroup and is the kernel of the conjugation action).** $Z(G)$ is a closed subgroup of $G$, and it is normal: $gZ(G)g^{-1} = Z(G)$ for all $g$. For the conjugation action $g * h := ghg^{-1}$ of $G$ on itself (Example 4), $\{g \in G : g * h = h \text{ for all } h\} = Z(G)$; hence the conjugation action is effective if and only if $Z(G) = \{e\}$.

> [!note]- Proof of Corollary 4
> **Subgroup.** $e \in Z(G)$ since $eh = h = he$ for all $h$. If $g_1, g_2 \in Z(G)$ then for every $h$,
> $$(g_1 g_2) h = g_1 (g_2 h) = g_1 (h g_2) = (g_1 h) g_2 = (h g_1) g_2 = h (g_1 g_2) \qquad \text{(associativity; } g_2 \in Z(G) \text{; associativity; } g_1 \in Z(G) \text{; associativity)},$$
> so $g_1 g_2 \in Z(G)$. If $g \in Z(G)$ then for every $h$, $g^{-1} h = g^{-1} h (g g^{-1}) = g^{-1} (h g) g^{-1} = g^{-1} (g h) g^{-1} = (g^{-1} g) h g^{-1} = h g^{-1}$ (insert $gg^{-1} = e$; associativity; $g \in Z(G)$; associativity; $g^{-1}g = e$), so $g^{-1} \in Z(G)$.
>
> **Normal.** For $z \in Z(G)$ and $g \in G$, $g z g^{-1} = z g g^{-1} = z$ ($z$ commutes with $g$), so $g Z(G) g^{-1} \subseteq Z(G)$; applying this with $g^{-1}$ in place of $g$ and conjugating by $g$ gives the reverse inclusion.
>
> **Closed.** For each $h \in G$ the map $c_h : G \to G$, $c_h(g) := g h g^{-1} h^{-1}$, is smooth (a composition of multiplications and inversions), hence continuous, and $\{g : gh = hg\} = c_h^{-1}(\{e\})$ (since $ghg^{-1}h^{-1} = e$ if and only if $gh = hg$, by multiplying on the right by $hg$). The singleton $\{e\}$ is closed because $G$ is Hausdorff (as in Proposition 3(ii)), so each $c_h^{-1}(\{e\})$ is closed, and $Z(G) = \bigcap_{h \in G} c_h^{-1}(\{e\})$ is an intersection of closed sets, hence closed.
>
> **Kernel of conjugation.** For $g \in G$: $g * h = h$ for all $h$ if and only if $g h g^{-1} = h$ for all $h$, if and only if $gh = hg$ for all $h$ (multiply on the right by $g$, respectively by $g^{-1}$), if and only if $g \in Z(G)$. By Proposition 2 the conjugation action is effective if and only if this kernel is $\{e\}$, that is, if and only if $Z(G) = \{e\}$. $\blacksquare$

> **Corollary 5 (a compact group acts properly).** If $G$ is compact, then every smooth (indeed every continuous) left or right action of $G$ on $M$ is proper.

The proof uses two point-set lemmas whose vault pages do not yet carry complete proofs, so we prove them here, and two whose vault pages do: [[Thm - Tychonoff Theorem for Finite Products]] (if $X$ and $Y$ are compact then $X \times Y$ is compact in the product topology) and [[Thm - Closed Subset of Compact is Compact]] (a closed subset of a compact space is compact in the subspace topology).

> [!note]- Lemma A: a compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed
> **Statement.** Let $X$ be a Hausdorff space and $K \subseteq X$ compact. Then $K$ is closed in $X$.
>
> **Proof.** We show that $X \setminus K$ is open, that is, that every $y \in X \setminus K$ has an open neighbourhood disjoint from $K$. Fix such a $y$. **Separate $y$ from each point of $K$:** for every $x \in K$ we have $x \ne y$, so by the Hausdorff property there are open sets $U_x \ni x$ and $V_x \ni y$ with $U_x \cap V_x = \emptyset$. **Use compactness of $K$:** the family $\{U_x : x \in K\}$ is a cover of $K$ by open subsets of $X$ (each $x \in K$ lies in $U_x$), so by compactness of $K$ there are finitely many points $x_1, \dots, x_n \in K$ with $K \subseteq U_{x_1} \cup \dots \cup U_{x_n}$ (if $K = \emptyset$ then $K$ is closed because its complement is the open set $X$, so we may assume $K \ne \emptyset$ and take $n \ge 1$). **Intersect the corresponding neighbourhoods of $y$:** $V := V_{x_1} \cap \dots \cap V_{x_n}$ is open (a finite intersection of open sets) and contains $y$. If $z \in V \cap K$ then $z \in U_{x_i}$ for some $i$ (the $U_{x_i}$ cover $K$) and $z \in V \subseteq V_{x_i}$, contradicting $U_{x_i} \cap V_{x_i} = \emptyset$. Hence $V \cap K = \emptyset$, so $V \subseteq X \setminus K$ is the required neighbourhood. Therefore $X \setminus K$ is open and $K$ is closed. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma B: the continuous image of a compact space is compact
> **Statement.** Let $f : X \to Y$ be continuous and $K \subseteq X$ compact. Then $f(K)$ is compact.
>
> **Proof.** Let $\{V_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ be a cover of $f(K)$ by open subsets of $Y$; we must extract a finite subcover. **Pull back the cover:** each $f^{-1}(V_\alpha)$ is open in $X$ (continuity of $f$), and for $x \in K$ we have $f(x) \in f(K) \subseteq \bigcup_\alpha V_\alpha$, so $x \in f^{-1}(V_\alpha)$ for some $\alpha$; thus $\{f^{-1}(V_\alpha)\}$ is an open cover of $K$. **Apply compactness of $K$:** there are $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$ with $K \subseteq f^{-1}(V_{\alpha_1}) \cup \dots \cup f^{-1}(V_{\alpha_n})$. **Push forward:** if $y \in f(K)$, say $y = f(x)$ with $x \in K$, then $x \in f^{-1}(V_{\alpha_i})$ for some $i$, so $y = f(x) \in V_{\alpha_i}$. Hence $f(K) \subseteq V_{\alpha_1} \cup \dots \cup V_{\alpha_n}$, a finite subcover. Therefore $f(K)$ is compact. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Proof of Corollary 5
> Let $G$ be compact and $\theta$ a left action (for a right action, apply Corollary 3(c), which shows that properness is unchanged by converting to a left action; or repeat the argument below with the factors exchanged). Let $K \subseteq M \times M$ be compact. We must show that $\Theta^{-1}(K) \subseteq G \times M$ is compact.
>
> **Step 0: $\Theta$ is continuous.** The components of $\Theta$ are $\theta$, which is smooth, and the projection $(g, x) \mapsto x$, which is continuous; a map into a product is continuous if and only if its components are. Hence $\Theta$ is continuous.
>
> **Step 1: $K$ is closed in $M \times M$.** The product $M \times M$ is Hausdorff: if $(x, y) \ne (x', y')$ then $x \ne x'$ or $y \ne y'$; in the first case choose disjoint open $U \ni x$, $U' \ni x'$ in $M$ (Hausdorff property of $M$), and then $U \times M$ and $U' \times M$ are disjoint open neighbourhoods of the two points; the second case is the same with the factors exchanged. By Lemma A, the compact set $K$ is closed in $M \times M$.
>
> **Step 2: $\Theta^{-1}(K)$ is closed in $G \times M$.** It is the preimage of the closed set $K$ (Step 1) under the continuous map $\Theta$ (Step 0).
>
> **Step 3: $K_2 := \operatorname{pr}_2(K) \subseteq M$ is compact,** where $\operatorname{pr}_2 : M \times M \to M$ is the projection onto the second factor: it is continuous, and Lemma B applies.
>
> **Step 4: $\Theta^{-1}(K) \subseteq G \times K_2$.** If $(g, x) \in \Theta^{-1}(K)$ then $(g \cdot x, x) = \Theta(g, x) \in K$, so $x = \operatorname{pr}_2(g \cdot x, x) \in \operatorname{pr}_2(K) = K_2$, and $g \in G$ because $G$ is the first factor of the domain of $\Theta$.
>
> **Step 5: $G \times K_2$ is compact.** $G$ is compact by hypothesis and $K_2$ is compact by Step 3, so their product is compact by the finite Tychonoff theorem, restated above. Here $G \times K_2$ carries the product topology of $G$ with the subspace $K_2 \subseteq M$; this coincides with the subspace topology that $G \times K_2$ inherits from $G \times M$, because the basic open sets of the former are $U \times (V \cap K_2) = (U \times V) \cap (G \times K_2)$ with $U \subseteq G$, $V \subseteq M$ open, which are exactly the traces on $G \times K_2$ of the basic open sets of $G \times M$.
>
> **Step 6: $\Theta^{-1}(K)$ is closed in $G \times K_2$ and hence compact.** By Step 4, $\Theta^{-1}(K) = \Theta^{-1}(K) \cap (G \times K_2)$, the trace on $G \times K_2$ of a set closed in $G \times M$ (Step 2), which is closed in the subspace topology of $G \times K_2$, and that topology is the compact product topology by Step 5. By the theorem that a closed subset of a compact space is compact (restated above), $\Theta^{-1}(K)$ is compact.
>
> Therefore the preimage of every compact subset of $M \times M$ under $\Theta$ is compact, which is the definition of properness. Nowhere did we use smoothness beyond continuity, so the statement holds for continuous actions as well. $\blacksquare$

> **Corollary 6 (the quotient map is open; the orbit space is second countable).** For every left action, the projection $\pi : M \to G \backslash M$ is an open map: for every open $U \subseteq M$ the set $\pi(U)$ is open in $G \backslash M$. Consequently, since $M$ is second countable, so is $G \backslash M$. The same holds for right actions and $M / G$.

> [!note]- Proof of Corollary 6
> **Saturation of an open set.** Let $U \subseteq M$ be open. We claim $\pi^{-1}(\pi(U)) = \bigcup_{g \in G} \theta_g(U)$. Indeed, $x \in \pi^{-1}(\pi(U))$ if and only if $\pi(x) = \pi(u)$ for some $u \in U$, if and only if $x$ and $u$ lie in the same orbit for some $u \in U$ (definition of $\pi$), if and only if $x = g \cdot u$ for some $g \in G$ and $u \in U$ (Proposition 3(i): the orbit of $u$ is its equivalence class), if and only if $x \in \theta_g(U)$ for some $g$.
>
> **Each $\theta_g(U)$ is open.** By Proposition 1, $\theta_g$ is a diffeomorphism, in particular a homeomorphism, so it maps open sets to open sets.
>
> **Conclude.** $\pi^{-1}(\pi(U))$ is a union of open sets, hence open in $M$; by the definition of the quotient topology, $\pi(U)$ is open in $G \backslash M$. For second countability, let $\{B_n\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}}$ be a countable basis of $M$. We claim $\{\pi(B_n)\}$ is a basis of $G \backslash M$: each $\pi(B_n)$ is open by what was just shown, and if $W \subseteq G \backslash M$ is open and $\pi(x) \in W$, then $x \in \pi^{-1}(W)$, which is open in $M$, so there is $n$ with $x \in B_n \subseteq \pi^{-1}(W)$, whence $\pi(x) \in \pi(B_n) \subseteq \pi(\pi^{-1}(W)) \subseteq W$. $\blacksquare$

We now verify the examples. In each one the action is checked to be a smooth action (Definition 1), and then each of effectiveness, freeness, and transitivity is decided; properness is decided where it is not already settled by Corollary 5.

**Example 1 (the trivial action; Bär Examples 1.5.3.1 and 1.5.6.1).** Let $G$ be any Lie group, $M$ any manifold, and $g \cdot x := x$. *Smooth action:* the map $G \times M \to M$ is the projection to the second factor, which is smooth; Compatibility reads $x = x$ and Identity reads $x = x$. *Orbits and stabilisers:* $G \cdot x = \{x\}$ and $G_x = G$ for every $x$. *Effective if and only if $G = \{e\}$:* by Proposition 2(d) effectiveness means $\bigcap_x G_x = \{e\}$, and here $\bigcap_x G_x = G$ (or $\bigcap_x G_x = G$ vacuously also when $M = \emptyset$), so effectiveness is the statement $G = \{e\}$. *Free if and only if $G = \{e\}$ (when $M \ne \emptyset$):* by Proposition 3(iii), freeness means $G_x = \{e\}$ for every $x$, and $G_x = G$. Hence, as Bär states, for the trivial action on a non-empty manifold "effective", "free", and "$G = \{e\}$" are the same condition. *Transitive if and only if $M$ has at most one point:* there is one orbit per point. *Proper if and only if $G$ is compact (for $M \ne \emptyset$):* if $G$ is compact, Corollary 5 applies. Conversely, pick $x_0 \in M$; the singleton $K := \{(x_0, x_0)\}$ is compact, and $\Theta^{-1}(K) = \{(g, x) : (x, x) = (x_0, x_0)\} = G \times \{x_0\}$, whose image under the continuous projection to $G$ is $G$; if the action is proper this preimage is compact and then $G$ is compact by Lemma B. *Orbit space:* $\pi$ is a bijection $M \to G \backslash M$ that is continuous (by the definition of the quotient topology) and open (Corollary 6), hence a homeomorphism; the orbit space is $M$ itself.

**Example 2 (the action induced by a representation; Bär Examples 1.5.3.2 and 1.5.6.2).** Let $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ be a [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group|representation]] of $G$ on a finite-dimensional real or complex vector space $V$, and set $g \cdot v := \varrho(g)(v)$. *Smooth action:* the map $(g, v) \mapsto \varrho(g) v$ is the composition of $(g, v) \mapsto (\varrho(g), v)$, smooth because $\varrho$ is smooth, with the evaluation map $\operatorname{Aut}(V) \times V \to V$, $(A, v) \mapsto Av$, which in a basis is given by polynomials of degree two in the entries of $A$ and $v$, hence smooth. Compatibility is $\varrho(gh) v = \varrho(g)(\varrho(h) v)$, the homomorphism property of $\varrho$; Identity is $\varrho(e) = \mathrm{id}_V$. *Never transitive unless $V = \{0\}$:* every $\varrho(g)$ is linear, so $\varrho(g)(0) = 0$ and the orbit of $0$ is $\{0\}$; if $V \ne \{0\}$ then $\{0\} \ne V$, so by Proposition 3(iv) the action is not transitive. When $V = \{0\}$ the action is transitive because $M$ has one point. *Never free unless $G = \{e\}$:* $G_0 = G$ since every $g$ fixes $0$, so by Proposition 3(iii) freeness forces $G = \{e\}$. *Effective if and only if $\varrho$ is injective:* $\bigcap_v G_v = \{g : \varrho(g) v = v \text{ for all } v\} = \{g : \varrho(g) = \mathrm{id}_V\} = \ker \varrho$, so Proposition 2(d) says the action is effective if and only if $\ker \varrho = \{e\}$, that is, if and only if the representation is faithful. *Properness* depends on $\varrho$: it holds when $G$ is compact (Corollary 5) and fails, for instance, for the representation of $\mathbb{R}$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ by $t \mapsto \operatorname{diag}(e^t, e^{-t})$ — the argument of Non-example 8 applies verbatim, the origin playing no role in it.

**Example 3 (left multiplication; Bär Examples 1.5.3.3 and 1.5.6.3).** Let $G$ act on $M := G$ by $g * h := gh$. *Smooth action:* the action map is the multiplication $G \times G \to G$, smooth by the definition of a Lie group. Compatibility, $(gk) * h = g * (k * h)$, reads $(gk)h = g(kh)$, which is associativity; Identity, $e * h = h$, reads $eh = h$, which is the defining property of the neutral element — exactly as Bär remarks. *Free:* if $g * h = h$, that is, $gh = h$, then multiplying on the right by $h^{-1}$ gives $g = g h h^{-1} = h h^{-1} = e$. Hence $G_h = \{e\}$ for every $h$ and the action is free by Proposition 3(iii). *Effective:* by Corollary 2, since $G \ne \emptyset$. *Transitive:* given $x, y \in G$, the element $g := y x^{-1}$ satisfies $g * x = y x^{-1} x = y$. *Proper:* the shear map $\Theta(g, h) = (gh, h)$ is a homeomorphism $G \times G \to G \times G$ with inverse $(a, b) \mapsto (a b^{-1}, b)$ (check: $(gh, h) \mapsto (gh h^{-1}, h) = (g, h)$ and $(a, b) \mapsto (ab^{-1} b, b) = (a, b)$; both maps are continuous as compositions of multiplication, inversion, and projections). For compact $K$, $\Theta^{-1}(K)$ is the image of $K$ under the continuous inverse, hence compact by Lemma B. *Orbits and orbit space:* the single orbit is $G$, and $G \backslash G$ is a point. Right multiplication $h \cdot g := hg$ is a right action by the same verification with the sides exchanged, and it is likewise free, transitive, and proper.

**Example 4 (conjugation; Bär Examples 1.5.3.3 and 1.5.6.3, [[Def - Left and Right Translations and Conjugation on a Lie Group]]).** Let $G$ act on $M := G$ by $g * h := \alpha_g(h) := g h g^{-1}$. *Smooth action:* $(g, h) \mapsto ghg^{-1}$ is a composition of multiplications and an inversion, hence smooth. Compatibility: $(gk) * h = (gk) h (gk)^{-1} = g k h k^{-1} g^{-1} = g * (k * h)$ (using $(gk)^{-1} = k^{-1} g^{-1}$ and associativity). Identity: $e * h = e h e^{-1} = h$. *Effective if and only if $Z(G) = \{e\}$:* this is Corollary 4. For instance the conjugation action of $SO(3)$ on itself is effective (its centre is trivial), while that of $SU(2)$ is not (its centre is $\{\pm \mathbf{1}\}$); both computations are carried out on [[Ex - Conjugation and the Centre]]. *Not free unless $G = \{e\}$:* every $g$ satisfies $g * e = g e g^{-1} = e$, so $G_e = G$, and Proposition 3(iii) shows that freeness forces $G = \{e\}$. *Not transitive unless $G = \{e\}$:* the orbit of $e$ is $\{g e g^{-1} : g \in G\} = \{e\}$, so by Proposition 3(iv) transitivity forces $G = \{e\}$. Bär phrases this as "unless the group has only one conjugacy class"; since $\{e\}$ is always a conjugacy class on its own, the only group with a single conjugacy class is the trivial group, so the two phrasings agree. *Orbits and stabilisers:* the orbit of $h$ is its [[Def - Conjugacy Class|conjugacy class]] $\{g h g^{-1} : g \in G\}$ and its stabiliser is the centraliser $\{g : gh = hg\}$. *Proper:* when $G$ is compact, by Corollary 5.

**Example 5 (rotation of $S^2$ about the $z$-axis; Bär Examples 1.5.7.1 and 1.5.9).** Let $G := SO(2)$ act on $M := S^2 = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 = 1\}$ by
$$g \cdot x := \tilde{g} x, \qquad \tilde{g} := \begin{pmatrix} g & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in SO(3),$$
where $g \in SO(2)$ is a $2 \times 2$ block. *Well defined into $S^2$:* $\tilde{g}$ is orthogonal because $\tilde{g}^{\mathsf{T}} \tilde{g} = \operatorname{diag}(g^{\mathsf{T}} g, 1) = \mathbf{1}_3$, so $|\tilde{g} x|^2 = x^{\mathsf{T}} \tilde{g}^{\mathsf{T}} \tilde{g} x = |x|^2 = 1$. *Smooth action:* the map $SO(2) \times \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$, $(g, x) \mapsto \tilde{g} x$, is polynomial in the entries, hence smooth, and its restriction to the embedded submanifolds $SO(2) \times S^2 \to S^2$ is smooth (a smooth map into $\mathbb{R}^3$ with image in the embedded submanifold $S^2$ is smooth into $S^2$). Compatibility: $\widetilde{gh} = \tilde{g} \tilde{h}$ by block multiplication, so $(gh) \cdot x = \tilde{g} \tilde{h} x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$. Identity: $\tilde{\mathbf{1}}_2 = \mathbf{1}_3$. *Effective:* suppose $\tilde{g} x = x$ for all $x \in S^2$. Taking $x = e_1$ gives $\tilde{g} e_1 = (g_{11}, g_{21}, 0)^{\mathsf{T}} = e_1$, so the first column of $g$ is $(1, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$; taking $x = e_2$ gives the second column $(0, 1)^{\mathsf{T}}$; hence $g = \mathbf{1}_2$. (Only two points were needed, so the action is effective.) *Not free:* for every $g \in SO(2)$, $\tilde{g} e_3 = (0, 0, 1)^{\mathsf{T}} = e_3$, so $G_{e_3} = SO(2) \ne \{\mathbf{1}\}$ — likewise $G_{-e_3} = SO(2)$. By Proposition 3(iii) the action is not free. *Not transitive:* the third coordinate is preserved, $(\tilde{g} x)_3 = x_3$, so $e_1$ (with $x_3 = 0$) and $e_3$ (with $x_3 = 1$) lie in different orbits, and Proposition 3(iv) shows that the action is not transitive. *Orbits (Bär Example 1.5.9):* we claim that the orbit of $x = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$ is the latitude circle
$$C_{x_3} := \{(y_1, y_2, x_3) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : y_1^2 + y_2^2 = 1 - x_3^2\},$$
which degenerates to the single pole $\pm e_3$ when $x_3 = \pm 1$. The inclusion $G \cdot x \subseteq C_{x_3}$ holds because $\tilde{g}$ preserves $x_3$ and $g$ preserves the norm of $(x_1, x_2)$. For the reverse inclusion let $y \in C_{x_3}$. If $x_3 = \pm 1$ then $y_1 = y_2 = 0 = x_1 = x_2$, so $y = x = e \cdot x$. If $|x_3| < 1$ put $r := \sqrt{1 - x_3^2} > 0$; then $(x_1, x_2) = r (\cos \alpha, \sin \alpha)$ and $(y_1, y_2) = r(\cos \beta, \sin \beta)$ for some angles $\alpha, \beta$ (polar coordinates on the circle of radius $r$), and the rotation $g$ by the angle $\beta - \alpha$ — the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} \cos(\beta - \alpha) & -\sin(\beta - \alpha) \\ \sin(\beta - \alpha) & \cos(\beta - \alpha) \end{pmatrix} \in SO(2)$ — satisfies $g (x_1, x_2)^{\mathsf{T}} = r(\cos \beta, \sin \beta)^{\mathsf{T}}$ by the addition formulae for sine and cosine, so $\tilde{g} x = y$. Hence $G \cdot x = C_{x_3}$. *The orbit space is the interval $[-1, 1]$:* let $z : S^2 \to [-1, 1]$, $z(x) := x_3$ (continuous, being the restriction of a coordinate projection; its image lies in $[-1, 1]$ because $x_3^2 \le |x|^2 = 1$). By the orbit computation, $x$ and $y$ lie in the same orbit if and only if $x_3 = y_3$, so the fibres of $z$ are exactly the orbits, and $z$ is surjective because the continuous map $s : [-1, 1] \to S^2$, $s(t) := (\sqrt{1 - t^2}, 0, t)$, satisfies $z \circ s = \mathrm{id}_{[-1, 1]}$. Define $\bar{z} : SO(2) \backslash S^2 \to [-1, 1]$ by $\bar{z}(G \cdot x) := x_3$; this is well defined (the value is constant on orbits) and injective (distinct orbits have distinct heights), and surjective since $z$ is. It is continuous by the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Quotient|universal property of the quotient]] — for an identification map $\pi : X \to Y$, a map $f : Y \to Z$ is continuous if and only if $f \circ \pi$ is continuous — because $\bar{z} \circ \pi = z$ is continuous. Its inverse is $\pi \circ s$: indeed $\bar{z}(\pi(s(t))) = z(s(t)) = t$, and $\pi(s(\bar{z}(\pi(x)))) = \pi(s(x_3)) = \pi(x)$ because $s(x_3)$ has third coordinate $x_3$ and so lies in the orbit of $x$. Since $\pi \circ s$ is continuous (a composition of continuous maps), $\bar{z}$ is a homeomorphism. Therefore the orbit space is the closed interval $[-1, 1]$, a manifold with boundary and not a manifold; its two boundary points are the images of the poles, the two points at which freeness fails.

**Example 6 (scalar multiplication of $U(1)$ on $S^{2n-1}$; Bär Example 1.5.7.2).** Let $G := U(1) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$ act on $M := S^{2n-1} = \{w \in \mathbb{C}^n : |w_1|^2 + \dots + |w_n|^2 = 1\}$ by $z \cdot w := (z w_1, \dots, z w_n)$. *Well defined into $S^{2n-1}$:* $\sum_k |z w_k|^2 = |z|^2 \sum_k |w_k|^2 = 1$. *Smooth action:* complex scalar multiplication $\mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}^n \to \mathbb{C}^n$ is polynomial in the real and imaginary parts, hence smooth, and its restriction to the embedded submanifolds $U(1) \times S^{2n-1} \to S^{2n-1}$ is smooth as in Example 5; Compatibility is $(z z') w = z (z' w)$, the associativity of complex multiplication, and Identity is $1 \cdot w = w$. *Free:* suppose $z \cdot w = w$ for some $w \in S^{2n-1}$. Since $w \ne 0$ there is an index $k$ with $w_k \ne 0$, and $z w_k = w_k$ gives $z = 1$ after dividing by $w_k$. So $G_w = \{1\}$ for every $w$ and the action is free by Proposition 3(iii). *Effective:* by Corollary 2. *Proper:* by Corollary 5, since $U(1)$ is compact (a closed and bounded subset of $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^2$). *Transitive if and only if $n = 1$:* when $n = 1$, $S^1 = U(1)$ and the action is left multiplication of $U(1)$ on itself, transitive by Example 3. When $n \ge 2$, the points $e_1 = (1, 0, \dots, 0)$ and $e_2 = (0, 1, 0, \dots, 0)$ of $S^{2n-1}$ are not in the same orbit, because $z \cdot e_1 = (z, 0, \dots, 0)$ has second coordinate $0 \ne 1$ for every $z$; so the action is not transitive by Proposition 3(iv). Bär's phrasing of the general obstruction is the same argument: $z \cdot w = w'$ forces $w$ and $w'$ to be complex-linearly dependent, and for $n \ge 2$ there are linearly independent unit vectors. *Orbits:* the orbit of $w$ is $\{zw : |z| = 1\} = (\mathbb{C} w) \cap S^{2n-1}$, the unit circle of the complex line through $w$. The orbit space is the complex projective space $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, treated on [[Def - Complex Projective Space as a Quotient]], where the quotient manifold theorem is applied to precisely the freeness and properness verified here.

**Non-example 7 (a map that fails Compatibility).** Let $GL(2; \mathbb{R})$ act on $\mathbb{R}^2$ by $A \bullet v := A^2 v$. This is smooth and satisfies Identity, but it is not an action: with $A := \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B := \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ we compute $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $B^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $A^2 B^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, while $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $(AB)^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Hence $A \bullet (B \bullet e_1) = A^2 B^2 e_1 = (5, 2)^{\mathsf{T}}$ but $(AB) \bullet e_1 = (5, 3)^{\mathsf{T}}$, so Compatibility fails at $(A, B, e_1)$. None of the adjectives on this page applies to $\bullet$, because they are defined only for actions.

**Non-example 8 (a free action that is not proper: the hyperbolic flow on the punctured plane).** Let $G := (\mathbb{R}, +)$ act on $M := \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ by $t \cdot (x, y) := (e^t x, e^{-t} y)$. *Smooth action:* the map is smooth (exponentials times coordinates), preserves $M$ (if $(x, y) \ne 0$ then $(e^t x, e^{-t} y) \ne 0$), and satisfies $(s + t) \cdot (x, y) = (e^{s} e^{t} x, e^{-s} e^{-t} y) = s \cdot (t \cdot (x, y))$ and $0 \cdot (x, y) = (x, y)$. *Free:* if $t \cdot (x, y) = (x, y)$ then $e^t x = x$ and $e^{-t} y = y$; since $(x, y) \ne 0$ one of $x, y$ is non-zero, giving $e^{t} = 1$ or $e^{-t} = 1$, so $t = 0$. *Effective:* by Corollary 2. *Not proper:* consider the continuous map $\gamma : [0, 1] \to M \times M$, $\gamma(s) := \big((1, s), (s, 1)\big)$ (both components are non-zero vectors, so $\gamma$ takes values in $M \times M$), and let $K := \gamma([0, 1])$, compact by Lemma B since $[0, 1]$ is compact. We compute $\Theta^{-1}(K) = \{(t, p) : (t \cdot p, p) \in K\}$. A point of $K$ is $\big((1, s), (s, 1)\big)$, so $(t, p) \in \Theta^{-1}(K)$ requires $p = (s, 1)$ and $t \cdot (s, 1) = (e^t s, e^{-t}) = (1, s)$ for some $s \in [0, 1]$. For $s = 0$ this would need $e^{-t} = 0$, impossible; for $s \in (0, 1]$ it holds precisely when $e^{-t} = s$, that is, $t = -\ln s \in [0, \infty)$, and then $e^t s = 1$ automatically. Hence $\Theta^{-1}(K) = \{(-\ln s, (s, 1)) : s \in (0, 1]\}$, whose image under the continuous projection to $\mathbb{R}$ is $[0, \infty)$. This set is not compact — the open cover $\{(-1, n) : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ has no finite subcover — so by Lemma B (contrapositive) $\Theta^{-1}(K)$ is not compact, and the action is not proper. *What goes wrong in the orbit space:* the orbits of $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ are the positive half-axes $\{(x, 0) : x > 0\}$ and $\{(0, y) : y > 0\}$; any open neighbourhood of $(1, 0)$ contains $(1, s)$ for small $s > 0$, any open neighbourhood of $(0, 1)$ contains $(s, 1)$, and these two points lie on the same orbit (with $t = -\ln s$, as computed). So every pair of open sets in $G \backslash M$ containing the two orbits intersects, and the orbit space is not Hausdorff; the quotient manifold theorem cannot apply, and indeed it is properness that it needs.

**Calibration check.** First, $SO(3)$ acting on $S^2$ by $A \cdot x := Ax$: it is a smooth action (matrix multiplication restricted to embedded submanifolds, $A^{\mathsf{T}} A = \mathbf{1}$ preserving the norm), effective (if $Ax = x$ for all $x \in S^2$ then $A e_i = e_i$ for $i = 1, 2, 3$, so $A = \mathbf{1}$), not free (the stabiliser of $e_3$ contains every $\tilde{g}$ of Example 5, so it is a copy of $SO(2)$), transitive (given $x \in S^2$, complete $x$ to an orthonormal basis $(u, v, x)$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$, replacing $u$ by $-u$ if necessary so that $\det(u \mid v \mid x) = 1$; the matrix $A := (u \mid v \mid x)$ lies in $SO(3)$ and satisfies $A e_3 = x$, so every point is in the orbit of $e_3$ — this is [[Ex - S^2 as a Homogeneous Space of SO(3)]]), and proper since $SO(3)$ is compact (Corollary 5). Second, the antipodal action of $\mathbb{Z} / 2 = \{\bar{0}, \bar{1}\}$ on $S^n$, $\bar{1} \cdot x := -x$: it is a smooth action (the two maps $x \mapsto x$ and $x \mapsto -x$ are smooth, and $(-1)(-1) = 1$ gives Compatibility), free ($-x = x$ would force $x = 0 \notin S^n$), hence effective, proper (a finite group with the discrete topology is compact, so Corollary 5 applies), and not transitive for $n \ge 1$ (every orbit $\{x, -x\}$ has two points while $S^n$ is infinite); its orbit space is real projective space, [[Ex - Real Projective Space is a Smooth Manifold]]. Third, the irrational line on the torus, $\mathbb{R}$ acting on $T^2 = S^1 \times S^1 \subseteq \mathbb{C}^2$ by $t \cdot (z_1, z_2) := (e^{2\pi i t} z_1, e^{2\pi i \alpha t} z_2)$ with $\alpha \notin \mathbb{Q}$: it is a smooth action (exponentials and products; $e^{2\pi i (s + t)} = e^{2\pi i s} e^{2\pi i t}$), free (if $t \cdot (z_1, z_2) = (z_1, z_2)$ then $e^{2\pi i t} = 1$ and $e^{2\pi i \alpha t} = 1$, so $t \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $\alpha t \in \mathbb{Z}$; if $t \ne 0$ then $\alpha = (\alpha t) / t \in \mathbb{Q}$, contradicting the choice of $\alpha$; so $t = 0$), hence effective, not transitive (the point $(1, -1)$ is not in the orbit of $(1, 1)$: $t \cdot (1, 1) = (1, -1)$ would need $t \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $\alpha t \in \tfrac12 + \mathbb{Z}$, so $t \ne 0$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{Q}$, a contradiction), and not proper: $T^2 \times T^2$ is compact (a finite product of compact sets, $S^1$ being closed and bounded in $\mathbb{C}$), $\Theta^{-1}(T^2 \times T^2) = \mathbb{R} \times T^2$, and its continuous image under the projection to $\mathbb{R}$ is $\mathbb{R}$, which is not compact, so by Lemma B the preimage is not compact. The reader who can carry out these three decisions without consulting the examples above has the definitions in hand.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Fundamental vector fields *(from this chapter)*
> Differentiating the orbit map $\theta^{(x)} : G \to M$ at $e$ turns each $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ into a vector field on $M$, the [[Def - Fundamental Vector Field of a Group Action|fundamental vector field]]; for a free action the map $X \mapsto \bar{X}(x)$ is injective at every $x$, which is why a free action of a positive-dimensional group forces $M$ to carry a nowhere-vanishing vector field.

> [!tip] The quotient manifold theorem *(from this chapter)*
> For a free and proper action the orbit space is a smooth manifold of dimension $\dim M - \dim G$ and the projection is a submersion: [[Thm - Quotient Manifold Theorem for Free Proper Actions]]. Example 5 (freeness fails, the quotient acquires a boundary) and Non-example 8 (properness fails, the quotient is not Hausdorff) are the two failure modes that its two hypotheses exclude, and Corollary 5 is why Bär can state the theorem for compact groups.

> [!tip] Discrete groups and properly discontinuous actions *(from this chapter)*
> For a zero-dimensional group, "free and proper" is equivalent to Bär's "properly discontinuous", and the quotient is a covering: [[Def - Discrete Group and Properly Discontinuous Action]].

> [!tip] Principal bundles *(from Gauge Theory III)*
> A principal $G$-bundle is a manifold with a free right $G$-action whose orbit space is the base and whose orbits are the fibres; the fibres are torsors — free and transitive $G$-spaces — and Corollary 3 is the reason the series can take the action on the right while Bär's group-action chapter is written on the left.

> [!tip] Homogeneous spaces *(from Differential Geometry XI)*
> A transitive action identifies $M$ with $G / G_x$: [[Thm - Orbit-Stabilizer for Lie Group Actions]] and [[Def - Homogeneous Space]]. The sphere $S^2 = SO(3) / SO(2)$ of the calibration check is the first example, and the failure of freeness there — the stabiliser is the $SO(2)$ being divided out — is the whole content of the identification.
