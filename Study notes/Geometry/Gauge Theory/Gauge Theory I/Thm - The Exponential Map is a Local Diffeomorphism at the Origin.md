# Notation

This page depends on the following standing conventions of the series. Manifolds are smooth, Hausdorff, and second countable, and "smooth" means $C^\infty$. A [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] $G$ is a smooth manifold carrying a group structure whose multiplication $\mu : G \times G \to G$, $\mu(g, h) = gh$, and inversion $\operatorname{inv} : G \to G$, $\operatorname{inv}(g) = g^{-1}$, are smooth maps; $e \in G$ is the identity element and $n := \dim G$. The [[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group|Lie algebra]] of $G$ is $\mathfrak{g} := T_eG$, with the bracket transported from the bracket of [[Def - Left-Invariant Vector Field|left-invariant vector fields]]: for $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ we write $X^L$ for the unique left-invariant vector field on $G$ with $X^L(e) = X$, namely $X^L(g) = d(L_g)_e(X)$ where $L_g : G \to G$, $L_g(h) = gh$, is left translation (see [[Def - Left and Right Translations and Conjugation on a Lie Group]]), and $[X, Y] := [X^L, Y^L](e)$. The bracket is bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the Jacobi identity ([[Def - Lie Algebra]]); a Lie algebra whose bracket vanishes identically is called abelian ([[Def - Lie Subalgebra and Abelian Lie Algebra]]).

For $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, $\gamma_X : \mathbb{R} \to G$ denotes the integral curve of $X^L$ with $\gamma_X(0) = e$; it is defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$ and is a group homomorphism $(\mathbb{R}, +) \to G$ — both facts are proved on [[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]]. The [[Def - Exponential Map of a Lie Group|exponential map]] is
$$\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G, \qquad \exp(X) := \gamma_X(1),$$
and the same sibling page proves that $\exp$ is smooth, that $\gamma_X(t) = \exp(tX)$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$, and consequently that $\exp((s + t)X) = \exp(sX)\exp(tX)$, $\exp(0) = e$, and $\exp(-X) = \exp(X)^{-1}$.

The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is a finite-dimensional real vector space, and we regard it as a smooth manifold through the atlas generated by any linear isomorphism $\beta : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ (two such isomorphisms differ by an invertible linear map of $\mathbb{R}^n$, which is smooth with smooth inverse, so the smooth structure does not depend on $\beta$). For a point $p$ of a finite-dimensional real vector space $E$ we use the canonical identification
$$\iota_p : E \to T_pE, \qquad \iota_p(v) := \sigma_v'(0), \quad \sigma_v(t) := p + tv,$$
the velocity at time $0$ of the affine line through $p$ in direction $v$; Lemma 1 below proves that $\iota_p$ is a linear isomorphism. Every statement of the form "$d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$" on this page means $d\exp_0 \circ \iota_0 = \operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$ as maps $\mathfrak{g} \to T_eG = \mathfrak{g}$, and "$d_e\operatorname{inv} = -\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$" means $d\operatorname{inv}_e(X) = -X$ for every $X \in T_eG$.

The [[Def - The Differential of a Smooth Map|differential]] of a smooth map $F : M \to N$ at $p \in M$ is the linear map $dF_p : T_pM \to T_{F(p)}N$, $(dF_p v)(f) = v(f \circ F)$; Bär writes $d_p F$ and, for a Lie group homomorphism $\varphi$, abbreviates $\varphi_* := d_e\varphi$. The [[Def - Velocity of a Curve|velocity]] of a smooth curve $\sigma : J \to M$ ($J \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ an open interval) at $t_0 \in J$ is $\sigma'(t_0) := d\sigma_{t_0}\big(\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t_0}\big) \in T_{\sigma(t_0)}M$. A [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]] is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse. A topological space is [[Def - Connected Space|connected]] if its only subsets that are both open and closed are the empty set and the whole space. The full symbol registry for the chapter is on the topic page [[Gauge Theory I — Lie Groups, Representations, and Group Actions]].

# Statement

> **Theorem (the exponential map is a local diffeomorphism at the origin).** Let $G$ be a Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_eG$ and exponential map $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$. Then:
>
> **(i)** *(Bär, Lemma 1.4.4.)* The differential of $\exp$ at $0 \in \mathfrak{g}$ is the identity: under the canonical identification $\iota_0 : \mathfrak{g} \to T_0\mathfrak{g}$,
> $$d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}} : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}.$$
>
> **(ii)** *(Bär, Corollary 1.4.5.)* There exist an open neighbourhood $U \subseteq \mathfrak{g}$ of $0$ and an open neighbourhood $V \subseteq G$ of $e$ such that the restriction
> $$\exp|_U : U \to V$$
> is a diffeomorphism.
>
> **(iii)** *(Bär, Corollary 1.4.6.)* The differential at $e$ of the inversion map $\operatorname{inv} : G \to G$, $g \mapsto g^{-1}$, is minus the identity:
> $$d_e\operatorname{inv} = -\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}} : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}.$$

> **Corollary A (the Lie algebra of an abelian Lie group is abelian).** *(Bär, Remark 1.4.11.)* If $G$ is an abelian Lie group, then $[X, Y] = 0$ for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$; that is, $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] = 0$.

> **Corollary B (exponential coordinates, and connected Lie groups are generated by the image of $\exp$).** Let $U$ and $V$ be as in part (ii) and let $\beta : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be a linear isomorphism.
>
> **(a)** The map $\chi := \beta \circ (\exp|_U)^{-1} : V \to \beta(U) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is a smooth chart of $G$ centred at $e$ (that is, $\chi$ belongs to the maximal atlas of $G$ and $\chi(e) = 0$), and for every $g \in G$ the map $\chi \circ L_{g^{-1}} : gV \to \beta(U)$ is a smooth chart of $G$ centred at $g$.
>
> **(b)** If $G$ is connected, then every $g \in G$ is a finite product of exponentials: there exist $k \geq 1$ and $X_1, \dots, X_k \in \mathfrak{g}$ with $g = \exp(X_1)\exp(X_2)\cdots\exp(X_k)$.

The three parts of the theorem form a single chain: part (ii) is part (i) fed into the inverse function theorem, and part (iii) is obtained by differentiating the identity $\exp(-X) = \exp(X)^{-1}$, which is only usable because part (i) tells us what $d_0\exp$ is. Corollary A combines part (iii) with the fact that the differential of a Lie group homomorphism preserves brackets; Corollary B is the standard way part (ii) is consumed downstream — as a supply of charts near every point of $G$, and as the "open subgroup" argument that lets one pass from a neighbourhood of $e$ to all of a connected group.

# Motivation

The exponential map was defined on the preceding page as "flow along the left-invariant vector field $X^L$ for unit time, starting at $e$". That definition makes $\exp$ smooth and makes each line $\{tX : t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ map onto a one-parameter subgroup of $G$, but it says nothing yet about how the different lines fit together. Does $\exp$ fold, crush, or otherwise lose information near $0$? The answer of this page is that it does not: to first order at the origin, $\exp$ is exactly the identification $T_0\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{g} = T_eG$, and so — by the inverse function theorem — it is a diffeomorphism from a small ball in $\mathfrak{g}$ onto a small neighbourhood of $e$ in $G$.

Why does this matter so much? Because it is the single mechanism that lets one transport questions about the group $G$ near the identity into questions about the vector space $\mathfrak{g}$ near the origin, where they become linear algebra. A Lie group has no preferred coordinates; the exponential map manufactures a preferred chart $(\exp|_U)^{-1}$ at $e$, and left translation carries that chart to every other point. These "exponential coordinates", or canonical coordinates of the first kind, are the coordinates in which multiplication near $e$ is governed by the Lie bracket (through the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff series, which we do not need here), in which closed subgroups are seen to be embedded submanifolds (the closed subgroup theorem), and in which continuous homomorphisms are proved to be smooth.

The page also records two consequences that look elementary but are used repeatedly. First, the derivative of inversion at $e$ is $-\operatorname{id}$: the group operation "inverse" linearises to the vector-space operation "negation". Second, if the group is commutative then the bracket on its Lie algebra is zero. This second fact is the first instance of a general principle that will run through the whole series — algebraic properties of $G$ (commutativity, being a normal subgroup, being the kernel of a homomorphism) are reflected by algebraic properties of $\mathfrak{g}$ (vanishing bracket, being an ideal, being the kernel of the induced Lie algebra homomorphism) — and its proof is a tiny model of how such transfers work: express the group property as the statement that some map is a homomorphism, differentiate, and read off the algebraic consequence.

In the chapters on connections and gauge transformations, part (ii) is invoked whenever a gauge transformation or a holonomy element close to the identity needs to be written as $\exp$ of a Lie-algebra element — for instance to compute the infinitesimal action of the gauge group on the space of connections, or to show that the holonomy group of a connection is a Lie subgroup. Corollary B(b) is the tool that upgrades a statement proved for elements of the form $\exp(X)$ to a statement about all elements of a connected group, and is used in this chapter's classification of representations of $SU(2)$.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The hypothesis of the theorem is only "$G$ is a Lie group", so the real question of recognition is: when is a map one is handed *secretly* the exponential map of a Lie group, so that the local-diffeomorphism conclusion and the formula $d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}$ become available?

The first disguised source is **the matrix exponential on a closed subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$**. If $G \subseteq GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a closed subgroup, then the power series $e^X = \sum_{k \geq 0} X^k/k!$ restricted to the subspace $\mathfrak{g} \subseteq \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ is the exponential map of $G$ — this is the content of [[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential]]. The bridge is that $t \mapsto e^{tX}$ is a smooth homomorphism $\mathbb{R} \to G$ with initial velocity $X$, hence is $\gamma_X$. Once this is recognised, the present theorem says at once that $X \mapsto e^X$ is a diffeomorphism from a neighbourhood of $0$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ onto a neighbourhood of the identity matrix in $G$, with inverse the matrix logarithm; one does not need to analyse the convergence of the logarithm series to know that a local inverse exists. *Example problem:* show that every matrix $A \in SO(n)$ sufficiently close to $1_n$ is of the form $e^X$ with $X$ antisymmetric, and that $X$ is unique if it is required to be small.

The second disguised source is **a flow, or one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms**. If $Y$ is a complete vector field on a manifold $M$ and $\phi_t$ its flow, then $t \mapsto \phi_t$ is a homomorphism from $\mathbb{R}$ into $\operatorname{Diff}(M)$; when the flow happens to lie inside a finite-dimensional Lie group $G$ acting on $M$ — for instance a rotation group acting on a sphere — then $\phi_t = \theta(\exp(tX))$ for a unique $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, where $\theta$ is the action, because one-parameter subgroups of $G$ are exactly the curves $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ ([[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]]). The bridge is that a one-parameter group of symmetries is the image of a line in $\mathfrak{g}$ under $\exp$. *Example problem:* the rotations of $S^2$ about a fixed axis by angle $t$ form a one-parameter subgroup of $SO(3)$; identify the antisymmetric matrix $X$ with $\exp(tX)$ equal to that rotation, and conclude from part (ii) that every rotation close to the identity is a rotation about some axis by some small angle.

The third disguised source is **a Lie group homomorphism whose differential is an isomorphism**. Suppose $F : G \to H$ is a Lie group homomorphism and $F_* = d_eF : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is bijective. By the [[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map|naturality of the exponential map]] (for every Lie group homomorphism $F$, $F \circ \exp_G = \exp_H \circ F_*$), near $e$ the map $F$ factors as $\exp_H \circ F_* \circ (\exp_G|_U)^{-1}$, a composite of the local diffeomorphism $(\exp_G|_U)^{-1}$, the linear isomorphism $F_*$, and the local diffeomorphism $\exp_H|_{U'}$. So $F$ is itself a local diffeomorphism at $e$, hence at every point by left translation. The bridge is that the theorem of this page turns a *linear* condition on $F_*$ into a *smooth* conclusion about $F$. *Example problem:* the adjoint homomorphism $\operatorname{Ad} : SU(2) \to SO(3)$ has bijective differential; conclude that it is a local diffeomorphism and hence, being a surjective homomorphism with kernel $\{\pm 1\}$, a two-sheeted covering map.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

Combine part (ii) with **naturality of $\exp$ and connectedness**. If $F_1, F_2 : G \to H$ are Lie group homomorphisms with $(F_1)_* = (F_2)_*$ and $G$ is connected, then $F_1 = F_2$: by naturality, $F_i(\exp X) = \exp((F_i)_*X)$, so $F_1$ and $F_2$ agree on $\exp(\mathfrak{g})$, hence on all finite products of exponentials, hence on all of $G$ by Corollary B(b). The extra ingredient is connectedness; the payoff is that a homomorphism out of a connected Lie group is determined by a linear map, which is the uniqueness half of the Lie correspondence.

Combine Corollary B(b) with **a property that is closed under products**. Whenever one wants to show that every element of a connected Lie group $G$ satisfies some property $P$, it suffices to show that $P$ holds for every $\exp(X)$ and that the set of elements satisfying $P$ is closed under multiplication. For instance, to show that a connected Lie group $G$ with abelian Lie algebra is abelian, one shows that $\exp(X)$ and $\exp(Y)$ commute for all $X, Y$ (which needs the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula or a flow argument, not proved on this page), and then the set of pairs of commuting elements is closed under products in each slot. The payoff is that "generated by exponentials" converts statements about $\mathfrak{g}$ into statements about $G$.

Combine part (i) with **the chain rule**. The identity $d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}$ is what makes every "differentiate at $t = 0$" computation in Lie theory come out cleanly: $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} F(\exp(tX)) = dF_e(X)$ for any smooth $F$ defined near $e$, because $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ has velocity $X$ at $t = 0$. Part (iii) is the first example ($F = \operatorname{inv}$), and the formula $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(tX)}Y = [X, Y]$ on [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]] is the next. The payoff is a uniform recipe for computing differentials at the identity: evaluate along the one-parameter subgroups.

Combine Corollary B(a) with **the closed subgroup theorem**. Exponential coordinates are the coordinates in which one proves that a closed subgroup $H \subseteq G$ is an embedded submanifold with Lie algebra $\{X \in \mathfrak{g} : \exp(tX) \in H \text{ for all } t\}$ — see [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]]. The extra ingredient is closedness of $H$; the payoff is that all the classical matrix groups are Lie groups with computable Lie algebras, which is how [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]] and [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]] are established.

# Why Is It True

Consider the simplest case first: $G = (\mathbb{R}, +)$, so $\mathfrak{g} = T_0\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}$. The left-invariant vector field with value $X \in \mathbb{R}$ at $0$ is the constant field $X\,\partial_t$, its integral curve through $0$ is $\gamma_X(t) = tX$, and so $\exp(X) = X$: the exponential map of the additive group is the identity map $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$. Its differential at $0$ is of course the identity, it is a global diffeomorphism, and inversion $t \mapsto -t$ has derivative $-1$. Next take $G = U(1) = \{e^{i\theta}\}$ with $\mathfrak{g} = i\mathbb{R}$: here $\exp(i\theta) = e^{i\theta}$ wraps the line around the circle. It is not injective — $\exp(2\pi i) = \exp(0)$ — but near $0$ it is a diffeomorphism onto an arc, its derivative at $0$ is the identity $i\theta \mapsto i\theta$, and $\operatorname{inv}(e^{i\theta}) = e^{-i\theta}$ has derivative $-1$ at $\theta = 0$. The general theorem says that this picture is exact for every Lie group in a neighbourhood of the identity: whatever $G$ does globally, near $e$ it is a slightly bent copy of its Lie algebra, and $\exp$ is the map that straightens the bend.

**The mechanism behind part (i) is that $\exp$ restricted to each line through the origin is, by construction, the one-parameter subgroup whose initial velocity is the direction of that line — so $\exp$ moves every direction at $0$ to itself.** The differential $d_0\exp$ is a linear map; to compute it, one only needs its values on velocity vectors of curves through $0$, and the most convenient curves are the lines $s \mapsto sX$. Their images are the curves $s \mapsto \exp(sX) = \gamma_X(s)$, whose velocity at $s = 0$ is $X^L(e) = X$ by the very definition of $\gamma_X$ as an integral curve. There is no computation to do; the definition of $\exp$ was chosen so that this is true.

**The mechanism behind part (ii) is the inverse function theorem: a smooth map whose differential at a point is invertible is a diffeomorphism near that point.** This is where the analysis enters. The theorem of this page contributes the linear-algebraic input ($d_0\exp$ is the identity, hence invertible), and the [[Thm - The Rank Theorem|rank theorem]] — the manifold form of the inverse function theorem — returns the smooth conclusion. Nothing about Lie groups is used in this step except that $\dim \mathfrak{g} = \dim G$, which holds because $\mathfrak{g} = T_eG$.

**The mechanism behind part (iii) is that inversion and negation are intertwined by $\exp$, so their differentials at the fixed points $e$ and $0$ are intertwined by $d_0\exp$, which by part (i) is the identity.** The identity $\exp(-X) = (\exp X)^{-1}$ says exactly that the square
$$\mathfrak{g} \xrightarrow{\;-\operatorname{id}\;} \mathfrak{g}, \qquad G \xrightarrow{\;\operatorname{inv}\;} G, \qquad \text{with vertical arrows } \exp,$$
commutes. Differentiating a commuting square at the origin produces a commuting square of linear maps, and when the vertical arrows of the differentiated square are the identity, the horizontal arrows must coincide.

**The mechanism behind Corollary A is that a group-theoretic identity, once it makes some map a homomorphism, differentiates to a Lie-algebraic identity.** Commutativity of $G$ is precisely the statement that $\operatorname{inv}$ is a homomorphism (in general $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1}$, and reversing the order is allowed exactly when $G$ is abelian). The differential of a homomorphism preserves brackets; but by part (iii) that differential is $-\operatorname{id}$, and $-\operatorname{id}$ preserves brackets if and only if $-[X,Y] = [-X,-Y] = [X,Y]$, which forces the bracket to vanish.

**The mechanism behind Corollary B(b) is that a subgroup containing a neighbourhood of the identity is open, an open subgroup is also closed because its complement is a union of its translates, and a connected space has no nontrivial subsets that are both open and closed.** The exponential map supplies the neighbourhood; left translations, being homeomorphisms, spread openness to the whole subgroup; the coset decomposition of $G$ turns openness into closedness.

# What Makes This Hard

Nothing in the proof is long, but two points are routinely mishandled. The first is the identification $T_0\mathfrak{g} \cong \mathfrak{g}$: the statement "$d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}$" only makes sense once the tangent space to a vector space at a point has been identified with the vector space itself, and the identification must be the one for which the velocity of $s \mapsto sX$ is $X$; with any other identification the statement would be "$d_0\exp$ is some fixed linear isomorphism", which is equally true and equally useful but not the identity. The second point is the passage from "$d_0\exp$ is invertible" to "$\exp$ is a diffeomorphism near $0$": the [[Thm - The Rank Theorem|rank theorem]] as stated in this vault requires constant rank on a *neighbourhood* of the point, so one has to observe first that full rank is an open condition — the Jacobian determinant in charts is continuous and nonzero at $0$, hence nonzero nearby — and then that the normal form $\hat F(x) = x$ delivered by the rank theorem really does produce a diffeomorphism onto an open set, which is a short argument with charts that is usually skipped. The common error in Corollary A is to forget that $\operatorname{inv}$ is a homomorphism *only* for abelian $G$; for non-abelian $G$ it is an anti-homomorphism, $-\operatorname{id}$ is then an anti-homomorphism of Lie algebras (which every linear map $-\operatorname{id}$ trivially is, since $-[X,Y] = [Y,X]$), and no conclusion about the bracket follows.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** Compute $d_0\exp$ on the velocity vector of the line $s \mapsto sX$, using that $\exp(sX) = \gamma_X(s)$ is an integral curve of $X^L$; this gives part (i). Feed part (i) into the rank theorem (after checking that full rank persists on a neighbourhood) to get part (ii). Differentiate the identity $\operatorname{inv} \circ \exp = \exp \circ (-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}})$ at $0$ with the chain rule to get part (iii). For Corollary A, show $\operatorname{inv}$ is a homomorphism when $G$ is abelian, apply the theorem that differentials of homomorphisms preserve brackets, and solve $-[X,Y] = [X,Y]$. For Corollary B, use $(\exp|_U)^{-1}$ as a chart and translate it, and run the open-subgroup argument.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Identify $T_p E$ with $E$ for a finite-dimensional vector space $E$, and compute differentials of linear maps.** Show that $\iota_p(v) := \tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}(p + tv)$ is a linear isomorphism $E \to T_pE$ and that a linear map $L : E \to E'$ satisfies $dL_p \circ \iota_p = \iota_{L(p)} \circ L$.
   - *Hint:* Work in the global chart given by a basis: the velocity of $t \mapsto p + tv$ has components $v^i$, so $\iota_p(v) = \sum_i v^i \partial_i|_p$.
   - *Why needed:* Without it, "$d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}$" and "$d_0(-\operatorname{id}) = -\operatorname{id}$" are not even statements.

2. **Velocity of an image curve.** Show $(F \circ \sigma)'(t_0) = dF_{\sigma(t_0)}(\sigma'(t_0))$ for smooth $F$ and any smooth curve $\sigma$.
   - *Hint:* Both sides are $d(F \circ \sigma)_{t_0}$ and $dF_{\sigma(t_0)} \circ d\sigma_{t_0}$ applied to $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t_0}$; use the chain rule.
   - *Why needed:* It converts "$d_0\exp$ applied to $X$" into "the velocity of $s \mapsto \exp(sX)$ at $0$".

3. **Part (i).** Show $d\exp_0(\iota_0(X)) = X$.
   - *Hint:* $\exp(sX) = \gamma_X(s)$, and $\gamma_X'(0) = X^L(e) = X$ because $\gamma_X$ is an integral curve of $X^L$ through $e$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the statement, and it is the invertibility input for part (ii).

4. **Full rank is an open condition.** Show that if $\dim M = \dim N$ and $dF_p$ is bijective, then $dF_q$ is bijective for all $q$ in a neighbourhood of $p$.
   - *Hint:* In charts, $\det D\hat F$ is a continuous function that is nonzero at $\varphi(p)$.
   - *Why needed:* The rank theorem requires constant rank on a neighbourhood.

5. **Part (ii).** Apply the rank theorem with $r = m = n$ to get charts in which $\exp$ is the identity, then show that this normal form means $\exp|_U : U \to \exp(U)$ is a diffeomorphism onto an open set.
   - *Hint:* If $\psi \circ \exp \circ \varphi^{-1} = \operatorname{id}$ on $\varphi(U)$, then $\exp|_U = \psi^{-1} \circ \varphi$ and $\exp(U) = \psi^{-1}(\varphi(U))$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the statement.

6. **Part (iii).** Verify $\operatorname{inv} \circ \exp = \exp \circ (-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}})$ on all of $\mathfrak{g}$, differentiate at $0$ by the chain rule, and cancel $d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}$.
   - *Hint:* $\operatorname{inv}(\exp X) = (\exp X)^{-1} = \exp(-X)$ by the sibling page; the differential of the linear map $-\operatorname{id}$ is $-\operatorname{id}$ by subgoal 1.
   - *Why needed:* It is the statement, and Corollary A depends on it.

7. **Corollary A.** Show $\operatorname{inv}$ is a Lie group homomorphism when $G$ is abelian; apply [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]] to get $-[X,Y] = [-X,-Y]$; conclude $[X,Y] = 0$.
   - *Hint:* $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1} = g^{-1}h^{-1}$ uses commutativity once; bilinearity gives $[-X,-Y] = [X,Y]$; then $2[X,Y] = 0$ in a real vector space.
   - *Why needed:* It is the corollary.

8. **Corollary B.** (a) A diffeomorphism from an open subset of $G$ onto an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is smoothly compatible with every chart, hence lies in the maximal atlas; compose with $L_{g^{-1}}$ to centre at $g$. (b) The set $H$ of finite products of exponentials is a subgroup containing the open set $V$; every $hV$ is open and contained in $H$, so $H$ is open; $G \setminus H$ is a union of open cosets, so $H$ is closed; connectedness gives $H = G$.
   - *Hint:* $hV = L_h(V)$ is open because $L_h$ is a homeomorphism with inverse $L_{h^{-1}}$; $gH \cap H = \emptyset$ for $g \notin H$ because $gh = h'$ would give $g = h'h^{-1} \in H$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the corollary, and the topic page's Targets and the representation-theory page rely on it.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: The tangent space to a vector space, and differentials of linear maps
> **Statement:** Let $E$ be a real vector space of finite dimension $n$, regarded as a smooth manifold through the atlas generated by a linear isomorphism $\beta : E \to \mathbb{R}^n$. For $p \in E$ define $\iota_p : E \to T_pE$ by $\iota_p(v) := \sigma_v'(0)$, where $\sigma_v(t) := p + tv$. Then:
> (a) $\iota_p$ is a linear isomorphism, and $\iota_p(v) = \sum_{i=1}^n \beta(v)^i\, \partial_i|_p$ where $\partial_i|_p$ are the coordinate basis vectors of the chart $\beta$;
> (b) $\iota_p$ does not depend on the choice of $\beta$;
> (c) if $E'$ is another finite-dimensional real vector space and $L : E \to E'$ is linear, then $L$ is smooth and $dL_p \circ \iota_p = \iota_{L(p)} \circ L$ for every $p \in E$; in words, under the identifications $\iota$ the differential of a linear map at any point is the map itself.
>
> **Hint:** Compute the velocity of $t \mapsto p + tv$ in the chart $\beta$; the components are constant, equal to $\beta(v)^i$. For (c), $L \circ \sigma_v$ is again an affine line, $t \mapsto L(p) + tL(v)$.
>
> **Why needed:** It gives the precise meaning of "$d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$" and "$d_e\operatorname{inv} = -\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$", and part (c) is used with $L = -\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$ in the proof of part (iii).
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We assume that $E$ is a real vector space of dimension $n$ with the smooth structure generated by the chart $\beta$, and that $p \in E$; we need to show (a), (b), and (c).
> >
> > **(a) Formula for $\iota_p$.** Fix $v \in E$. The curve $\sigma_v : \mathbb{R} \to E$, $\sigma_v(t) = p + tv$, has coordinate representative
> > $$\beta \circ \sigma_v(t) = \beta(p) + t\,\beta(v) \qquad \text{(since } \beta \text{ is linear)},$$
> > which is a smooth (affine) map $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$; hence $\sigma_v$ is smooth. By the coordinate picture of the differential ([[Def - The Differential of a Smooth Map]]: $dF_p(\partial/\partial x^i|_p) = \sum_j (\partial \hat F^j/\partial x^i)\, \partial/\partial y^j|_{F(p)}$ for the coordinate representative $\hat F$), applied to $F = \sigma_v$ with the identity chart on $\mathbb{R}$ and the chart $\beta$ on $E$,
> > $$\sigma_v'(0) = d(\sigma_v)_0\Big(\tfrac{d}{dt}\Big|_0\Big) = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\big(\beta(p)^i + t\,\beta(v)^i\big)\, \partial_i|_p = \sum_{i=1}^n \beta(v)^i\, \partial_i|_p \qquad \text{(derivative of an affine function of } t\text{)}.$$
> > This is the asserted formula. The right-hand side is linear in $v$ because $\beta$ is linear, so $\iota_p$ is linear. It is injective because the coordinate vectors $\partial_1|_p, \dots, \partial_n|_p$ are linearly independent — they form a basis of $T_pE$ ([[Thm - Dimension of the Tangent Space]]) — so $\iota_p(v) = 0$ forces $\beta(v)^i = 0$ for all $i$, hence $\beta(v) = 0$, hence $v = 0$ since $\beta$ is injective. It is surjective because $\dim T_pE = n = \dim E$ (same theorem) and an injective linear map between vector spaces of the same finite dimension is bijective (rank–nullity). Therefore $\iota_p$ is a linear isomorphism.
> >
> > **(b) Independence of $\beta$.** The definition $\iota_p(v) = \sigma_v'(0)$ mentions only the curve $\sigma_v$ and the velocity of a curve, which is defined through the differential $d(\sigma_v)_0$; neither depends on a choice of chart. (The formula in (a) does mention $\beta$, but it is a formula for a chart-independent object.) Hence $\iota_p$ is independent of $\beta$.
> >
> > **(c) Differentials of linear maps.** Let $\beta' : E' \to \mathbb{R}^{n'}$ be a linear isomorphism defining the smooth structure of $E'$. The coordinate representative of $L$ is $\beta' \circ L \circ \beta^{-1} : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^{n'}$, a linear map between Euclidean spaces, hence smooth; so $L$ is smooth. Now fix $v \in E$. By Lemma 2 below (whose proof uses only the chain rule and does not depend on this lemma),
> > $$dL_p(\iota_p(v)) = dL_p(\sigma_v'(0)) = (L \circ \sigma_v)'(0) \qquad \text{(Lemma 2)}.$$
> > But $L \circ \sigma_v(t) = L(p + tv) = L(p) + t\,L(v)$ (linearity of $L$), which is the affine line through $L(p)$ in direction $L(v)$; by definition of $\iota_{L(p)}$ its velocity at $0$ is $\iota_{L(p)}(L(v))$. Hence
> > $$dL_p(\iota_p(v)) = \iota_{L(p)}(L(v)) \qquad \text{(definition of } \iota_{L(p)}\text{)},$$
> > which is the identity $dL_p \circ \iota_p = \iota_{L(p)} \circ L$. Therefore, under the identifications $\iota$, the differential of a linear map at any point is the map itself. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The velocity of an image curve is the differential applied to the velocity
> **Statement:** Let $F : M \to N$ be a smooth map between smooth manifolds, $J \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ an open interval, $\sigma : J \to M$ a smooth curve, and $t_0 \in J$. Then $F \circ \sigma$ is a smooth curve in $N$ and
> $$(F \circ \sigma)'(t_0) = dF_{\sigma(t_0)}\big(\sigma'(t_0)\big).$$
>
> **Hint:** Unfold the definition of velocity as $d\sigma_{t_0}(d/dt|_{t_0})$ and apply the chain rule to $F \circ \sigma$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the device that reduces the computation of $d_0\exp$ on a vector $X$ to the computation of the velocity of the one-parameter subgroup $s \mapsto \exp(sX)$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We assume $F$, $\sigma$, $t_0$ as in the statement; we need to show the displayed identity. The composite $F \circ \sigma : J \to N$ is smooth as a composite of smooth maps ([[Def - Smooth Map between Manifolds]]). By the definition of the velocity of a curve ([[Def - Velocity of a Curve]]), $(F \circ \sigma)'(t_0) = d(F \circ \sigma)_{t_0}\big(\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t_0}\big)$. By the [[Thm - Chain Rule for the Differential|chain rule for the differential]] — for smooth maps $F : M \to N$ and $\sigma : J \to M$ one has $d(F \circ \sigma)_{t_0} = dF_{\sigma(t_0)} \circ d\sigma_{t_0}$ —
> > $$(F \circ \sigma)'(t_0) = d(F \circ \sigma)_{t_0}\Big(\tfrac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t_0}\Big) = dF_{\sigma(t_0)}\Big(d\sigma_{t_0}\Big(\tfrac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t_0}\Big)\Big) = dF_{\sigma(t_0)}\big(\sigma'(t_0)\big) \qquad \text{(chain rule; then the definition of } \sigma'(t_0)\text{)}.$$
> > Therefore the velocity of the image curve is the differential applied to the velocity. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The differential of $\exp$ at the origin is the identity (Bär, Lemma 1.4.4)
> **Statement:** For every $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, $d\exp_0(\iota_0(X)) = X$; that is, $d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$ under the identification $\iota_0 : \mathfrak{g} \to T_0\mathfrak{g}$ of Lemma 1.
>
> **Hint:** $\iota_0(X)$ is the velocity of $s \mapsto sX$; push this curve forward by $\exp$ and use $\exp(sX) = \gamma_X(s)$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is part (i) of the theorem and the invertibility hypothesis for the rank theorem in part (ii); it is also used to cancel the vertical arrows in part (iii).
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We assume $X \in \mathfrak{g}$; we need to show $d\exp_0(\iota_0(X)) = X$.
> >
> > **Represent $\iota_0(X)$ as a velocity.** Let $\sigma_X : \mathbb{R} \to \mathfrak{g}$, $\sigma_X(s) := sX = 0 + sX$. By the definition of $\iota_0$ (Lemma 1 with $p = 0$, $v = X$), $\sigma_X'(0) = \iota_0(X)$.
> >
> > **Push forward by $\exp$.** The exponential map is smooth ([[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]], which proves smoothness of $\exp$ from the smooth dependence of flows on initial data). By Lemma 2 applied to $F = \exp$, $\sigma = \sigma_X$, $t_0 = 0$,
> > $$d\exp_0(\iota_0(X)) = d\exp_0(\sigma_X'(0)) = (\exp \circ \sigma_X)'(0) \qquad \text{(Lemma 2)}.$$
> >
> > **Identify the image curve.** For every $s \in \mathbb{R}$, $(\exp \circ \sigma_X)(s) = \exp(sX) = \gamma_X(s)$ by the identity $\gamma_X(t) = \exp(tX)$ proved on [[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]] (Bär's equation (1.6): the curve $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ is the integral curve $\gamma_X$ of the left-invariant field $X^L$ with $\gamma_X(0) = e$). Hence $\exp \circ \sigma_X = \gamma_X$ as curves $\mathbb{R} \to G$, and
> > $$(\exp \circ \sigma_X)'(0) = \gamma_X'(0) \qquad \text{(equal curves have equal velocities)}.$$
> >
> > **Evaluate the velocity of the integral curve.** By definition, $\gamma_X$ is an integral curve of $X^L$: $\gamma_X'(t) = X^L(\gamma_X(t))$ for all $t$ ([[Def - Integral Curve of a Vector Field]]). At $t = 0$,
> > $$\gamma_X'(0) = X^L(\gamma_X(0)) = X^L(e) = X \qquad \text{(integral curve condition; } \gamma_X(0) = e\text{; } X^L \text{ is the left-invariant field with } X^L(e) = X\text{)}.$$
> >
> > **Conclude.** Combining the three displayed lines, $d\exp_0(\iota_0(X)) = X$ for every $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, which says precisely that $d\exp_0 \circ \iota_0 = \operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$. Therefore $d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$ under the canonical identification. In particular $d\exp_0 : T_0\mathfrak{g} \to T_eG$ is a linear isomorphism, being the composite of the isomorphism $\iota_0^{-1}$ with the identity. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 4: Full rank at a point is full rank on a neighbourhood
> **Statement:** Let $M$ and $N$ be smooth manifolds of the same dimension $n$, $F : M \to N$ smooth, and $p \in M$ a point at which $dF_p : T_pM \to T_{F(p)}N$ is bijective. Then there is an open neighbourhood $W_1$ of $p$ in $M$ such that $dF_q$ is bijective — equivalently, $\operatorname{rank} dF_q = n$ — for every $q \in W_1$. In particular $F$ has constant rank $n$ on a neighbourhood of $p$.
>
> **Hint:** The Jacobian determinant of the coordinate representative is continuous and nonzero at $\varphi(p)$.
>
> **Why needed:** The rank theorem in the vault requires constant rank on a neighbourhood, not merely at a point; this lemma supplies that hypothesis from part (i).
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We assume the hypotheses of the statement; we need to produce $W_1$.
> >
> > **Step 0 — charts.** Choose a smooth chart $(W', \psi)$ of $N$ around $F(p)$ and a smooth chart $(W, \varphi)$ of $M$ around $p$; replacing $W$ by $W \cap F^{-1}(W')$ — which is open because $F$ is continuous and contains $p$ — we may assume $F(W) \subseteq W'$. The coordinate representative $\hat F := \psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1} : \varphi(W) \to \psi(W') \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is smooth (definition of a smooth map between manifolds).
> >
> > **Step 1 — the rank of $dF_q$ is the rank of a Jacobian.** For $q \in W$, by the coordinate picture of the differential ([[Def - The Differential of a Smooth Map]]), the matrix of $dF_q$ with respect to the coordinate bases $\{\partial/\partial x^i|_q\}$ of $T_qM$ and $\{\partial/\partial y^j|_{F(q)}\}$ of $T_{F(q)}N$ is the Jacobian matrix $D\hat F(\varphi(q)) = \big(\partial \hat F^j/\partial x^i(\varphi(q))\big)_{j,i}$. Since the coordinate vectors form bases of the respective tangent spaces ([[Thm - Dimension of the Tangent Space]]), $dF_q$ is bijective if and only if the $n \times n$ matrix $D\hat F(\varphi(q))$ is invertible, if and only if $\det D\hat F(\varphi(q)) \neq 0$ (a square matrix is invertible if and only if its determinant is nonzero).
> >
> > **Step 2 — the determinant is continuous.** Define $\delta : W \to \mathbb{R}$, $\delta(q) := \det D\hat F(\varphi(q))$. Each entry $\partial \hat F^j/\partial x^i$ is a continuous function on $\varphi(W)$ because $\hat F$ is smooth; the determinant is a polynomial in the entries, hence continuous; and $\varphi$ is continuous. So $\delta$ is a composite of continuous maps and is continuous.
> >
> > **Step 3 — an open neighbourhood.** By hypothesis $dF_p$ is bijective, so by Step 1, $\delta(p) \neq 0$. Put $W_1 := \delta^{-1}(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}) = \{q \in W : \delta(q) \neq 0\}$. This set is open in $W$ (preimage of the open set $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ under the continuous map $\delta$), hence open in $M$ (as $W$ is open in $M$), and it contains $p$. For every $q \in W_1$, $\delta(q) \neq 0$, so by Step 1, $dF_q$ is bijective and $\operatorname{rank} dF_q = n$.
> >
> > Therefore $F$ has constant rank $n$ on the open neighbourhood $W_1$ of $p$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 5: The identity normal form yields a diffeomorphism onto an open set
> **Statement:** Let $M$ and $N$ be smooth manifolds of the same dimension $n$, $F : M \to N$ smooth, $p \in M$. Suppose there are smooth charts $(U, \varphi)$ of $M$ centred at $p$ and $(V_0, \psi)$ of $N$ centred at $F(p)$ with $F(U) \subseteq V_0$ and
> $$\psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}(x) = x \qquad \text{for all } x \in \varphi(U).$$
> Then $V := F(U)$ is open in $N$, and $F|_U : U \to V$ is a diffeomorphism with inverse $\varphi^{-1} \circ \psi|_V$.
>
> **Hint:** Solve the displayed identity for $F$: on $U$, $F = \psi^{-1} \circ \varphi$.
>
> **Why needed:** The rank theorem delivers the normal form; this lemma converts the normal form into the statement actually claimed in part (ii).
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We assume the hypotheses; we need to show that $F(U)$ is open and that $F|_U$ is a diffeomorphism onto it. Throughout we use that a smooth chart $\varphi : U \to \varphi(U)$ is a diffeomorphism from the open subset $U \subseteq M$ onto the open subset $\varphi(U) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$: its coordinate representative with respect to itself is the identity, so $\varphi$ and $\varphi^{-1}$ are smooth ([[Def - Smooth Map between Manifolds]]); the same holds for $\psi : V_0 \to \psi(V_0)$.
> >
> > **Step 1 — $\varphi(U) \subseteq \psi(V_0)$.** Let $x \in \varphi(U)$. Then $\varphi^{-1}(x) \in U$, so $F(\varphi^{-1}(x)) \in F(U) \subseteq V_0$, and the normal form gives $x = \psi\big(F(\varphi^{-1}(x))\big) \in \psi(V_0)$. Hence $\varphi(U) \subseteq \psi(V_0)$.
> >
> > **Step 2 — $F|_U = \psi^{-1} \circ \varphi$.** Let $u \in U$ and put $x := \varphi(u) \in \varphi(U)$. The normal form with this $x$ reads $\psi(F(u)) = \psi(F(\varphi^{-1}(x))) = x$. Applying $\psi^{-1}$ (which is defined on $\psi(V_0) \ni x$ by Step 1),
> > $$F(u) = \psi^{-1}(x) = \psi^{-1}(\varphi(u)) \qquad \text{(apply } \psi^{-1} \text{ to } \psi(F(u)) = x\text{)}.$$
> > So $F|_U = \psi^{-1} \circ \varphi|_U$.
> >
> > **Step 3 — $V = F(U)$ is open.** By Step 2, $F(U) = \psi^{-1}(\varphi(U))$. The set $\varphi(U)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and contained in $\psi(V_0)$ (Step 1), hence open in $\psi(V_0)$. Since $\psi : V_0 \to \psi(V_0)$ is a homeomorphism, $\psi^{-1}(\varphi(U))$ is open in $V_0$, and since $V_0$ is open in $N$, it is open in $N$. So $V := F(U)$ is open in $N$.
> >
> > **Step 4 — $F|_U : U \to V$ is bijective with inverse $\varphi^{-1} \circ \psi|_V$.** By Step 2, $F|_U$ is the composite of the bijection $\varphi : U \to \varphi(U)$ and the bijection $\psi^{-1}|_{\varphi(U)} : \varphi(U) \to \psi^{-1}(\varphi(U)) = V$; a composite of bijections is a bijection, with inverse the composite of the inverses in reverse order, namely $\varphi^{-1} \circ \psi|_V : V \to U$.
> >
> > **Step 5 — smoothness of both maps.** $F|_U$ is smooth because the restriction of a smooth map to an open subset is smooth. The inverse $\varphi^{-1} \circ \psi|_V$ is smooth because $\psi|_V : V \to \varphi(U)$ is smooth (restriction of the smooth map $\psi$ to the open subset $V \subseteq V_0$, with image inside the open set $\varphi(U)$ by Step 3) and $\varphi^{-1} : \varphi(U) \to U$ is smooth, and composites of smooth maps are smooth.
> >
> > Therefore $F|_U : U \to V$ is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse, that is, a diffeomorphism onto the open set $V = F(U)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 6: Inversion and negation are intertwined by $\exp$
> **Statement:** $\operatorname{inv} \circ \exp = \exp \circ (-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}})$ as maps $\mathfrak{g} \to G$; that is, $(\exp X)^{-1} = \exp(-X)$ for every $X \in \mathfrak{g}$.
>
> **Hint:** This is the identity $\exp(-X) = \exp(X)^{-1}$ from the preceding page, read as a statement about maps.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the commuting square whose differentiation gives part (iii).
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We need to show that for every $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, $\operatorname{inv}(\exp(X)) = \exp(-X)$. By [[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]], the curve $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ is a group homomorphism $(\mathbb{R}, +) \to G$, so $\exp(sX)\exp(tX) = \exp((s+t)X)$ for all $s, t \in \mathbb{R}$, and $\exp(0) = e$. Taking $s = 1$, $t = -1$,
> > $$\exp(X)\exp(-X) = \exp(0 \cdot X) = \exp(0) = e \qquad \text{(homomorphism property; } 0 \cdot X = 0 \in \mathfrak{g}\text{)},$$
> > and with $s = -1$, $t = 1$, $\exp(-X)\exp(X) = e$ likewise. Hence $\exp(-X)$ is a two-sided inverse of $\exp(X)$ in the group $G$; by uniqueness of inverses in a group, $\exp(-X) = (\exp X)^{-1} = \operatorname{inv}(\exp X)$. Since $(-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}})(X) = -X$, this says $(\operatorname{inv} \circ \exp)(X) = (\exp \circ (-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}))(X)$ for every $X$. Therefore the two maps coincide. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 7: On an abelian Lie group, inversion is a Lie group homomorphism
> **Statement:** If $G$ is an abelian Lie group, then $\operatorname{inv} : G \to G$ is a Lie group homomorphism, that is, a smooth map with $\operatorname{inv}(gh) = \operatorname{inv}(g)\operatorname{inv}(h)$ for all $g, h \in G$.
>
> **Hint:** $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1}$ always; commutativity lets the factors be swapped.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the hypothesis under which the bracket-preservation theorem applies to $\operatorname{inv}$, giving Corollary A.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We assume $G$ is abelian, so $ab = ba$ for all $a, b \in G$; we need to show $\operatorname{inv}$ is smooth and multiplicative. Smoothness of $\operatorname{inv}$ is part of the definition of a Lie group ([[Def - Lie Group]]). For multiplicativity, let $g, h \in G$. In any group, $(gh)(h^{-1}g^{-1}) = g(hh^{-1})g^{-1} = gg^{-1} = e$ (associativity, then the inverse axiom twice), and likewise $(h^{-1}g^{-1})(gh) = e$, so $(gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1}$ by uniqueness of inverses. Then
> > $$\operatorname{inv}(gh) = (gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1} = g^{-1}h^{-1} = \operatorname{inv}(g)\operatorname{inv}(h) \qquad \text{(inverse of a product; } G \text{ abelian, applied to } a = h^{-1}, b = g^{-1}\text{; definition of } \operatorname{inv}\text{)}.$$
> > Therefore $\operatorname{inv}$ is a smooth homomorphism $G \to G$, that is, a [[Def - Lie Group Homomorphism|Lie group homomorphism]]. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 8: A subgroup containing a neighbourhood of the identity is open and closed
> **Statement:** Let $G$ be a Lie group and $H \subseteq G$ a subgroup that contains an open subset $V \subseteq G$ with $e \in V$. Then $H$ is open in $G$ and closed in $G$. Consequently, if $G$ is connected, $H = G$.
>
> **Hint:** $hV = L_h(V)$ is an open neighbourhood of $h$ inside $H$; the complement of $H$ is the union of the cosets $gH$ with $g \notin H$, each of which is open.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the open-subgroup argument that proves Corollary B(b) once part (ii) has supplied the neighbourhood $V = \exp(U)$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We assume $H \leq G$ is a subgroup and $V \subseteq H$ is open in $G$ with $e \in V$; we need to show that $H$ is open, that $H$ is closed, and that $H = G$ when $G$ is connected.
> >
> > **Step 0 — left translations are homeomorphisms.** For $a \in G$ the map $L_a : G \to G$, $L_a(x) = ax$, is smooth (it is the composite of $x \mapsto (a, x)$, smooth, with the smooth multiplication $\mu$), and $L_{a^{-1}} \circ L_a = \operatorname{id}_G = L_a \circ L_{a^{-1}}$ (associativity and the inverse axiom). Hence $L_a$ is a diffeomorphism, in particular a homeomorphism, and it maps open sets to open sets ([[Def - Left and Right Translations and Conjugation on a Lie Group]]).
> >
> > **Step 1 — $H$ is open.** Let $h \in H$. The set $hV := L_h(V)$ is open in $G$ by Step 0, contains $h = he$ since $e \in V$, and is contained in $H$: every element of $hV$ is $hv$ with $v \in V \subseteq H$, and $H$ is closed under multiplication. So every point of $H$ has an open neighbourhood contained in $H$, that is, $H$ is open.
> >
> > **Step 2 — $H$ is closed.** We show $G \setminus H$ is open. Let $g \in G \setminus H$. The coset $gH = L_g(H)$ is open by Step 1 and Step 0, and contains $g = ge$ since $e \in H$. We claim $gH \cap H = \emptyset$: if some $h \in H$ satisfied $gh = h'$ with $h' \in H$, then $g = h'h^{-1} \in H$ (subgroup closed under inverses and products), contradicting $g \notin H$. So $gH$ is an open neighbourhood of $g$ contained in $G \setminus H$. Hence $G \setminus H$ is open, and $H$ is closed.
> >
> > **Step 3 — connected case.** Suppose $G$ is [[Def - Connected Space|connected]]: the only subsets of $G$ that are both open and closed are $\emptyset$ and $G$. By Steps 1 and 2, $H$ is both open and closed, and $H \neq \emptyset$ since $e \in H$. Therefore $H = G$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 9: A diffeomorphism onto an open subset of Euclidean space is a chart
> **Statement:** Let $M$ be a smooth $n$-manifold, $W \subseteq M$ open, and $\chi : W \to \chi(W) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ a diffeomorphism onto an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then $(W, \chi)$ belongs to the maximal smooth atlas of $M$.
>
> **Hint:** Compatibility with a chart $(W', \theta)$ means that $\theta \circ \chi^{-1}$ and $\chi \circ \theta^{-1}$ are smooth on their (open) domains; both are composites of smooth maps.
>
> **Why needed:** It is what makes $(\exp|_U)^{-1}$, composed with a linear isomorphism, a legitimate chart of $G$ in Corollary B(a).
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We need to show that $(W, \chi)$ is smoothly compatible with every chart $(W', \theta)$ of the maximal atlas of $M$; maximality then forces $(W, \chi)$ to belong to the atlas. Let $(W', \theta)$ be such a chart with $W \cap W' \neq \emptyset$ (if the intersection is empty there is nothing to check). The transition map $\theta \circ \chi^{-1} : \chi(W \cap W') \to \theta(W \cap W')$ has open domain, because $\chi$ is a homeomorphism onto the open set $\chi(W)$ and $W \cap W'$ is open in $W$. It is smooth because $\chi^{-1} : \chi(W) \to W$ is a smooth map of manifolds (hypothesis: $\chi$ is a diffeomorphism) and, by the definition of smoothness of a map into $M$ ([[Def - Smooth Map between Manifolds]]), its composite with the chart $\theta$ on the open set $\chi^{-1}(W \cap W')$ is a smooth map into $\mathbb{R}^n$. The reverse transition map $\chi \circ \theta^{-1} : \theta(W \cap W') \to \chi(W \cap W')$ has open domain for the same reason applied to $\theta$, and is smooth because $\theta^{-1}$ is smooth on $\theta(W \cap W')$ (a chart is a diffeomorphism onto its image) and $\chi$ is smooth, so the composite is a smooth map from an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ into $\mathbb{R}^n$. Hence $(W, \chi)$ is smoothly compatible with every chart of the atlas, and by maximality of the atlas $(W, \chi)$ is one of its charts. $\blacksquare$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $G$ be a Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_eG$, $n := \dim G$, and let $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ be its exponential map. We must prove parts (i), (ii), (iii) of the theorem, then Corollary A, then Corollary B.
>
> **Step 0 — the objects are well defined.** The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is a real vector space of dimension $n$ ([[Thm - Dimension of the Tangent Space]]: $\dim T_eG = \dim G$), regarded as a smooth manifold as in the Notation section, so $\dim \mathfrak{g} = \dim G = n$. The exponential map is defined and smooth, and satisfies $\exp(tX) = \gamma_X(t)$, $\exp(0) = e$, and $\exp(-X) = \exp(X)^{-1}$, by [[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]]. The tangent space $T_0\mathfrak{g}$ is identified with $\mathfrak{g}$ through the linear isomorphism $\iota_0$ of Lemma 1, and $T_eG = \mathfrak{g}$ by definition. The inversion map $\operatorname{inv} : G \to G$ is smooth by the definition of a Lie group, and fixes $e$ (since $e^{-1} = e$), so $d\operatorname{inv}_e$ is a linear map $T_eG \to T_eG$, that is, $\mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$.
>
> **Part I — $d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$.** We need to show $d\exp_0(\iota_0(X)) = X$ for all $X \in \mathfrak{g}$. This is Lemma 3. In particular $d\exp_0 : T_0\mathfrak{g} \to T_eG$ is a linear isomorphism (it equals $\iota_0^{-1}$ followed by the identity of $\mathfrak{g}$).
>
> **Part II — $\exp$ is a diffeomorphism from a neighbourhood of $0$ onto a neighbourhood of $e$.** We need to find open sets $U \ni 0$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ and $V \ni e$ in $G$ with $\exp|_U : U \to V$ a diffeomorphism.
>
> *Apply the open-condition lemma.* By Part I, $d\exp_0$ is bijective, and $\dim \mathfrak{g} = \dim G = n$ (Step 0). By Lemma 4 applied to $F = \exp$, $M = \mathfrak{g}$, $N = G$, $p = 0$, there is an open neighbourhood $W_1$ of $0$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ on which $\exp$ has constant rank $n$.
>
> *Apply the rank theorem.* We invoke the [[Thm - The Rank Theorem|rank theorem]]: *if $F : M \to N$ is a smooth map between manifolds of dimensions $m$ and $n$ with constant rank $r$ on a neighbourhood of $p \in M$, then there are smooth charts $(U, \varphi)$ of $M$ centred at $p$ and $(V_0, \psi)$ of $N$ centred at $F(p)$ with $F(U) \subseteq V_0$ such that $\psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1}(x^1, \dots, x^m) = (x^1, \dots, x^r, 0, \dots, 0)$ on $\varphi(U)$.* Its hypotheses hold for $F = \exp$, $p = 0$, $m = n = r$ (constant rank $n$ on $W_1$, by the previous paragraph). Hence there are smooth charts $(U, \varphi)$ of $\mathfrak{g}$ centred at $0$ and $(V_0, \psi)$ of $G$ centred at $\exp(0) = e$, with $\exp(U) \subseteq V_0$, such that
> $$\psi \circ \exp \circ \varphi^{-1}(x) = x \qquad \text{for all } x \in \varphi(U) \qquad \text{(rank theorem with } r = m = n\text{: the normal form } (x^1, \dots, x^n) \mapsto (x^1, \dots, x^n)\text{)}.$$
>
> *Convert the normal form into a diffeomorphism.* By Lemma 5 applied to $F = \exp$ and these charts, the set $V := \exp(U)$ is open in $G$ and $\exp|_U : U \to V$ is a diffeomorphism. The set $U$ is an open neighbourhood of $0$ (it is the domain of a chart centred at $0$), and $V$ contains $\exp(0) = e$. This proves part (ii).
>
> **Part III — $d_e\operatorname{inv} = -\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$.** We need to show $d\operatorname{inv}_e(X) = -X$ for all $X \in \mathfrak{g} = T_eG$.
>
> *Write down the commuting square.* By Lemma 6,
> $$\operatorname{inv} \circ \exp = \exp \circ (-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}) \qquad \text{as maps } \mathfrak{g} \to G \qquad \text{(Lemma 6: } (\exp X)^{-1} = \exp(-X)\text{)}.$$
> Both sides are smooth: $\exp$ is smooth (Step 0), $\operatorname{inv}$ is smooth (Step 0), and $-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$ is smooth as a linear map (Lemma 1(c)).
>
> *Differentiate the left-hand side at $0$.* By the [[Thm - Chain Rule for the Differential|chain rule for the differential]] — $d(F \circ H)_q = dF_{H(q)} \circ dH_q$ for smooth $H : M \to N$, $F : N \to P$ and $q \in M$ — applied with $H = \exp$, $F = \operatorname{inv}$, $q = 0$, and using $\exp(0) = e$,
> $$d(\operatorname{inv} \circ \exp)_0 = d\operatorname{inv}_{\exp(0)} \circ d\exp_0 = d\operatorname{inv}_e \circ d\exp_0 \qquad \text{(chain rule; } \exp(0) = e\text{)}.$$
>
> *Differentiate the right-hand side at $0$.* By the chain rule with $H = -\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$, $F = \exp$, $q = 0$, and $(-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}})(0) = 0$,
> $$d(\exp \circ (-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}))_0 = d\exp_{0} \circ d(-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}})_0 \qquad \text{(chain rule; } (-\operatorname{id})(0) = 0\text{)}.$$
>
> *Evaluate on $\iota_0(X)$ and cancel.* Since the two maps in the commuting square are equal, so are their differentials at $0$, and applying them to $\iota_0(X)$ for an arbitrary $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ gives
> $$d\operatorname{inv}_e\big(d\exp_0(\iota_0(X))\big) = d\exp_0\big(d(-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}})_0(\iota_0(X))\big) \qquad \text{(equal maps have equal differentials; the two displayed chain-rule identities)}.$$
> On the left, $d\exp_0(\iota_0(X)) = X$ by Part I. On the right, $d(-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}})_0(\iota_0(X)) = \iota_0\big((-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}})(X)\big) = \iota_0(-X)$ by Lemma 1(c) (the differential of a linear map is the map itself under $\iota$; here $L = -\operatorname{id}$, $p = 0$, $L(p) = 0$), and then $d\exp_0(\iota_0(-X)) = -X$ by Part I. Substituting,
> $$d\operatorname{inv}_e(X) = -X \qquad \text{(Part I on the left; Lemma 1(c) and Part I on the right)}.$$
> Since $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ was arbitrary, $d_e\operatorname{inv} = -\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$. This proves part (iii). (Bär proves this by restricting the square to the neighbourhoods $U$, $V$ of part (ii) so that all four arrows are diffeomorphisms; as the computation shows, the square commutes on all of $\mathfrak{g}$ and the restriction is not needed for the differential at $0$.)
>
> **Part IV — Corollary A: if $G$ is abelian then $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}] = 0$.** Assume $G$ is abelian; we need to show $[X, Y] = 0$ for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$.
>
> *Inversion is a homomorphism.* By Lemma 7, $\operatorname{inv} : G \to G$ is a Lie group homomorphism.
>
> *Its differential preserves brackets.* We invoke [[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism]]: *if $F : G \to H$ is a Lie group homomorphism, then $F_* = dF_{e_G} : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{h}$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism, that is, linear with $F_*[X, Y] = [F_*X, F_*Y]$ for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$.* Applied to $F = \operatorname{inv}$ (with $H = G$), and using Part III to identify $\operatorname{inv}_* = d\operatorname{inv}_e = -\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$, we get for all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$
> $$-[X, Y] = \operatorname{inv}_*([X, Y]) = [\operatorname{inv}_*X, \operatorname{inv}_*Y] = [-X, -Y] \qquad \text{(Part III; bracket preservation; Part III again)}.$$
>
> *Solve for the bracket.* The bracket on $\mathfrak{g}$ is bilinear ([[Def - Lie Algebra]]), so $[-X, -Y] = (-1)(-1)[X, Y] = [X, Y]$. Combining with the previous display, $-[X, Y] = [X, Y]$, hence $2[X, Y] = 0$ (add $[X, Y]$ to both sides). Since $\mathfrak{g}$ is a real vector space, multiplying by the scalar $\tfrac12$ gives $[X, Y] = 0$.
>
> Therefore the bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$ vanishes identically: the Lie algebra of an abelian Lie group is abelian. This proves Corollary A.
>
> **Part V — Corollary B.** Let $U$, $V$ be as in Part II and let $\beta : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be a linear isomorphism.
>
> *(a) Exponential coordinates.* The map $\chi := \beta \circ (\exp|_U)^{-1} : V \to \beta(U)$ is a composite of the diffeomorphism $(\exp|_U)^{-1} : V \to U$ (Part II) and the diffeomorphism $\beta|_U : U \to \beta(U)$ (a linear isomorphism is a diffeomorphism by Lemma 1(c) applied to $\beta$ and $\beta^{-1}$, and its restriction to the open set $U$ is a diffeomorphism onto the open set $\beta(U)$, open because $\beta$ is a homeomorphism). Hence $\chi$ is a diffeomorphism from the open subset $V \subseteq G$ onto the open subset $\beta(U) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, and by Lemma 9 $(V, \chi)$ is a smooth chart of $G$. It is centred at $e$: $\chi(e) = \beta((\exp|_U)^{-1}(e)) = \beta(0) = 0$, because $\exp(0) = e$ and $0 \in U$, so $(\exp|_U)^{-1}(e) = 0$. For $g \in G$, the left translation $L_{g^{-1}} : G \to G$ is a diffeomorphism (Lemma 8, Step 0) and maps $gV$ onto $V$ (as $L_{g^{-1}}(gv) = v$ and $L_g$ is its inverse), so $gV = L_g(V)$ is open and $\chi \circ L_{g^{-1}}|_{gV} : gV \to \beta(U)$ is a diffeomorphism onto an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$; by Lemma 9 it is a smooth chart of $G$. It is centred at $g$: $\chi(L_{g^{-1}}(g)) = \chi(e) = 0$. This proves (a).
>
> *(b) Connected groups are generated by exponentials.* Assume $G$ is connected. Define
> $$H := \{\exp(X_1)\exp(X_2)\cdots\exp(X_k) : k \geq 1,\ X_1, \dots, X_k \in \mathfrak{g}\} \subseteq G.$$
> We check that $H$ is a subgroup. It contains $e = \exp(0)$ ($k = 1$, $X_1 = 0$; Step 0). It is closed under multiplication: the product of $\exp(X_1)\cdots\exp(X_k)$ and $\exp(Y_1)\cdots\exp(Y_l)$ is $\exp(X_1)\cdots\exp(X_k)\exp(Y_1)\cdots\exp(Y_l)$, again a finite product of exponentials. It is closed under inverses: by the rule $(ab)^{-1} = b^{-1}a^{-1}$ applied $k - 1$ times and by Lemma 6,
> $$\big(\exp(X_1)\cdots\exp(X_k)\big)^{-1} = \exp(X_k)^{-1}\cdots\exp(X_1)^{-1} = \exp(-X_k)\cdots\exp(-X_1) \in H \qquad \text{(inverse of a product; Lemma 6)}.$$
> So $H$ is a subgroup of $G$. It contains the set $V = \exp(U)$ of Part II (each $\exp(X)$ with $X \in U$ is a product with $k = 1$), and $V$ is open in $G$ with $e \in V$ (Part II). By Lemma 8, $H$ is open and closed in $G$; since $G$ is connected, $H = G$. Hence every $g \in G$ is a finite product $\exp(X_1)\cdots\exp(X_k)$. This proves (b).
>
> Therefore: the differential of $\exp$ at the origin is the identity of $\mathfrak{g}$; $\exp$ restricts to a diffeomorphism from an open neighbourhood of $0$ onto an open neighbourhood of $e$; the differential of inversion at $e$ is $-\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$; an abelian Lie group has an abelian Lie algebra; exponential coordinates are smooth charts centred at every point of $G$; and a connected Lie group is generated by the image of its exponential map. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Matrix analysis — the matrix logarithm without series.** For $G = GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ the exponential map is $X \mapsto e^X = \sum_{k \geq 0} X^k/k!$ ([[Ex - The Exponential Map of GL(n,R) is the Matrix Exponential]]). The theorem says that there is a neighbourhood of $0$ in $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R})$ on which $X \mapsto e^X$ is injective with smooth inverse defined on a neighbourhood of $1_n$; this inverse is the matrix logarithm. The exercise is to derive from part (ii) alone — without the logarithm series $\log(1 + A) = \sum_{k \geq 1} (-1)^{k+1}A^k/k$ — that every invertible matrix sufficiently close to $1_n$ has a unique small logarithm, and then to check by differentiating $e^{\log A} = A$ that the derivative of $\log$ at $1_n$ is the identity, consistent with part (i). The application is non-obvious because the natural instinct is to prove convergence of the logarithm series and compose; the theorem makes that unnecessary.

**Riemannian geometry — normal coordinates as the same argument in a different setting.** The [[Def - The Riemannian Exponential Map|Riemannian exponential map]] $\exp_p : T_pM \to M$ of a Riemannian manifold sends $v$ to $\gamma_v(1)$, where $\gamma_v$ is the geodesic with initial velocity $v$; exactly as here, its differential at $0 \in T_pM$ is the identity because $\exp_p(tv) = \gamma_v(t)$, and so it is a local diffeomorphism — [[Ex - The Exponential Map on a Sphere is a Local Diffeomorphism]] carries this out for the round sphere. The exercise is to write both proofs side by side and to isolate the single fact both use: that the map is defined by evaluating at time $1$ a family of curves that is homogeneous under rescaling of time. For a Lie group with a bi-invariant metric the two exponential maps coincide, which is the route to surjectivity of $\exp$ on compact connected groups ([[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Compact Connected Lie Group is Surjective]]).

**Dynamical systems — linearising a flow at a fixed point of the group parameter.** Let $Y$ be a complete vector field on a manifold $M$ with flow $\phi : \mathbb{R} \times M \to M$. For fixed $x \in M$ the curve $t \mapsto \phi_t(x)$ has velocity $Y(x)$ at $t = 0$; this is the statement $\gamma_X'(0) = X$ of Lemma 3 in the situation where "the group" is the one-parameter group $\{\phi_t\}$. The exercise is to show that if the flow of $Y$ factors through a finite-dimensional Lie group action, $\phi_t = \theta(\exp(tX))$ for an action $\theta$ of $G$ on $M$ and some $X \in \mathfrak{g}$, then $Y$ is the fundamental vector field of $X$ — see [[Def - Fundamental Vector Field of a Group Action]] — and that consequently the differential at $e$ of $g \mapsto \theta(g)(x)$ sends $X$ to $Y(x)$. The non-obvious content is that the identity $d_0\exp = \operatorname{id}$ is what allows one to read off a tangent vector on $M$ from a tangent vector on $G$ without any further computation.

**Quantum mechanics — unitaries near the identity are exponentials of skew-adjoint operators.** In a finite-dimensional Hilbert space $\mathbb{C}^N$, the unitary group $U(N)$ is a compact Lie group with Lie algebra the skew-Hermitian matrices $\{A : A^* = -A\}$, and $\exp(A) = e^A$. Part (ii) says that every unitary matrix sufficiently close to $1_N$ is $e^{-iHt}$ for a unique small Hermitian $H$ — the Hamiltonian of the evolution — and that $H$ depends smoothly on the unitary. The exercise is to prove this from part (ii) together with [[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential]], and then to compute, using part (iii), that the time-reversal $e^{-iHt} \mapsto e^{iHt}$ has differential $-\operatorname{id}$ on the Lie algebra. This is non-obvious as an application because physics texts usually assert the existence of $H$ by diagonalising the unitary; the theorem gives smooth dependence on parameters for free.

**Topology — connected Lie groups and the identity component.** Corollary B(b) says that a connected Lie group is generated by any neighbourhood of the identity. The exercise is to use this to show that a Lie group homomorphism $F : G \to H$ from a connected $G$ is determined by $F_* = d_eF$ (via [[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map]]: $F(\exp X) = \exp(F_*X)$), and then to deduce that a connected Lie group whose Lie algebra is abelian has abelian identity component — the converse to Corollary A — assuming the fact that $\exp(X)$ and $\exp(Y)$ commute when $[X, Y] = 0$, which requires an argument about commuting flows not carried out on this page.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields|One-parameter subgroups and the definition of $\exp$]]** — the input. That page constructs $\exp$, proves it is smooth, and proves the identities $\exp(tX) = \gamma_X(t)$ and $\exp(-X) = \exp(X)^{-1}$; the present page differentiates those identities. The order of the two pages reflects the order of ideas: first the curves, then the map, then the local structure of the map.

- **[[Thm - The Rank Theorem|The rank theorem and the inverse function theorem]]** — the engine of part (ii). The rank theorem, in the case $r = m = n$, is the inverse function theorem on manifolds: a smooth map with invertible differential at a point is a diffeomorphism near that point. The vault proves the rank theorem from the Euclidean [[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]], and Lemmas 4 and 5 above are the bookkeeping that adapts the vault's constant-rank formulation to the pointwise hypothesis available here.

- **[[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism|Differentials of homomorphisms preserve brackets]]** — the input for Corollary A. The construction there is that a homomorphism $F$ carries the left-invariant field $X^L$ to the left-invariant field $(F_*X)^L$ (because $F \circ L_g = L_{F(g)} \circ F$), and that related vector fields have related brackets. Applied to $F = \operatorname{inv}$ on an abelian group, with $\operatorname{inv}_* = -\operatorname{id}$ from part (iii), it forces $[X, Y] = 0$.

- **[[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map|Naturality of $\exp$]]** — the companion identity $F \circ \exp_G = \exp_H \circ F_*$. Together with part (ii) it shows that a homomorphism with bijective differential is a local diffeomorphism, and together with Corollary B(b) it shows that homomorphisms out of connected groups are determined by their differentials. Naturality is proved by the same "both sides are one-parameter subgroups with the same initial velocity" argument that underlies Lemma 3.

- **[[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad|The differential of $\operatorname{Ad}$ is $\operatorname{ad}$]]** — the next differentiation at the identity. There, the curve $t \mapsto \operatorname{Ad}_{\exp(tX)}Y$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ is differentiated at $t = 0$ to produce $[X, Y]$; the computation is legitimate because $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$ has velocity $X$ at $t = 0$, which is part (i) of this page. Corollary A can be re-derived from that page: on an abelian group $\operatorname{Ad}_g = \operatorname{id}$ for every $g$, so its derivative $\operatorname{ad}_X = [X, \cdot]$ vanishes.

- **[[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem|The closed subgroup theorem]]** — the principal consumer of exponential coordinates. Its proof shows that for a closed subgroup $H \subseteq G$ the set $\mathfrak{h} = \{X : \exp(tX) \in H \text{ for all } t\}$ is a subspace and that, in the chart $(\exp|_U)^{-1}$ of Corollary B(a) restricted to a product neighbourhood adapted to $\mathfrak{h} \oplus \mathfrak{h}'$, the subgroup $H$ becomes the slice $\mathfrak{h} \cap U$. The theorem is what makes the classical matrix groups of [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]] Lie groups.

- **[[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential|Matrix groups]]** — the concrete case. For a closed subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, part (i) is the statement $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} e^{tX} = X$, part (ii) is the existence of a smooth local logarithm, and part (iii) is $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}(e^{tX})^{-1} = \tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0} e^{-tX} = -X$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Exponential coordinates (canonical coordinates of the first kind) *(from Lie theory)*
> The chart $(\exp|_U)^{-1}$ of Corollary B(a), and its left translates, are the coordinates in which the multiplication of $G$ near $e$ is expressed by the **Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula** $\log(\exp X \exp Y) = X + Y + \tfrac12[X, Y] + \tfrac{1}{12}[X,[X,Y]] - \tfrac{1}{12}[Y,[X,Y]] + \cdots$; the formula is not proved in this series, and nothing here depends on it.

> [!tip] Continuous homomorphisms are smooth *(from Lie theory)*
> In exponential coordinates one proves that every continuous homomorphism between Lie groups is smooth, and hence that a topological group carries at most one Lie group structure compatible with its topology. The vault's [[Def - One-Parameter Subgroup]] page records the one-dimensional case; the general statement is **not proved and not used in this series**.

> [!tip] Homomorphisms out of connected groups are determined by their differentials *(from the Lie correspondence)*
> Corollary B(b) combined with [[Thm - Naturality of the Exponential Map]] gives: if $G$ is connected and $F_1, F_2 : G \to H$ are Lie group homomorphisms with $(F_1)_* = (F_2)_*$, then $F_1 = F_2$. This is used on [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] to pass between representations of $SU(2)$ and of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$.

> [!tip] Infinitesimal gauge transformations *(from chapter V)*
> On [[Def - Gauge Transformation]] and [[Thm - Gauge Transformations Act on Connections and Curvature]], a gauge transformation close to the identity is written as $\exp$ of a section of the adjoint bundle, using part (ii) fibrewise; differentiating the action on connections along $t \mapsto \exp(t\xi)$ produces the infinitesimal gauge action $A \mapsto d^A\xi$, and the computation of the velocity at $t = 0$ is part (i) applied in each fibre.
