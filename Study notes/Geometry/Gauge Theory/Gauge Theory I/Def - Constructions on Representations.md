# Notation

This page depends on the following standing conventions of the series. Lie groups are smooth, Hausdorff, second countable manifolds; "smooth" means $C^\infty$. Representations are **left** actions: a representation is a Lie group homomorphism $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, and $\varrho(g)$ acts on the left of $V$. The ground field is $\mathbb K \in \{\mathbb R, \mathbb C\}$; every vector space on this page is finite-dimensional.

This is a compound page: it defines six interlocking constructions — the direct sum $\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2$, the tensor product $\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2$, the exterior power $\Lambda^k \varrho$, the symmetric power $\odot^k \varrho$, the dual $\varrho^*$, and the complexification $\varrho_{\mathbb C}$ — because they are all instances of a single principle (a functorial construction on vector spaces transports representations) and because their verifications share the same three-step template (a well-defined linear map, a group homomorphism, smoothness through matrix entries).

Throughout, $G$ is a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] with neutral element $e \in G$, and $\operatorname{inv} : G \to G$, $g \mapsto g^{-1}$, is its inversion map, which is smooth by the definition of a Lie group. For a finite-dimensional $\mathbb K$-vector space $V$ we write $\operatorname{End}(V)$ for the $\mathbb K$-vector space of linear maps $V \to V$ and $\operatorname{Aut}(V) = GL(V) \subset \operatorname{End}(V)$ for the group of invertible ones; after a choice of basis $\mathcal B = (e_1, \dots, e_n)$ of $V$ these become $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb K)$ and $GL(n; \mathbb K)$, and $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ carries the Lie group structure of the open subset $GL(n; \mathbb K) \subset \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb K) = \mathbb K^{n^2}$ (see [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]]). For $T \in \operatorname{End}(V)$ we write $[T]_{\mathcal B} = (T_{ij})_{i,j=1}^n$ for its matrix in $\mathcal B$, defined by $T e_j = \sum_{i=1}^n T_{ij} e_i$. A **representation** of $G$ is a [[Def - Lie Group Homomorphism|Lie group homomorphism]] $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, that is, a group homomorphism that is also a smooth map ([[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]]); $\varrho$, $\varrho_1 : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V_1)$, and $\varrho_2 : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V_2)$ always denote representations of the *same* group $G$.

The linear-algebraic ingredients are as follows. $V_1 \oplus V_2 = V_1 \times V_2$ is the direct sum with componentwise operations; we write $v_1 \oplus v_2$ for the pair $(v_1, v_2)$. $V_1 \otimes V_2$ is the [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces|tensor product]], with elementary tensors $v_1 \otimes v_2$, characterised by the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|universal property]]: every bilinear map $\Gamma : V_1 \times V_2 \to U$ factors through a unique linear map $\hat\Gamma : V_1 \otimes V_2 \to U$ with $\hat\Gamma(v_1 \otimes v_2) = \Gamma(v_1, v_2)$; and if $(e_i)$ and $(f_j)$ are bases of $V_1$ and $V_2$ then $(e_i \otimes f_j)$ is a basis of $V_1 \otimes V_2$. The $k$-fold tensor power $V^{\otimes k}$ is defined recursively by $V^{\otimes 1} = V$ and $V^{\otimes k} = V^{\otimes (k-1)} \otimes V$, with elementary tensors $v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k := (v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_{k-1}) \otimes v_k$. $S_k$ is the [[Def - Symmetric Group|symmetric group]] on $\{1, \dots, k\}$, with composition read right to left, and $\operatorname{sgn} : S_k \to \{\pm 1\}$ is the sign homomorphism. For $\sigma \in S_k$ the **permutation operator** $P_\sigma : V^{\otimes k} \to V^{\otimes k}$ is the linear map with $P_\sigma(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)} \otimes \dots \otimes v_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}$, so that $P_\sigma$ moves the vector in slot $i$ to slot $\sigma(i)$. The **symmetrisation** and **alternation** operators are
$$\operatorname{Sym}_k = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} P_\sigma, \qquad \operatorname{Alt}_k = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) \, P_\sigma,$$
both linear maps $V^{\otimes k} \to V^{\otimes k}$; their images are the **symmetric tensors** $\odot^k V := \operatorname{Sym}_k(V^{\otimes k})$ and the **alternating tensors** $\Lambda^k V := \operatorname{Alt}_k(V^{\otimes k})$, and we write $v_1 \odot \dots \odot v_k := \operatorname{Sym}_k(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k)$ and $v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k := \operatorname{Alt}_k(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k)$. A **multi-index** of length $k$ is a tuple $J = (j_1, \dots, j_k) \in \{1, \dots, n\}^k$; we write $e_J := e_{j_1} \otimes \dots \otimes e_{j_k}$, call $J$ *increasing* if $j_1 < \dots < j_k$ and *nondecreasing* if $j_1 \le \dots \le j_k$, and for $\sigma \in S_k$ we write $J \circ \sigma := (j_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, j_{\sigma(k)})$. $V^* = \mathcal L(V, \mathbb K)$ is the [[Def - Dual Space|dual space]] (the linear algebra pages write $V'$), $\lambda, \mu$ denote its elements, and for $T \in \operatorname{End}(V)$ the [[Def - Dual Map|dual map]] $T^* \in \operatorname{End}(V^*)$ is $T^*(\lambda) = \lambda \circ T$ (the linear algebra pages write $T'$); the [[Def - Dual Basis|dual basis]] of $\mathcal B$ is $(\varepsilon^1, \dots, \varepsilon^n)$ with $\varepsilon^i(e_j) = \delta_{ij}$. For a *real* vector space $V$, $V_{\mathbb C} := V \otimes_{\mathbb R} \mathbb C$ is its complexification, where $\mathbb C$ is regarded as a real vector space of dimension two with basis $(1, i)$. The standard representation of a matrix group $G \subset GL(n; \mathbb K)$ is the inclusion $\varrho_{st} : G \hookrightarrow GL(n; \mathbb K) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb K^n)$, and $\det : GL(n; \mathbb K) \to GL(1; \mathbb K) = \mathbb K^\times$ is the [[Def - Determinant|determinant]].

> [!warning] Convention: the dual representation uses $g^{-1}$, and the symbol $\odot^k$
> Bär (Def. 1.3.11.5) and the whole series define the dual representation by $\varrho^*(g) := \varrho(g^{-1})^*$, never by $\varrho(g)^*$ — the latter reverses the order of multiplication and is not a homomorphism (Corollary B below). Bär's symbol $\odot^k V$ for the symmetric power is retained; other texts write $S^k V$, $\operatorname{Sym}^k V$, or $V^{\odot k}$. Bär does not say which model of $\Lambda^k V$ and $\odot^k V$ he uses; the series fixes the *subspace* model (images of $\operatorname{Alt}_k$ and $\operatorname{Sym}_k$ inside $V^{\otimes k}$, with the projector normalised by $1/k!$ exactly as on [[Thm - Symmetrization and Alternation Projectors]]), so that $v \wedge w = \tfrac12 (v \otimes w - w \otimes v)$ and $v \odot w = \tfrac12 (v \otimes w + w \otimes v)$. Texts that use $v \wedge w = v \otimes w - w \otimes v$, or the quotient model $V^{\otimes k} / \langle \dots \rangle$, differ from ours by a fixed linear isomorphism that commutes with every $\Lambda^k \varrho(g)$; the representations obtained are equivalent, and nothing on this page depends on the choice.

The full registry for the chapter is on [[Gauge Theory I — Lie Groups, Representations, and Group Actions]].

# Axiom Motivation

Having defined what a representation of a Lie group is, we now ask the question that decides whether representation theory has any internal structure at all: given one or two representations of a fixed group $G$, how do we manufacture new ones? Two motivations press on us at once. The first is internal to the subject. We shall see on [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] that every complex representation of $U(1)$ is a direct sum of the one-dimensional characters $z \mapsto z^k$, and that every complex representation of $SU(2)$ is a direct sum of the symmetric powers $\odot^k \varrho_{st}$ of the standard representation; without direct sums and symmetric powers those theorems cannot even be stated. The second motivation is the one that matters for the rest of the series. In chapter III a principal $G$-bundle $P$ together with a representation $\varrho$ produces an associated vector bundle $P \times_\varrho V$, and every operation we later want to perform on vector bundles — take a direct sum, a tensor product, an exterior power, a dual, a complexification, the determinant line bundle — must be performed on the representation first, so that the associated bundle of the constructed representation is the constructed bundle. Every construction on this page is therefore a construction we shall need on bundles, and the verifications here are the verifications we shall later be allowed to skip.

What must a construction achieve? Suppose we are given a functorial operation $F$ on finite-dimensional vector spaces — $F(V) = V \oplus W$, $V \otimes W$, $\Lambda^k V$, $V^*$, and so on — and a representation $\varrho$ on $V$. We want a representation on $F(V)$, and there is only one natural candidate: apply $F$ to each automorphism $\varrho(g)$. For this to be a representation three things must hold, and each is a genuine clause rather than a formality. First, $F(\varrho(g))$ must be a well-defined linear automorphism of $F(V)$; for the tensor product this is not automatic, because a formula prescribed on the elementary tensors $v_1 \otimes v_2$ does not by itself define anything on the whole space, and the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|universal property]] is precisely the tool that makes it define something. Second, $g \mapsto F(\varrho(g))$ must be a group homomorphism, which requires $F$ to preserve composition *in the right order*. Third, the map must be smooth, which requires $F$ to act on matrix entries by smooth functions. The definitions below are designed so that all three hold, and the corollaries verify them one by one.

Consider what breaks if the clauses are dropped or altered. Take the order of composition first. The dual map $T \mapsto T^*$ is contravariant: $(ST)^* = T^* S^*$ ([[Def - Dual Map]]). If we naively set $\varrho^\sharp(g) := \varrho(g)^*$, then $\varrho^\sharp(g_1 g_2) = \varrho(g_2)^* \varrho(g_1)^* = \varrho^\sharp(g_2) \varrho^\sharp(g_1)$, an *anti*-homomorphism; for a nonabelian group this is not a homomorphism, and we exhibit two explicit matrices in $GL(2; \mathbb R)$ below for which $(AB)^t \ne A^t B^t$. The repair is to compose with inversion, which is itself an anti-homomorphism ($(g_1 g_2)^{-1} = g_2^{-1} g_1^{-1}$), so that two order reversals cancel: $\varrho^*(g) := \varrho(g^{-1})^*$. This is Bär's Remark on p. 17 and our Corollary B. Note that the alternative repair "$g \mapsto \varrho(g)^{-1}$ without the dual" fails for the same reason, since $\varrho(g_1 g_2)^{-1} = \varrho(g_2)^{-1} \varrho(g_1)^{-1}$; it is the *pairing* of two anti-homomorphisms that produces a homomorphism.

Take next the requirement that the same group element act in every tensor factor. The formula $(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g)(v_1 \otimes v_2) = \varrho_1(g) v_1 \otimes \varrho_2(g) v_2$ uses one $g$ twice. If $\varrho_1$ and $\varrho_2$ were representations of *different* groups $G_1$ and $G_2$, the same formula with $(g_1, g_2)$ in place of $(g, g)$ would define a representation of the product group $G_1 \times G_2$ (the external tensor product), and the tensor product of two representations of one group $G$ is exactly the restriction of that external tensor product to the diagonal subgroup $\{(g, g)\} \cong G$. This diagonal action is also what makes the symmetric and exterior powers possible: $\varrho(g)^{\otimes k}$ commutes with every permutation operator $P_\sigma$ because the *same* automorphism sits in every slot, and this commutation is what forces $\varrho(g)^{\otimes k}$ to preserve the subspaces $\odot^k V$ and $\Lambda^k V$. An action that is not diagonal need not preserve them: on $\mathbb R^2 \otimes \mathbb R^2$ the perfectly good representation $g \mapsto \varrho_{st}(g) \otimes \operatorname{id}$ of $GL(2; \mathbb R)$ sends the symmetric tensor $e_1 \otimes e_1$ to $e_2 \otimes e_1$ when $g$ is the coordinate swap, and $e_2 \otimes e_1$ is not symmetric (non-instance below). So "$\Lambda^k$ and $\odot^k$ of a representation" is a construction on *one* representation, and the clause "the same $g$ in every factor" is what the construction rests on.

Take next the clause "$\varrho$ real" in the definition of complexification. The construction $V_{\mathbb C} = V \otimes_{\mathbb R} \mathbb C$ makes sense for any real vector space, and a complex vector space *is* a real vector space; but complexifying the underlying real representation of a complex representation does not return the original. We verify below that the real two-dimensional rotation representation of $U(1)$ — which is the standard complex representation $z \mapsto z$ on $\mathbb C \cong \mathbb R^2$ viewed as real — complexifies to $\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_{-1}$, a two-dimensional complex representation with both $z$ and $\bar z$ as eigenvalues, not to $\varrho_1$. The clause is there to keep the reader from expecting an identity that is false.

Finally, take smoothness. It is tempting to regard it as automatic, and in a sense it is: every construction here is polynomial in the matrix entries of $\varrho(g)$, or polynomial in the entries of $\varrho(g^{-1}) = \varrho(\operatorname{inv}(g))$, and polynomials of smooth functions are smooth. But this is a claim about each construction separately, and it has to be checked, because the smooth structure on $\operatorname{Aut}(\Lambda^k V)$ is the one induced from a basis of $\Lambda^k V$, and one must know how $\Lambda^k \varrho(g)$ looks in such a basis. The restriction lemma (Lemma 6 below) settles this uniformly: the restriction of a smooth representation to an invariant subspace is smooth, because in a basis adapted to the subspace the matrix of the restriction is a block of the matrix of the original. With that, could a reader invent the definitions from what has been said? We believe so: apply the functor to each $\varrho(g)$; where the functor is contravariant, precompose with inversion; where the target is a subspace of a tensor power, act diagonally and restrict; and check smoothness through matrix entries.

# The Definition

Let $G$ be a Lie group, let $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ and $\varrho_j : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V_j)$ ($j = 1, 2$) be representations of $G$ on finite-dimensional $\mathbb K$-vector spaces, and let $k \ge 1$ be an integer. Each of the following six formulas defines a representation of $G$; that it does — that the map is a well-defined linear automorphism for every $g$, that $g \mapsto$ (the map) is a group homomorphism, and that it is smooth — is proved in Corollary A of the Examples / Corollaries section, and the justification for the existence of each linear map is recorded beside its formula.

1. **The direct sum representation** $\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2 : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V_1 \oplus V_2)$ is
$$(\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(g)(v_1 \oplus v_2) := \varrho_1(g) v_1 \oplus \varrho_2(g) v_2 \qquad (g \in G,\ v_1 \in V_1,\ v_2 \in V_2).$$
   With respect to a basis of $V_1 \oplus V_2$ obtained by listing a basis of $V_1$ and then a basis of $V_2$, the matrix is block diagonal:
$$[(\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(g)] = \begin{pmatrix} [\varrho_1(g)] & 0 \\ 0 & [\varrho_2(g)] \end{pmatrix}.$$

2. **The tensor product representation** $\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2 : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V_1 \otimes V_2)$ is the map whose value at $g$ is the unique linear map $V_1 \otimes V_2 \to V_1 \otimes V_2$ with
$$(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g)(v_1 \otimes v_2) := \varrho_1(g) v_1 \otimes \varrho_2(g) v_2 \qquad (v_1 \in V_1,\ v_2 \in V_2)$$
   on elementary tensors, extended linearly to all of $V_1 \otimes V_2$. Such a linear map exists and is unique by the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|universal property of the tensor product]], because for fixed $g$ the map $V_1 \times V_2 \to V_1 \otimes V_2$, $(v_1, v_2) \mapsto \varrho_1(g) v_1 \otimes \varrho_2(g) v_2$, is bilinear: it is the composite of the linear maps $\varrho_1(g)$ and $\varrho_2(g)$ in each slot with the bilinear map $\otimes$. We write $\varrho^{\otimes k} := \varrho \otimes \dots \otimes \varrho$ ($k$ factors) for the $k$-fold tensor power, the representation on $V^{\otimes k}$ with $\varrho^{\otimes k}(g)(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = \varrho(g) v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes \varrho(g) v_k$; its existence is Lemma 2 below (the $k$-fold universal property, obtained by induction on $k$).

3. **The exterior power (antisymmetric tensor product, or wedge product) representation** $\Lambda^k \varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(\Lambda^k V)$ is the restriction of $\varrho^{\otimes k}$ to the subspace $\Lambda^k V = \operatorname{Alt}_k(V^{\otimes k})$ of alternating tensors:
$$(\Lambda^k \varrho)(g) := \varrho^{\otimes k}(g) \big|_{\Lambda^k V}, \qquad \text{so that} \qquad (\Lambda^k \varrho)(g)(v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k) = \varrho(g) v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \varrho(g) v_k.$$
   The restriction is meaningful because $\varrho^{\otimes k}(g)$ maps $\Lambda^k V$ into itself (Lemma 5 below), and the displayed formula on decomposable wedges is Lemma 5(c). If $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ is a basis of $V$, then $\{e_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge e_{i_k} : 1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le n\}$ is a basis of $\Lambda^k V$ (Lemma 4), so $\dim \Lambda^k V = \binom{n}{k}$ and $\Lambda^k V = 0$ for $k > n$.

4. **The symmetric power (symmetric tensor product) representation** $\odot^k \varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(\odot^k V)$ is the restriction of $\varrho^{\otimes k}$ to the subspace $\odot^k V = \operatorname{Sym}_k(V^{\otimes k})$ of symmetric tensors:
$$(\odot^k \varrho)(g) := \varrho^{\otimes k}(g) \big|_{\odot^k V}, \qquad \text{so that} \qquad (\odot^k \varrho)(g)(v_1 \odot \dots \odot v_k) = \varrho(g) v_1 \odot \dots \odot \varrho(g) v_k.$$
   Again the restriction is meaningful by Lemma 5 and the formula on decomposable symmetric tensors is Lemma 5(c). If $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ is a basis of $V$, then $\{e_{i_1} \odot \dots \odot e_{i_k} : 1 \le i_1 \le \dots \le i_k \le n\}$ is a basis of $\odot^k V$ (Lemma 4), so $\dim \odot^k V = \binom{n + k - 1}{k}$, the number of multisets of size $k$ drawn from $n$ symbols.

5. **The dual representation** $\varrho^* : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V^*)$ is
$$\varrho^*(g) := \varrho(g^{-1})^*, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \varrho^*(g)(\lambda) = \lambda \circ \varrho(g^{-1}) \qquad (\lambda \in V^*).$$
   Here $\varrho(g^{-1})^* \in \operatorname{End}(V^*)$ is the [[Def - Dual Map|dual map]] of the automorphism $\varrho(g^{-1}) \in \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, which exists for every linear map. In the dual basis $(\varepsilon^1, \dots, \varepsilon^n)$ of a basis $\mathcal B$ of $V$, the matrix of $\varrho^*(g)$ is the transpose of the matrix of $\varrho(g^{-1})$, that is, $[\varrho^*(g)] = \big([\varrho(g)]^{-1}\big)^t$ ([[Thm - Matrix of Dual Map is Transpose]]). Equivalently, $\varrho^*$ is characterised by the invariance of the evaluation pairing: $\big(\varrho^*(g) \lambda\big)\big(\varrho(g) v\big) = \lambda(v)$ for all $g$, $\lambda$, $v$.

6. **The complexification** of a *real* representation $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$, $V$ a real vector space, is the complex representation
$$\varrho_{\mathbb C} : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V_{\mathbb C}), \qquad \varrho_{\mathbb C}(g) := \varrho(g) \otimes \operatorname{id}_{\mathbb C}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \varrho_{\mathbb C}(g)(v \otimes w) = \varrho(g) v \otimes w \quad (v \in V,\ w \in \mathbb C),$$
   on the complex vector space $V_{\mathbb C} = V \otimes_{\mathbb R} \mathbb C$, whose complex scalar multiplication is $z \cdot (v \otimes w) := v \otimes (zw)$ (Lemma 7 below constructs this structure and shows that $\varrho_{\mathbb C}(g)$ is $\mathbb C$-linear). The linear map $\varrho(g) \otimes \operatorname{id}_{\mathbb C}$ exists by the universal property applied to the $\mathbb R$-bilinear map $(v, w) \mapsto \varrho(g) v \otimes w$. In matrices: if $\mathcal B = (e_1, \dots, e_n)$ is a real basis of $V$, then $(e_1 \otimes 1, \dots, e_n \otimes 1)$ is a complex basis of $V_{\mathbb C}$, and in it the matrix of $\varrho_{\mathbb C}(g)$ is the real matrix $[\varrho(g)]_{\mathcal B}$ regarded as a complex matrix. So, as Bär says, "the representation $\varrho$ is given by real matrices; if we now consider them as complex matrices, then we have the complexification."

**The smallest cases.** For $G = U(1)$ and the characters $\varrho_k(z) = z^k$ on $V = \mathbb C$, all six constructions can be written out with $1 \times 1$ matrices: $(\varrho_k \oplus \varrho_l)(z) = \operatorname{diag}(z^k, z^l)$ on $\mathbb C^2$; $(\varrho_k \otimes \varrho_l)(z)(u \otimes w) = z^k u \otimes z^l w = z^{k+l} (u \otimes w)$ on the one-dimensional space $\mathbb C \otimes \mathbb C$; $\Lambda^1 \varrho_k = \odot^1 \varrho_k = \varrho_k$ and $\Lambda^2 \mathbb C = 0$; $\varrho_k^*(z)(\lambda) = \lambda \circ \varrho_k(z^{-1}) = z^{-k} \lambda$ on $\mathbb C^*$; and complexification does not apply, since $\varrho_k$ is complex. These are verified in the Examples below.

# Categorical Definition

Each construction on this page is the image of the representation under a functor on finite-dimensional vector spaces, with one adjustment for contravariance. Regard the group $G$ as a category with one object whose morphisms are the elements of $G$; a representation is then a functor $\varrho : G \to \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb K}$ (this is the categorical reading on [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]]), and the smoothness condition is an extra requirement on the morphism part. Now let $F : \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb K} \to \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb K}$ be a covariant functor. Then $F \circ \varrho$ is again a functor $G \to \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb K}$, that is, $g \mapsto F(\varrho(g))$ is a group homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Aut}(F(V))$: functors preserve composition and identities, so $F(\varrho(g_1 g_2)) = F(\varrho(g_1) \varrho(g_2)) = F(\varrho(g_1)) F(\varrho(g_2))$ and $F(\varrho(e)) = F(\operatorname{id}_V) = \operatorname{id}_{F(V)}$, and $F(\varrho(g))$ is invertible with inverse $F(\varrho(g^{-1}))$ because $F(\varrho(g)) F(\varrho(g^{-1})) = F(\varrho(g) \varrho(g^{-1})) = F(\operatorname{id}_V) = \operatorname{id}_{F(V)}$ and $F(\varrho(g^{-1})) F(\varrho(g)) = F(\varrho(g^{-1}) \varrho(g)) = F(\operatorname{id}_V) = \operatorname{id}_{F(V)}$. This is the whole homomorphism half of Corollary A, stated once for all covariant constructions. The direct sum is the functor $(-) \oplus W$ applied to the pair (more precisely, the biproduct functor $\mathbf{Vect} \times \mathbf{Vect} \to \mathbf{Vect}$ applied to the functor $(\varrho_1, \varrho_2) : G \to \mathbf{Vect} \times \mathbf{Vect}$ obtained from the diagonal $G \to G \times G$); the tensor product is the monoidal product functor applied in the same way, and here the diagonal $g \mapsto (g, g)$ is the categorical form of the clause "the same $g$ in both factors"; $\Lambda^k$ and $\odot^k$ are the functors $V \mapsto \operatorname{Alt}_k(V^{\otimes k})$ and $V \mapsto \operatorname{Sym}_k(V^{\otimes k})$, which are functors precisely because $T^{\otimes k}$ commutes with the projectors (Lemma 5); the complexification is the extension-of-scalars functor $(-) \otimes_{\mathbb R} \mathbb C : \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb R} \to \mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb C}$ ([[Def - Restriction and Extension of Scalars]]).

The dual is the one contravariant functor on the list: $V \mapsto V^*$, $T \mapsto T^*$, with $(ST)^* = T^* S^*$. A contravariant functor out of $G$ is a functor out of the opposite category $G^{\mathrm{op}}$, whose morphisms compose in reversed order; and the inversion map $\operatorname{inv} : G \to G^{\mathrm{op}}$, $g \mapsto g^{-1}$, is an isomorphism of categories (of groups), since $(g_1 g_2)^{-1} = g_2^{-1} g_1^{-1}$ is exactly composition in $G^{\mathrm{op}}$. The dual representation is the composite $G \xrightarrow{\operatorname{inv}} G^{\mathrm{op}} \xrightarrow{\varrho^{\mathrm{op}}} \mathbf{Vect}^{\mathrm{op}} \xrightarrow{(-)^*} \mathbf{Vect}$, and the $g^{-1}$ in its formula is the inversion isomorphism made visible. In this reading, Corollary B (the naive dual is an anti-homomorphism) says only that a contravariant functor composed with a covariant one is contravariant, and that one needs the inversion to turn it back.

What the categorical reading does *not* supply is smoothness. A functor on $\mathbf{Vect}_{\mathbb K}$ need not act smoothly on $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ in general — there is nothing in the axioms of a functor that constrains the map $\operatorname{End}(V) \to \operatorname{End}(F(V))$ beyond preserving composition — so smoothness is verified construction by construction, in each case by writing $F(\varrho(g))$ in a basis and observing that its entries are polynomials in the entries of $\varrho(g)$ or of $\varrho(g^{-1})$. Functors of this kind, whose action on morphisms is polynomial in matrix entries, are exactly the ones that transport smooth representations to smooth representations.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**The representation ring.** Direct sum and tensor product make the set of equivalence classes of finite-dimensional representations of $G$ into a commutative semiring, with the trivial one-dimensional representation as multiplicative unit and the zero representation as additive unit; its Grothendieck group is the representation ring $R(G)$ of representation theory and $K$-theory. The dual is the involution $\varrho \mapsto \varrho^*$ of that ring, and the exterior powers make it a $\lambda$-ring. For $U(1)$ the theorem [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] identifies $R(U(1))$ with the Laurent polynomial ring $\mathbb Z[t, t^{-1}]$, $\varrho_k \mapsto t^k$, and the rules $\varrho_k \otimes \varrho_l \cong \varrho_{k+l}$ and $\varrho_k^* \cong \varrho_{-k}$ verified below are the ring multiplication and the involution $t \mapsto t^{-1}$. For $SU(2)$ the same theorem gives $R(SU(2)) \cong \mathbb Z[\varrho_1]$, with the Clebsch–Gordan rule $\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_1 \cong \varrho_2 \oplus \varrho_0$ (drilled in [[Ex - Decomposing the Tensor Square of the Standard Representation of SU(2)]]) as the first instance of the multiplication table.

**Quantum mechanics and spin.** In quantum mechanics a system with symmetry group $G$ has a Hilbert space carrying a unitary representation of $G$, and the Hilbert space of a composite system is the tensor product of the Hilbert spaces of its parts. The tensor product representation on this page is therefore the rule for how two symmetric subsystems transform jointly, and the decomposition of $\varrho_j \otimes \varrho_{j'}$ into irreducibles of $SU(2)$ is the addition of angular momenta. Symmetric and exterior powers are the state spaces of $k$ identical bosons and $k$ identical fermions respectively; the Pauli exclusion principle is the vanishing $\Lambda^k V = 0$ for $k > \dim V$. The dual representation is the transformation rule for bras, which is why the pairing $\big(\varrho^*(g) \lambda\big)\big(\varrho(g) v\big) = \lambda(v)$ is invariant: a matrix element between a transformed bra and a transformed ket is unchanged.

**Vector bundles.** In chapter III every representation $\varrho$ of the structure group $G$ of a principal bundle $P$ produces an associated bundle $P \times_\varrho V$ ([[Def - Associated Bundle]]), and the constructions on this page correspond one-to-one to the [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles|operations on vector bundles]]: the associated bundle of $\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2$ is the Whitney sum, of $\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2$ the tensor product bundle, of $\Lambda^k \varrho$ the exterior power bundle, of $\varrho^*$ the dual bundle, and of $\varrho_{\mathbb C}$ the complexified bundle. In particular $\Lambda^n \varrho_{st} = \det$ (verified below) says that the top exterior power of a rank-$n$ vector bundle is the line bundle associated to the determinant representation, the determinant line bundle, whose triviality is orientability. The identity $\varrho_k \otimes \varrho_l \cong \varrho_{k+l}$ for $U(1)$ says that the associated line bundles of a $U(1)$-bundle satisfy $L_k \otimes L_l \cong L_{k+l}$, that is, $L_k = L^{\otimes k}$.

**True name:** each construction is "apply the functor to every $\varrho(g)$, and precompose with $g \mapsto g^{-1}$ when the functor is contravariant." The official definitions are already operational — each gives a formula for the automorphism $F(\varrho(g))$ on a spanning set — and the only non-obvious operational content is the pair of facts that $\varrho(g)^{\otimes k}$ commutes with every permutation of tensor slots (so that restriction to $\Lambda^k V$ and $\odot^k V$ is legitimate) and that a formula on elementary tensors determines a linear map (the universal property).

# Examples / Corollaries

We begin with the seven lemmas that carry the well-definedness of the objects in the definition, then prove Corollary A (each construction is a representation) and Corollary B (Bär's Theorem on the dual, T1.3.3), then verify instances and non-instances.

> [!note]- Lemma 1: smoothness of a map into $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ is smoothness of its matrix entries
> **Statement.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional $\mathbb K$-vector space with basis $\mathcal B = (e_1, \dots, e_n)$, let $M$ be a smooth manifold, and let $\phi : M \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ be a map. Then $\phi$ is smooth if and only if each matrix entry $m \mapsto \phi(m)_{ij} \in \mathbb K$ (defined by $\phi(m) e_j = \sum_i \phi(m)_{ij} e_i$) is a smooth function on $M$, where for $\mathbb K = \mathbb C$ "smooth" means that real and imaginary parts are smooth. The condition does not depend on the basis.
>
> **Full proof.** We are given the basis $\mathcal B$ and the map $\phi$, and we need to show the equivalence and its basis-independence.
>
> **Identify the smooth structure on $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$.** The map $\operatorname{End}(V) \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb K)$, $T \mapsto [T]_{\mathcal B}$, is a $\mathbb K$-linear isomorphism (a linear map is determined by, and can be prescribed arbitrarily on, a basis), and $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb K) = \mathbb K^{n^2}$ is identified with $\mathbb R^{n^2}$ or $\mathbb R^{2n^2}$ by taking entries, or real and imaginary parts of entries, as coordinates. This single global chart defines the smooth structure on $\operatorname{End}(V)$; the subset $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ corresponds to $GL(n; \mathbb K) = \{A : \det A \ne 0\}$, which is open (preimage of the open set $\mathbb K \setminus \{0\}$ under the continuous polynomial map $\det$), and $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ carries the open-submanifold structure, with the restriction of the same chart ([[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]], where $GL(n; \mathbb K)$ is shown to be a Lie group in exactly this way).
>
> **Reduce to components.** A map from a manifold into an open subset $U \subset \mathbb R^N$ with the identity chart is smooth if and only if its $N$ real component functions are smooth (this is the definition of a [[Def - Smooth Map between Manifolds|smooth map]] read in the chart of the target). The component functions of $\phi$ in the chart above are exactly the entries $\phi(m)_{ij}$ (for $\mathbb K = \mathbb R$) or their real and imaginary parts (for $\mathbb K = \mathbb C$). This proves the equivalence for the basis $\mathcal B$.
>
> **Basis independence.** If $\mathcal B' = (e_1', \dots, e_n')$ is another basis and $C \in GL(n; \mathbb K)$ is the change-of-basis matrix with $e_j' = \sum_i C_{ij} e_i$, then $[T]_{\mathcal B'} = C^{-1} [T]_{\mathcal B} C$ for every $T$ (the standard change-of-basis formula), so each entry $\phi(m)'_{ij} = \sum_{a, b} (C^{-1})_{ia} \phi(m)_{ab} C_{bj}$ is a linear combination, with constant coefficients, of the entries in $\mathcal B$; a constant-coefficient linear combination of smooth functions is smooth, and the relation is symmetric under $C \leftrightarrow C^{-1}$. Therefore smoothness of the entries in one basis is equivalent to smoothness of the entries in any other. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: the $k$-fold tensor power, its universal property, and its basis
> **Statement.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional $\mathbb K$-vector space and $k \ge 1$. (a) For every $\mathbb K$-vector space $U$ and every $k$-linear map $\Gamma : V^k \to U$ there is a unique linear map $\hat\Gamma : V^{\otimes k} \to U$ with $\hat\Gamma(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = \Gamma(v_1, \dots, v_k)$ for all $v_1, \dots, v_k \in V$. (b) The elementary tensors $v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k$ span $V^{\otimes k}$; if $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ is a basis of $V$, then $\{e_J : J \in \{1, \dots, n\}^k\}$ is a basis of $V^{\otimes k}$, so $\dim V^{\otimes k} = n^k$. (c) The map $(v_1, \dots, v_k) \mapsto v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k$ is $k$-linear. (d) For every linear map $T : V \to V$ there is a unique linear map $T^{\otimes k} : V^{\otimes k} \to V^{\otimes k}$ with $T^{\otimes k}(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = T v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes T v_k$; moreover $(ST)^{\otimes k} = S^{\otimes k} T^{\otimes k}$ and $\operatorname{id}_V^{\otimes k} = \operatorname{id}_{V^{\otimes k}}$, and in the basis $(e_J)$ the matrix entries of $T^{\otimes k}$ are the products $(T^{\otimes k})_{IJ} = T_{i_1 j_1} T_{i_2 j_2} \cdots T_{i_k j_k}$.
>
> **Full proof.** We argue by induction on $k$, the case $k = 1$ being trivial for (a), (b), (c) ($V^{\otimes 1} = V$, a $1$-linear map is a linear map) and (d) ($T^{\otimes 1} = T$). Assume (a)–(c) for $k - 1 \ge 1$; recall $V^{\otimes k} = V^{\otimes (k-1)} \otimes V$.
>
> **(c) for $k$.** By definition $v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k = (v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_{k-1}) \otimes v_k$. This is linear in $v_k$ and linear in $(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_{k-1})$ (bilinearity of $\otimes : V^{\otimes(k-1)} \times V \to V^{\otimes k}$, [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]]), and $(v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}) \mapsto v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_{k-1}$ is $(k-1)$-linear by the induction hypothesis (c); a linear map composed with a multilinear map in one slot is multilinear in that slot. Hence the $k$-fold map is $k$-linear.
>
> **(b) for $k$.** By the basis theorem for a tensor product of two spaces ([[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]]), if $(b_\alpha)$ is a basis of $V^{\otimes (k-1)}$ and $(e_j)$ of $V$ then $(b_\alpha \otimes e_j)$ is a basis of $V^{\otimes k}$. Taking $(b_\alpha) = (e_{J'})_{J' \in \{1, \dots, n\}^{k-1}}$ (induction hypothesis (b)) gives the basis $e_{J'} \otimes e_j = e_{(J', j)}$, $J = (J', j)$ ranging over $\{1, \dots, n\}^k$. Since these basis vectors are elementary tensors, elementary tensors span. The count is $n^{k-1} \cdot n = n^k$.
>
> **(a) for $k$.** *Existence.* Let $\Gamma : V^k \to U$ be $k$-linear. For each fixed $v \in V$ the map $\Gamma_v : V^{k-1} \to U$, $(v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}) \mapsto \Gamma(v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}, v)$, is $(k-1)$-linear, so by the induction hypothesis (a) there is a unique linear $\hat\Gamma_v : V^{\otimes (k-1)} \to U$ with $\hat\Gamma_v(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_{k-1}) = \Gamma(v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}, v)$. Define $\Phi : V^{\otimes(k-1)} \times V \to U$ by $\Phi(t, v) := \hat\Gamma_v(t)$. Then $\Phi$ is linear in $t$ for fixed $v$ (because $\hat\Gamma_v$ is linear). It is linear in $v$ for fixed $t$: for $v, v' \in V$ and $c \in \mathbb K$, the two linear maps $\hat\Gamma_{cv + v'}$ and $c \hat\Gamma_v + \hat\Gamma_{v'}$ agree on every elementary tensor $v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_{k-1}$ (both give $\Gamma(v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}, cv + v') = c\Gamma(v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}, v) + \Gamma(v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}, v')$ by linearity of $\Gamma$ in its last slot), and elementary tensors span $V^{\otimes (k-1)}$ (induction hypothesis (b)), so the two linear maps are equal. Hence $\Phi$ is bilinear, and by the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|universal property of the tensor product]] — for finite-dimensional $V_1, V_2$ and any bilinear $\Phi : V_1 \times V_2 \to U$ there is a unique linear $\hat\Phi : V_1 \otimes V_2 \to U$ with $\hat\Phi(v_1 \otimes v_2) = \Phi(v_1, v_2)$ — there is a linear $\hat\Gamma := \hat\Phi : V^{\otimes k} \to U$ with $\hat\Gamma((v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_{k-1}) \otimes v_k) = \Phi(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_{k-1}, v_k) = \hat\Gamma_{v_k}(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_{k-1}) = \Gamma(v_1, \dots, v_k)$, as required. *Uniqueness.* Two linear maps with the stated property agree on all elementary tensors, which span by (b); hence they are equal.
>
> **(d) for $k$.** For linear $T : V \to V$ the map $(v_1, \dots, v_k) \mapsto T v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes T v_k$ is $k$-linear (compose the linear $T$ in each slot with the $k$-linear map of (c)), so $T^{\otimes k}$ exists and is unique by (a). For $S, T \in \operatorname{End}(V)$, the linear maps $(ST)^{\otimes k}$ and $S^{\otimes k} T^{\otimes k}$ agree on elementary tensors: $S^{\otimes k} T^{\otimes k}(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = S^{\otimes k}(T v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes T v_k) = ST v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes ST v_k = (ST)^{\otimes k}(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k)$ (definition of $T^{\otimes k}$, then of $S^{\otimes k}$, then of $(ST)^{\otimes k}$); by (b) they are equal. For $T = \operatorname{id}_V$, the map $\operatorname{id}_V^{\otimes k}$ sends $v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k$ to $\operatorname{id}_V v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes \operatorname{id}_V v_k = v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k$, so it agrees with $\operatorname{id}_{V^{\otimes k}}$ on elementary tensors and hence, by (b), $\operatorname{id}_V^{\otimes k} = \operatorname{id}_{V^{\otimes k}}$. For the matrix entries, write $T e_j = \sum_i T_{ij} e_i$; then
> $$T^{\otimes k} e_J = T e_{j_1} \otimes \dots \otimes T e_{j_k} = \sum_{i_1, \dots, i_k} T_{i_1 j_1} \cdots T_{i_k j_k} \, e_{i_1} \otimes \dots \otimes e_{i_k} = \sum_I T_{i_1 j_1} \cdots T_{i_k j_k} \, e_I \qquad \text{(definition of } T^{\otimes k} \text{; } k\text{-linearity (c) expanded slot by slot)},$$
> which exhibits the entry $(T^{\otimes k})_{IJ} = T_{i_1 j_1} \cdots T_{i_k j_k}$ in the basis $(e_J)$ of (b). This completes the induction. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: permutation operators, symmetrisation, and alternation on $V^{\otimes k}$
> **Statement.** Let $V$ be finite-dimensional over $\mathbb K$ and $k \ge 1$. (a) For each $\sigma \in S_k$ the permutation operator $P_\sigma$ (with $P_\sigma(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)} \otimes \dots \otimes v_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}$) exists, is unique, and satisfies $P_\sigma P_\tau = P_{\sigma\tau}$ and $P_{\operatorname{id}} = \operatorname{id}$; in particular every $P_\sigma$ is invertible with inverse $P_{\sigma^{-1}}$. On basis tensors, $P_\sigma e_J = e_{J \circ \sigma^{-1}}$. (b) $\operatorname{Sym}_k^2 = \operatorname{Sym}_k$ and $\operatorname{Alt}_k^2 = \operatorname{Alt}_k$. (c) For every $\tau \in S_k$: $P_\tau \operatorname{Sym}_k = \operatorname{Sym}_k P_\tau = \operatorname{Sym}_k$ and $P_\tau \operatorname{Alt}_k = \operatorname{Alt}_k P_\tau = \operatorname{sgn}(\tau) \operatorname{Alt}_k$. (d) $\odot^k V = \operatorname{Sym}_k(V^{\otimes k}) = \{t \in V^{\otimes k} : P_\tau t = t \text{ for all } \tau \in S_k\}$, and $\Lambda^k V = \operatorname{Alt}_k(V^{\otimes k}) = \{t \in V^{\otimes k} : P_\tau t = \operatorname{sgn}(\tau) t \text{ for all } \tau \in S_k\}$. (e) The maps $(v_1, \dots, v_k) \mapsto v_1 \odot \dots \odot v_k$ and $(v_1, \dots, v_k) \mapsto v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k$ are $k$-linear; the first is symmetric under every permutation of its arguments and the second is alternating (it vanishes whenever two arguments coincide, and changes sign under a transposition of two arguments).
>
> **Full proof.** We need to establish (a)–(e) in turn; throughout we use the sign homomorphism $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)$ and $\operatorname{sgn}(\tau^{-1}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\tau)$ ([[Def - Symmetric Group]]), and the fact that $\mathbb K$ has characteristic zero, so that $k! \ne 0$ in $\mathbb K$.
>
> **(a) Existence, composition rule, and action on basis tensors.** For fixed $\sigma$ the map $(v_1, \dots, v_k) \mapsto v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)} \otimes \dots \otimes v_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}$ is $k$-linear (it is the $k$-linear map of Lemma 2(c) with its arguments relabelled), so $P_\sigma$ exists and is unique by Lemma 2(a). For the composition rule, fix $\sigma, \tau$ and an elementary tensor, and put $w_i := v_{\tau^{-1}(i)}$, so that $P_\tau(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = w_1 \otimes \dots \otimes w_k$; then
> $$P_\sigma P_\tau (v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = P_\sigma(w_1 \otimes \dots \otimes w_k) = w_{\sigma^{-1}(1)} \otimes \dots \otimes w_{\sigma^{-1}(k)} \qquad \text{(definition of } P_\tau \text{, then of } P_\sigma),$$
> and $w_{\sigma^{-1}(i)} = v_{\tau^{-1}(\sigma^{-1}(i))} = v_{(\sigma\tau)^{-1}(i)}$ (since $(\sigma\tau)^{-1} = \tau^{-1}\sigma^{-1}$), so the right-hand side is $P_{\sigma\tau}(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k)$. Two linear maps agreeing on the spanning set of elementary tensors (Lemma 2(b)) are equal, so $P_\sigma P_\tau = P_{\sigma\tau}$. For $\sigma = \operatorname{id}$ the defining formula reads $P_{\operatorname{id}}(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k$, so $P_{\operatorname{id}}$ agrees with the identity on the spanning set of elementary tensors and is therefore the identity; then $P_\sigma P_{\sigma^{-1}} = P_{\operatorname{id}} = \operatorname{id} = P_{\sigma^{-1}} P_\sigma$. Finally $P_\sigma e_J = e_{j_{\sigma^{-1}(1)}} \otimes \dots \otimes e_{j_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}} = e_{J \circ \sigma^{-1}}$ by the definition of $P_\sigma$ and of $J \circ \sigma^{-1}$.
>
> **(b) Idempotence.** Using (a) and the fact that for fixed $\sigma$ the map $\tau \mapsto \sigma\tau$ is a bijection of $S_k$,
> $$\operatorname{Sym}_k^2 = \frac{1}{(k!)^2} \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} \sum_{\tau \in S_k} P_\sigma P_\tau = \frac{1}{(k!)^2} \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} \sum_{\rho \in S_k} P_\rho = \frac{1}{(k!)^2} \cdot k! \sum_{\rho \in S_k} P_\rho = \operatorname{Sym}_k \qquad \text{(by (a), } P_\sigma P_\tau = P_{\sigma\tau} \text{; substitute } \rho = \sigma\tau \text{; the inner sum no longer depends on } \sigma).$$
> For $\operatorname{Alt}_k$ the same substitution gives, using $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{sgn}(\tau) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma\tau) = \operatorname{sgn}(\rho)$,
> $$\operatorname{Alt}_k^2 = \frac{1}{(k!)^2} \sum_{\sigma} \sum_{\tau} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\operatorname{sgn}(\tau) P_{\sigma\tau} = \frac{1}{(k!)^2} \sum_\sigma \sum_\rho \operatorname{sgn}(\rho) P_\rho = \operatorname{Alt}_k \qquad \text{(sign is a homomorphism; substitute } \rho = \sigma\tau).$$
>
> **(c) Absorption of permutations.** For $\tau \in S_k$,
> $$P_\tau \operatorname{Sym}_k = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_\sigma P_{\tau\sigma} = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_\rho P_\rho = \operatorname{Sym}_k, \qquad \operatorname{Sym}_k P_\tau = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_\sigma P_{\sigma\tau} = \operatorname{Sym}_k \qquad \text{(by (a); } \sigma \mapsto \tau\sigma \text{ and } \sigma \mapsto \sigma\tau \text{ are bijections of } S_k),$$
> $$P_\tau \operatorname{Alt}_k = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_\sigma \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) P_{\tau\sigma} = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_\rho \operatorname{sgn}(\tau^{-1}\rho) P_\rho = \operatorname{sgn}(\tau) \operatorname{Alt}_k \qquad \text{(substitute } \rho = \tau\sigma \text{, so } \sigma = \tau^{-1}\rho \text{; } \operatorname{sgn}(\tau^{-1}\rho) = \operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\operatorname{sgn}(\rho)),$$
> and $\operatorname{Alt}_k P_\tau = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_\sigma \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma) P_{\sigma\tau} = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_\rho \operatorname{sgn}(\rho\tau^{-1}) P_\rho = \operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\operatorname{Alt}_k$ by the substitution $\rho = \sigma\tau$.
>
> **(d) Images as fixed-point sets.** *Symmetric case.* If $t = \operatorname{Sym}_k(s)$ for some $s$, then $P_\tau t = P_\tau \operatorname{Sym}_k s = \operatorname{Sym}_k s = t$ for every $\tau$ (by (c)). Conversely, if $P_\tau t = t$ for every $\tau$, then $\operatorname{Sym}_k t = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_\tau P_\tau t = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_\tau t = t$ (there are $k!$ terms), so $t = \operatorname{Sym}_k t$ lies in the image. *Alternating case.* If $t = \operatorname{Alt}_k(s)$ then $P_\tau t = \operatorname{sgn}(\tau) t$ by (c). Conversely, if $P_\tau t = \operatorname{sgn}(\tau) t$ for every $\tau$, then $\operatorname{Alt}_k t = \frac{1}{k!}\sum_\tau \operatorname{sgn}(\tau) P_\tau t = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_\tau \operatorname{sgn}(\tau)^2 t = t$ (since $\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)^2 = 1$), so $t$ lies in the image.
>
> **(e) Multilinearity, symmetry, and the alternating property.** Both maps are the composite of the $k$-linear map $(v_1, \dots, v_k) \mapsto v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k$ (Lemma 2(c)) with a linear map ($\operatorname{Sym}_k$ or $\operatorname{Alt}_k$), hence $k$-linear. For $\tau \in S_k$, the tensor $v_{\tau^{-1}(1)} \otimes \dots \otimes v_{\tau^{-1}(k)}$ equals $P_\tau(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k)$, so $v_{\tau^{-1}(1)} \odot \dots \odot v_{\tau^{-1}(k)} = \operatorname{Sym}_k P_\tau (v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = v_1 \odot \dots \odot v_k$ and $v_{\tau^{-1}(1)} \wedge \dots \wedge v_{\tau^{-1}(k)} = \operatorname{Alt}_k P_\tau(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = \operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\, v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k$ (both by (c)); as $\tau$ ranges over $S_k$ so does $\tau^{-1}$, giving symmetry of $\odot$ and the sign rule for $\wedge$ under every permutation, in particular the sign change under a transposition. If $v_a = v_b$ with $a \ne b$, let $\tau = (a\, b)$; then $P_\tau(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k$ (swapping two equal entries changes nothing), so $v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k = \operatorname{Alt}_k P_\tau (v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = \operatorname{sgn}(\tau)\, v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k = - v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k$, and since $2 \ne 0$ in $\mathbb K$ this forces $v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k = 0$. $\blacksquare$
>
> *Relation to the vault's covariant version.* [[Thm - Symmetrization and Alternation Projectors]] proves (b)–(d) for the operators $\operatorname{Sym}, \operatorname{Alt}$ on covariant tensors $T^k(V^*) = $ multilinear forms on a real $V$; the argument there is the argument above with $P_\sigma$ replaced by the precomposition of a form with a permutation of its arguments. We have reproved the statements on $V^{\otimes k}$ over either field so that the page is self-contained and so that the complex case $\mathbb K = \mathbb C$, which the series needs for $SU(2)$, is covered.

> [!note]- Lemma 4: bases of $\Lambda^k V$ and $\odot^k V$, and their dimensions
> **Statement.** Let $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ be a basis of $V$ and $k \ge 1$. (a) $\{e_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge e_{i_k} : 1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le n\}$ is a basis of $\Lambda^k V$; hence $\dim \Lambda^k V = \binom{n}{k}$, and $\Lambda^k V = 0$ for $k > n$. (b) $\{e_{i_1} \odot \dots \odot e_{i_k} : 1 \le i_1 \le \dots \le i_k \le n\}$ is a basis of $\odot^k V$; hence $\dim \odot^k V = \binom{n+k-1}{k}$.
>
> **Full proof.** We need to show that each family spans the relevant image and is linearly independent. Write $e_I^\wedge := \operatorname{Alt}_k e_I = e_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge e_{i_k}$ and $e_I^\odot := \operatorname{Sym}_k e_I = e_{i_1} \odot \dots \odot e_{i_k}$ for a multi-index $I$. Recall from Lemma 3(a) that $P_\sigma e_J = e_{J \circ \sigma^{-1}}$, so
> $$e_J^\wedge = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, e_{J \circ \sigma^{-1}}, \qquad e_J^\odot = \frac{1}{k!} \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} e_{J \circ \sigma^{-1}} \qquad \text{(definitions of } \operatorname{Alt}_k, \operatorname{Sym}_k \text{ applied to } e_J). \tag{4.1}$$
> Every multi-index appearing in (4.1) is a rearrangement of $J$, so $e_J^\wedge$ and $e_J^\odot$ lie in the span of $\{e_{J'} : J' \text{ a rearrangement of } J\}$. We call the set of rearrangements of $J$ its *orbit*; two multi-indices have the same orbit if and only if they have the same underlying multiset of entries, and distinct orbits are disjoint.
>
> **Spanning.** Since the $e_J$ ($J \in \{1, \dots, n\}^k$) span $V^{\otimes k}$ (Lemma 2(b)), their images $e_J^\wedge$ span $\operatorname{Alt}_k(V^{\otimes k}) = \Lambda^k V$ and the $e_J^\odot$ span $\odot^k V$. It remains to reduce an arbitrary $J$ to the normal forms. For any $J$ and $\pi \in S_k$, Lemma 3(a) gives $e_{J \circ \pi} = P_{\pi^{-1}} e_J$, and Lemma 3(c) gives
> $$e_{J \circ \pi}^\wedge = \operatorname{Alt}_k P_{\pi^{-1}} e_J = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi^{-1})\, e_J^\wedge = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi)\, e_J^\wedge, \qquad e_{J \circ \pi}^\odot = \operatorname{Sym}_k P_{\pi^{-1}} e_J = e_J^\odot. \tag{4.2}$$
> *Alternating case.* If $J$ has a repeated entry $j_a = j_b$, $a \ne b$, then with $\pi = (a\, b)$ we have $J \circ \pi = J$, so (4.2) gives $e_J^\wedge = -e_J^\wedge$, hence $e_J^\wedge = 0$ (characteristic zero). If $J$ has distinct entries, sorting them gives the unique increasing $I$ with the same underlying set and a unique $\pi \in S_k$ with $J = I \circ \pi$; then $e_J^\wedge = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi) e_I^\wedge$ by (4.2). Hence $\Lambda^k V$ is spanned by $\{e_I^\wedge : I \text{ increasing}\}$; for $k > n$ there is no increasing multi-index of length $k$ in $\{1, \dots, n\}$, so $\Lambda^k V = 0$. *Symmetric case.* For any $J$, sorting gives the unique nondecreasing $I$ with the same multiset and some $\pi$ with $J = I \circ \pi$; then $e_J^\odot = e_I^\odot$ by (4.2). Hence $\odot^k V$ is spanned by $\{e_I^\odot : I \text{ nondecreasing}\}$.
>
> **Linear independence.** *Alternating case.* For increasing $I$, expand $e_I^\wedge$ by (4.1): since the entries of $I$ are distinct, $I \circ \sigma^{-1} = I$ holds only for $\sigma = \operatorname{id}$, so the coefficient of the basis vector $e_I$ in $e_I^\wedge$ is exactly $\frac{1}{k!} \ne 0$, and every other basis vector occurring lies in the orbit of $I$. Two distinct increasing multi-indices have distinct underlying sets, hence disjoint orbits. Now suppose $\sum_{I \text{ increasing}} c_I e_I^\wedge = 0$. Expanding in the basis $(e_J)$ of $V^{\otimes k}$, the coefficient of $e_I$ receives contributions only from the term $c_I e_I^\wedge$ (the orbits are disjoint), and that contribution is $c_I / k!$; so $c_I / k! = 0$, that is, $c_I = 0$ for every $I$. *Symmetric case.* For nondecreasing $I$, all coefficients in the expansion (4.1) of $e_I^\odot$ are the positive number $\frac{1}{k!}$ (and several $\sigma$ may contribute to the same $e_{J'}$, adding positive numbers), so the coefficient of $e_I$ in $e_I^\odot$ is a positive rational, in particular nonzero, and all basis vectors occurring lie in the orbit of $I$. Distinct nondecreasing multi-indices have distinct multisets, hence disjoint orbits, and the same coefficient comparison shows that $\sum_I c_I e_I^\odot = 0$ forces every $c_I = 0$.
>
> **Counting.** Increasing multi-indices of length $k$ in $\{1, \dots, n\}$ are the $k$-element subsets, $\binom{n}{k}$ of them; nondecreasing ones are the multisets of size $k$, of which there are $\binom{n+k-1}{k}$ (a nondecreasing sequence $i_1 \le \dots \le i_k$ corresponds bijectively to the strictly increasing sequence $i_1 < i_2 + 1 < \dots < i_k + k - 1$ in $\{1, \dots, n+k-1\}$). $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 5: $T^{\otimes k}$ commutes with permutations and preserves $\Lambda^k V$ and $\odot^k V$
> **Statement.** Let $T : V \to V$ be linear and $k \ge 1$. (a) $T^{\otimes k} P_\sigma = P_\sigma T^{\otimes k}$ for every $\sigma \in S_k$. (b) $T^{\otimes k} \operatorname{Sym}_k = \operatorname{Sym}_k T^{\otimes k}$ and $T^{\otimes k} \operatorname{Alt}_k = \operatorname{Alt}_k T^{\otimes k}$; consequently $T^{\otimes k}(\odot^k V) \subseteq \odot^k V$ and $T^{\otimes k}(\Lambda^k V) \subseteq \Lambda^k V$. (c) $T^{\otimes k}(v_1 \odot \dots \odot v_k) = T v_1 \odot \dots \odot T v_k$ and $T^{\otimes k}(v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k) = T v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge T v_k$.
>
> **Full proof.** **(a)** On an elementary tensor,
> $$T^{\otimes k} P_\sigma (v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = T^{\otimes k}(v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)} \otimes \dots \otimes v_{\sigma^{-1}(k)}) = T v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)} \otimes \dots \otimes T v_{\sigma^{-1}(k)} \qquad \text{(definition of } P_\sigma \text{, then of } T^{\otimes k}),$$
> $$P_\sigma T^{\otimes k}(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = P_\sigma(T v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes T v_k) = T v_{\sigma^{-1}(1)} \otimes \dots \otimes T v_{\sigma^{-1}(k)} \qquad \text{(definition of } T^{\otimes k} \text{, then of } P_\sigma \text{ applied to the vectors } T v_i).$$
> The two right-hand sides coincide, and elementary tensors span (Lemma 2(b)), so $T^{\otimes k} P_\sigma = P_\sigma T^{\otimes k}$.
>
> **(b)** Summing (a) over $\sigma$ with the weights $\frac{1}{k!}$, respectively $\frac{\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)}{k!}$, and using linearity of composition, $T^{\otimes k} \operatorname{Sym}_k = \operatorname{Sym}_k T^{\otimes k}$ and $T^{\otimes k} \operatorname{Alt}_k = \operatorname{Alt}_k T^{\otimes k}$. If $t \in \odot^k V$ then $t = \operatorname{Sym}_k t$ (Lemma 3(d), since $t$ is in the image of the idempotent $\operatorname{Sym}_k$, and $\operatorname{Sym}_k t = t$ for image elements by Lemma 3(b)), so $T^{\otimes k} t = T^{\otimes k} \operatorname{Sym}_k t = \operatorname{Sym}_k (T^{\otimes k} t) \in \odot^k V$. If $t \in \Lambda^k V$ then $t = \operatorname{Alt}_k t$ (Lemma 3(b) and (d)), so $T^{\otimes k} t = T^{\otimes k} \operatorname{Alt}_k t = \operatorname{Alt}_k (T^{\otimes k} t) \in \Lambda^k V$.
>
> **(c)** $T^{\otimes k}(v_1 \odot \dots \odot v_k) = T^{\otimes k} \operatorname{Sym}_k(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = \operatorname{Sym}_k T^{\otimes k}(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = \operatorname{Sym}_k(T v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes T v_k) = T v_1 \odot \dots \odot T v_k$ (definition of $\odot$; (b); definition of $T^{\otimes k}$; definition of $\odot$), and $T^{\otimes k}(v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k) = T^{\otimes k} \operatorname{Alt}_k(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = \operatorname{Alt}_k T^{\otimes k}(v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes v_k) = \operatorname{Alt}_k(T v_1 \otimes \dots \otimes T v_k) = T v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge T v_k$ (definition of $\wedge$; (b); definition of $T^{\otimes k}$; definition of $\wedge$). $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 6: the restriction of a smooth representation to an invariant subspace is a smooth representation
> **Statement.** Let $\phi : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(W)$ be a representation and $U \subseteq W$ a subspace with $\phi(g)(U) \subseteq U$ for all $g \in G$. Then $\phi(g)|_U \in \operatorname{Aut}(U)$ for every $g$, and $\phi|_U : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(U)$, $g \mapsto \phi(g)|_U$, is a representation.
>
> **Full proof.** We need to show that each restriction is an automorphism of $U$, that $g \mapsto \phi(g)|_U$ is a homomorphism, and that it is smooth.
>
> **Automorphism.** $\phi(g)|_U : U \to U$ is linear (restriction of a linear map) and injective (restriction of the injective map $\phi(g)$); an injective linear endomorphism of a finite-dimensional space is bijective. Alternatively, $\phi(g^{-1})|_U$ is a two-sided inverse: $\phi(g)|_U \phi(g^{-1})|_U = \phi(g g^{-1})|_U = \operatorname{id}_U$ and $\phi(g^{-1})|_U \phi(g)|_U = \phi(g^{-1} g)|_U = \operatorname{id}_U$, using that both maps preserve $U$ (so the composites are defined on $U$) and that $\phi$ is a homomorphism with $\phi(e) = \operatorname{id}_W$.
>
> **Homomorphism.** For $g, h \in G$ and $u \in U$, $\phi(gh)|_U(u) = \phi(gh)(u) = \phi(g)\phi(h)(u) = \phi(g)|_U \phi(h)|_U (u)$, where the middle equality is the homomorphism property of $\phi$ and the last uses $\phi(h)(u) \in U$.
>
> **Smoothness.** Choose a basis $(u_1, \dots, u_m)$ of $U$ and extend it to a basis $(u_1, \dots, u_m, w_{m+1}, \dots, w_N)$ of $W$. For $j \le m$, $\phi(g) u_j \in U$, so $\phi(g) u_j = \sum_{i=1}^m \phi(g)_{ij} u_i$ with no $w$-components; thus the matrix of $\phi(g)$ in this basis has the block form $\begin{pmatrix} A(g) & B(g) \\ 0 & D(g) \end{pmatrix}$ with $A(g) = (\phi(g)_{ij})_{i,j \le m}$ the matrix of $\phi(g)|_U$ in the basis $(u_1, \dots, u_m)$. By Lemma 1 applied to $\phi$ (which is smooth by hypothesis), every entry $g \mapsto \phi(g)_{ij}$ is smooth on $G$; in particular the entries of $A(g)$ are smooth; by Lemma 1 applied to $\phi|_U$ and the basis $(u_1, \dots, u_m)$, $\phi|_U$ is smooth. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 7: the complex structure on $V_{\mathbb C}$ and $\mathbb C$-linearity of $\varrho(g) \otimes \operatorname{id}_{\mathbb C}$
> **Statement.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space with basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$, and let $V_{\mathbb C} = V \otimes_{\mathbb R} \mathbb C$. (a) For each $z \in \mathbb C$ there is a unique $\mathbb R$-linear map $m_z : V_{\mathbb C} \to V_{\mathbb C}$ with $m_z(v \otimes w) = v \otimes (zw)$; these satisfy $m_1 = \operatorname{id}$, $m_z m_{z'} = m_{zz'}$, $m_{z + z'} = m_z + m_{z'}$, and $m_a = a \cdot \operatorname{id}$ for real $a$. Consequently $z \cdot t := m_z(t)$ makes $V_{\mathbb C}$ a complex vector space whose underlying real structure is the given one. (b) $(e_1 \otimes 1, \dots, e_n \otimes 1)$ is a $\mathbb C$-basis of $V_{\mathbb C}$, so $\dim_{\mathbb C} V_{\mathbb C} = \dim_{\mathbb R} V$; every element of $V_{\mathbb C}$ can be written uniquely as $u \otimes 1 + u' \otimes i$ with $u, u' \in V$. (c) For every $\mathbb R$-linear $T : V \to V$ the map $T \otimes \operatorname{id}_{\mathbb C}$ is $\mathbb C$-linear, and its matrix in the basis of (b) is the real matrix of $T$ in $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$.
>
> **Full proof.** **(a)** For fixed $z$, the map $V \times \mathbb C \to V_{\mathbb C}$, $(v, w) \mapsto v \otimes (zw)$, is $\mathbb R$-bilinear (multiplication by $z$ is $\mathbb R$-linear on $\mathbb C$, and $\otimes$ is bilinear), so $m_z$ exists and is unique by the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|universal property]] (over the field $\mathbb R$, with $\mathbb C$ as a two-dimensional real vector space). Each identity is checked on elementary tensors, which span ([[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]]): $m_1(v \otimes w) = v \otimes w$; $m_z m_{z'}(v \otimes w) = m_z(v \otimes z'w) = v \otimes zz'w = m_{zz'}(v \otimes w)$; $m_{z+z'}(v \otimes w) = v \otimes (z + z')w = v \otimes zw + v \otimes z'w = (m_z + m_{z'})(v \otimes w)$ (bilinearity of $\otimes$); and for $a \in \mathbb R$, $m_a(v \otimes w) = v \otimes aw = a(v \otimes w)$ (bilinearity). The axioms of a complex vector space for $z \cdot t := m_z t$ are: additivity in $t$ ($m_z$ is additive), additivity in $z$ ($m_{z+z'} = m_z + m_{z'}$), associativity $z(z' t) = (zz')t$ ($m_z m_{z'} = m_{zz'}$), and $1 \cdot t = t$; the real scalar multiplication is recovered by $m_a = a\operatorname{id}$.
>
> **(b)** By the basis theorem for tensor products, $\{e_j \otimes 1, e_j \otimes i : j = 1, \dots, n\}$ is a *real* basis of $V_{\mathbb C}$ (tensor the basis $(e_j)$ of $V$ with the basis $(1, i)$ of $\mathbb C$). *Spanning over $\mathbb C$:* $e_j \otimes i = m_i(e_j \otimes 1) = i \cdot (e_j \otimes 1)$, so every real basis vector, hence every element, is a $\mathbb C$-linear combination of the $e_j \otimes 1$. *Independence over $\mathbb C$:* if $\sum_j (a_j + i b_j)(e_j \otimes 1) = 0$ with $a_j, b_j \in \mathbb R$, then, expanding $(a_j + i b_j) \cdot (e_j \otimes 1) = a_j (e_j \otimes 1) + b_j (e_j \otimes i)$ (by (a)), we get $\sum_j a_j (e_j \otimes 1) + b_j (e_j \otimes i) = 0$, and real linear independence forces all $a_j = b_j = 0$. The unique representation $u \otimes 1 + u' \otimes i$ follows by grouping the real basis expansion: $\sum_j a_j e_j \otimes 1 + \sum_j b_j e_j \otimes i = (\sum_j a_j e_j) \otimes 1 + (\sum_j b_j e_j) \otimes i$ (bilinearity), with $u = \sum a_j e_j$, $u' = \sum b_j e_j$ uniquely determined.
>
> **(c)** $T \otimes \operatorname{id}_{\mathbb C}$ exists by the universal property applied to the bilinear map $(v, w) \mapsto Tv \otimes w$. For $\mathbb C$-linearity it suffices, given $\mathbb R$-linearity, to show $(T \otimes \operatorname{id}) m_z = m_z (T \otimes \operatorname{id})$ for each $z$; on elementary tensors, $(T \otimes \operatorname{id}) m_z (v \otimes w) = (T \otimes \operatorname{id})(v \otimes zw) = Tv \otimes zw = m_z(Tv \otimes w) = m_z (T \otimes \operatorname{id})(v \otimes w)$, and elementary tensors span. For the matrix, write $T e_j = \sum_i T_{ij} e_i$ with $T_{ij} \in \mathbb R$; then $(T \otimes \operatorname{id})(e_j \otimes 1) = T e_j \otimes 1 = \sum_i T_{ij} (e_i \otimes 1)$ (bilinearity), so the matrix in the basis $(e_i \otimes 1)$ is $(T_{ij})$, a real matrix regarded as complex. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary A (each of the six constructions is a representation).** *Let $\varrho, \varrho_1, \varrho_2$ be representations of the Lie group $G$ as in the Definition. Then $\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2$, $\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2$, $\varrho^{\otimes k}$, $\Lambda^k \varrho$, $\odot^k \varrho$, $\varrho^*$, and (for real $\varrho$) $\varrho_{\mathbb C}$ are representations of $G$: for every $g$ the prescribed map is a linear automorphism, the assignment is a group homomorphism, and it is smooth.*

> [!note]- Proof of Corollary A
> We treat the constructions in order; in each case the three items to show are (i) automorphism, (ii) homomorphism, (iii) smoothness, and we invoke the hypotheses that $\varrho_j(gh) = \varrho_j(g)\varrho_j(h)$, $\varrho_j(e) = \operatorname{id}$, and $\varrho_j$ is smooth, by name where used.
>
> **Direct sum.** (i) $(\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(g)$ is linear because each component is linear and the operations on $V_1 \oplus V_2$ are componentwise; it is bijective because $(\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(g^{-1})$ is a two-sided inverse: $(\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(g)(\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(g^{-1})(v_1 \oplus v_2) = \varrho_1(g)\varrho_1(g^{-1}) v_1 \oplus \varrho_2(g)\varrho_2(g^{-1}) v_2 = v_1 \oplus v_2$ (homomorphism property of $\varrho_1, \varrho_2$ and $\varrho_j(e) = \operatorname{id}$), and in the other order $(\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(g^{-1})(\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(g)(v_1 \oplus v_2) = \varrho_1(g^{-1})\varrho_1(g) v_1 \oplus \varrho_2(g^{-1})\varrho_2(g) v_2 = v_1 \oplus v_2$ by the same two facts. (ii) For $g, h \in G$,
> $$(\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(gh)(v_1 \oplus v_2) = \varrho_1(gh) v_1 \oplus \varrho_2(gh) v_2 = \varrho_1(g)\varrho_1(h) v_1 \oplus \varrho_2(g)\varrho_2(h) v_2 = (\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(g)\big((\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2)(h)(v_1 \oplus v_2)\big) \qquad \text{(definition; } \varrho_j \text{ homomorphisms; definition twice)}.$$
> (iii) In a basis of $V_1 \oplus V_2$ formed by a basis of $V_1$ followed by a basis of $V_2$, the matrix is block diagonal with blocks $[\varrho_1(g)]$ and $[\varrho_2(g)]$ and zeros elsewhere (the definition, read on basis vectors $e_i \oplus 0$ and $0 \oplus f_j$); every entry is an entry of $[\varrho_1(g)]$, an entry of $[\varrho_2(g)]$, or the constant $0$, hence smooth by Lemma 1 applied to the smooth maps $\varrho_1, \varrho_2$; by Lemma 1 again, $\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_2$ is smooth.
>
> **Tensor product.** (i) Existence and linearity of $(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g)$ were established in the Definition via the universal property. It is invertible with inverse $(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g^{-1})$: on elementary tensors, $(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g)(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g^{-1})(v_1 \otimes v_2) = \varrho_1(g)\varrho_1(g^{-1}) v_1 \otimes \varrho_2(g)\varrho_2(g^{-1}) v_2 = v_1 \otimes v_2$ (definition twice; $\varrho_j$ homomorphisms with $\varrho_j(e) = \operatorname{id}$), so the composite agrees with the identity on the spanning set of elementary tensors ([[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]]) and hence equals it; in the other order, $(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g^{-1})(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g)(v_1 \otimes v_2) = \varrho_1(g^{-1})\varrho_1(g) v_1 \otimes \varrho_2(g^{-1})\varrho_2(g) v_2 = v_1 \otimes v_2$ by the same two facts, and again elementary tensors span. (ii) For $g, h \in G$ the linear maps $(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(gh)$ and $(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g)(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(h)$ agree on elementary tensors:
> $$(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g)\big((\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(h)(v_1 \otimes v_2)\big) = \varrho_1(g)\varrho_1(h) v_1 \otimes \varrho_2(g)\varrho_2(h) v_2 = \varrho_1(gh) v_1 \otimes \varrho_2(gh) v_2 = (\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(gh)(v_1 \otimes v_2) \qquad \text{(definition twice; } \varrho_j \text{ homomorphisms; definition)},$$
> hence they are equal. (iii) In the basis $(e_i \otimes f_j)$ of $V_1 \otimes V_2$, with $\varrho_1(g) e_{j} = \sum_i a_{ij}(g) e_i$ and $\varrho_2(g) f_{j'} = \sum_{i'} b_{i'j'}(g) f_{i'}$,
> $$(\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2)(g)(e_j \otimes f_{j'}) = \varrho_1(g) e_j \otimes \varrho_2(g) f_{j'} = \sum_{i, i'} a_{ij}(g) b_{i'j'}(g)\, e_i \otimes f_{i'} \qquad \text{(definition; bilinearity of } \otimes),$$
> so the matrix entries are the products $a_{ij}(g) b_{i'j'}(g)$ (the Kronecker product of the two matrices); products of smooth functions are smooth, and $a_{ij}, b_{i'j'}$ are smooth by Lemma 1 applied to $\varrho_1, \varrho_2$, so $\varrho_1 \otimes \varrho_2$ is smooth by Lemma 1. **The $k$-fold power** $\varrho^{\otimes k}(g) = \varrho(g)^{\otimes k}$: (i) exists by Lemma 2(d) and is invertible with inverse $\varrho(g^{-1})^{\otimes k}$ since $\varrho(g)^{\otimes k}\varrho(g^{-1})^{\otimes k} = (\varrho(g)\varrho(g^{-1}))^{\otimes k} = \operatorname{id}_V^{\otimes k} = \operatorname{id}$ (Lemma 2(d), homomorphism property, Lemma 2(d)); (ii) $\varrho(gh)^{\otimes k} = (\varrho(g)\varrho(h))^{\otimes k} = \varrho(g)^{\otimes k}\varrho(h)^{\otimes k}$ (homomorphism property; Lemma 2(d)); (iii) the entries are the products $\varrho(g)_{i_1 j_1} \cdots \varrho(g)_{i_k j_k}$ (Lemma 2(d)), smooth by Lemma 1.
>
> **Exterior and symmetric powers.** By Lemma 5(b) applied to $T = \varrho(g)$, the subspaces $\Lambda^k V$ and $\odot^k V$ are invariant under every $\varrho^{\otimes k}(g)$. By the paragraph just proved, $\varrho^{\otimes k}$ is a representation on $V^{\otimes k}$. Lemma 6, applied to $\phi = \varrho^{\otimes k}$, $W = V^{\otimes k}$, and $U = \Lambda^k V$ (respectively $U = \odot^k V$), shows that $\Lambda^k \varrho = \varrho^{\otimes k}|_{\Lambda^k V}$ (respectively $\odot^k \varrho$) is a representation: (i), (ii), (iii) are all contained in Lemma 6. The formulas $(\Lambda^k \varrho)(g)(v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_k) = \varrho(g) v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \varrho(g) v_k$ and $(\odot^k \varrho)(g)(v_1 \odot \dots \odot v_k) = \varrho(g) v_1 \odot \dots \odot \varrho(g) v_k$ are Lemma 5(c) with $T = \varrho(g)$; they determine $\Lambda^k \varrho(g)$ and $\odot^k \varrho(g)$ because decomposable wedges (respectively symmetric products) span $\Lambda^k V$ (respectively $\odot^k V$) by Lemma 4.
>
> **Dual.** (i) $\varrho^*(g) = \varrho(g^{-1})^*$ is linear ([[Def - Dual Map]]: the dual of a linear map is linear). It is invertible with inverse $\varrho^*(g^{-1}) = \varrho(g)^*$: by contravariance of the dual, $(ST)^* = T^* S^*$ ([[Def - Dual Map]]), we have $\varrho(g^{-1})^* \varrho(g)^* = (\varrho(g)\varrho(g^{-1}))^* = (\operatorname{id}_V)^* = \operatorname{id}_{V^*}$ and $\varrho(g)^*\varrho(g^{-1})^* = (\varrho(g^{-1})\varrho(g))^* = \operatorname{id}_{V^*}$ (homomorphism property of $\varrho$ and $\varrho(e) = \operatorname{id}$; the dual of the identity is the identity). (ii) This is Corollary B below, proved in full there. (iii) The map $g \mapsto \varrho(g^{-1}) = (\varrho \circ \operatorname{inv})(g)$ is smooth as a composite of the smooth maps $\operatorname{inv} : G \to G$ (smooth by the definition of a Lie group) and $\varrho$ (smooth by hypothesis); by Lemma 1 its entries $g \mapsto \varrho(g^{-1})_{ij}$ are smooth. By [[Thm - Matrix of Dual Map is Transpose]] — for a linear map $T : V \to W$ with matrix $\mathcal M(T)$ in bases of $V$ and $W$, the matrix of the dual map $T^*$ in the dual bases is $\mathcal M(T)^t$ — the entries of $\varrho^*(g) = \varrho(g^{-1})^*$ in the dual basis $(\varepsilon^i)$ are $\varrho^*(g)_{ij} = \varrho(g^{-1})_{ji}$, smooth functions; hence $\varrho^*$ is smooth by Lemma 1. Finally, the pairing identity: $\big(\varrho^*(g)\lambda\big)\big(\varrho(g) v\big) = \big(\lambda \circ \varrho(g^{-1})\big)\big(\varrho(g) v\big) = \lambda\big(\varrho(g^{-1})\varrho(g) v\big) = \lambda(v)$ (definition of $\varrho^*$; definition of composition; homomorphism property).
>
> **Complexification.** (i) $\varrho_{\mathbb C}(g) = \varrho(g) \otimes \operatorname{id}_{\mathbb C}$ exists, is $\mathbb C$-linear, and has matrix $[\varrho(g)]$ in the basis $(e_i \otimes 1)$, by Lemma 7(c). It is invertible with inverse $\varrho_{\mathbb C}(g^{-1})$: on elementary tensors $\varrho_{\mathbb C}(g)\varrho_{\mathbb C}(g^{-1})(v \otimes w) = \varrho(g)\varrho(g^{-1}) v \otimes w = v \otimes w$ (definition twice; homomorphism property), and elementary tensors span. (ii) $\varrho_{\mathbb C}(g)\varrho_{\mathbb C}(h)(v \otimes w) = \varrho(g)\varrho(h) v \otimes w = \varrho(gh) v \otimes w = \varrho_{\mathbb C}(gh)(v \otimes w)$ (definition twice; homomorphism property; definition), and elementary tensors span. (iii) The matrix entries of $\varrho_{\mathbb C}(g)$ in the complex basis $(e_i \otimes 1)$ are the real entries $\varrho(g)_{ij}$ (Lemma 7(c)), which are smooth real-valued functions by Lemma 1 applied to $\varrho$, hence smooth as complex-valued functions (imaginary part identically zero); so $\varrho_{\mathbb C}$ is smooth by Lemma 1 (complex case).
>
> Therefore each of the six constructions produces a representation of $G$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary B (Bär's Theorem T1.3.3: the naive dual is an anti-homomorphism; the inverse repairs it).** *Let $\varrho : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ be a representation. (a) The map $\varrho^\sharp : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V^*)$, $\varrho^\sharp(g) := \varrho(g)^*$, satisfies $\varrho^\sharp(g_1 g_2) = \varrho^\sharp(g_2)\varrho^\sharp(g_1)$ for all $g_1, g_2 \in G$; it is a group homomorphism if and only if $\varrho(g_1)^* \varrho(g_2)^* = \varrho(g_2)^* \varrho(g_1)^*$ for all $g_1, g_2$, which fails in general. (b) The map $\varrho^* : G \to \operatorname{Aut}(V^*)$, $\varrho^*(g) := \varrho(g^{-1})^*$, is a group homomorphism: $\varrho^*(g_1 g_2) = \varrho^*(g_1)\varrho^*(g_2)$ for all $g_1, g_2 \in G$.*

> [!note]- Proof of Corollary B
> We are given the representation $\varrho$, and we need to show the two composition rules. Both rest on the contravariance of the dual map, $(ST)^* = T^* S^*$ for linear maps $S, T : V \to V$, which is proved on [[Def - Dual Map]] by the computation $((ST)^*\lambda)(v) = \lambda(STv) = (S^*\lambda)(Tv) = (T^*(S^*\lambda))(v)$.
>
> **(a) The naive candidate reverses order.** For $g_1, g_2 \in G$,
> $$\varrho^\sharp(g_1 g_2) = \varrho(g_1 g_2)^* = \big(\varrho(g_1)\varrho(g_2)\big)^* = \varrho(g_2)^* \varrho(g_1)^* = \varrho^\sharp(g_2)\varrho^\sharp(g_1) \qquad \text{(definition of } \varrho^\sharp \text{; } \varrho \text{ is a homomorphism; contravariance of the dual; definition of } \varrho^\sharp).$$
> Hence $\varrho^\sharp$ is a homomorphism if and only if $\varrho^\sharp(g_2)\varrho^\sharp(g_1) = \varrho^\sharp(g_1)\varrho^\sharp(g_2)$ for all $g_1, g_2$, that is, if and only if the automorphisms $\varrho(g)^*$ commute pairwise. That this fails in general is shown by the explicit non-instance below ($G = GL(2; \mathbb R)$, $\varrho = \varrho_{st}$), where two matrices with $(AB)^t \ne A^t B^t$ are exhibited. This is Bär's Remark R1.3.3: "$(\varrho(g_1)\varrho(g_2))^* = \varrho(g_2)^*\varrho(g_1)^*$ reverses the order."
>
> **(b) Two reversals cancel.** For $g_1, g_2 \in G$,
> $$\varrho^*(g_1 g_2) = \varrho\big((g_1 g_2)^{-1}\big)^* = \varrho\big(g_2^{-1} g_1^{-1}\big)^* \qquad \text{(definition of } \varrho^* \text{; } (g_1 g_2)^{-1} = g_2^{-1} g_1^{-1} \text{ in any group)}$$
> $$= \big(\varrho(g_2^{-1})\varrho(g_1^{-1})\big)^* = \varrho(g_1^{-1})^*\varrho(g_2^{-1})^* = \varrho^*(g_1)\varrho^*(g_2) \qquad \text{(} \varrho \text{ is a homomorphism; contravariance of the dual; definition of } \varrho^* \text{ twice)}.$$
> Therefore $\varrho^*$ is a group homomorphism; together with the smoothness and invertibility established in Corollary A, it is a representation. The mechanism is that inversion in $G$ and dualisation in $\mathbf{Vect}$ are both order-reversing, and their composite is order-preserving. $\blacksquare$

**Is an instance — $\Lambda^n \varrho_{st} = \det$ for $G = GL(n; \mathbb K)$.** Let $\varrho_{st} = \operatorname{id} : GL(n; \mathbb K) \to \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb K^n)$ and let $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ be the standard basis. By Lemma 4(a), $\Lambda^n \mathbb K^n$ is one-dimensional with basis the single vector $\omega := e_1 \wedge \dots \wedge e_n$ (the only increasing multi-index of length $n$ is $(1, \dots, n)$). Hence $\operatorname{Aut}(\Lambda^n \mathbb K^n) = GL(1; \mathbb K) = \mathbb K^\times$, and $\Lambda^n \varrho_{st}(A)$ is multiplication by a scalar $\chi(A)$ defined by $\Lambda^n\varrho_{st}(A)\,\omega = \chi(A)\,\omega$. We claim $\chi(A) = \det A$. Consider the map $\alpha : (\mathbb K^n)^n \to \Lambda^n \mathbb K^n \cong \mathbb K$, $\alpha(v_1, \dots, v_n) :=$ the coefficient of $\omega$ in $v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_n$. By Lemma 3(e), $(v_1, \dots, v_n) \mapsto v_1 \wedge \dots \wedge v_n$ is $n$-linear and alternating, and taking the coefficient of $\omega$ is linear, so $\alpha$ is an [[Def - Alternating Multilinear Form|alternating $n$-linear form]] on $\mathbb K^n$ with $\alpha(e_1, \dots, e_n) = 1$. By the characterisation of the determinant as the unique alternating $n$-linear form on $\mathbb K^n$ taking the value $1$ on the standard basis ([[Def - Determinant]], characterisation 2, which rests on $\dim (\mathbb K^n)^{(n)}_{\mathrm{alt}} = 1$ proved on [[Def - Alternating Multilinear Form]]), $\alpha(v_1, \dots, v_n) = \det(v_1 \mid \dots \mid v_n)$, the determinant of the matrix with columns $v_j$. Now
$$\Lambda^n \varrho_{st}(A)\, \omega = A e_1 \wedge \dots \wedge A e_n = \alpha(A e_1, \dots, A e_n)\, \omega = \det(A e_1 \mid \dots \mid A e_n)\, \omega = \det(A)\, \omega \qquad \text{(Lemma 5(c); definition of } \alpha \text{; the characterisation; the columns of } A \text{ are the } A e_j),$$
so $\chi(A) = \det A$, that is, $\Lambda^n \varrho_{st} = \det : GL(n; \mathbb K) \to GL(1; \mathbb K)$. As a check on the clause "homomorphism" in Corollary A: since $\Lambda^n \varrho_{st}$ is a homomorphism, $\det(AB) = \chi(AB) = \chi(A)\chi(B) = \det(A)\det(B)$, which is consistent with [[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative]] — for $n \times n$ matrices $A, B$, $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$ — and indeed rederives it from Lemma 2(d) and Lemma 6. For a matrix group $G \subset GL(n; \mathbb K)$ the standard representation is the restriction of $\operatorname{id}_{GL(n;\mathbb K)}$ to $G$, and the displayed computation holds verbatim for $A \in G$ (nothing in it used more than $A \in GL(n; \mathbb K)$), so $\Lambda^n \varrho_{st} = \det|_G$; for $G = SL(n; \mathbb K)$, $SO(n)$, or $SU(n)$ this is the trivial representation, and for $O(n)$ it is the sign character $A \mapsto \det A \in \{\pm 1\}$.

**Is an instance — the symmetric powers of the standard representation of $SU(2)$, and $\dim \odot^k \mathbb C^2 = k + 1$.** Let $\varrho_1 = \varrho_{st} : SU(2) \to \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb C^2)$ and $\varrho_k := \odot^k \varrho_1$ for $k \ge 2$, as in Bär's Example 1.3.15 and on [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]]. By Lemma 4(b) with $n = 2$, a basis of $\odot^k \mathbb C^2$ is indexed by the nondecreasing multi-indices $(1, \dots, 1, 2, \dots, 2)$ with $j$ entries equal to $2$, $j = 0, 1, \dots, k$; these are the $k + 1$ vectors
$$e_1^{\odot k},\ e_1^{\odot (k-1)} \odot e_2,\ \dots,\ e_1 \odot e_2^{\odot (k-1)},\ e_2^{\odot k}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \dim_{\mathbb C} \odot^k \mathbb C^2 = k + 1 = \binom{2 + k - 1}{k},$$
which is Bär's basis $e_1 \odot \dots \odot e_1,\ e_2 \odot e_1 \odot \dots \odot e_1,\ \dots,\ e_2 \odot \dots \odot e_2$ (the order of factors is immaterial by the symmetry in Lemma 3(e)). For the diagonal element $g = \operatorname{diag}(e^{i\varphi}, e^{-i\varphi})$, Lemma 5(c) gives $\varrho_k(g)\big(e_1^{\odot(k-j)} \odot e_2^{\odot j}\big) = (e^{i\varphi} e_1)^{\odot (k-j)} \odot (e^{-i\varphi} e_2)^{\odot j} = e^{i(k-2j)\varphi}\, e_1^{\odot(k-j)} \odot e_2^{\odot j}$ (multilinearity, Lemma 3(e)), so in this basis $\varrho_k(g)$ is diagonal with entries $e^{ik\varphi}, e^{i(k-2)\varphi}, \dots, e^{-ik\varphi}$; for $k = 2$ these are $e^{2i\varphi}, 1, e^{-2i\varphi}$, matching Bär's matrix for $\varrho_2$. The irreducibility of $\varrho_k$ and the identification $\varrho_2 \cong (\operatorname{Ad}_{SU(2)})_{\mathbb C}$ are proved on [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] and drilled in [[Ex - rho_2 of SU(2) is the Complexified Adjoint Representation]]; here we have only verified that the objects exist and have the stated dimension.

**Is an instance — tensor products and duals of the characters of $U(1)$.** For $k \in \mathbb Z$ let $\varrho_k : U(1) \to GL(1; \mathbb C) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb C)$, $\varrho_k(z) = z^k$ (a representation: $(zz')^k = z^k z'^k$, and $z \mapsto z^k$ is smooth on $U(1) \subset \mathbb C$). *Tensor product.* The map $\mathbb C \times \mathbb C \to \mathbb C$, $(u, w) \mapsto uw$, is bilinear, so by the universal property it induces a linear map $\mu : \mathbb C \otimes \mathbb C \to \mathbb C$ with $\mu(u \otimes w) = uw$; it is an isomorphism because both spaces are one-dimensional (basis $1 \otimes 1$, by the basis theorem) and $\mu(1 \otimes 1) = 1 \ne 0$. It intertwines $\varrho_k \otimes \varrho_l$ with $\varrho_{k+l}$:
$$\mu\big((\varrho_k \otimes \varrho_l)(z)(u \otimes w)\big) = \mu(z^k u \otimes z^l w) = z^{k+l} uw = \varrho_{k+l}(z)\, \mu(u \otimes w) \qquad \text{(definition of the tensor product representation; definition of } \mu \text{; definition of } \varrho_{k+l}),$$
and since $u \otimes w$ span, $\mu \circ (\varrho_k \otimes \varrho_l)(z) = \varrho_{k+l}(z) \circ \mu$ for every $z$; so $\varrho_k \otimes \varrho_l \cong \varrho_{k+l}$. (Bär's text writes "$z^k v \otimes z^l w$" for "$z^k u \otimes z^l w$" in this computation, a misprint of $u$ as $v$.) *Dual.* For $\lambda \in \mathbb C^* = \mathcal L(\mathbb C, \mathbb C)$, $\varrho_k^*(z)(\lambda) = \lambda \circ \varrho_k(z^{-1}) = \lambda \circ (\text{multiplication by } z^{-k}) = z^{-k}\lambda$ (definition of $\varrho^*$; linearity of $\lambda$), so under the isomorphism $\mathbb C^* \to \mathbb C$, $\lambda \mapsto \lambda(1)$, the dual representation $\varrho_k^*$ becomes multiplication by $z^{-k}$, that is, $\varrho_k^* \cong \varrho_{-k}$. These two identities are Bär's T1.3.4 and are drilled in [[Ex - Tensor Products and Duals of the Representations of U(1)]].

**Is an instance — complexifying the real rotation representation of $U(1)$ gives $\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_{-1}$.** Let $\tau : U(1) \to \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb R^2)$ be the real representation $\tau(e^{i\theta}) = R_\theta := \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$, which is $\varrho_1$ on $\mathbb C = \mathbb R^2$ (with $1 \leftrightarrow (1, 0)$, $i \leftrightarrow (0, 1)$) regarded as a real representation: it is a homomorphism because $R_\theta R_{\theta'} = R_{\theta + \theta'}$ (angle-addition formulas) and smooth because its entries are $\cos\theta = \operatorname{Re} z$, $\sin\theta = \operatorname{Im} z$. By the Definition, item 6, and Lemma 7(c), $\tau_{\mathbb C}(e^{i\theta})$ is the same matrix $R_\theta$ acting on $\mathbb C^2$. Put $f_+ := (1, -i)^t$ and $f_- := (1, i)^t$ in $\mathbb C^2$; they are linearly independent (the determinant $\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -i & i \end{pmatrix} = 2i \ne 0$). We compute
$$R_\theta f_+ = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta - i\cos\theta \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{i\theta} \\ -i e^{i\theta} \end{pmatrix} = e^{i\theta} f_+ \qquad \text{(matrix multiplication; } -ie^{i\theta} = -i\cos\theta + \sin\theta),$$
$$R_\theta f_- = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta - i\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta + i\cos\theta \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-i\theta} \\ i e^{-i\theta} \end{pmatrix} = e^{-i\theta} f_- \qquad \text{(matrix multiplication; } ie^{-i\theta} = i\cos\theta + \sin\theta).$$
Hence in the basis $(f_+, f_-)$ of $\mathbb C^2$ the representation $\tau_{\mathbb C}$ is $\operatorname{diag}(z, z^{-1})$ with $z = e^{i\theta}$, which is $(\varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_{-1})(z)$ by item 1 of the Definition; the change of basis $T : \mathbb C^2 \to \mathbb C^2$ sending $f_\pm$ to the standard basis is an equivalence $\tau_{\mathbb C} \cong \varrho_1 \oplus \varrho_{-1}$. This verifies the claim in Axiom Motivation that complexifying the underlying real representation of a complex representation does not return it: $\tau_{\mathbb C}$ is two-dimensional and contains the conjugate character $\varrho_{-1}$ as well as $\varrho_1$.

**Is NOT an instance — the naive dual $g \mapsto \varrho(g)^*$ for $G = GL(2; \mathbb R)$.** Take $\varrho = \varrho_{st}$ on $\mathbb R^2$ and the standard basis with its dual basis, so that by [[Thm - Matrix of Dual Map is Transpose]] the matrix of $\varrho^\sharp(A) = A^*$ is $A^t$. Let
$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \text{so} \quad AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad (AB)^t = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad A^t B^t = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$$
(all by direct matrix multiplication; both $A$ and $B$ have determinant $1$, so they lie in $GL(2; \mathbb R)$). Thus $\varrho^\sharp(AB) = (AB)^t \ne A^t B^t = \varrho^\sharp(A)\varrho^\sharp(B)$, and $\varrho^\sharp$ is not a homomorphism, confirming Corollary B(a) on a concrete pair. The repaired map $\varrho^*(A) = ((A^{-1})^*)$ has matrix $(A^{-1})^t$, and for the same pair $((AB)^{-1})^t = (B^{-1}A^{-1})^t = (A^{-1})^t (B^{-1})^t$, as Corollary B(b) predicts. The identification of $\varrho_{st}^*$ with $A \mapsto (A^{-1})^t$ is drilled in [[Ex - The Dual Representation Requires the Inverse]].

**Is NOT an instance — a non-diagonal action on $V \otimes V$ does not preserve $\odot^2 V$.** Let $G = GL(2; \mathbb R)$, $V = \mathbb R^2$, and consider $\phi := \varrho_{st} \otimes \varrho_{\mathrm{triv}}$ on $V \otimes V$, where $\varrho_{\mathrm{triv}}(g) = \operatorname{id}_V$; by Corollary A this *is* a representation, with $\phi(g)(v \otimes w) = gv \otimes w$. Let $g = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (so $g e_1 = e_2$). The tensor $e_1 \otimes e_1$ is symmetric: $P_{(1\,2)}(e_1 \otimes e_1) = e_1 \otimes e_1$, so it lies in $\odot^2 V$ by Lemma 3(d). But $\phi(g)(e_1 \otimes e_1) = e_2 \otimes e_1$, and $P_{(1\,2)}(e_2 \otimes e_1) = e_1 \otimes e_2 \ne e_2 \otimes e_1$ because $e_1 \otimes e_2$ and $e_2 \otimes e_1$ are distinct members of the basis $(e_i \otimes e_j)$ of $V \otimes V$. So $\phi(g)(e_1 \otimes e_1) \notin \odot^2 V$: the subspace $\odot^2 V$ is not $\phi(G)$-invariant, and there is no "symmetric square of $\phi$". The construction $\odot^k \varrho$ exists only for the diagonal action $\varrho^{\otimes k}$ of a single representation, where Lemma 5 applies; the hypothesis "the same $\varrho(g)$ in every factor" in Lemma 5(a) is what the example violates ($g$ in the first factor, $\operatorname{id}$ in the second).

**Corollary C (double dual).** *For every representation $\varrho$, the double dual $\varrho^{**}$ is equivalent to $\varrho$.* Proof: the evaluation map $\operatorname{ev} : V \to V^{**}$, $\operatorname{ev}(v)(\lambda) := \lambda(v)$, is a linear isomorphism for finite-dimensional $V$ ([[Thm - Dimension of Dual Space]] gives $\dim V^{**} = \dim V$, and $\operatorname{ev}$ is injective because if $\lambda(v) = 0$ for all $\lambda$ then $v = 0$, taking $\lambda$ to be a dual basis vector with $\lambda(v) \ne 0$ whenever $v \ne 0$). It intertwines: for $v \in V$, $\lambda \in V^*$, and $g \in G$,
$$\big(\varrho^{**}(g)\operatorname{ev}(v)\big)(\lambda) = \operatorname{ev}(v)\big(\varrho^*(g^{-1})\lambda\big) = \big(\varrho^*(g^{-1})\lambda\big)(v) = \big(\lambda \circ \varrho(g)\big)(v) = \lambda(\varrho(g) v) = \operatorname{ev}(\varrho(g) v)(\lambda) \qquad \text{(definition of } \varrho^{**} = (\varrho^*)^* \text{; definition of } \operatorname{ev} \text{; definition of } \varrho^* \text{ with } (g^{-1})^{-1} = g \text{; composition; definition of } \operatorname{ev}),$$
so $\varrho^{**}(g) \circ \operatorname{ev} = \operatorname{ev} \circ \varrho(g)$ for all $g$, and $\operatorname{ev}$ is an equivalence. $\blacksquare$

**Calibration check.** First, verify that $\dim(\mathbb C^2 \otimes \mathbb C^2) = 4 = 3 + 1 = \dim \odot^2 \mathbb C^2 + \dim \Lambda^2 \mathbb C^2$ using Lemma 4, and identify the alternating line: it is spanned by $e_1 \wedge e_2 = \tfrac12 (e_1 \otimes e_2 - e_2 \otimes e_1)$, on which $\Lambda^2 \varrho_{st}$ of $SU(2)$ acts by $\det = 1$, that is, trivially — this is the invariant vector of [[Ex - Decomposing the Tensor Square of the Standard Representation of SU(2)]]. Second, verify from the example above that $\Lambda^n \varrho_{st}(A) = \det A$ by computing $A e_1 \wedge A e_2$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ directly: $(a e_1 + c e_2) \wedge (b e_1 + d e_2) = ad\, e_1 \wedge e_2 + cb\, e_2 \wedge e_1 = (ad - bc)\, e_1 \wedge e_2$, using bilinearity, $e_i \wedge e_i = 0$, and $e_2 \wedge e_1 = -e_1 \wedge e_2$ (Lemma 3(e)). Third, confirm that for $U(1)$ the two rules $\varrho_k \otimes \varrho_l \cong \varrho_{k+l}$ and $\varrho_k^* \cong \varrho_{-k}$ are consistent with Corollary C: $\varrho_k^{**} \cong \varrho_{-(-k)} = \varrho_k$. If you can also say in one sentence why $\odot^k$ and $\Lambda^k$ need the same $g$ in every tensor slot while $\otimes$ of two different representations does not, you have understood the page.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The representation theory of $U(1)$ and $SU(2)$ *(from this chapter)*
> With direct sums, symmetric powers, tensor products, duals, and complexifications in hand, [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]] can state and prove that every complex representation of $U(1)$ is a direct sum of the characters $\varrho_k$ and every complex representation of $SU(2)$ a direct sum of the symmetric powers $\odot^k \varrho_{st}$, and can identify $\odot^2 \varrho_{st}$ with the complexified adjoint representation.

> [!tip] Operations on associated bundles *(from Gauge Theory III)*
> A principal $G$-bundle $P$ and a representation $\varrho$ give the [[Def - Associated Bundle|associated bundle]] $P \times_\varrho V$; the constructions of this page become the [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles|operations on vector bundles]] — Whitney sum, tensor product, exterior powers, dual, complexification — performed on the representation before associating. In particular the determinant line bundle of a rank-$n$ bundle is associated to $\Lambda^n \varrho_{st} = \det$, and the tensor powers $L^{\otimes k}$ of the line bundle of a $U(1)$-bundle are associated to $\varrho_k$.

> [!tip] Conjugate representations and real forms *(from representation theory; not developed in this series)*
> For a complex representation $\varrho$ on $V$ one may also form the **complex conjugate representation** $\bar\varrho$ on the conjugate space $\bar V$ (same set, scalar multiplication by $\bar z$), with $\bar\varrho(g) = \varrho(g)$ as a map of sets; the complexification of the underlying real representation of $\varrho$ is $\varrho \oplus \bar\varrho$, which is what the $U(1)$ example above exhibits with $\bar\varrho_1 = \varrho_{-1}$. Whether a complex representation is the complexification of a real one — whether it has a **real form** — is decided by the Frobenius–Schur indicator; neither notion is used in this series.
