# Notation

Throughout, $G$ is a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] with identity $e$, and $\mathfrak{g}=T_eG$ is its [[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group|Lie algebra]], the tangent space at the identity equipped with the bracket $[\cdot,\cdot]$ of [[Def - Left-Invariant Vector Field|left-invariant vector fields]]. The [[Def - Exponential Map of a Lie Group|exponential map]] is $\exp\colon\mathfrak{g}\to G$; for $X\in\mathfrak{g}$ the curve $t\mapsto\exp(tX)$ is the unique [[Def - One-Parameter Subgroup|one-parameter subgroup]] with initial velocity $X$, so that $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}\exp(tX)=X$ and $\exp\big((s+t)X\big)=\exp(sX)\exp(tX)$.

$M$ is a smooth manifold; $\mathfrak{X}(M)$ denotes the space of smooth vector fields on $M$, a vector field being a smooth section of the tangent bundle $TM$. A **smooth left action** of $G$ on $M$ is a smooth map $\theta\colon G\times M\to M$, written $\theta(g,x)=g\cdot x$, with $(gh)\cdot x=g\cdot(h\cdot x)$ and $e\cdot x=x$ for all $g,h\in G$ and $x\in M$; we write $\theta_g\colon M\to M$, $\theta_g(x)=g\cdot x$, for the diffeomorphism induced by a fixed $g$, and $\ell_p\colon G\to M$, $\ell_p(g)=g\cdot p$, for the **orbit map** through a fixed $p\in M$ (see [[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group]] and [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions]]). A **smooth right action** is a smooth map $M\times G\to M$, $(p,g)\mapsto p\cdot g$, with $p\cdot(gh)=(p\cdot g)\cdot h$ and $p\cdot e=p$; its orbit map is $r_p\colon G\to M$, $r_p(g)=p\cdot g$.

We write $d_qF\colon T_qN\to T_{F(q)}N'$ for the differential (tangent map) of a smooth map $F\colon N\to N'$ at $q$. For a smooth curve $c\colon I\to N$ we write $c'(t_0)=\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=t_0}c(t)\in T_{c(t_0)}N$ for its velocity.

> [!warning] Convention: three names for one construction
> Bär (source B) writes the orbit map of a **left** action as $R_p\colon G\to M$, $R_p(g)=g\cdot p$, and sets $\bar{X}(p):=d_eR_p(X)$. Because this series reserves $R_g$ for **right translation** on a Lie group and, later, for the right action $R_g(p)=p\cdot g$ on a principal bundle, we rename Bär's orbit map $\ell_p$ and keep his overbar notation $\bar{X}$ for the left-action fundamental field. Haydys (source A, Definition 2.2.10) works on the right and writes the fundamental field of $\xi\in\mathfrak{g}$ as $K_\xi(p):=\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_{0}\big(p\cdot\exp(t\xi)\big)$. The series' standing convention (Lie groups act on bundles on the right) writes this right-action field as $\xi_P$, so that $\xi_P=K_\xi$. The three symbols $\bar{X}$ (Bär, left), $K_\xi$ (Haydys, right) and $\xi_P$ (this series, right) name the same construction applied on the corresponding side; the one-line conversion between the two sides is $p\ast g:=g^{-1}\cdot p$, which turns a left action into a right action and sends $\bar{X}\mapsto-\bar{X}$ under the induced identifications (the sign is the subject of **[[Thm - The Fundamental Vector Field Map is a Lie Algebra Homomorphism for Right Actions]]** and is not needed for the present definition).

# Axiom Motivation

A [[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group|smooth action]] of $G$ on $M$ is, by [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions|the homomorphism reading]], a Lie group homomorphism $g\mapsto\theta_g$ from $G$ into the group $\operatorname{Diff}(M)$ of diffeomorphisms of $M$. This is a global, finite object: to each group element it assigns an entire diffeomorphism. Very often what one actually wants is the *infinitesimal* version of this datum — not "where does the element $g$ send the point $x$", but "in what direction, and how fast, does $x$ begin to move when we switch on the one-parameter subgroup generated by $X\in\mathfrak{g}$". That infinitesimal datum is a vector field on $M$, one for each $X$, and the assignment $X\mapsto(\text{that vector field})$ is the object this page defines. It is the exact analogue, at the level of actions, of the passage from a Lie group $G$ to its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$: there one differentiates the group multiplication near $e$; here one differentiates the action near the identity.

The desideratum is therefore a linear map $\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{X}(M)$ that reads off, from an infinitesimal group direction $X$, the infinitesimal motion it induces at every point of $M$ simultaneously. Three design choices produce it, and each is forced.

**First, differentiate along a curve in $G$ that starts at $e$ with velocity $X$.** An element $X\in\mathfrak{g}=T_eG$ is a velocity at the identity; to feel it, we must move through $G$ starting at $e$ in the direction $X$ and watch what the action does to a fixed point $p$. The natural such curve is the one-parameter subgroup $t\mapsto\exp(tX)$, whose velocity at $t=0$ is exactly $X$. One might worry that the construction depends on this particular choice of curve. It does not: if $c\colon(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\to G$ is *any* smooth curve with $c(0)=e$ and $c'(0)=X$, then by the chain rule
$$\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}c(t)\cdot p=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}\ell_p\big(c(t)\big)=d_e\ell_p\big(c'(0)\big)=d_e\ell_p(X),$$
which is independent of $c$. So the value depends only on $X$ and $p$, and we are entitled to compute it with the convenient representative $\exp(tX)$. If we *dropped* this independence — say, defined the field by a curve that did not start at $e$, or whose velocity was some $X'\neq X$ — we would either be reading the wrong Lie-algebra direction or be measuring the action at the wrong basepoint, and the resulting assignment would fail to be the differential $d_e\ell_p$ and hence would not be linear in $X$.

**Second, differentiate the orbit map, not the diffeomorphism.** There are two smooth maps hiding in the symbol $g\cdot p$: fixing $g$ gives the diffeomorphism $\theta_g\colon x\mapsto g\cdot x$ of $M$, while fixing $p$ gives the orbit map $\ell_p\colon g\mapsto g\cdot p$ from $G$ to $M$. The infinitesimal generator lives on $M$ and must vary the *group* variable, so it is the orbit map $\ell_p$ that we differentiate at $e$; its differential $d_e\ell_p\colon\mathfrak{g}\to T_pM$ deposits, at each $p$, a tangent vector for each $X$. Differentiating $\theta_g$ instead would answer a different question (how the fixed transformation $\theta_g$ distorts tangent vectors), and would not produce a vector field indexed by $\mathfrak{g}$ at all.

**Third, the action must be smooth for the output to be a vector field.** The whole point is that $p\mapsto\bar{X}(p)$ be a *smooth* section of $TM$, an element of $\mathfrak{X}(M)$, and not merely a rough assignment of a tangent vector to each point. Smoothness of the assignment is inherited from smoothness of the action $\theta$, as the smoothness proof below makes precise. If we weakened the hypothesis on $\theta$ from smooth to merely continuous, the pointwise velocity $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_0\exp(tX)\cdot p$ might not even exist, and where it did exist it need not depend smoothly on $p$; the construction would leave $\mathfrak{X}(M)$ and lose every property that makes it useful.

There is one feature that distinguishes fundamental vector fields from arbitrary vector fields, and it is worth naming now because it is exactly what the definition buys and what a naive strengthening would break. **Every fundamental vector field is complete**: its flow is the global one-parameter group $t\mapsto\theta_{\exp(tX)}$, defined for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$, because $\exp(tX)$ is defined for all $t$. This is not an extra axiom we impose; it is a theorem (proved below) that follows from the group structure. It also tells us which vector fields can *not* arise this way: an incomplete field, one whose integral curves escape to infinity in finite time, is never a fundamental vector field of any Lie group action, no matter how the action is chosen. The non-example at the end of the page is exactly such a field. A reader who has understood why completeness is automatic could reconstruct the definition — differentiate the orbit map along $\exp(tX)$ — from the single requirement that the resulting field's flow be a one-parameter group coming from the action.

# The Definition

Let $G$ be a Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}=T_eG$, and let $\theta\colon G\times M\to M$, $\theta(g,x)=g\cdot x$, be a smooth **left** action of $G$ on a smooth manifold $M$. For a point $p\in M$ let $\ell_p\colon G\to M$, $\ell_p(g)=g\cdot p$, be the orbit map, whose differential at the identity is the linear map $d_e\ell_p\colon\mathfrak{g}\to T_pM$.

For $X\in\mathfrak{g}$ the **fundamental vector field** of $X$ (also called the *infinitesimal generator* of the action in the direction $X$) is the vector field $\bar{X}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)$ defined pointwise by
$$\bar{X}(p):=d_e\ell_p(X)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\exp(tX)\cdot p\in T_pM,\qquad p\in M.$$

The two right-hand expressions are equal because $\ell_p(\exp(tX))=\exp(tX)\cdot p$ and $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_0\exp(tX)=X$, so the chain rule gives $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_0\ell_p(\exp(tX))=d_e\ell_p(X)$. For each fixed $p$ the map
$$\mathfrak{g}\to T_pM,\qquad X\mapsto\bar{X}(p)=d_e\ell_p(X),$$
is **linear**, being the differential $d_e\ell_p$ of a smooth map; consequently $\overline{aX+bY}=a\bar{X}+b\bar{Y}$ for scalars $a,b$ and $X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}$, so $X\mapsto\bar{X}$ is a linear map $\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{X}(M)$.

For a smooth **right** action $M\times G\to M$, $(p,g)\mapsto p\cdot g$, with orbit map $r_p(g)=p\cdot g$, the fundamental vector field of $\xi\in\mathfrak{g}$ is defined symmetrically and denoted $\xi_P$ (Haydys' $K_\xi$):
$$\xi_P(p):=d_er_p(\xi)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}p\cdot\exp(t\xi)\in T_pM,\qquad p\in M.$$

That both formulas define genuine **smooth** vector fields is the first thing to prove.

> [!note]- Proof that $\bar{X}$ is a smooth vector field
> We must check two things: that $\bar{X}(p)\in T_pM$ for each $p$ (so that $\bar{X}$ is a section of $TM$), and that $p\mapsto\bar{X}(p)$ is smooth.
>
> **Membership in $T_pM$.** The curve $t\mapsto\exp(tX)\cdot p$ passes through $p$ at $t=0$ (because $\exp(0)=e$ and $e\cdot p=p$ by the action axiom), so its velocity at $t=0$ lies in $T_pM$. Equivalently, $\ell_p(e)=e\cdot p=p$, hence $d_e\ell_p$ maps $T_eG=\mathfrak{g}$ into $T_pM$. Thus $\bar{X}(p)\in T_pM$, and $\bar{X}$ is a section of $TM$.
>
> **Smoothness.** Fix $X\in\mathfrak{g}$ and consider the map
> $$\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\times M\to M,\qquad \sigma(t,p):=\exp(tX)\cdot p=\theta\big(\exp(tX),\,p\big).$$
> This map is smooth, being the composition $(t,p)\mapsto(\exp(tX),p)\mapsto\theta(\exp(tX),p)$ of the smooth map $t\mapsto\exp(tX)$ (smoothness of $\exp$, from **[[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]]**, which establishes that $\exp\colon\mathfrak{g}\to G$ is smooth) with the smooth action $\theta$. The partial derivative in the first slot,
> $$\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t}\colon\mathbb{R}\times M\to TM,\qquad (t,p)\mapsto\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\sigma(t,p),$$
> is smooth, because the partial derivative of a smooth map with respect to a coordinate direction is smooth (in any chart the components of $\partial\sigma/\partial t$ are partial derivatives of the smooth coordinate expressions of $\sigma$, hence smooth). Restricting to $t=0$ and using $\sigma(0,p)=e\cdot p=p$ gives the smooth map
> $$p\mapsto\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big|_{t=0}\sigma(t,p)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}\exp(tX)\cdot p=\bar{X}(p),$$
> which is a smooth section of $TM$ over $M$ (its foot point is $\sigma(0,p)=p$). Therefore $\bar{X}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)$.
>
> **The $G\times M$ formulation.** The same fact can be phrased as Bär phrases it: let $X^{L}\in\mathfrak{X}(G)$ be the left-invariant vector field with $X^{L}(e)=X$, and let $W\in\mathfrak{X}(G\times M)$ be the smooth field $W_{(g,p)}=\big(X^{L}(g),0_p\big)$ (left-invariant $X$ in the group slot, zero in the manifold slot). Along the embedded submanifold $\{e\}\times M$ we have $W_{(e,p)}=(X,0_p)$, and the smooth bundle map $d\theta\colon T(G\times M)\to TM$ sends it to
> $$d\theta_{(e,p)}(X,0_p)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}\theta\big(\exp(tX),p\big)=\bar{X}(p),$$
> because the curve $t\mapsto(\exp(tX),p)$ has velocity $(X,0_p)$ at $t=0$. As the composition of the smooth section $p\mapsto W_{(e,p)}$ with the smooth map $d\theta$, this is again smooth, which is what "$\bar{X}$ is $d\theta$ applied to the field $(X,0)$" means. The identical argument, with $r_p$ in place of $\ell_p$, shows $\xi_P\in\mathfrak{X}(M)$ for a right action. $\blacksquare$

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The fundamental-field construction is best understood not as a single vector field but as a **linear map**
$$\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{X}(M),\qquad X\longmapsto\bar{X},$$
the *infinitesimal action* (or *infinitesimal generator map*) of the group action. It is the differential, at the identity, of the action homomorphism. Recall that a left action is the same thing as a Lie group homomorphism $\Theta\colon G\to\operatorname{Diff}(M)$, $\Theta(g)=\theta_g$ (see [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions]]). The infinite-dimensional "Lie algebra" of the diffeomorphism group $\operatorname{Diff}(M)$ is $\mathfrak{X}(M)$ with its bracket of vector fields (defined and shown to be a Lie algebra in **[[Thm - The Space of Vector Fields is a Lie Algebra under the Lie Bracket]]**). Differentiating $\Theta$ at $e$ produces precisely the map $X\mapsto\bar{X}$: switching on the one-parameter subgroup $\exp(tX)$ inside $G$ produces the one-parameter family of diffeomorphisms $\theta_{\exp(tX)}$ inside $\operatorname{Diff}(M)$, whose "velocity at $t=0$" is the vector field $\bar{X}$ generating it.

This structural reading anticipates two facts developed elsewhere in the chapter, which we state here without leaning on them.

First, the map $X\mapsto\bar{X}$ interacts with the Lie brackets on the two sides. For a **right** action it is a Lie algebra homomorphism, $[\xi,\eta]_M=[\xi_M,\eta_M]$; for a **left** action, with the standard bracket conventions, it is a Lie algebra *anti*-homomorphism, $[\bar{X},\bar{Y}]=-\overline{[X,Y]}$. This sign, which distinguishes the two sides, is proved in **[[Thm - The Fundamental Vector Field Map is a Lie Algebra Homomorphism for Right Actions]]**; it is the reason the series lets Lie groups act on principal bundles on the right, so that the infinitesimal action is an honest homomorphism.

Second, when the action is **free**, the map $X\mapsto\bar{X}(p)$ is injective at every point (proved in the corollaries below), so its image
$$\{\bar{X}(p):X\in\mathfrak{g}\}\subseteq T_pM$$
is a linear subspace of $T_pM$ isomorphic to $\mathfrak{g}$, and it is exactly the tangent space to the orbit through $p$. In the setting of a principal $G$-bundle $\pi\colon P\to M$ with its free right action, this subspace is the **vertical subspace** $V_p:=\ker d_p\pi=\{\,\xi_P(p):\xi\in\mathfrak{g}\,\}\cong\mathfrak{g}$ (Haydys, Definition 2.2.10). The vertical subspace is the object on which connections are normalised — a principal connection is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued one-form $\omega$ with $\omega(\xi_P)=\xi$ — and it is developed in full in **Gauge Theory III** and **Gauge Theory IV**. We record only its origin here: it is the pointwise image of the infinitesimal action.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** Officially $\bar{X}(p)=d_e\ell_p(X)$; operationally, $\bar{X}$ is *the velocity field of the flow that switches on the subgroup $\exp(tX)$*. Its flow is $t\mapsto\theta_{\exp(tX)}$, and this operational description is the one used in practice: to find $\bar{X}$ one writes down how $\exp(tX)$ moves points and differentiates at $t=0$. The formula $d_e\ell_p(X)$ is the coordinate-free repackaging of that same computation.

In **classical mechanics and geometric mechanics**, fundamental vector fields are the infinitesimal symmetries of a system: a Lie group $G$ acting on a phase space by symplectomorphisms produces, for each $X\in\mathfrak{g}$, a fundamental field $\bar{X}$, and Noether's theorem pairs each such field with a conserved quantity (a component of the momentum map $\mu\colon M\to\mathfrak{g}^\ast$, characterised by $d\langle\mu,X\rangle=\iota_{\bar{X}}\omega$). The reader with a background in this area will recognise $\bar{X}$ as the Hamiltonian vector field of the momentum component in the symmetric case; the present, purely differential-geometric, construction is what that specialises.

In **Lie theory** the construction is the action analogue of the adjoint representation. Applying it to the action of $G$ on *itself* by left multiplication, the fundamental field of $X$ is the **right**-invariant field with value $X$ at $e$ (because left multiplication commutes with right translations); applying it to the action by right multiplication gives the left-invariant field; applying it to the action by conjugation gives a field whose flow is $\alpha_{\exp(tX)}$ and whose linearisation at $e$ is $\operatorname{ad}_X=[X,\cdot]$, tying the construction to the [[Def - Adjoint Representation|adjoint representation]]. Thus left-invariant and right-invariant vector fields, and the bracket itself, are all special cases of the single construction "fundamental field of an action".

The unifying compression: **the fundamental-field map $\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{X}(M)$ is the differential of the action, and every "infinitesimal symmetry" in geometry is one of its values.** Where the group $G$ is the symmetry and $M$ the space it moves, $\bar{X}$ is the answer to "what is the flow generated by the symmetry direction $X$".

# Examples / Corollaries

**Corollary 1 (the flow of a fundamental vector field).** *For a smooth left action and $X\in\mathfrak{g}$, the family of diffeomorphisms $t\mapsto\theta_{\exp(tX)}$ is the flow of $\bar{X}$; in particular $\bar{X}$ is complete, and for each $p$ the curve $t\mapsto\exp(tX)\cdot p$ is the maximal integral curve of $\bar{X}$ through $p$.*

This is the first half of Bär's Theorem/Remark 1.5.19, written in full.

> [!note]- Proof of Corollary 1
> **What must be shown.** Recall (see [[Def - Flow of a Vector Field]]) that a curve $\gamma$ is an [[Def - Integral Curve of a Vector Field|integral curve]] of $\bar{X}$ if $\gamma'(t)=\bar{X}(\gamma(t))$ for all $t$. We show that $\gamma_p(t):=\exp(tX)\cdot p$ satisfies $\gamma_p'(t)=\bar{X}(\gamma_p(t))$ for every $t\in\mathbb{R}$ and every $p$, with $\gamma_p(0)=p$; since $\gamma_p$ is defined for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$, this makes $\theta_{\exp(tX)}$ the global flow.
>
> **Compute the velocity at an arbitrary time $t_0$.** Fix $t_0\in\mathbb{R}$ and set $q:=\exp(t_0X)\cdot p=\gamma_p(t_0)$. Then
> $$\gamma_p'(t_0)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=t_0}\exp(tX)\cdot p=\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\exp\big((t_0+s)X\big)\cdot p\qquad\text{(reparametrise }t=t_0+s\text{, chain rule)}$$
> $$=\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\big(\exp(sX)\exp(t_0X)\big)\cdot p\qquad\text{(one-parameter subgroup law }\exp((t_0+s)X)=\exp(sX)\exp(t_0X)\text{, from [[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]])}$$
> $$=\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\exp(sX)\cdot\big(\exp(t_0X)\cdot p\big)\qquad\text{(action axiom }(gh)\cdot p=g\cdot(h\cdot p)\text{)}$$
> $$=\frac{d}{ds}\Big|_{s=0}\exp(sX)\cdot q=\bar{X}(q)=\bar{X}\big(\gamma_p(t_0)\big)\qquad\text{(definition of }\bar{X}\text{ at the point }q\text{).}$$
> **Conclude.** The curve $\gamma_p$ therefore solves $\gamma_p'(t)=\bar{X}(\gamma_p(t))$ with $\gamma_p(0)=\exp(0)\cdot p=p$, so it is an integral curve of $\bar{X}$ through $p$, defined on all of $\mathbb{R}$. By uniqueness of integral curves (**[[Thm - Fundamental Theorem on Flows]]**: through each point there is a unique maximal integral curve, and two integral curves agreeing at one time agree wherever both are defined) $\gamma_p$ is the maximal integral curve through $p$; since it is defined for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$, the field $\bar{X}$ is complete and its flow is $(t,p)\mapsto\gamma_p(t)=\exp(tX)\cdot p=\theta_{\exp(tX)}(p)$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 2 (zeros of $\bar{X}$ are fixed points of $\exp(tX)$).** *For a smooth left action, $X\in\mathfrak{g}$ and $p\in M$,*
$$\bar{X}(p)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \exp(tX)\cdot p=p\ \text{ for all }t\in\mathbb{R}.$$

> [!note]- Proof of Corollary 2
> **What must be shown.** An equivalence; we prove both directions, using Corollary 1 and the uniqueness clause of **[[Thm - Fundamental Theorem on Flows]]**.
>
> **Direction ($\Leftarrow$).** Assume $\exp(tX)\cdot p=p$ for all $t$. Then the curve $\gamma_p(t)=\exp(tX)\cdot p$ is the constant curve $t\mapsto p$. Its velocity at $t=0$ is $0$, and by the definition of $\bar{X}$ this velocity is $\bar{X}(p)$; hence $\bar{X}(p)=0$.
>
> **Direction ($\Rightarrow$).** Assume $\bar{X}(p)=0$. Then $p$ is an equilibrium of $\bar{X}$, so the constant curve $c(t)\equiv p$ satisfies $c'(t)=0=\bar{X}(p)=\bar{X}(c(t))$ for all $t$; that is, $c$ is an integral curve of $\bar{X}$ through $p$. By Corollary 1 the curve $\gamma_p(t)=\exp(tX)\cdot p$ is also an integral curve of $\bar{X}$ through $p$. Two integral curves of the same field through the same point coincide (uniqueness in **[[Thm - Fundamental Theorem on Flows]]**), so $\exp(tX)\cdot p=\gamma_p(t)=c(t)=p$ for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary 3 (a free action gives a nowhere-vanishing vector field).** *If the left action is [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions|free]], then for every $p\in M$ the linear map $\mathfrak{g}\to T_pM$, $X\mapsto\bar{X}(p)$, is injective. Consequently, if in addition $\dim G\geq 1$ (equivalently $\mathfrak{g}\neq\{0\}$), then any nonzero $X\in\mathfrak{g}$ yields a fundamental vector field $\bar{X}$ that vanishes nowhere on $M$.*

> [!note]- Proof of Corollary 3
> **What must be shown.** First injectivity of the linear map $X\mapsto\bar{X}(p)$ for each $p$; then, from injectivity, the nowhere-vanishing conclusion.
>
> **Injectivity.** The map $X\mapsto\bar{X}(p)=d_e\ell_p(X)$ is linear, so it suffices to show its kernel is trivial. Suppose $\bar{X}(p)=0$. By Corollary 2, $\exp(tX)\cdot p=p$ for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$. **Invoke freeness:** an action is free when $g\cdot x=x$ for some $x$ forces $g=e$ (see [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions]]). Applying this with $x=p$ and $g=\exp(tX)$ gives $\exp(tX)=e$ for every $t\in\mathbb{R}$. Differentiating this identity at $t=0$,
> $$X=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}\exp(tX)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}e=0\qquad\text{(the constant curve }t\mapsto e\text{ has zero velocity).}$$
> Hence the kernel is $\{0\}$ and $X\mapsto\bar{X}(p)$ is injective.
>
> **Nowhere-vanishing field.** Assume $\dim G\geq 1$ and choose any $X\in\mathfrak{g}$ with $X\neq 0$. For each $p\in M$, injectivity of $X\mapsto\bar{X}(p)$ and $X\neq 0$ give $\bar{X}(p)\neq 0$. As $p$ was arbitrary, $\bar{X}$ vanishes at no point of $M$. $\blacksquare$

**Consequence (topological obstruction to free actions).** Corollary 3 says that a positive-dimensional Lie group can act freely on $M$ only if $M$ admits a nowhere-vanishing vector field. A nowhere-vanishing vector field on a closed manifold forces the Euler characteristic to vanish, $\chi(M)=0$; this is the **Poincaré–Hopf theorem**, whose corollary the **Hairy Ball Theorem** states that the even-dimensional spheres $S^{2n}$ carry no nowhere-vanishing vector field, so $\chi(S^{2n})=2\neq 0$. It follows that **no positive-dimensional Lie group acts freely on $S^{2n}$.** These two named results (Poincaré–Hopf and the Hairy Ball Theorem) are proved in **Gauge Theory III**, on the page **Thm - Hairy Ball Theorem**, as an application of Brouwer degree; we state the consequence here as a forward reference and do not use it to prove anything on this page.

**Is an instance — rotation of $S^2$ about the $z$-axis (Bär, Example 1.5.18).** Let $G=SO(2)$ act on $M=S^2\subseteq\mathbb{R}^3$ by rotation about the $z$-axis,
$$g\cdot x=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}x,\qquad g=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\in SO(2).$$
The Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{so}(2)=\Big\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-a\\a&0\end{smallmatrix}\right):a\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}$; take the generator $X=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, for which $\exp(tX)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\cos t&-\sin t\\\sin t&\cos t\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ (computed in **[[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential]]**). We verify the value of $\bar{X}$ clause by clause. Writing $x=(x^1,x^2,x^3)$,
$$\exp(tX)\cdot x=\big(\cos t\,x^1-\sin t\,x^2,\ \sin t\,x^1+\cos t\,x^2,\ x^3\big),$$
and differentiating at $t=0$ (term by term, using $\tfrac{d}{dt}\cos t|_0=0$, $\tfrac{d}{dt}\sin t|_0=1$),
$$\bar{X}(x)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}\exp(tX)\cdot x=\big(-x^2,\ x^1,\ 0\big)=x^1\,\partial_{x^2}-x^2\,\partial_{x^1}.$$
*Tangency to $S^2$:* $\langle\bar{X}(x),x\rangle=(-x^2)x^1+(x^1)x^2+0\cdot x^3=0$, so $\bar{X}(x)\in T_xS^2$ (a vector tangent to the sphere is orthogonal to the position vector). *Tangency to latitude circles:* $\bar{X}(x^3)=0$, so $\bar{X}$ has no component along the axis and moves points within a level set of $x^3$, that is, along a circle of constant latitude. In cylindrical–spherical terms, with azimuthal angle $\varphi$ defined by $x^1=\rho\cos\varphi$, $x^2=\rho\sin\varphi$ (where $\rho=\sqrt{(x^1)^2+(x^2)^2}$ is the distance to the axis), one has $\partial_\varphi=x^1\partial_{x^2}-x^2\partial_{x^1}$, so
$$\bar{X}=\partial_\varphi,$$
the generator of rotation, exactly as claimed. *Zeros:* $\bar{X}(x)=0$ exactly when $x^1=x^2=0$, i.e. at the two poles $(0,0,\pm 1)$; these are precisely the points fixed by every $\exp(tX)$ (the rotations fix the axis), in agreement with Corollary 2. The action is not free (the poles have nontrivial stabiliser), so Corollary 3 does not apply, and indeed $\bar{X}$ *does* vanish, at the poles — consistent with $\chi(S^2)=2\neq 0$.

**Is NOT an instance — an incomplete vector field (velocity field of a non-group flow).** On $M=\mathbb{R}$ consider the vector field $V=x^2\,\partial_x$. Its integral curves solve the ordinary differential equation $\dot{x}=x^2$. Separating variables, for an initial value $x(0)=x_0>0$,
$$\int\frac{dx}{x^2}=\int dt\ \Longrightarrow\ -\frac{1}{x}=t-\frac{1}{x_0}\ \Longrightarrow\ x(t)=\frac{x_0}{1-x_0 t},$$
which is defined only for $t<1/x_0$ and blows up as $t\to(1/x_0)^-$. Thus $V$ is **incomplete**: its maximal integral curves are not defined for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$, so its "flow" is defined only on a proper open subset of $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ and is not a one-parameter *group* of diffeomorphisms of $\mathbb{R}$. By Corollary 1, every fundamental vector field of a Lie group action is complete, its flow being the everywhere-defined family $\theta_{\exp(tX)}$. Therefore $V$ is not the fundamental vector field of any smooth Lie group action on $\mathbb{R}$, for any group $G$ and any $X\in\mathfrak{g}$: were it $\bar{X}$ for some action, its integral curve through $x_0=1$ would be $t\mapsto\exp(tX)\cdot 1$, defined for all $t$, contradicting the finite-time blow-up computed above. This is exactly the failure mode named in the Axiom Motivation — an incomplete field cannot come from a group.

**Calibration check.** Three quick verifications from the material on this page. *(1)* For the trivial action $g\cdot x=x$, the orbit map $\ell_p$ is constant, so $d_e\ell_p=0$ and $\bar{X}=0$ for every $X$ — every fundamental field vanishes identically, matching the fact that the trivial action fixes every point. *(2)* For left multiplication of $G$ on itself, $\ell_p(g)=g\cdot p=gp$, so $\bar{X}(p)=d_e\ell_p(X)=d_eR_p(X)$ where $R_p$ is right translation; hence $\bar{X}$ is the *right*-invariant field with value $X$ at $e$, and (the action being free and transitive) it vanishes nowhere, consistent with Corollary 3 and with $\chi(G)=0$ for any positive-dimensional Lie group. *(3)* Re-derive the sphere example's zero set directly from Corollary 2: $\exp(tX)\cdot p=p$ for all $t$ means $p$ is on the rotation axis; intersected with $S^2$ this is $\{(0,0,\pm1)\}$, so $\bar{X}$ vanishes exactly there — no computation of $\bar{X}$ needed.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The infinitesimal action is a Lie algebra (anti)homomorphism *(from Gauge Theory I, §1.5)*
> The linear map $X\mapsto\bar{X}$ respects brackets up to a side-dependent sign: a homomorphism for right actions, an anti-homomorphism for left actions. This is **[[Thm - The Fundamental Vector Field Map is a Lie Algebra Homomorphism for Right Actions]]**, and it is what makes the infinitesimal action a *representation* of $\mathfrak{g}$ on $\mathfrak{X}(M)$.

> [!tip] The vertical subspace and principal connections *(from Gauge Theory III–IV)*
> For a free right action — in particular the structure-group action on a principal bundle $\pi\colon P\to M$ — the values $\{\xi_P(p):\xi\in\mathfrak{g}\}$ fill out the vertical subspace $V_p=\ker d_p\pi\cong\mathfrak{g}$. A **principal connection** is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued one-form $\omega$ normalised by $\omega(\xi_P)=\xi$ and equivariant under the action; the fundamental fields are the objects on which the normalisation is written. This is the entry point to **Gauge Theory III** (principal bundles) and **Gauge Theory IV** (connections and curvature).

> [!tip] The quotient manifold theorem *(from Gauge Theory I, §1.5)*
> Corollary 3 supplies the transversality input for the slice construction: for a free action the orbit through $p$ is an immersed submanifold with tangent space $\{\bar{X}(p):X\in\mathfrak{g}\}\cong\mathfrak{g}$ at $p$, and a slice is a complement to it. This feeds **[[Thm - Quotient Manifold Theorem for Free Proper Actions]]**, the theorem that turns free proper actions into smooth quotient manifolds.
