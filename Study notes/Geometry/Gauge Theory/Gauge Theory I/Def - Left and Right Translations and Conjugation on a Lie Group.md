# Notation

Throughout, $G$ is a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]]: a smooth manifold (Hausdorff, second countable, of class $C^\infty$) carrying a group structure whose multiplication $m : G \times G \to G$, $m(g, h) = gh$, and inversion $i : G \to G$, $i(g) = g^{-1}$, are smooth maps. We write $e \in G$ for the neutral element and $n = \dim G$ for the dimension of $G$ as a manifold. Following the series conventions, Lie groups act on principal bundles on the right and on manifolds on the left; on this page the only actions in sight are the actions of $G$ on itself by multiplication, and the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ of $G$ is the space of left-invariant vector fields, identified with $T_eG$ by the extension result proved below.

For a fixed $g \in G$ we write $L_g : G \to G$, $L_g(h) = gh$, for the **left translation** by $g$; $R_g : G \to G$, $R_g(h) = hg$, for the **right translation** by $g$; and $\alpha_g : G \to G$, $\alpha_g(h) = ghg^{-1}$, for the **conjugation** by $g$. For a smooth manifold $M$ we write $\mathfrak{X}(M)$ for the real vector space of smooth [[Def - Smooth Vector Field|vector fields]] on $M$, $T_pM$ for the [[Def - The Tangent Space|tangent space]] at $p \in M$, and, for a smooth map $F : M \to N$ and a point $p \in M$, $d_pF : T_pM \to T_{F(p)}N$ for the [[Def - The Differential of a Smooth Map|differential]] of $F$ at $p$, defined on a tangent vector $v$ (a derivation at $p$) by $(d_pF(v))(f) = v(f \circ F)$ for every smooth function $f$ on $N$. The vault's differential-geometry chapters write $dF_p$ for the same linear map; the two notations denote the same object, and this page follows Bär's placement of the point as a subscript on $d$. A vector field $X \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ has value $X(p) \in T_pM$ at $p$; we write $X(p)$ rather than $X_p$ so that the point at which a field is evaluated is never confused with the point at which a differential is taken. For a [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]] $F : M \to N$ and $X \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ we write $dF(X) \in \mathfrak{X}(N)$ for the **push-forward** of $X$ along $F$, defined below; the vault's chapter [[Differential Geometry V — Vector Fields, Flows, and the Lie Bracket]] writes $F_*X$ for the same vector field, and the theorem [[Thm - Pushforward of Vector Fields under a Diffeomorphism]] there is the source of its basic properties. The [[Def - The Lie Bracket of Vector Fields|Lie bracket]] of $X, Y \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ is the vector field $[X, Y]$ acting on smooth functions by $[X, Y]f = X(Yf) - Y(Xf)$.

> [!warning] Convention: the letter $L$ in Bär, and the letters $\theta$ and $\ell$ in this series
> Bär uses the letter $L$ three times over: $L_g$ for left translation on the group (this page), $L_g$ again for the map $p \mapsto g \cdot p$ of a left action of $G$ on a manifold $M$ (his §1.5), and $L_p : G \to P$, $g \mapsto p \cdot g$, for the orbit map of the right action on a principal bundle (his §2.2). The series reserves $L_g$ and $R_g$ for the translations on $G$ itself, writes $\theta_g : M \to M$ for the diffeomorphism $p \mapsto g \cdot p$ (or $p \mapsto p \cdot g$) that a group element induces on a $G$-manifold, and writes $\ell_p : G \to M$ for the orbit map through $p$. Where a later page quotes Bär's $L_p$, the conversion is $L_p = \ell_p$; where it quotes his $L_g$ acting on $M$, the conversion is $L_g = \theta_g$. Bär also writes the differential of $F$ at $p$ as $d_pF$ and the push-forward of a vector field as $dF(X)$; the vault's differential-geometry chapters write $dF_p$ and $F_*X$. The series uses Bär's $d_pF$ and $dF(X)$, and every page that quotes a differential-geometry page translates $F_*X = dF(X)$ on the spot.

This is a compound page: it defines four interlocking notions — the left and right translations $L_g$ and $R_g$, the conjugation $\alpha_g = L_g \circ R_{g^{-1}}$, the push-forward $dF(X)$ of a vector field along a diffeomorphism, and the extension $X(g) = d_eL_g(X_0)$ of a tangent vector at the identity to a left-invariant vector field — because they are the four pieces of one construction. The translations make $G$ homogeneous; the push-forward is the operation that transports a vector field along a translation, so that "left-invariant" can be said at all; the conjugation is what remains of the translations once we ask for maps that also respect the group law; and the extension result is the reason the Lie algebra of $G$, defined as a space of vector fields, is a finite-dimensional object living at the single point $e$.

# Axiom Motivation

The problem that these definitions solve is the following. A Lie group is at once a manifold and a group, and the whole subject of Lie theory rests on the fact that the group structure makes the manifold look the same at every point. On a general manifold there is no canonical way to compare a tangent vector at one point with a tangent vector at another: one needs a connection, and different connections give different comparisons. On a Lie group there is a canonical way, and it costs nothing: to move from the point $e$ to the point $g$, multiply on the left by $g$. The map that does this, $L_g(h) = gh$, is the first object we must name. Since multiplication is not commutative in general, multiplying on the right, $R_g(h) = hg$, is a genuinely different map, and both must be recorded.

What must the translations be, for them to serve this purpose? First, each $L_g$ must be a diffeomorphism of $G$, since we want to transport tangent vectors and vector fields, and only a diffeomorphism has an invertible differential at every point and a well-defined push-forward on vector fields. This is not a further axiom: it follows from the Lie group axioms, because $L_g$ is the smooth multiplication with its first argument frozen, and $L_{g^{-1}}$ is a smooth inverse. If one dropped the requirement that $m$ be smooth — keeping only a topological group — the translations would be homeomorphisms but would have no differentials, and nothing on this page could be said. Second, the translations must compose the way the group does: $L_g \circ L_h = L_{gh}$. This is associativity, and it is what makes the transport from $e$ to $g$ and then from $g$ to $kg$ agree with the transport from $e$ to $kg$ directly. Without it, the vectors transported to a point along different routes would disagree, and "left-invariant vector field" would be an empty notion.

Why single out conjugation? Left translation moves $e$ to $g$, so it cannot be a group homomorphism unless $g = e$: a homomorphism sends $e$ to $e$. This is the content of Bär's Remark 1.2.4 — the translations are diffeomorphisms of $G$ but they are not homomorphisms — and it is verified below on $G = \mathbb{R}$, where $L_g(h) = g + h$ visibly fails additivity. If we want a self-map of $G$ that is both a diffeomorphism and a homomorphism, we must fix $e$, and the natural candidate is to undo the right translation after the left one: $\alpha_g = L_g \circ R_{g^{-1}}$, $h \mapsto ghg^{-1}$. That this is a Lie group automorphism, with inverse $\alpha_{g^{-1}}$, is the first proposition of the page. The differential $d_e\alpha_g : T_eG \to T_eG$ is then a linear automorphism of the one vector space that Lie theory cares about, and it will be the [[Def - Adjoint Representation|adjoint representation]] $\operatorname{Ad}_g$ of the series conventions. Dropping the $R_{g^{-1}}$ factor would leave us with $L_g$, which does not fix $e$ and so induces no map $T_eG \to T_eG$ at all; replacing $R_{g^{-1}}$ by $R_g$ would give $h \mapsto ghg$, which is not a homomorphism either ($ghkg \ne ghg \cdot gkg$ unless $g^2 = e$) and does not fix $e$ unless $g^2 = e$. Conjugation is the unique combination of one left and one right translation that fixes the identity, and it is precisely the combination that respects the group law.

The push-forward requires a separate motivation, because it is the one notion on this page that makes sense on an arbitrary manifold. Given a smooth map $F : M \to N$ and a vector field $X$ on $M$, one would like to define a vector field on $N$ by $q \mapsto d_pF(X(p))$ where $F(p) = q$. Two things go wrong for a general $F$: a point $q$ may have no preimage, in which case there is no candidate vector, or it may have several, in which case the candidates $d_pF(X(p))$ for the different preimages $p$ may disagree. The non-example below — $F(x) = x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ with $X = \partial_x$ — exhibits both failures at once. When $F$ is a diffeomorphism, every $q$ has exactly one preimage $F^{-1}(q)$, and the formula $dF(X)(q) := d_{F^{-1}(q)}F\big(X(F^{-1}(q))\big)$ is forced. What must be checked is that the result is smooth and that it is characterised by the commutative square $dF \circ X = dF(X) \circ F$; that the push-forward respects the Lie bracket, Bär's equation (1.5), is the property that makes the left-invariant fields closed under bracket, and is wikilinked to the page that proves it.

Finally, why is the extension result part of the definition page rather than a theorem elsewhere? Because it is the statement that makes the definition of $\mathfrak{g}$ usable. As defined, a left-invariant vector field is a smooth section of $TG$ satisfying infinitely many conditions, one for each $g \in G$. The extension result says that the whole field is determined by the single vector $X(e)$, that every vector at $e$ arises, and that the field so produced is automatically smooth. The first two claims are two lines of the chain rule; the third is the only place on the page where the smoothness of the multiplication map is used in a non-trivial way, and it is written out in coordinates below. A reader who has understood why $X(g) = d_eL_g(X(e))$ follows from left-invariance, why $g \mapsto d_eL_g(X_0)$ is left-invariant, and why it is smooth, could have invented every definition on this page.

# The Definition

Let $G$ be a Lie group and fix $g \in G$.

**(a) Translations.** The **left translation** by $g$ and the **right translation** by $g$ are the maps
$$L_g : G \to G, \quad L_g(h) := g \cdot h, \qquad\qquad R_g : G \to G, \quad R_g(h) := h \cdot g .$$

**(b) Conjugation.** The **conjugation** by $g$ is the map
$$\alpha_g : G \to G, \qquad \alpha_g(h) := \big(L_g \circ R_{g^{-1}}\big)(h) = g \cdot h \cdot g^{-1} .$$
(The two descriptions agree: $L_g(R_{g^{-1}}(h)) = L_g(hg^{-1}) = g(hg^{-1}) = ghg^{-1}$ by associativity.)

**(c) Push-forward of a vector field.** Let $M$ and $N$ be smooth manifolds, $F : M \to N$ a diffeomorphism, and $X \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$. The **push-forward** of $X$ along $F$ is the vector field $dF(X)$ on $N$ defined pointwise by
$$dF(X)(q) := d_{F^{-1}(q)}F\Big(X\big(F^{-1}(q)\big)\Big) \in T_qN \qquad (q \in N).$$
Bär states this for $N = M$; nothing changes for two different manifolds, and the more general form is the one we use when a Lie group homomorphism transports fields. In the notation of [[Thm - Pushforward of Vector Fields under a Diffeomorphism]], $dF(X) = F_*X$.

**(d) Extension of a tangent vector at the identity.** For $X_0 \in T_eG$, the **left-invariant extension** of $X_0$ is the vector field
$$X : G \to TG, \qquad X(g) := d_eL_g(X_0) \in T_gG .$$

A vector field $X \in \mathfrak{X}(G)$ is [[Def - Left-Invariant Vector Field|left-invariant]] when $dL_g(X) = X$ for every $g \in G$, and $\mathfrak{g} := \{X \in \mathfrak{X}(G) : X \text{ is left-invariant}\}$ is the [[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group|Lie algebra of the Lie group]]. The remainder of this section states and proves, at the level of the vault's proof standard, the four facts that give these definitions their content.

> **Proposition A (translations are diffeomorphisms).** Let $G$ be a Lie group and $g, h \in G$. Then:
>
> (i) $L_g$ and $R_g$ are smooth maps $G \to G$.
>
> (ii) $L_g \circ L_h = L_{gh}$, $R_g \circ R_h = R_{hg}$, $L_e = R_e = \mathrm{id}_G$, and $L_g \circ R_h = R_h \circ L_g$.
>
> (iii) $L_g$ and $R_g$ are diffeomorphisms of $G$, with inverses $L_g^{-1} = L_{g^{-1}}$ and $R_g^{-1} = R_{g^{-1}}$.
>
> (iv) For every $k \in G$ the differential $d_kL_g : T_kG \to T_{gk}G$ is a linear isomorphism with inverse $d_{gk}L_{g^{-1}}$, and $d_kR_g : T_kG \to T_{kg}G$ is a linear isomorphism with inverse $d_{kg}R_{g^{-1}}$.

> [!note]- Proof of Proposition A
> We are given a Lie group $G$, so that $m : G \times G \to G$ and $i : G \to G$ are smooth, and elements $g, h \in G$. We must show the four assertions in turn.
>
> **Part (i): smoothness of the translations.** **Factor $L_g$ through the product:** define the map $\iota_g : G \to G \times G$, $\iota_g(k) := (g, k)$. Then for every $k \in G$,
> $$(m \circ \iota_g)(k) = m(g, k) = gk = L_g(k) \qquad \text{(by the definitions of } m, \iota_g, L_g\text{)},$$
> so $L_g = m \circ \iota_g$. **Show $\iota_g$ is smooth:** let $k_0 \in G$. Choose a smooth chart $(U, \varphi)$ of $G$ with $g \in U$ and a smooth chart $(V, \psi)$ of $G$ with $k_0 \in V$. By [[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]] — the product atlas $\{(U_\alpha \times U_\beta, \varphi_\alpha \times \varphi_\beta)\}$ is a smooth atlas defining the product smooth structure on $G \times G$ — the pair $(U \times V, \varphi \times \psi)$ is a smooth chart of $G \times G$ containing $\iota_g(k_0) = (g, k_0)$. Now $\iota_g(V) \subseteq U \times V$ because $g \in U$, and the coordinate representative of $\iota_g$ with respect to these charts is
> $$(\varphi \times \psi) \circ \iota_g \circ \psi^{-1} : \psi(V) \to \varphi(U) \times \psi(V), \qquad x \mapsto (\varphi(g), x) \qquad \text{(evaluate: } \psi^{-1}(x) \mapsto (g, \psi^{-1}(x)) \mapsto (\varphi(g), x)\text{)}.$$
> This map from an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ has first $n$ components constant and last $n$ components the identity, so it is $C^\infty$. By the definition of a [[Def - Smooth Map between Manifolds|smooth map between manifolds]] (smooth at $k_0$ means: there exist charts around $k_0$ and around the image point, with $\iota_g$ mapping the first chart domain into the second, such that the coordinate representative is $C^\infty$), $\iota_g$ is smooth at $k_0$; as $k_0$ was arbitrary, $\iota_g$ is smooth. **Conclude for $L_g$:** $m$ is smooth by the Lie group axiom, $\iota_g$ is smooth, and the composition of smooth maps is smooth ([[Ex - Composition of Smooth Maps is Smooth]]), so $L_g = m \circ \iota_g$ is smooth. **The same for $R_g$:** define $\jmath_g : G \to G \times G$, $\jmath_g(k) := (k, g)$; then $(m \circ \jmath_g)(k) = m(k, g) = kg = R_g(k)$, so $R_g = m \circ \jmath_g$. With charts $(V, \psi)$ around $k_0$ and $(U, \varphi)$ around $g$ the coordinate representative of $\jmath_g$ is $(\psi \times \varphi) \circ \jmath_g \circ \psi^{-1} : \psi(V) \to \psi(V) \times \varphi(U)$, $x \mapsto (x, \varphi(g))$, whose first $n$ components are the identity and whose last $n$ components are constant; it is $C^\infty$, so $\jmath_g$ is smooth, and $R_g = m \circ \jmath_g$ is smooth as a composition of smooth maps.
>
> **Part (ii): composition laws.** For every $k \in G$,
> $$(L_g \circ L_h)(k) = g(hk) = (gh)k = L_{gh}(k) \qquad \text{(by associativity of the group law)},$$
> $$(R_g \circ R_h)(k) = (kh)g = k(hg) = R_{hg}(k) \qquad \text{(by associativity)},$$
> $$L_e(k) = ek = k, \qquad R_e(k) = ke = k \qquad \text{(by the neutral element axiom)},$$
> $$(L_g \circ R_h)(k) = g(kh) = (gk)h = (R_h \circ L_g)(k) \qquad \text{(by associativity)}.$$
> Since the equalities hold for every $k$, the four identities of maps follow. Note the order reversal in the second identity: right translations compose contravariantly.
>
> **Part (iii): the translations are diffeomorphisms.** By Part (ii) applied with $h = g^{-1}$ and then with the roles exchanged,
> $$L_g \circ L_{g^{-1}} = L_{gg^{-1}} = L_e = \mathrm{id}_G, \qquad L_{g^{-1}} \circ L_g = L_{g^{-1}g} = L_e = \mathrm{id}_G \qquad \text{(by Part (ii) and the inverse axiom)}.$$
> Hence $L_g$ is a bijection with inverse $L_{g^{-1}}$. Both $L_g$ and $L_{g^{-1}}$ are smooth by Part (i), so $L_g$ is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse, which is the definition of a [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]]. In the same way,
> $$R_g \circ R_{g^{-1}} = R_{g^{-1}g} = R_e = \mathrm{id}_G, \qquad R_{g^{-1}} \circ R_g = R_{gg^{-1}} = R_e = \mathrm{id}_G \qquad \text{(by Part (ii))},$$
> so $R_g$ is a diffeomorphism with inverse $R_{g^{-1}}$.
>
> **Part (iv): the differentials are isomorphisms.** We use the [[Thm - Chain Rule for the Differential|chain rule for the differential]]: for smooth maps $F : M \to N$, $H : N \to P$ and $p \in M$, $d_p(H \circ F) = d_{F(p)}H \circ d_pF$, and $d_p\mathrm{id}_M = \mathrm{id}_{T_pM}$. Fix $k \in G$. Then
> $$d_{gk}L_{g^{-1}} \circ d_kL_g = d_k\big(L_{g^{-1}} \circ L_g\big) = d_k\mathrm{id}_G = \mathrm{id}_{T_kG} \qquad \text{(chain rule; Part (iii))},$$
> $$d_kL_g \circ d_{gk}L_{g^{-1}} = d_{gk}\big(L_g \circ L_{g^{-1}}\big) = d_{gk}\mathrm{id}_G = \mathrm{id}_{T_{gk}G} \qquad \text{(chain rule, noting } L_{g^{-1}}(gk) = k \text{; Part (iii))}.$$
> So $d_kL_g$ is a linear map with two-sided inverse $d_{gk}L_{g^{-1}}$, hence a linear isomorphism $T_kG \to T_{gk}G$. The identical computation with $R$ in place of $L$ and $kg$ in place of $gk$ — $d_{kg}R_{g^{-1}} \circ d_kR_g = d_k(R_{g^{-1}} \circ R_g) = \mathrm{id}_{T_kG}$ and $d_kR_g \circ d_{kg}R_{g^{-1}} = d_{kg}(R_g \circ R_{g^{-1}}) = \mathrm{id}_{T_{kg}G}$, each line by the chain rule and Part (iii) — shows that $d_kR_g$ is a linear isomorphism with inverse $d_{kg}R_{g^{-1}}$.
>
> Therefore the left and right translations are diffeomorphisms of $G$ composing according to the group law, and their differentials are linear isomorphisms between the tangent spaces at corresponding points. $\blacksquare$

> **Proposition B (conjugation is a Lie group automorphism; translations are not homomorphisms — Bär's Remark after Definition 1.2.3).** Let $G$ be a Lie group and $g, h \in G$. Then:
>
> (i) $\alpha_g$ is a diffeomorphism of $G$ with inverse $\alpha_g^{-1} = \alpha_{g^{-1}}$.
>
> (ii) $\alpha_g$ is a group homomorphism: $\alpha_g(hk) = \alpha_g(h)\alpha_g(k)$ for all $h, k \in G$. Consequently $\alpha_g$ is a [[Def - Lie Group Homomorphism|Lie group isomorphism]] $G \to G$, that is, a Lie group automorphism of $G$; in particular $\alpha_g(e) = e$.
>
> (iii) $\alpha_{gh} = \alpha_g \circ \alpha_h$ and $\alpha_e = \mathrm{id}_G$.
>
> (iv) $L_g$ is a group homomorphism if and only if $g = e$, and $R_g$ is a group homomorphism if and only if $g = e$. In particular, for $g \ne e$ the translations are diffeomorphisms that are not Lie group homomorphisms.
>
> (v) $\alpha_g = \mathrm{id}_G$ for every $g \in G$ if and only if $G$ is abelian.

> [!note]- Proof of Proposition B
> We are given $G$ and $g, h \in G$, and must prove the five assertions.
>
> **Part (i): $\alpha_g$ is a diffeomorphism.** By definition $\alpha_g = L_g \circ R_{g^{-1}}$. By Proposition A(iii), $L_g$ and $R_{g^{-1}}$ are diffeomorphisms; a composition of diffeomorphisms is a diffeomorphism, because $L_g \circ R_{g^{-1}}$ is smooth as a composition of smooth maps ([[Ex - Composition of Smooth Maps is Smooth]]) and has the smooth two-sided inverse $R_{g^{-1}}^{-1} \circ L_g^{-1} = R_g \circ L_{g^{-1}}$. **Identify the inverse:** for every $k \in G$,
> $$\alpha_{g^{-1}}\big(\alpha_g(k)\big) = g^{-1}\big(gkg^{-1}\big)(g^{-1})^{-1} = g^{-1}\big(gkg^{-1}\big)g = (g^{-1}g)k(g^{-1}g) = eke = k \qquad \text{(by } (g^{-1})^{-1} = g\text{, associativity, and the inverse and neutral element axioms)},$$
> and in the same way $\alpha_g(\alpha_{g^{-1}}(k)) = g(g^{-1}kg)g^{-1} = (gg^{-1})k(gg^{-1}) = k$. Hence $\alpha_g^{-1} = \alpha_{g^{-1}}$.
>
> **Part (ii): $\alpha_g$ is a homomorphism.** For $h, k \in G$,
> $$\alpha_g(hk) = g(hk)g^{-1} = gh\,e\,kg^{-1} = gh(g^{-1}g)kg^{-1} = (ghg^{-1})(gkg^{-1}) = \alpha_g(h)\alpha_g(k) \qquad \text{(insert } e = g^{-1}g \text{ between } h \text{ and } k\text{; regroup by associativity)}.$$
> So $\alpha_g$ is a group homomorphism. It is smooth by Part (i), so it is a Lie group homomorphism $G \to G$ in the sense of [[Def - Lie Group Homomorphism]] (a smooth map that is a group homomorphism); it is bijective with smooth inverse $\alpha_{g^{-1}}$ by Part (i), and $\alpha_{g^{-1}}$ is itself a group homomorphism by the computation just made with $g^{-1}$ in place of $g$. A bijective Lie group homomorphism whose inverse is a Lie group homomorphism is a Lie group isomorphism, so $\alpha_g$ is a Lie group automorphism of $G$. Every group homomorphism fixes the neutral element — from $\alpha_g(e) = \alpha_g(ee) = \alpha_g(e)\alpha_g(e)$, multiplying by $\alpha_g(e)^{-1}$ gives $e = \alpha_g(e)$ — and directly $\alpha_g(e) = geg^{-1} = gg^{-1} = e$.
>
> **Part (iii): conjugation is multiplicative in $g$.** For every $k \in G$,
> $$(\alpha_g \circ \alpha_h)(k) = g\big(hkh^{-1}\big)g^{-1} = (gh)k\big(h^{-1}g^{-1}\big) = (gh)k(gh)^{-1} = \alpha_{gh}(k) \qquad \text{(associativity; the identity } (gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1}\text{, which holds since } (gh)(h^{-1}g^{-1}) = g(hh^{-1})g^{-1} = gg^{-1} = e\text{)}.$$
> Also $\alpha_e(k) = eke^{-1} = k$, so $\alpha_e = \mathrm{id}_G$.
>
> **Part (iv): translations are homomorphisms only for $g = e$.** **Direction 1 ($L_g$ a homomorphism $\Rightarrow g = e$):** assume $L_g(hk) = L_g(h)L_g(k)$ for all $h, k \in G$. Take $h = k = e$: $L_g(e) = L_g(e)L_g(e)$, that is, $g = g \cdot g$ (since $L_g(e) = ge = g$). Multiplying both sides on the left by $g^{-1}$ gives $e = g$. **Direction 2 ($g = e \Rightarrow L_g$ a homomorphism):** $L_e = \mathrm{id}_G$ by Proposition A(ii), and the identity map is a group homomorphism. **The same for $R_g$:** if $R_g$ is a homomorphism then $R_g(e) = R_g(e)R_g(e)$, that is, $g = gg$, so $g = e$; and $R_e = \mathrm{id}_G$ is a homomorphism. For $g \ne e$, then, $L_g$ and $R_g$ are diffeomorphisms (Proposition A(iii)) that are not group homomorphisms, hence not Lie group homomorphisms. (The failure is visible at a general pair as well: $L_g(hk) = ghk$ while $L_g(h)L_g(k) = ghgk$, and cancelling $gh$ on the left and $k$ on the right the two agree if and only if $e = g$.)
>
> **Part (v): trivial conjugation characterises abelian groups.** $\alpha_g = \mathrm{id}_G$ for all $g$ means $ghg^{-1} = h$ for all $g, h \in G$; multiplying on the right by $g$, this is equivalent to $gh = hg$ for all $g, h$, which is the definition of an [[Def - Abelian Group|abelian group]]. Both implications are the same chain of equivalences read in the two directions.
>
> Therefore conjugation by $g$ is a Lie group automorphism of $G$, the assignment $g \mapsto \alpha_g$ is multiplicative, and the translations, though diffeomorphisms, are homomorphisms only in the trivial case $g = e$. $\blacksquare$

> **Proposition C (the push-forward of a vector field — Bär's Remark 1.2.4).** Let $F : M \to N$ be a diffeomorphism of smooth manifolds and $X, Y \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$. Then:
>
> (i) $dF(X)$ is a smooth vector field on $N$.
>
> (ii) The square commutes: $d_pF\big(X(p)\big) = dF(X)\big(F(p)\big)$ for every $p \in M$, that is, $dF \circ X = dF(X) \circ F$ as maps $M \to TN$; and $dF(X)$ is the only vector field on $N$ with this property.
>
> (iii) (Bär's equation (1.5)) $dF([X, Y]) = [dF(X), dF(Y)]$.
>
> (iv) $dF : \mathfrak{X}(M) \to \mathfrak{X}(N)$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear; $d(\mathrm{id}_M)(X) = X$; if $H : N \to P$ is a further diffeomorphism then $d(H \circ F)(X) = dH\big(dF(X)\big)$; and $d(F^{-1})\big(dF(X)\big) = X$, so $dF$ is a linear isomorphism with inverse $d(F^{-1})$.

> [!note]- Proof of Proposition C
> We are given a diffeomorphism $F : M \to N$ — a smooth bijection whose inverse $F^{-1} : N \to M$ is smooth — and smooth vector fields $X, Y$ on $M$. Write $n = \dim M = \dim N$ (the dimensions agree because $d_pF$ is a linear isomorphism $T_pM \to T_{F(p)}N$ by the [[Thm - Chain Rule for the Differential|chain rule]], Corollary 1, and $\dim T_pM = \dim M$ by [[Thm - Dimension of the Tangent Space]]).
>
> **Part (i), Step 0: the formula defines a rough vector field.** For $q \in N$ the point $p := F^{-1}(q) \in M$ is well defined because $F$ is bijective; $X(p) \in T_pM$ because $X$ is a vector field; and $d_pF : T_pM \to T_{F(p)}N = T_qN$ because $F(p) = q$. Hence $dF(X)(q) = d_pF(X(p)) \in T_qN$, and $q \mapsto dF(X)(q)$ is a section of $TN$, that is, a [[Def - Vector Field on a Manifold|vector field]] on $N$ without, as yet, any smoothness claim.
>
> **Part (i), Step 1: smoothness in coordinates.** We use the chart criterion of [[Def - Smooth Vector Field]]: a vector field is smooth if and only if in every smooth chart $(V, (y^j))$ its component functions in the expansion $\sum_j Y^j \partial/\partial y^j$ are smooth on $V$; it suffices to exhibit, around each point, one chart in which the components are smooth, because smoothness of a function is local and the components in overlapping charts are related by the smooth Jacobian of the transition map. Fix $q_0 \in N$ and put $p_0 := F^{-1}(q_0)$. **Choose charts:** let $(V, \psi = (y^1, \dots, y^n))$ be a smooth chart of $N$ with $q_0 \in V$, and let $(U', \varphi)$ be a smooth chart of $M$ with $p_0 \in U'$. Set $U := U' \cap F^{-1}(V)$; this is open in $M$ (since $F$ is continuous and $U'$, $V$ are open) and contains $p_0$, and $(U, \varphi|_U = (x^1, \dots, x^n))$ is again a smooth chart (a restriction of a chart to an open subset). Then $F(U) \subseteq V$, and $F(U)$ is open in $N$ because $F$ is a homeomorphism, so $(F(U), \psi|_{F(U)})$ is a smooth chart of $N$ containing $q_0$. **Expand $X$ on $U$:** since $X$ is smooth, there are smooth functions $X^1, \dots, X^n : U \to \mathbb{R}$ with
> $$X(p) = \sum_{i=1}^n X^i(p)\, \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\Big|_p \qquad (p \in U) \qquad \text{(chart criterion of smooth vector fields)}.$$
> **Apply the coordinate formula for the differential:** with $\hat F := \psi \circ F \circ \varphi^{-1} : \varphi(U) \to \psi(V)$, which is $C^\infty$ because $F$ is smooth, [[Def - The Differential of a Smooth Map]] (coordinate picture) gives
> $$d_pF\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\Big|_p\Big) = \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{\partial \hat F^j}{\partial x^i}\big(\varphi(p)\big)\, \frac{\partial}{\partial y^j}\Big|_{F(p)} \qquad (p \in U) \qquad \text{(Jacobian of the coordinate representative)}.$$
> **Combine:** for $q \in F(U)$, with $p = F^{-1}(q) \in U$, linearity of $d_pF$ and the two displays give
> $$dF(X)(q) = d_pF\big(X(p)\big) = \sum_{j=1}^n \Bigg(\sum_{i=1}^n X^i\big(F^{-1}(q)\big)\, \frac{\partial \hat F^j}{\partial x^i}\big(\varphi(F^{-1}(q))\big)\Bigg) \frac{\partial}{\partial y^j}\Big|_q \qquad \text{(definition of } dF(X)\text{; expansion of } X\text{; coordinate formula; linearity of } d_pF\text{)}.$$
> So the $j$-th component of $dF(X)$ in the chart $(F(U), \psi)$ is the function
> $$q \mapsto \sum_{i=1}^n \big(X^i \circ F^{-1}\big)(q) \cdot \Big(\frac{\partial \hat F^j}{\partial x^i} \circ \varphi \circ F^{-1}\Big)(q) \qquad (q \in F(U)).$$
> Each factor is smooth on $F(U)$: $F^{-1}$ is smooth (hypothesis that $F$ is a diffeomorphism) and maps $F(U)$ into $U$; $X^i$ is smooth on $U$; $\varphi$ is smooth on $U$ (a chart map is a diffeomorphism onto its image); and $\partial \hat F^j / \partial x^i$ is $C^\infty$ on $\varphi(U)$ because a partial derivative of a $C^\infty$ function is $C^\infty$. Compositions and finite sums of products of smooth functions are smooth ([[Ex - Composition of Smooth Maps is Smooth]] and the algebra of smooth functions). Hence all components of $dF(X)$ are smooth on the chart $F(U)$ around $q_0$. As $q_0 \in N$ was arbitrary, $dF(X)$ is a smooth vector field on $N$.
>
> **Part (ii): the commutative square and uniqueness.** **Existence:** for $p \in M$ put $q := F(p)$; then $F^{-1}(q) = p$, so by the definition of the push-forward
> $$dF(X)\big(F(p)\big) = dF(X)(q) = d_{F^{-1}(q)}F\big(X(F^{-1}(q))\big) = d_pF\big(X(p)\big) \qquad \text{(definition of } dF(X)\text{; } F^{-1}(F(p)) = p\text{)}.$$
> Reading the two sides as maps $M \to TN$, this is $dF(X) \circ F = dF \circ X$, where $dF : TM \to TN$ denotes the map whose restriction to $T_pM$ is $d_pF$; this is Bär's commutative diagram. **Uniqueness:** let $Z \in \mathfrak{X}(N)$ satisfy $Z(F(p)) = d_pF(X(p))$ for all $p \in M$. Given $q \in N$, put $p := F^{-1}(q)$ (surjectivity of $F$); then
> $$Z(q) = Z\big(F(p)\big) = d_pF\big(X(p)\big) = dF(X)(q) \qquad \text{(by the hypothesis on } Z\text{; by the existence computation)},$$
> so $Z = dF(X)$. This is Lemma 2 of [[Thm - Pushforward of Vector Fields under a Diffeomorphism]] in the vault's notation $F_*X$.
>
> **Part (iii): naturality of the bracket.** This is the statement proved on the page [[Thm - The Space of Vector Fields is a Lie Algebra under the Lie Bracket]]: for a smooth manifold $M$, $(\mathfrak{X}(M), [\cdot, \cdot])$ with $[X, Y]f = X(Yf) - Y(Xf)$ is a Lie algebra, and for every diffeomorphism $F : M \to N$ and all $X, Y \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ one has $F_*[X, Y] = [F_*X, F_*Y]$, which in the notation of this page reads $dF([X, Y]) = [dF(X), dF(Y)]$. We cite that page rather than the corollary on the differential-geometry page [[Thm - Pushforward of Vector Fields under a Diffeomorphism]], because the latter derives the bracket identity from a page the series has flagged as not proved to the vault's standard; the mechanism of the proof is that, by Part (ii), $(dF(X)f) \circ F = X(f \circ F)$ for every smooth $f$ on $N$ (evaluate the derivation $d_pF(X(p))$ on $f$), so applying this twice expresses $[dF(X), dF(Y)]f \circ F$ as $[X, Y](f \circ F)$, and surjectivity of $F$ then identifies $[dF(X), dF(Y)]$ with $dF([X, Y])$ by the uniqueness of Part (ii).
>
> **Part (iv): linearity, functoriality, and invertibility.** **Linearity:** for $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $q \in N$, with $p = F^{-1}(q)$,
> $$dF(aX + bY)(q) = d_pF\big(aX(p) + bY(p)\big) = a\, d_pF\big(X(p)\big) + b\, d_pF\big(Y(p)\big) = a\, dF(X)(q) + b\, dF(Y)(q) \qquad \text{(pointwise definition of } aX + bY\text{; linearity of } d_pF\text{)}.$$
> **Identity:** $d(\mathrm{id}_M)(X)(p) = d_p\mathrm{id}_M(X(p)) = X(p)$ (since $\mathrm{id}_M^{-1}(p) = p$ and $d_p\mathrm{id}_M = \mathrm{id}_{T_pM}$ by the chain rule page). **Functoriality:** for a diffeomorphism $H : N \to P$ and $r \in P$, put $q := H^{-1}(r)$ and $p := F^{-1}(q)$, so that $(H \circ F)^{-1}(r) = F^{-1}(H^{-1}(r)) = p$; then
> $$d(H \circ F)(X)(r) = d_p(H \circ F)\big(X(p)\big) = d_{q}H\Big(d_pF\big(X(p)\big)\Big) = d_qH\big(dF(X)(q)\big) = dH\big(dF(X)\big)(r) \qquad \text{(definition; chain rule with } F(p) = q\text{; definition of } dF(X)\text{; definition of } dH(\cdot)\text{)}.$$
> **Invertibility:** applying functoriality to $F^{-1} \circ F = \mathrm{id}_M$ and using the identity case, $d(F^{-1})(dF(X)) = d(F^{-1} \circ F)(X) = d(\mathrm{id}_M)(X) = X$, and applying it to $F \circ F^{-1} = \mathrm{id}_N$ gives $dF(d(F^{-1})(Z)) = Z$ for every $Z \in \mathfrak{X}(N)$. Hence $dF$ is a linear bijection $\mathfrak{X}(M) \to \mathfrak{X}(N)$ with inverse $d(F^{-1})$.
>
> Therefore the push-forward along a diffeomorphism is a well-defined smooth vector field, characterised by the commutative square, compatible with brackets, and functorial. $\blacksquare$

> **Proposition D (extension of a tangent vector at $e$ to a left-invariant field — Bär's discussion after Definition 1.2.6).** Let $G$ be a Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ of left-invariant vector fields.
>
> (i) If $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ then $X(g) = d_eL_g\big(X(e)\big)$ for every $g \in G$; a left-invariant vector field is determined by its value at $e$.
>
> (ii) For every $X_0 \in T_eG$ the rough vector field $X(g) := d_eL_g(X_0)$ is smooth and left-invariant, satisfies $X(e) = X_0$, and is the unique left-invariant vector field with value $X_0$ at $e$.
>
> (iii) The map $\Lambda : T_eG \to \mathfrak{g}$, $\Lambda(X_0)(g) := d_eL_g(X_0)$, is a linear isomorphism with inverse the evaluation map $\varepsilon : \mathfrak{g} \to T_eG$, $\varepsilon(X) := X(e)$. In particular $\dim_{\mathbb{R}} \mathfrak{g} = \dim G$.

> [!note]- Proof of Proposition D
> We are given the Lie group $G$; we must prove the three assertions. Throughout, "left-invariant" means $dL_g(X) = X$ for every $g \in G$, with $dL_g(X)$ the push-forward of Proposition C along the diffeomorphism $L_g$ of Proposition A(iii).
>
> **Part (i): a left-invariant field is determined at $e$.** Let $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $g \in G$. Evaluating the identity $dL_g(X) = X$ at the point $g$ and unfolding the definition of the push-forward,
> $$X(g) = dL_g(X)(g) = d_{L_g^{-1}(g)}L_g\Big(X\big(L_g^{-1}(g)\big)\Big) = d_{g^{-1}g}L_g\big(X(g^{-1}g)\big) = d_eL_g\big(X(e)\big) \qquad \text{(left-invariance; definition of } dL_g(X)\text{; } L_g^{-1} = L_{g^{-1}} \text{ by Proposition A(iii); } g^{-1}g = e\text{)}.$$
> This is Bär's chain of equalities $X(g) = dL_g(X)(g) = d_{L_{g^{-1}}(g)}L_g(X(L_{g^{-1}}(g))) = d_eL_g(X(e))$.
>
> **Part (ii), Step 0: $X$ is a rough vector field with $X(e) = X_0$.** For $g \in G$, $d_eL_g : T_eG \to T_{L_g(e)}G = T_gG$ since $L_g(e) = ge = g$; so $X(g) = d_eL_g(X_0) \in T_gG$ and $X$ is a section of $TG$. At $e$: $X(e) = d_eL_e(X_0) = d_e\mathrm{id}_G(X_0) = X_0$ (by $L_e = \mathrm{id}_G$, Proposition A(ii), and $d_e\mathrm{id}_G = \mathrm{id}_{T_eG}$, [[Thm - Chain Rule for the Differential]]).
>
> **Part (ii), Step 1: $X$ is left-invariant.** Fix $g \in G$; we must show $dL_g(X)(k) = X(k)$ for every $k \in G$. Put $h := g^{-1}k$, so that $L_g^{-1}(k) = L_{g^{-1}}(k) = h$ (Proposition A(iii)) and $gh = k$. Then
> $$dL_g(X)(k) = d_hL_g\big(X(h)\big) = d_hL_g\big(d_eL_h(X_0)\big) = d_e\big(L_g \circ L_h\big)(X_0) = d_eL_{gh}(X_0) = d_eL_k(X_0) = X(k)$$
> where the equalities hold, in order, by the definition of the push-forward, the definition of $X$, the [[Thm - Chain Rule for the Differential|chain rule]] (with $L_h(e) = h$), Proposition A(ii), $gh = k$, and the definition of $X$. Since $k$ was arbitrary, $dL_g(X) = X$; since $g$ was arbitrary, $X$ is left-invariant — granted that $X$ is smooth, which is Step 2. (The push-forward was defined for smooth fields; the pointwise computation above uses only the formula, and Step 2 shows that $X$ is in fact smooth, so the identity $dL_g(X) = X$ is an identity between smooth vector fields.)
>
> **Part (ii), Step 2: $X$ is smooth.** We use the chart criterion of [[Def - Smooth Vector Field]] again: it suffices to show that around every $g_0 \in G$ there is a chart in which the component functions of $X$ are smooth.
>
> **Construct a curve with velocity $X_0$:** let $(V, \psi = (z^1, \dots, z^n))$ be a smooth chart of $G$ with $e \in V$, and let $v = (v^1, \dots, v^n) \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be the components of $X_0$ in this chart, $X_0 = \sum_i v^i \,\partial/\partial z^i|_e$. Since $\psi(V)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^n$ there is $\epsilon > 0$ with $\psi(e) + tv \in \psi(V)$ for $|t| < \epsilon$, and we define
> $$\gamma : (-\epsilon, \epsilon) \to G, \qquad \gamma(t) := \psi^{-1}\big(\psi(e) + tv\big).$$
> Then $\gamma$ is smooth (its coordinate representative $t \mapsto \psi(e) + tv$ is affine), $\gamma(0) = e$, and by the coordinate expression of the [[Def - Velocity of a Curve|velocity of a curve]] — the components of $\gamma'(0)$ in the chart $\psi$ are the derivatives at $0$ of the coordinate functions $\psi^i \circ \gamma$, here $\frac{d}{dt}(\psi^i(e) + tv^i) = v^i$ — we have $\gamma'(0) = \sum_i v^i\, \partial/\partial z^i|_e = X_0$.
>
> **Express $X$ as a velocity:** for every $g \in G$,
> $$X(g) = d_eL_g(X_0) = d_eL_g\big(\gamma'(0)\big) = (L_g \circ \gamma)'(0) \qquad \text{(definition of } X\text{; } \gamma'(0) = X_0\text{; velocity of a composite curve)}.$$
> The last equality is property 2 of [[Def - Velocity of a Curve]], $(F \circ \gamma)'(t_0) = d_{\gamma(t_0)}F(\gamma'(t_0))$ for a smooth map $F$ and a smooth curve $\gamma$, proved in [[Thm - Equivalence of Tangent Vector Definitions]] (naturality of the curve picture of the differential).
> So $X(g)$ is the velocity at $t = 0$ of the curve $t \mapsto g\gamma(t)$.
>
> **Introduce the two-variable map:** define $\Phi : G \times (-\epsilon, \epsilon) \to G$, $\Phi(g, t) := m\big(g, \gamma(t)\big) = g\gamma(t)$. Then $\Phi = m \circ (\mathrm{id}_G \times \gamma)$, and $\mathrm{id}_G \times \gamma : G \times (-\epsilon, \epsilon) \to G \times G$ is smooth: in a product chart $(U_1 \times (-\epsilon, \epsilon), \varphi_1 \times \mathrm{id})$ of the domain (with $(-\epsilon, \epsilon)$ carrying its standard chart, and the product structure given by [[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]]) and a product chart $(U_1 \times V, \varphi_1 \times \psi)$ of the target, its coordinate representative is $(x, t) \mapsto (x, \psi(e) + tv)$, which is $C^\infty$ on the open set where it is defined. Hence $\Phi$ is smooth as the composition of the smooth maps $m$ (Lie group axiom) and $\mathrm{id}_G \times \gamma$ ([[Ex - Composition of Smooth Maps is Smooth]]).
>
> **Localise near $g_0$:** fix $g_0 \in G$ and a smooth chart $(U, \varphi = (x^1, \dots, x^n))$ of $G$ with $g_0 \in U$. Since $\Phi(g_0, 0) = g_0\gamma(0) = g_0e = g_0 \in U$ and $\Phi$ is continuous, the set $W := \Phi^{-1}(U)$ is open in $G \times (-\epsilon, \epsilon)$ and contains $(g_0, 0)$. Open sets of the product topology contain basic open boxes around each of their points, so there are an open $U_0 \subseteq U$ with $g_0 \in U_0$ and a $\delta \in (0, \epsilon]$ with $U_0 \times (-\delta, \delta) \subseteq W$. Thus for $g \in U_0$ and $|t| < \delta$ the point $g\gamma(t)$ lies in the chart domain $U$.
>
> **Compute the components of $X$ on $U_0$:** for $g \in U_0$ the curve $t \mapsto \Phi(g, t) = L_g(\gamma(t))$, $|t| < \delta$, takes values in $U$, so by the coordinate expression of the velocity its velocity at $t = 0$ has $i$-th component $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big|_{t=0}\, x^i\big(\Phi(g, t)\big)$. Combining with the display expressing $X(g)$ as this velocity,
> $$X(g) = \sum_{i=1}^n X^i(g)\, \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\Big|_g \quad \text{with} \quad X^i(g) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big|_{t=0}\, x^i\big(\Phi(g, t)\big) \qquad (g \in U_0) \qquad \text{(velocity of } t \mapsto L_g(\gamma(t)) \text{ in the chart } \varphi\text{)}.$$
> **Show the components are smooth:** let $\hat\Phi := \varphi \circ \Phi \circ (\varphi^{-1} \times \mathrm{id}) : (\varphi \times \mathrm{id})\big(W \cap (U \times (-\epsilon, \epsilon))\big) \to \varphi(U)$ be the coordinate representative of $\Phi$; its domain is an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}$ containing $\varphi(U_0) \times (-\delta, \delta)$, and $\hat\Phi$ is $C^\infty$ there because $\Phi$ is smooth ([[Def - Smooth Map between Manifolds]], characterisation (c): the coordinate representative with respect to any pair of charts is smooth wherever defined). For $g \in U_0$ and $|t| < \delta$ we have $x^i(\Phi(g, t)) = \hat\Phi^i(\varphi(g), t)$, so
> $$X^i(g) = \frac{\partial \hat\Phi^i}{\partial t}\big(\varphi(g), 0\big) \qquad (g \in U_0) \qquad \text{(substitute } \hat\Phi \text{ into the previous display)}.$$
> The partial derivative $\partial \hat\Phi^i / \partial t$ of a $C^\infty$ function is $C^\infty$ on the open domain of $\hat\Phi$; the map $x \mapsto (x, 0)$ from $\varphi(U_0)$ into that domain is $C^\infty$; and $\varphi : U_0 \to \varphi(U_0)$ is smooth. Hence $X^i = \big(\partial_t\hat\Phi^i\big)(\cdot, 0) \circ \varphi$ is a composition of smooth maps, hence smooth on $U_0$ ([[Ex - Composition of Smooth Maps is Smooth]]). By the chart criterion, $X$ is smooth on the neighbourhood $U_0$ of $g_0$; since $g_0 \in G$ was arbitrary, $X \in \mathfrak{X}(G)$.
>
> **Part (ii), Step 3: uniqueness.** Let $Y \in \mathfrak{g}$ with $Y(e) = X_0$. By Part (i), $Y(g) = d_eL_g(Y(e)) = d_eL_g(X_0) = X(g)$ for every $g \in G$, so $Y = X$. Combining Steps 0–3, $X$ is the unique left-invariant vector field with $X(e) = X_0$.
>
> **Part (iii): the linear isomorphism $T_eG \cong \mathfrak{g}$.** **Step 0 — $\Lambda$ takes values in $\mathfrak{g}$ and $\mathfrak{g}$ is a vector space:** by Part (ii), $\Lambda(X_0) \in \mathfrak{g}$ for every $X_0 \in T_eG$. The set $\mathfrak{g}$ is a linear subspace of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$: if $X, Y \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ then $dL_g(aX + bY) = a\, dL_g(X) + b\, dL_g(Y) = aX + bY$ for every $g$ (by Proposition C(iv), linearity of the push-forward, and left-invariance of $X$ and $Y$), so $aX + bY \in \mathfrak{g}$; and the zero field is left-invariant since $dL_g(0) = 0$ by linearity. **Step 1 — $\Lambda$ is linear:** for $X_0, Y_0 \in T_eG$, $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $g \in G$,
> $$\Lambda(aX_0 + bY_0)(g) = d_eL_g(aX_0 + bY_0) = a\, d_eL_g(X_0) + b\, d_eL_g(Y_0) = \big(a\Lambda(X_0) + b\Lambda(Y_0)\big)(g) \qquad \text{(definition of } \Lambda\text{; linearity of } d_eL_g\text{; pointwise operations in } \mathfrak{X}(G)\text{)}.$$
> **Step 2 — $\varepsilon$ is linear:** $\varepsilon(aX + bY) = (aX + bY)(e) = aX(e) + bY(e) = a\varepsilon(X) + b\varepsilon(Y)$ by the pointwise definition of the vector space operations on fields. **Step 3 — the two maps are mutually inverse:** for $X_0 \in T_eG$, $(\varepsilon \circ \Lambda)(X_0) = \Lambda(X_0)(e) = X_0$ by Part (ii), Step 0; for $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ and $g \in G$, $(\Lambda \circ \varepsilon)(X)(g) = d_eL_g(X(e)) = X(g)$ by Part (i), so $\Lambda \circ \varepsilon = \mathrm{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$. Hence $\Lambda$ is a linear bijection with inverse $\varepsilon$. **Step 4 — dimension:** $\dim \mathfrak{g} = \dim T_eG = \dim G$, the last equality by [[Thm - Dimension of the Tangent Space]].
>
> Therefore every tangent vector at the identity extends uniquely to a left-invariant vector field, the extension is smooth, and $\mathfrak{g}$ is a real vector space of dimension $\dim G$ canonically isomorphic to $T_eG$. $\blacksquare$

The same isomorphism, together with the bracket transported from $\mathfrak{X}(G)$ to $T_eG$, is established on the differential-geometry page [[Thm - Left-Invariant Vector Fields Form a Lie Algebra]]; the present page proves the vector-space statement in the form and notation that the series uses, and the sibling page [[Thm - The Space of Vector Fields is a Lie Algebra under the Lie Bracket]] supplies the bracket naturality that makes $\mathfrak{g}$ a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{X}(G)$.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The translations are the two regular actions of $G$ on itself, and the definitions of this page are the statement that these actions are smooth and that one of them, twisted by the other, is an action by automorphisms.

**Left translation is a smooth left action.** By Proposition A(ii), $L_{gh} = L_g \circ L_h$ and $L_e = \mathrm{id}_G$, so the assignment $g \mapsto L_g$ is a group homomorphism from $G$ to the group $\operatorname{Diff}(G)$ of diffeomorphisms of $G$ under composition; the associated map $G \times G \to G$, $(g, h) \mapsto L_g(h) = gh$, is the multiplication $m$ itself, which is smooth. In the language of [[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group]], the multiplication is a smooth left action of $G$ on the manifold $G$, and the series writes $\theta_g = L_g$ when it wants to view it that way. This action is free — $L_g(h) = h$ forces $g = e$ by cancellation — and transitive — $L_{kh^{-1}}(h) = k$ — so $G$ is a torsor over itself: a set on which $G$ acts freely and transitively, which is $G$ with the location of the identity forgotten. This is the model for the fibres of a principal $G$-bundle in chapter III, and the orbit map $\ell_p : G \to P$, $g \mapsto p \cdot g$, there is exactly a right translation transported to the fibre.

**Right translation is a smooth right action.** By Proposition A(ii), $R_g \circ R_h = R_{hg}$, so $g \mapsto R_g$ is an anti-homomorphism into $\operatorname{Diff}(G)$: composing right translations reverses the order. Equivalently, $g \mapsto R_{g^{-1}}$ is a homomorphism, because $R_{g^{-1}} \circ R_{h^{-1}} = R_{h^{-1}g^{-1}} = R_{(gh)^{-1}}$. The map $(h, g) \mapsto hg = R_g(h)$ is a smooth right action of $G$ on itself, and it is this action — not the left one — that the series uses on principal bundles, where $R_g(p) = p \cdot g$. The two actions commute, $L_g \circ R_h = R_h \circ L_g$ (Proposition A(ii)), which is associativity read as a statement about actions.

**Conjugation is the action by inner automorphisms.** By Proposition B(ii)–(iii), $g \mapsto \alpha_g$ is a group homomorphism from $G$ to the [[Def - Automorphism Group|automorphism group]] of $G$, whose image consists of the **inner automorphisms** $h \mapsto ghg^{-1}$ and whose kernel, by Proposition B(v) applied elementwise, is the set of $g$ with $gh = hg$ for all $h$ — the centre $Z(G)$ of $G$ ([[Ex - Inner automorphisms and the centre]]; the Lie-theoretic version, with the centres of $U(n)$, $SU(2)$, and $SO(3)$ computed, is the sibling exercise [[Ex - Conjugation and the Centre]]). Because each $\alpha_g$ fixes $e$, the differentials $d_e\alpha_g$ form a homomorphism $G \to GL(T_eG)$, $g \mapsto d_e\alpha_g$ — this is functoriality of the differential at the fixed point, $d_e\alpha_{gh} = d_e(\alpha_g \circ \alpha_h) = d_e\alpha_g \circ d_e\alpha_h$ by the chain rule and Proposition B(iii) — and this homomorphism is the adjoint representation $\operatorname{Ad}$ of the series conventions, treated on [[Def - Adjoint Representation]] and, with its smoothness and its differential $\operatorname{ad}$, on the sibling page [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]].

**Push-forward is a functor.** By Proposition C(iv), the assignment $M \mapsto \mathfrak{X}(M)$, $F \mapsto dF$ is a covariant functor from the category of smooth manifolds and diffeomorphisms to the category of real vector spaces; by Proposition C(iii) it takes values in Lie algebras and Lie algebra homomorphisms. Left-invariance of a field is the statement that it is a fixed point of the induced action $g \mapsto dL_g$ of $G$ on $\mathfrak{X}(G)$, and $\mathfrak{g}$ is the fixed-point subspace of this action. That the fixed-point subspace of a Lie algebra action by Lie algebra automorphisms is a Lie subalgebra is the one-line argument of the sibling page.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

In group theory the maps of this page are the oldest objects in the subject. The left translations are the permutations by which Cayley's theorem embeds an abstract group into a symmetric group: $g \mapsto L_g$ is an injective homomorphism $G \to \operatorname{Sym}(G)$, injective because $L_g = L_h$ evaluated at $e$ gives $g = h$. The inner automorphisms $\alpha_g$ and the normal subgroups they preserve are the material of [[Def - Normal Subgroup]], and the statement that a subgroup is normal if and only if it is invariant under every $\alpha_g$ is the algebraic shadow of the statement, proved in later chapters, that a Lie subalgebra is an ideal if and only if it is invariant under $\operatorname{Ad}_g = d_e\alpha_g$ for all $g$ in a connected group. What Lie theory adds is the smooth structure: the same maps are diffeomorphisms, so they have differentials, and the differentials at $e$ carry all the local information.

In differential geometry the translations are what distinguishes a Lie group from a manifold that merely happens to be diffeomorphic to one. The sphere $S^3$ is a Lie group ($SU(2)$, see [[Ex - SU(2) is Diffeomorphic to S^3]]) and so admits a family of diffeomorphisms $L_g$ acting freely and transitively; a consequence is that its tangent bundle is trivial, since the left-invariant extensions of a basis of $T_eG$ give $n$ pointwise linearly independent vector fields — Proposition D(ii) together with Proposition A(iv), which says that $d_eL_g$ is an isomorphism and hence carries a basis to a basis. The sphere $S^2$ admits no such structure, and indeed no nowhere-vanishing vector field at all. The push-forward is the operation that makes "invariant under a diffeomorphism" meaningful for vector fields, and its bracket naturality is what makes invariant fields closed under bracket; both are used again in chapter III for the fundamental vector fields of a bundle action and in chapter V for gauge transformations.

In the theory of connections, Proposition D is the statement that $TG$ carries a canonical global frame, and hence a canonical flat connection — the one in which the left-invariant fields are parallel ([[Ex - The Tangent Bundle of a Lie Group has a Canonical Flat Connection]]). The Maurer–Cartan form of chapter IV, $\theta_g = d_gL_{g^{-1}} : T_gG \to T_eG$, is the inverse of the isomorphism $d_eL_g$ of Proposition A(iv), read as a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued one-form; the local connection forms of every principal bundle in the series are pulled back from it.

**True name.** The operational content of the definitions is this: *left translation is the free transitive action of $G$ on itself that identifies every tangent space with $T_eG$ through the isomorphism $d_eL_g$, and conjugation is the part of that action which survives the demand that $e$ stay fixed.* A left-invariant vector field is, under this identification, a constant: the same vector $X_0 \in T_eG$ placed at every point by $d_eL_g$. The push-forward $dF(X)$ is "the vector field $X$ seen through the renaming $F$", and left-invariance is "invisible to every renaming by a left translation". Everything on the page is a consequence of the group axioms and the chain rule, with a single genuinely analytic input — the smoothness of the multiplication map — used once, in Step 2 of Proposition D.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the additive group $\mathbb{R}^n$.** Let $G = (\mathbb{R}^n, +)$, a Lie group because addition and negation are linear, hence smooth. Here $L_g(h) = g + h = R_g(h)$, so the translations are ordinary translations of Euclidean space and left and right translation coincide; $\alpha_g(h) = g + h - g = h$, so $\alpha_g = \mathrm{id}$ for every $g$, in agreement with Proposition B(v) since $\mathbb{R}^n$ is abelian. In the standard chart $\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{R}^n}$, $L_g$ has coordinate representative $x \mapsto x + g$, whose Jacobian is the identity matrix, so by the coordinate formula for the differential $d_hL_g(\partial_{x^i}|_h) = \partial_{x^i}|_{g+h}$. **Verify Proposition D:** for $X_0 = \sum_i v^i \partial_{x^i}|_0$, the left-invariant extension is $X(g) = d_0L_g(X_0) = \sum_i v^i \partial_{x^i}|_g$, the constant vector field with components $v$; it is smooth (constant components) and left-invariant, since $dL_g(X)(k) = d_{k-g}L_g(X(k-g)) = \sum_i v^i \partial_{x^i}|_k = X(k)$. Conversely a left-invariant field has $X(g) = d_0L_g(X(0))$, so its components are constant. Hence $\mathfrak{g} = \{\text{constant vector fields}\} \cong \mathbb{R}^n$, of dimension $n = \dim G$, as Proposition D(iii) predicts. **Verify Proposition B(iv) on $G = \mathbb{R}$ — the counterexample of the spec:** for $g \ne 0$, $L_g(h + k) = g + h + k$ while $L_g(h) + L_g(k) = 2g + h + k$; these differ by $g \ne 0$, so $L_g$ is not a homomorphism of $(\mathbb{R}, +)$, though it is a diffeomorphism with inverse $h \mapsto h - g$. For $g = 0$, $L_0 = \mathrm{id}$ is a homomorphism. This is exactly the dichotomy of Proposition B(iv).

**Is an instance — the general linear group $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$.** Let $G = GL(n; \mathbb{R})$, an open subset of $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$, with matrix multiplication ([[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]]; [[Ex - The General Linear Group is a Smooth Manifold]]). For $A \in G$, $L_A(B) = AB$ is the restriction to $G$ of a linear map of $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R})$, so in the global chart given by matrix entries its differential at every point is the same linear map: $d_BL_A(Y) = AY$ for $Y \in T_BG = \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R})$ ([[Ex - Tangent Space of the General Linear Group at the Identity]] identifies $T_1G$ with all matrices, and the same identification holds at every point of an open subset of a vector space). Likewise $d_BR_A(Y) = YA$, and $\alpha_A(B) = ABA^{-1}$ is the matrix conjugation, whose differential at $1$ is $d_1\alpha_A(Y) = AYA^{-1}$ (the composite of $Y \mapsto AY$ and $Y \mapsto YA^{-1}$ by the chain rule), which is the formula $\operatorname{Ad}_A Y = AYA^{-1}$ of the series conventions. **Verify Proposition D:** for $Y \in T_1G$ the left-invariant extension is $X(A) = d_1L_A(Y) = AY$, whose entries are polynomial in the entries of $A$, hence smooth; left-invariance: $dL_A(X)(C) = d_{A^{-1}C}L_A\big(X(A^{-1}C)\big) = A\,(A^{-1}C\,Y) = CY = X(C)$. **Verify Proposition B(iv):** for $n = 1$, $G = \mathbb{R}^\times$ and $L_a(bc) = abc$ while $L_a(b)L_a(c) = a^2bc$, which agree for all $b, c$ if and only if $a = 1$.

**Is an instance — the circle $U(1)$.** For $G = U(1) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$ with complex multiplication ([[Thm - SO(2) is Isomorphic to U(1)]]), $L_{e^{i\varphi}}(z) = e^{i\varphi}z$ is rotation of the circle by the angle $\varphi$; it is a diffeomorphism with inverse rotation by $-\varphi$, and since $U(1)$ is abelian, $\alpha_g = \mathrm{id}$ for all $g$. In the angle chart $z = e^{i\theta}$, $L_{e^{i\varphi}}$ reads $\theta \mapsto \theta + \varphi$, so $d_{e^{i\theta}}L_{e^{i\varphi}}(\partial_\theta) = \partial_\theta$, and the left-invariant extension of $X_0 = c\,\partial_\theta|_{1}$ is the field $c\,\partial_\theta$ of constant angular speed. Thus $\mathfrak{u}(1) \cong \mathbb{R}$, one-dimensional, as $\dim U(1) = 1$ requires.

**Is NOT an instance — a push-forward along a non-injective map.** Let $M = N = \mathbb{R}$, $F(x) = x^2$, and $X = \partial_x$. The map $F$ is smooth but not a diffeomorphism: it is neither injective ($F(1) = F(-1)$) nor surjective (no $x$ has $x^2 = -1$). The would-be formula $q \mapsto d_pF(X(p))$ with $F(p) = q$ fails in both ways predicted in the Axiom Motivation: at $q = -1$ there is no $p$ at all; at $q = 1$ the two preimages give $d_1F(\partial_x|_1) = 2\,\partial_x|_1$ and $d_{-1}F(\partial_x|_{-1}) = -2\,\partial_x|_1$ (the Jacobian of $x \mapsto x^2$ at $\pm 1$ is $\pm 2$), which disagree. So there is no vector field on $\mathbb{R}$ that is $F$-related to $\partial_x$, and the hypothesis that $F$ be a diffeomorphism in Proposition C cannot be dropped. (What survives for general smooth maps is the *relation* of [[Def - F-Related Vector Fields]], not a construction.)

**Is NOT an instance — inversion is a diffeomorphism but not a homomorphism.** The inversion $i : G \to G$, $i(g) = g^{-1}$, is a diffeomorphism (smooth by the Lie group axiom, and its own inverse since $(g^{-1})^{-1} = g$), and it fixes $e$. It is not, in general, a group homomorphism: $i(gh) = (gh)^{-1} = h^{-1}g^{-1}$, whereas $i(g)i(h) = g^{-1}h^{-1}$. On $G = GL(2; \mathbb{R})$ take $g = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $h = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$; then $g^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $h^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and
$$h^{-1}g^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad g^{-1}h^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{(multiply out the entries)},$$
which differ. So fixing $e$ is necessary but not sufficient for a diffeomorphism of $G$ to be an automorphism; conjugation is singled out by Proposition B(ii), not by the fixed point alone. (Inversion is a homomorphism exactly when $G$ is abelian, since $h^{-1}g^{-1} = g^{-1}h^{-1}$ for all $g, h$ is equivalent, after inverting both sides, to $gh = hg$.)

**Corollary — the differential of conjugation at the identity.** For every $g \in G$, $\alpha_g(e) = e$ (Proposition B(ii)), so $d_e\alpha_g : T_eG \to T_eG$ is a linear map of the single vector space $T_eG$, and it is an isomorphism with inverse $d_e\alpha_{g^{-1}}$: by the chain rule and Proposition B(i), $d_e\alpha_{g^{-1}} \circ d_e\alpha_g = d_e(\alpha_{g^{-1}} \circ \alpha_g) = d_e\mathrm{id}_G = \mathrm{id}_{T_eG}$, and symmetrically. By Proposition B(iii) and the chain rule, $d_e\alpha_{gh} = d_e\alpha_g \circ d_e\alpha_h$ and $d_e\alpha_e = \mathrm{id}$. Unwinding $\alpha_g = L_g \circ R_{g^{-1}}$ with the chain rule, $d_e\alpha_g = d_{g^{-1}}L_g \circ d_eR_{g^{-1}}$. This linear map is $\operatorname{Ad}_g$ ([[Def - Adjoint Representation]]); that $g \mapsto \operatorname{Ad}_g$ is smooth and that its differential is $\operatorname{ad}_X = [X, \cdot]$ are proved on [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]].

**Corollary — right-invariant fields.** Everything in Proposition D holds with $R$ in place of $L$: a vector field $X$ is right-invariant when $dR_g(X) = X$ for all $g$; a right-invariant field satisfies $X(g) = dR_g(X)(g) = d_{gg^{-1}}R_g(X(gg^{-1})) = d_eR_g(X(e))$ (by the definition of the push-forward and $R_g^{-1} = R_{g^{-1}}$); and for $X_0 \in T_eG$ the field $g \mapsto d_eR_g(X_0)$ is right-invariant because $d_hR_g(d_eR_h(X_0)) = d_e(R_g \circ R_h)(X_0) = d_eR_{hg}(X_0)$ (chain rule and Proposition A(ii)), and smooth by the argument of Step 2 with $\Phi(g, t) := \gamma(t)g$ in place of $g\gamma(t)$, every step of which goes through with the factors of the multiplication exchanged. The series uses left-invariant fields for $\mathfrak{g}$, in accordance with the standing conventions; the right-invariant fields reappear in chapter V as the generators of the gauge group's action.

**Corollary — the translations commute with the extension.** If $X$ is the left-invariant extension of $X_0$, then $dR_h(X)$ is again left-invariant for every $h \in G$: $dL_g(dR_h(X)) = d(L_g \circ R_h)(X) = d(R_h \circ L_g)(X) = dR_h(dL_g(X)) = dR_h(X)$, by the functoriality of Proposition C(iv), the commutation $L_g \circ R_h = R_h \circ L_g$ of Proposition A(ii), and left-invariance of $X$. Its value at $e$ is $dR_h(X)(e) = d_{h^{-1}}R_h(X(h^{-1})) = d_{h^{-1}}R_h\big(d_eL_{h^{-1}}(X_0)\big) = d_e(R_h \circ L_{h^{-1}})(X_0) = d_e\alpha_{h^{-1}}(X_0)$, using the definition of the push-forward, Proposition D(i), the chain rule, and $R_h \circ L_{h^{-1}} = L_{h^{-1}} \circ R_h = \alpha_{h^{-1}}$. So, by the uniqueness in Proposition D(ii), $dR_h(X) = \Lambda\big(d_e\alpha_{h^{-1}}(X_0)\big) = \Lambda(\operatorname{Ad}_{h^{-1}}X_0)$: pushing a left-invariant field forward along a right translation is the adjoint action on its value at $e$. This identity is the group-level source of the equivariance $R_g^*\omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\omega$ imposed on connection forms in chapter IV.

**Calibration check.** First, verify directly from the definitions that $L_g \circ R_h = R_h \circ L_g$ and that $\alpha_g = R_{g^{-1}} \circ L_g$ as well as $L_g \circ R_{g^{-1}}$ — both are associativity, and both orderings give $h \mapsto ghg^{-1}$. Second, for $G = GL(2; \mathbb{R})$ and $Y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, write out the left-invariant extension $X(A) = AY$ in the four matrix entries $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ — it is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & a \\ 0 & c \end{pmatrix}$, so $X = a\,\partial_b + c\,\partial_d$ — and check by hand that its components are smooth and that $X(1) = Y$. Third, explain in one sentence why the smoothness of $g \mapsto d_eL_g(X_0)$ is not immediate from the smoothness of each $L_g$ separately: the family $\{L_g\}$ is parametrised by $g$, and it is the joint smoothness of $(g, h) \mapsto gh$, not the smoothness of each $L_g$, that makes the components $\partial_t\hat\Phi^i(\varphi(g), 0)$ smooth in $g$. If all three are clear, the definitions of this page have been understood.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The Lie algebra as a finite-dimensional object *(from this chapter)*
> Proposition D(iii) identifies the space $\mathfrak{g}$ of left-invariant vector fields with $T_eG$; the sibling page [[Thm - The Space of Vector Fields is a Lie Algebra under the Lie Bracket]] shows $\mathfrak{g}$ is closed under the bracket, and [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]] then computes $\mathfrak{gl}(n)$, $\mathfrak{o}(n)$, $\mathfrak{sl}(n)$, $\mathfrak{u}(n)$, $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ as spaces of matrices, with the bracket the commutator.

> [!tip] The adjoint representation *(from this chapter)*
> The differential $d_e\alpha_g$ of conjugation is $\operatorname{Ad}_g$; [[Thm - Ad is a Smooth Representation and its Differential is ad]] proves that $g \mapsto \operatorname{Ad}_g$ is a smooth representation of $G$ on $\mathfrak{g}$ with differential $\operatorname{ad}$, the first representation of the series and the one through which every connection form, curvature form, and gauge transformation transforms.

> [!tip] One-parameter subgroups and the exponential map *(from this chapter)*
> The integral curves of the left-invariant fields constructed here are the one-parameter subgroups $t \mapsto \exp(tX)$; [[Thm - One-Parameter Subgroups are the Integral Curves of Left-Invariant Vector Fields]] proves this and the completeness of left-invariant fields, using that the flow of a left-invariant field commutes with every $L_g$ — the flow-naturality face of the push-forward.

> [!tip] Fundamental vector fields and the Maurer–Cartan form *(from chapters I, III, and IV)*
> On a manifold with a right $G$-action, the orbit maps $\ell_p$ push the left-invariant fields forward to the fundamental vector fields of [[Def - Fundamental Vector Field of a Group Action]]; on $G$ itself, the inverse $d_gL_{g^{-1}}$ of the isomorphism $d_eL_g$ of Proposition A(iv) is the left Maurer–Cartan form of [[Def - The Maurer-Cartan Form]], from which every local connection form in the series is built.
