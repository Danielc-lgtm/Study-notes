# Problem Statement

Throughout the series, manifolds are smooth, Hausdorff, and second countable, and "smooth" means $C^\infty$. Write $\mathbb{H}$ for the real associative algebra of **[[Def - Quaternions|quaternions]]**: the four-dimensional real vector space with basis $1, i, j, k$ and the multiplication generated by $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = -1$ and $ij = k$ (equivalently $ijk = -1$), from which $ji = -k$, $jk = i$, $kj = -i$, $ki = j$, $ik = -j$ follow. A quaternion is $q = a + bi + cj + dk$ with $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}$; its conjugate is $\bar{q} = a - bi - cj - dk$ and its norm is $|q| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2}$, so that $q\bar{q} = \bar{q}q = |q|^2$. We identify the complex numbers with the subalgebra $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R} + \mathbb{R}i \subseteq \mathbb{H}$, and we use the coordinatisation
$$q = z + wj, \qquad z = a + bi \in \mathbb{C}, \quad w = c + di \in \mathbb{C},$$
which is legitimate because $z + wj = (a + bi) + (c + di)j = a + bi + cj + dij = a + bi + cj + dk$, so every quaternion is $z + wj$ for a unique pair $(z, w) \in \mathbb{C}^2$. The **group of unit quaternions** is
$$Sp(1) = \{q \in \mathbb{H} : |q| = 1\} = \{q \in \mathbb{H} : q\bar{q} = 1\},$$
a Lie group under quaternion multiplication (Block C of [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]]); the **special unitary group of rank two** is
$$SU(2) = \{A \in \operatorname{Mat}(2 \times 2; \mathbb{C}) : A^* A = 1_2 \text{ and } \det A = 1\},$$
where $A^* = (\bar{A})^t$ is the conjugate transpose and $1_2$ the identity matrix, an embedded Lie subgroup of $GL(2; \mathbb{C})$ (Block B of [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]]). For a complex number $\zeta$ we write $\bar{\zeta}$ for its complex conjugate; this coincides with the quaternionic conjugate on the subalgebra $\mathbb{C} \subseteq \mathbb{H}$. This page depends on no sign or action convention.

Consider the map
$$\Phi : Sp(1) \longrightarrow SU(2), \qquad \Phi(q) = \Phi(z + wj) = \begin{pmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{pmatrix}.$$

Prove that:

- **(a)** $\Phi$ is well defined, that is, $\Phi(q) \in SU(2)$ for every $q \in Sp(1)$;
- **(b)** $\Phi$ is a group homomorphism, $\Phi(qq') = \Phi(q)\Phi(q')$, and it is bijective;
- **(c)** $\Phi$ is a diffeomorphism, hence a Lie group isomorphism $Sp(1) \cong SU(2)$;
- **(d)** both groups are, as smooth manifolds, the three-sphere $S^3 = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^4 : |x| = 1\}$.

This is the isomorphism $Sp(1) \cong SU(2)$ that Haydys records in §2.4 (page 25, with the displayed formula $q = z + wj \mapsto \left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$) and calls "easy to see"; here every clause of "easy to see" is verified. It is part of the content of Haydys's Definition 79 (item D2.4.6), which uses $Sp(1) \cong SU(2)$ silently in the classification of $SU(2)$-bundles.

**Recall:**

The tools are the definitions of the two groups, the quaternion multiplication law in the $z + wj$ coordinatisation, the parametrisation of a $2 \times 2$ special unitary matrix, and the definition of a Lie group isomorphism.

![[Def - Classical Matrix Groups#The Definition]]

The single computational fact about quaternions that drives the whole proof is the commutation rule between $j$ and a complex number: for every $\zeta = x + yi \in \mathbb{C}$,
$$j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}\, j.$$
This holds because $ji = -k = -ij = (-i)j = \bar{i}\,j$, so $j(x + yi) = xj + y(ji) = xj + y\bar{i}j = (x - yi)j = \bar{\zeta}j$, using that $j$ commutes with the real scalars $x, y$. A second fact, that quaternionic conjugation reverses products, $\overline{pq} = \bar{q}\,\bar{p}$, and is norm-multiplicative, $|pq| = |p|\,|q|$, is proved in Block C of [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]] (Identities 1 and 3 there) and restated where used.

![[Def - Lie Group Homomorphism#The Definition]]

A [[Def - Lie Group Homomorphism|Lie group homomorphism]] is a smooth map $F : G \to G'$ between Lie groups that respects multiplication, $F(gh) = F(g)F(h)$; it is a **Lie group isomorphism** when it is bijective and its inverse $F^{-1}$ is also a smooth homomorphism. A bijective homomorphism whose inverse map is smooth is automatically an isomorphism, because the inverse of a bijective homomorphism is a homomorphism (from $F(g)F(h) = F(gh)$ one gets $F^{-1}(xy) = F^{-1}(x)F^{-1}(y)$ by substituting $x = F(g)$, $y = F(h)$).

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class:** This is a *construct-and-verify-an-isomorphism* problem. We are handed an explicit map and must certify four separate properties — lands in the target, respects multiplication, is bijective, is smooth both ways — and the art is entirely in choosing the right coordinates so that each verification is a short computation rather than a struggle. The decisive choice, made for us by Haydys's formula, is the coordinatisation $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{C} \oplus \mathbb{C}j$, which turns the four real coordinates $(a, b, c, d)$ into the two complex coordinates $(z, w)$ that are exactly the first column of the matrix $\Phi(q)$.

**Assumption pattern:** The recognisable feature is that both groups are *norm-one* groups: $Sp(1)$ is the quaternions of norm one and $SU(2)$ is (as we will see) the matrices $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with $|z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1$. The single quaternion identity $j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}j$ is the entire reason the map is multiplicative: it converts the cross terms of a quaternion product into exactly the cross terms of a matrix product, with the bar landing in the right places to match the second row $(-\bar{w}, \bar{z})$ of the matrix.

**Theorem routing:** The route is: (0) record the quaternion product law $(z + wj)(z' + w'j) = (zz' - w\bar{w'}) + (zw' + w\bar{z'})j$ from $j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}j$; (a) compute $\det\Phi(q) = |z|^2 + |w|^2 = |q|^2$ and $\Phi(q)^*\Phi(q) = 1_2$ to place $\Phi(q)$ in $SU(2)$; (b) multiply the two matrices $\Phi(q)$, $\Phi(q')$ and read off that the result is $\Phi(qq')$, using the product law of Step 0, then get injectivity from the trivial kernel and surjectivity by solving $A^* = A^{-1}$ for a general $A \in SU(2)$; (c) observe both $\Phi$ and $\Phi^{-1}$ are *linear* in the real coordinates, hence smooth, and use that $Sp(1)$ and $SU(2)$ are embedded submanifolds so that smoothness of the ambient linear maps restricts and corestricts; (d) identify each group's underlying set with the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^4$ and cite [[Ex - SU(2) is Diffeomorphic to S^3]].

**Key decision point:** The one genuinely non-obvious move is the *surjectivity* argument. Injectivity and multiplicativity are direct once the coordinates are chosen, but surjectivity requires knowing what a general element of $SU(2)$ looks like, and this is not given — it must be extracted from the two defining equations $A^*A = 1_2$ and $\det A = 1$. The clean way is to rewrite $A^*A = 1_2$ as $A^* = A^{-1}$ and use the closed-form inverse of a $2 \times 2$ matrix of determinant one, $A^{-1} = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \delta & -\beta \\ -\gamma & \alpha \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ for $A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ \gamma & \delta \end{smallmatrix}\right)$; comparing this entry by entry with $A^* = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \bar{\alpha} & \bar{\gamma} \\ \bar{\beta} & \bar{\delta} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ forces $\delta = \bar{\alpha}$ and $\gamma = -\bar{\beta}$, which is precisely the shape $\Phi(z + wj)$ with $z = \alpha$, $w = \beta$. The temptation to avoid is to try to prove surjectivity by a dimension count or a connectedness argument; here the explicit inversion is both shorter and constructive.

# Legal Operations Used

The topic page's Legal Operations are not yet numbered when this page is written; each operation below is named descriptively so that the numbers can be reconciled once the topic page exists.

1. **Coordinatise $\mathbb{H}$ as $\mathbb{C} \oplus \mathbb{C}j$ and reduce quaternion algebra to the rule $j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}j$.** Writing $q = z + wj$ turns the quaternion product into a $2 \times 2$-matrix-shaped bilinear law in $(z, w)$; the only relation needed is that $j$ passes a complex scalar to its conjugate. Applied in Step 0.

2. **Verify membership in a matrix group by its defining equations.** To show $\Phi(q) \in SU(2)$, check $\det\Phi(q) = 1$ and $\Phi(q)^*\Phi(q) = 1_2$ directly, using $\det A = |z|^2 + |w|^2$ for a matrix of this shape. Applied in Step (a).

3. **Prove multiplicativity by matching a matrix product to the image of a product.** Multiply the two special forms and recognise the result as the image of the product quaternion computed in Step 0. Applied in Step (b).

4. **Get injectivity from a trivial kernel and surjectivity by solving the defining equations for a general target element.** For a homomorphism, injectivity is $\ker\Phi = \{1\}$; surjectivity is obtained by parametrising $SU(2)$ from $A^* = A^{-1}$. Applied in Step (b).

5. **Upgrade a bijective homomorphism to a Lie group isomorphism by exhibiting both directions as smooth restrictions of ambient linear maps.** Since $Sp(1) \subseteq \mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}^4$ and $SU(2) \subseteq \operatorname{Mat}(2 \times 2; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{R}^8$ are embedded submanifolds, a linear (hence smooth) ambient map that carries one into the other restricts to a smooth map, and its linear inverse does likewise. Applied in Step (c).

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Do not expand quaternion products into the $1, i, j, k$ basis. Instead write every quaternion as $z + wj$ with $z, w \in \mathbb{C}$ and learn the single rule that lets you move $j$ past a complex number. What is $j\zeta$ for $\zeta \in \mathbb{C}$? Compute $ji$ from $ij = k$.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> Using $j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}j$, expand $(z + wj)(z' + w'j)$. You should find the new complex components $Z = zz' - w\bar{w'}$ and $W = zw' + w\bar{z'}$. Now write down the matrix product $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z' & w' \\ -\bar{w'} & \bar{z'} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ and compare its first row with $(Z, W)$.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For well-definedness, the determinant of $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is $z\bar{z} - w(-\bar{w}) = |z|^2 + |w|^2$, and $|q|^2 = |z|^2 + |w|^2$ as well. The condition $A^*A = 1_2$ is a four-entry computation that also collapses to $|z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1$ on the diagonal and to $0$ off it.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For surjectivity, take a general $A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ \gamma & \delta \end{smallmatrix}\right) \in SU(2)$. The equation $A^*A = 1_2$ says $A^* = A^{-1}$. Write down both sides: $A^* = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \bar{\alpha} & \bar{\gamma} \\ \bar{\beta} & \bar{\delta} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$, and $A^{-1} = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \delta & -\beta \\ -\gamma & \alpha \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ because $\det A = 1$. Equate entries.

> [!note]- Hint 5
> For smoothness, notice that both $q \mapsto \Phi(q)$ and $A \mapsto \Phi^{-1}(A)$ are *linear* maps of the underlying real coordinates: $\Phi$ sends $(a, b, c, d)$ to a matrix whose entries are $a, b, c, d$ with signs, and $\Phi^{-1}$ reads $z, w$ off the first column of $A$. A linear map between Euclidean spaces is smooth. Then invoke that $Sp(1)$ and $SU(2)$ are embedded submanifolds.

# Solution

The plan is to fix coordinates first and let them do the work. Step 0 records the quaternion product in the $z + wj$ coordinates, from which every later step reads off. Step (a) verifies $\Phi$ lands in $SU(2)$; Step (b) proves multiplicativity by a single matrix multiplication and then bijectivity by a trivial kernel and an explicit parametrisation of $SU(2)$; Step (c) certifies smoothness of $\Phi$ and $\Phi^{-1}$ from their linearity; Step (d) identifies both groups with $S^3$.

**Step 0: The quaternion product law in the $z + wj$ coordinates.**

For $q = z + wj$ and $q' = z' + w'j$ with $z, w, z', w' \in \mathbb{C}$,
$$q q' = (zz' - w\bar{w'}) + (zw' + w\bar{z'})\,j.$$

> [!note]- Derivation
> We need to expand the product $(z + wj)(z' + w'j)$ and collect it back into the form $Z + Wj$ with $Z, W \in \mathbb{C}$.
>
> **Distribute the product.** By the distributive law in the associative algebra $\mathbb{H}$,
> $$(z + wj)(z' + w'j) = zz' + z(w'j) + (wj)z' + (wj)(w'j) \qquad \text{(distributivity; associativity of } \mathbb{H}\text{)}.$$
>
> **Move each $j$ to the right using $j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}j$.** The first term $zz'$ is already complex. The second term $z(w'j) = (zw')j$ is complex times $j$. In the third term, $(wj)z' = w(jz') = w(\bar{z'}j) = (w\bar{z'})j$, using $jz' = \bar{z'}j$ (the rule recalled above, valid for any $z' \in \mathbb{C}$). In the fourth term,
> $$(wj)(w'j) = w(jw')j = w(\bar{w'}j)j = w\bar{w'}\,j^2 = -w\bar{w'} \qquad \text{(} jw' = \bar{w'}j \text{; } j^2 = -1\text{)},$$
> which is complex. Collecting the complex parts and the parts carrying a single $j$,
> $$q q' = \big(zz' - w\bar{w'}\big) + \big(zw' + w\bar{z'}\big)\,j.$$
> This is the promised law; we abbreviate its components as $Z := zz' - w\bar{w'}$ and $W := zw' + w\bar{z'}$.

**Step (a): $\Phi$ is well defined — $\Phi(q) \in SU(2)$ for $q \in Sp(1)$.**

The matrix $A = \Phi(z + wj) = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ has $\det A = |z|^2 + |w|^2 = |q|^2 = 1$ and satisfies $A^*A = 1_2$; hence $A \in SU(2)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> We must show the two defining equations of $SU(2)$, namely $\det A = 1$ and $A^*A = 1_2$, both hold for $A = \Phi(q)$ when $|q| = 1$.
>
> **The determinant.** By the $2 \times 2$ determinant formula,
> $$\det A = z \cdot \bar{z} - w \cdot (-\bar{w}) = |z|^2 + |w|^2 \qquad \text{(} z\bar{z} = |z|^2 \text{, } w\bar{w} = |w|^2\text{)}.$$
> Now $|q|^2 = q\bar{q}$, and with $q = z + wj$ its conjugate is $\bar{q} = \bar{z} - wj$ — indeed the quaternionic conjugate of $z + wj = a + bi + cj + dk$ is $a - bi - cj - dk = (a - bi) - (c + di)j = \bar{z} - wj$. So, computing the norm from the real coordinates, $|q|^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 = |z|^2 + |w|^2$. Therefore $\det A = |z|^2 + |w|^2 = |q|^2 = 1$ since $q \in Sp(1)$.
>
> **The unitarity equation.** The conjugate transpose is $A^* = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \bar{z} & -w \\ \bar{w} & z \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ (transpose $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & -\bar{w} \\ w & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$, then conjugate every entry). Multiplying,
> $$A^*A = \begin{pmatrix} \bar{z} & -w \\ \bar{w} & z \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \bar{z}z + (-w)(-\bar{w}) & \bar{z}w + (-w)\bar{z} \\ \bar{w}z + z(-\bar{w}) & \bar{w}w + z\bar{z} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} |z|^2 + |w|^2 & 0 \\ 0 & |z|^2 + |w|^2 \end{pmatrix},$$
> the off-diagonal entries vanishing because $\bar{z}w - w\bar{z} = 0$ and $\bar{w}z - z\bar{w} = 0$ (multiplication in $\mathbb{C}$ is commutative), and the diagonal entries being $|z|^2 + |w|^2$. Since $|z|^2 + |w|^2 = |q|^2 = 1$, this is $1_2$.
>
> Both defining equations hold, so $A = \Phi(q) \in U(2)$ with $\det A = 1$, that is, $\Phi(q) \in SU(2)$. Hence $\Phi$ is well defined.

**Step (b): $\Phi$ is a bijective homomorphism.**

$\Phi(qq') = \Phi(q)\Phi(q')$ by the matrix product and Step 0; $\Phi$ is injective because its kernel is trivial; and $\Phi$ is surjective because every $A \in SU(2)$ has the form $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> We show three things: multiplicativity, injectivity, and surjectivity.
>
> **Multiplicativity.** Let $q = z + wj$, $q' = z' + w'j$. Multiply the two images:
> $$\Phi(q)\Phi(q') = \begin{pmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} z' & w' \\ -\bar{w'} & \bar{z'} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} zz' - w\bar{w'} & zw' + w\bar{z'} \\ -\bar{w}z' - \bar{z}\bar{w'} & -\bar{w}w' + \bar{z}\bar{z'} \end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{(matrix multiplication)}.$$
> By Step 0, the product $qq'$ has complex components $Z = zz' - w\bar{w'}$ and $W = zw' + w\bar{z'}$, so
> $$\Phi(qq') = \begin{pmatrix} Z & W \\ -\bar{W} & \bar{Z} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} zz' - w\bar{w'} & zw' + w\bar{z'} \\ -\overline{(zw' + w\bar{z'})} & \overline{(zz' - w\bar{w'})} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} zz' - w\bar{w'} & zw' + w\bar{z'} \\ -(\bar{z}\bar{w'} + \bar{w}z') & \bar{z}\bar{z'} - \bar{w}w' \end{pmatrix},$$
> the last equality expanding the conjugates with $\overline{\bar{z'}} = z'$. The two matrices agree entry by entry: the first rows are identical, the $(2,1)$ entries are both $-\bar{w}z' - \bar{z}\bar{w'}$, and the $(2,2)$ entries are both $\bar{z}\bar{z'} - \bar{w}w'$. Hence
> $$\Phi(qq') = \Phi(q)\Phi(q').$$
> This is exactly where the quaternion identity $j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}j$ of Step 0 pays off: it produced the bars on $\bar{w'}$ and $\bar{z'}$ that the matrix product independently produces.
>
> **Injectivity.** $\Phi$ is a homomorphism between groups, so it is injective if and only if its kernel is trivial. Suppose $\Phi(z + wj) = 1_2$, that is $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right) = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Reading the first row gives $z = 1$ and $w = 0$, so $q = 1 + 0j = 1$, the identity of $Sp(1)$. Thus $\ker\Phi = \{1\}$, and for a homomorphism this forces injectivity: if $\Phi(q_1) = \Phi(q_2)$ then $\Phi(q_1 q_2^{-1}) = \Phi(q_1)\Phi(q_2)^{-1} = 1_2$, so $q_1 q_2^{-1} = 1$, i.e. $q_1 = q_2$.
>
> **Surjectivity.** Let $A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ \gamma & \delta \end{smallmatrix}\right) \in SU(2)$, with $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta \in \mathbb{C}$, $\det A = \alpha\delta - \beta\gamma = 1$, and $A^*A = 1_2$. The equation $A^*A = 1_2$ says $A^* = A^{-1}$. On the one hand,
> $$A^* = \begin{pmatrix} \bar{\alpha} & \bar{\gamma} \\ \bar{\beta} & \bar{\delta} \end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{(conjugate transpose)};$$
> on the other hand, since $\det A = 1$, the closed-form inverse of a $2 \times 2$ matrix gives
> $$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\begin{pmatrix} \delta & -\beta \\ -\gamma & \alpha \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \delta & -\beta \\ -\gamma & \alpha \end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{(adjugate formula; } \det A = 1\text{)}.$$
> Equating $A^* = A^{-1}$ entry by entry:
> $$\bar{\alpha} = \delta, \qquad \bar{\gamma} = -\beta, \qquad \bar{\beta} = -\gamma, \qquad \bar{\delta} = \alpha.$$
> The first and fourth are the same relation ($\delta = \bar{\alpha}$); the second and third are the same relation ($\gamma = -\bar{\beta}$). Set $z := \alpha$ and $w := \beta$. Then $\delta = \bar{z}$ and $\gamma = -\bar{w}$, so
> $$A = \begin{pmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{pmatrix},$$
> and $\det A = z\bar{z} + w\bar{w} = |z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1$. Setting $q := z + wj$, we have $|q|^2 = |z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1$, so $q \in Sp(1)$, and $\Phi(q) = A$. Hence every $A \in SU(2)$ is in the image, and $\Phi$ is surjective.
>
> Being multiplicative, injective, and surjective, $\Phi$ is a bijective group homomorphism.

**Step (c): $\Phi$ is a diffeomorphism, hence a Lie group isomorphism.**

Both $\Phi$ and its set-theoretic inverse $\Phi^{-1}$ are restrictions of $\mathbb{R}$-linear maps between the ambient Euclidean spaces $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}^4$ and $\operatorname{Mat}(2 \times 2; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{R}^8$; since $Sp(1)$ and $SU(2)$ are embedded submanifolds, both restrictions are smooth, so $\Phi$ is a diffeomorphism and, with Step (b), a Lie group isomorphism.

> [!note]- Derivation
> We must show $\Phi : Sp(1) \to SU(2)$ and $\Phi^{-1} : SU(2) \to Sp(1)$ are both smooth as maps of manifolds.
>
> **$\Phi$ extends to an ambient linear map.** Define $\widetilde{\Phi} : \mathbb{H} \to \operatorname{Mat}(2 \times 2; \mathbb{C})$ by the same formula, $\widetilde{\Phi}(z + wj) = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$, on all of $\mathbb{H}$ (not only the unit quaternions). In the real coordinates $q = a + bi + cj + dk$, so $z = a + bi$ and $w = c + di$, the four complex matrix entries are
> $$A_{11} = a + bi, \quad A_{12} = c + di, \quad A_{21} = -c + di, \quad A_{22} = a - bi,$$
> whose eight real and imaginary parts are $\mathbb{R}$-linear functions of $(a, b, c, d)$. An $\mathbb{R}$-linear map between finite-dimensional real vector spaces is smooth (its partial derivatives are the constant matrix entries). So $\widetilde{\Phi} : \mathbb{R}^4 \to \mathbb{R}^8$ is smooth.
>
> **Restriction and corestriction to the submanifolds.** $Sp(1) \subseteq \mathbb{H}$ is an embedded submanifold (Block C of [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]]) and $SU(2) \subseteq \operatorname{Mat}(2 \times 2; \mathbb{C})$ is an embedded submanifold (Block B there). The restriction $\widetilde{\Phi}|_{Sp(1)}$ is smooth because the inclusion $Sp(1) \hookrightarrow \mathbb{H}$ is smooth and composition of smooth maps is smooth. Its image lies in $SU(2)$ by Step (a). A smooth map into an ambient manifold whose image lies in an *embedded* submanifold is smooth as a map into that submanifold — this is the characteristic property of embedded submanifolds ([[Def - Embedded Submanifold]]: for an embedded submanifold, a map into it is smooth if and only if it is smooth as a map into the ambient manifold). Hence $\Phi : Sp(1) \to SU(2)$ is smooth.
>
> **The inverse is smooth by the same argument.** By Step (b), $\Phi^{-1}(A) = z + wj$ where $z = A_{11}$ and $w = A_{12}$ are the first-column entries of $A$. Define $\widetilde{\Psi} : \operatorname{Mat}(2 \times 2; \mathbb{C}) \to \mathbb{H}$ by $\widetilde{\Psi}(A) = A_{11} + A_{12}\, j$, that is $(a', b', c', d') \mapsto a' + b'i + c'j + d'k$ where $A_{11} = a' + b'i$, $A_{12} = c' + d'i$; this reads off four of the eight real coordinates, hence is $\mathbb{R}$-linear, hence smooth $\mathbb{R}^8 \to \mathbb{R}^4$. Its restriction $\widetilde{\Psi}|_{SU(2)}$ is smooth, lands in $Sp(1)$ (Step b), and equals $\Phi^{-1}$; by the embedded-submanifold property again, $\Phi^{-1} : SU(2) \to Sp(1)$ is smooth.
>
> **Conclusion.** $\Phi$ is a bijective smooth map with smooth inverse, that is, a [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphism]]. Combined with the homomorphism property of Step (b), $\Phi$ is a Lie group isomorphism $Sp(1) \cong SU(2)$.

**Step (d): both groups are the three-sphere.**

As a set, $Sp(1) = \{q \in \mathbb{R}^4 : |q| = 1\} = S^3$, and the diffeomorphism $\Phi$ carries this smooth structure to $SU(2)$; so $SU(2) \cong Sp(1) \cong S^3$ as smooth manifolds.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **$Sp(1)$ is $S^3$.** Under the identification $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}^4$ by $q = a + bi + cj + dk \leftrightarrow (a, b, c, d)$, the norm $|q| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2}$ is the Euclidean norm, so $Sp(1) = \{q : |q| = 1\}$ is exactly the unit sphere $S^3 = \{(a, b, c, d) \in \mathbb{R}^4 : a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 = 1\}$. This is an equality of sets; the smooth structure of $Sp(1)$ as an embedded submanifold of $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}^4$ is the smooth structure of $S^3$ as the unit sphere, because both are the embedded-submanifold structure induced from $\mathbb{R}^4$ on the same subset.
>
> **$SU(2)$ is $S^3$.** By Step (c), $\Phi : Sp(1) \to SU(2)$ is a diffeomorphism, so $SU(2)$ is diffeomorphic to $Sp(1) = S^3$. Concretely, the map $SU(2) \to S^3 \subseteq \mathbb{C}^2 = \mathbb{R}^4$ sending $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ to $(z, w)$ (its first column) is a diffeomorphism onto $\{(z, w) : |z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1\} = S^3$; this is the map worked out in full on [[Ex - SU(2) is Diffeomorphic to S^3]], and it is $\Phi^{-1}$ followed by the coordinate identification $Sp(1) = S^3$. Hence $SU(2) \cong S^3$ as smooth manifolds.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** The map $\Phi : Sp(1) \to SU(2)$, $\Phi(z + wj) = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ (with $q = z + wj$, $z, w \in \mathbb{C}$), is a Lie group isomorphism, and both groups are diffeomorphic to $S^3$.
>
> **Coordinates.** Every quaternion is uniquely $z + wj$ with $z, w \in \mathbb{C}$, and $j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}j$ for $\zeta \in \mathbb{C}$ (from $ji = -k = \bar{i}j$). Hence $(z + wj)(z' + w'j) = (zz' - w\bar{w'}) + (zw' + w\bar{z'})j$, using $jz' = \bar{z'}j$, $jw' = \bar{w'}j$, $j^2 = -1$.
>
> **Well defined.** For $q = z + wj$ with $|q| = 1$, the matrix $A = \Phi(q)$ has $\det A = z\bar{z} + w\bar{w} = |z|^2 + |w|^2 = |q|^2 = 1$, and $A^*A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \bar{z} & -w \\ \bar{w} & z \end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right) = (|z|^2 + |w|^2)\,1_2 = 1_2$; so $A \in SU(2)$.
>
> **Homomorphism.** $\Phi(q)\Phi(q') = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} zz' - w\bar{w'} & zw' + w\bar{z'} \\ -\bar{w}z' - \bar{z}\bar{w'} & \bar{z}\bar{z'} - \bar{w}w' \end{smallmatrix}\right) = \Phi(qq')$, since $qq'$ has components $zz' - w\bar{w'}$ and $zw' + w\bar{z'}$ and the second row of $\Phi(qq')$ is $(-\overline{zw' + w\bar{z'}},\ \overline{zz' - w\bar{w'}})$.
>
> **Bijective.** $\Phi(z + wj) = 1_2$ forces $z = 1, w = 0$, so $\ker\Phi = \{1\}$ and $\Phi$ is injective. For $A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ \gamma & \delta \end{smallmatrix}\right) \in SU(2)$, $A^*A = 1_2$ gives $A^* = A^{-1}$; comparing $A^* = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \bar{\alpha} & \bar{\gamma} \\ \bar{\beta} & \bar{\delta} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with $A^{-1} = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \delta & -\beta \\ -\gamma & \alpha \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ (using $\det A = 1$) yields $\delta = \bar{\alpha}$, $\gamma = -\bar{\beta}$, so $A = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} \alpha & \beta \\ -\bar{\beta} & \bar{\alpha} \end{smallmatrix}\right) = \Phi(\alpha + \beta j)$ with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = \det A = 1$; thus $\Phi$ is surjective.
>
> **Isomorphism.** $\Phi$ extends to the $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}^4 \to \operatorname{Mat}(2 \times 2; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{R}^8$ with entries linear in $(a, b, c, d)$, hence smooth; its inverse $A \mapsto A_{11} + A_{12}j$ is also $\mathbb{R}$-linear, hence smooth. As $Sp(1)$ and $SU(2)$ are embedded submanifolds, both restrict to smooth maps of manifolds, so $\Phi$ is a diffeomorphism; with the homomorphism property it is a Lie group isomorphism.
>
> **Three-sphere.** $Sp(1) = \{q \in \mathbb{R}^4 : |q| = 1\} = S^3$ as embedded submanifolds of $\mathbb{R}^4$; via $\Phi$, $SU(2) \cong S^3$, realised by $A \mapsto$ (first column of $A$) $\in \{(z, w) : |z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1\}$, the diffeomorphism of [[Ex - SU(2) is Diffeomorphic to S^3]]. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Illegal but tempting: reading $q = z + wj$ as $q = z + jw$, or forgetting the bar in $j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}j$
> Two errors flip signs and break multiplicativity. First, the coordinatisation is $q = z + wj$ with the scalar $w$ on the *left* of $j$; writing $q = z + jw$ instead changes the bookkeeping, because $jw = \bar{w}j$, so the same quaternion would be recorded with $\bar{w}$ in place of $w$ and the matrix formula would have to be transposed in its treatment of the second column. Consistency of the single convention $q = z + wj$ throughout is what makes the product law of Step 0 line up with the matrix product of Step (b). Second, and more insidious, is to "simplify" $j\zeta$ to $\zeta j$ (dropping the conjugation). This would give the false product $(z + wj)(z' + w'j) = zz' - ww' + (zw' + wz')j$, whose components are *not* the entries of the matrix product $\Phi(q)\Phi(q')$; the homomorphism property would fail, and one could be misled into thinking $\Phi$ is not multiplicative. The bar is not optional: $\mathbb{H}$ is noncommutative precisely because $ji = -ij$, and $j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}j$ is the exact quantitative form of that noncommutativity. The extra condition that would make "$j\zeta = \zeta j$" legal is $\zeta \in \mathbb{R}$ — real scalars are central — which is why no error appears in the terms $zz'$ (both complex, multiplied directly) but only in the cross terms passing a $j$.

**Independent sanity check.** Two special values confirm the isomorphism. The quaternion $i \in Sp(1)$ maps to $\Phi(i) = \Phi(i + 0j) = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{smallmatrix}\right)$, which is $i\sigma_3$ in the Pauli notation used later in the chapter (with $\sigma_3 = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{smallmatrix}\right)$, so $i\sigma_3 = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{smallmatrix}\right)$), an element of $SU(2)$ of order four ($i^2 = -1$ maps to $-1_2$, $i^4 = 1$ to $1_2$); and $j \in Sp(1)$ maps to $\Phi(j) = \Phi(0 + 1\cdot j) = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right)$, which is $i\sigma_2$ (with $\sigma_2 = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right)$, so $i\sigma_2 = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right)$), also of order four, and $\Phi(i)\Phi(j) = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} i & 0 \\ 0 & -i \end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right) = \left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & i \\ i & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right) = \Phi(k)$, matching $ij = k$; so the multiplication table of the quaternion group $\{\pm 1, \pm i, \pm j, \pm k\}$ is reproduced inside $SU(2)$, exactly as the homomorphism property demands. The dimension count is also consistent: $\dim Sp(1) = 3$ (it is $S^3$) and $\dim SU(2) = 3$ (computed on [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]] as $\dim\mathfrak{su}(2) = n^2 - 1 = 3$ at $n = 2$), as an isomorphism requires.

# Key Takeaways

**The right coordinate system turns a noncommutative algebra into ordinary matrix arithmetic, and the whole isomorphism $Sp(1) \cong SU(2)$ is that single change of coordinates.** The reusable principle is that $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{C} \oplus \mathbb{C}j$ realises the quaternions as $2 \times 2$ complex matrices, with the noncommutativity of $\mathbb{H}$ carried entirely by the one rule $j\zeta = \bar{\zeta}j$. Once a quaternion $z + wj$ is written as $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$, quaternion multiplication *becomes* matrix multiplication — nothing further is verified, because the associative-algebra structures are literally the same. This is the prototype of a representation: an abstract algebra is understood by faithfully embedding it in matrices, where every computation is mechanical. The trigger to reach for this move is any appearance of $\mathbb{H}$, $Sp(1)$, or a norm-one condition in a quaternionic setting; the pattern is to split off the $j$-part, write the complex $2 \times 2$ matrix, and let matrix algebra replace quaternion algebra. The same coordinatisation recurs in chapter VIII, where $Sp(1) = SU(2) = \operatorname{Spin}(3)$ is the spin group in three dimensions and its action on $\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}^3$ is the double cover $SU(2) \to SO(3)$.

**To parametrise the elements of a matrix group given by equations, rewrite the equations as a statement about the inverse and use a closed form for the inverse.** The surjectivity step is the transferable diagnostic: faced with "what does a general element of $SU(2)$ look like?", we did not guess, we solved. The unitarity equation $A^*A = 1_2$ is the same as $A^* = A^{-1}$, and for a group of determinant one the inverse has the explicit adjugate form; equating the two closed expressions entry by entry forced the shape $\left(\begin{smallmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with no freedom left except $|z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1$. This "$A^* = A^{-1}$ plus a determinant normalisation" technique parametrises $SU(2)$, and the same idea — turn a preservation equation into a comparison of two formulas for the inverse — parametrises $SO(3)$ by Euler angles, $U(1)$ by a phase, and in general reduces a defining equation to a normal form. Whenever a group is presented implicitly by equations and an explicit list of its elements is wanted, the move is to isolate the inverse and exploit whatever closed form the low rank provides.

**An isomorphism of Lie groups is cheapest to certify when both directions are linear, and one should always look for that linearity before invoking heavier machinery.** The general theorem that a bijective Lie group homomorphism is automatically a diffeomorphism is true but expensive; here it was unnecessary, because $\Phi$ and $\Phi^{-1}$ are both restrictions of $\mathbb{R}$-linear maps of the ambient Euclidean spaces, and linear maps are visibly smooth. The transferable lesson is to separate the *algebraic* content of an isomorphism (multiplicativity and bijectivity) from its *smoothness*, and to check smoothness by extending the map to the ambient vector spaces where the groups sit as embedded submanifolds; the embedded-submanifold property then hands back smoothness of the restriction and corestriction for free. This is the same reasoning by which every homomorphism between matrix groups defined by polynomial or linear formulas in the entries is smooth without any differential-geometric labour, and it is worth internalising because it applies to essentially every concrete map between the classical groups that occurs in the series.
