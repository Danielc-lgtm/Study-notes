# Notation

Throughout this page, $M$ is a smooth manifold: as fixed by the standing conventions of the series, this means $M$ is $C^\infty$, Hausdorff, and second countable, hence also locally compact and locally Euclidean, and therefore metrizable — a smooth manifold carries a Riemannian metric, whose distance function induces the topology, so compactness on $M$ is sequential compactness and every point has a countable basis of open neighbourhoods. We use these point-set facts about manifolds without further comment, naming them at the point of use.

By $\Gamma$ we denote a **discrete group**: a group equipped with the discrete topology, viewed as a $0$-dimensional Lie group (defined below). We reserve $G$ for a general [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]]. A **left action** of $\Gamma$ on $M$ is a smooth map $\theta : \Gamma \times M \to M$, written $(g, p) \mapsto g \cdot p$, satisfying $e \cdot p = p$ and $g \cdot (h \cdot p) = (gh) \cdot p$ for all $g, h \in \Gamma$ and $p \in M$; because $\Gamma$ is $0$-dimensional, smoothness of $\theta$ is equivalent to the requirement that each $\theta_g : M \to M$, $\theta_g(p) = g \cdot p$, be a smooth map, and since $\theta_g$ has smooth inverse $\theta_{g^{-1}}$, each $\theta_g$ is a **diffeomorphism** of $M$. The full notion of a smooth action, in the form the series uses it, is [[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group|the smooth action of a Lie group]]; effectiveness, freeness, transitivity, properness, orbits, stabilisers, and the orbit space are defined on [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions]], whose vocabulary we recall here where it is used.

For $g \in \Gamma$ and $U \subseteq M$ we write $g \cdot U = \{g \cdot u : u \in U\} = \theta_g(U)$. The **orbit** of $p$ is $\Gamma \cdot p = \{g \cdot p : g \in \Gamma\}$; the action is **free** if $g \cdot p = p$ for some $p$ forces $g = e$ (equivalently, every stabiliser $\Gamma_p = \{g : g \cdot p = p\}$ is trivial). The action is **proper** if the map
$$\Phi : \Gamma \times M \to M \times M, \qquad \Phi(g, p) = (g \cdot p,\, p),$$
is proper, meaning the preimage $\Phi^{-1}(L)$ of every compact set $L \subseteq M \times M$ is compact. The **orbit space** $\Gamma \backslash M$ is the set of orbits with the [[Def - Quotient Topology and Identification Map|quotient topology]] induced by the projection $\pi : M \to \Gamma \backslash M$, $\pi(p) = \Gamma \cdot p$. The full symbol registry for the chapter is on [[Gauge Theory I — Lie Groups, Representations, and Group Actions]].

> [!warning] Convention: two definitions of "properly discontinuous"
> This page uses Bär's definition of a properly discontinuous action, the pair of conditions (i) and (ii) stated below. Much of the literature — Lee's *Introduction to Smooth Manifolds*, for one — never uses the phrase and instead hypothesises that a discrete group acts **smoothly, freely, and properly**. The two formulations describe exactly the same actions: Corollary 2 below proves that, for a discrete group acting on a manifold, Bär's (i)+(ii) holds **if and only if** the action is free and proper. Every later page of the series may therefore invoke whichever form is convenient. (This is also the promised reconciliation of the loose usage flagged on [[Thm - Quotient Manifold Theorem for Free Proper Actions]], where a free action is sometimes called "properly discontinuous" without the properness hypothesis being made explicit.)

> [!warning] Convention: side of the action and the orbit-space notation
> Bär writes the orbit space as $G \backslash M$ even for right actions; the series writes $\Gamma \backslash M$ for a left action and $M / \Gamma$ for a right action, matching the bundle notation $P / G$ used from chapter III onward. Nothing below depends on the side: a left action $g \cdot p$ becomes a right action by $p * g := g^{-1} \cdot p$ and conversely, and both conditions (i), (ii) are symmetric under this exchange, so every statement on this page has an identical right-handed twin obtained by replacing $g \cdot U$ with $U \cdot g$.

# Axiom Motivation

The whole of §1.5 is organised around a single question: **when does the orbit space of a group action inherit a smooth manifold structure that makes the projection a submersion?** For a compact Lie group $G$ acting freely, the answer is affirmative and is the content of [[Thm - Quotient Manifold Theorem for Free Proper Actions|the quotient manifold theorem]] — freeness removes the singular points where an orbit could collapse, and compactness supplies the properness that keeps distinct orbits apart, so that $G \backslash M$ is Hausdorff and locally modelled on a slice transverse to the orbits. We now want the corresponding criterion for the other extreme, a **discrete** group, where the orbits are $0$-dimensional and the quotient has the same dimension as $M$. Here the natural expectation is that $\pi : M \to \Gamma \backslash M$ should be not merely a submersion but a **covering map** — a local diffeomorphism whose fibres are the orbits. The definition of a properly discontinuous action is engineered to be exactly the hypothesis under which this happens, and the right way to arrive at it is to ask what a covering map requires.

A discrete group can never help itself to the compact-group argument, because of a basic incompatibility recorded in Corollary 1 below: **a discrete group is compact if and only if it is finite** (a discrete topological space is compact precisely when it is finite). The interesting discrete groups — $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Z}^2$, the fundamental group of a surface — are infinite, hence non-compact, so properness is no longer automatic and must be imposed by hand. The two clauses of the definition are precisely the two things a covering projection needs, split according to whether we are looking near one point or between two different orbits.

The first clause,

> (i) every $p \in M$ has a neighbourhood $U$ such that $g \cdot U \cap U \neq \varnothing$ implies $g = e$,

is the local requirement. It says that each $U$ is carried **off itself** by every non-identity group element, so that the translates $\{g \cdot U : g \in \Gamma\}$ are pairwise disjoint and $\pi$ restricted to $U$ is injective. This is the condition that makes $U$ an *evenly covered* neighbourhood, and it is what forces $\pi$ to be a local diffeomorphism. If we drop (i), the projection can fail to be locally injective, and the quotient can acquire a boundary or a singular point. The cleanest witness is the reflection action of $\Gamma = \mathbb{Z}/2$ on $M = \mathbb{R}$ by $g \cdot x = -x$, where $g$ is the non-trivial element. Condition (ii) holds for this action, because the orbits $\{x, -x\}$ are finite and any two distinct finite orbits in the Hausdorff space $\mathbb{R}$ can be separated by disjoint neighbourhoods. But (i) fails at the fixed point $0$: every neighbourhood $U$ of $0$ satisfies $g \cdot U = -U \ni 0$, so $g \cdot U \cap U \ni 0$ is non-empty while $g \neq e$. The action is not free — $0$ is a fixed point — and the quotient $\mathbb{Z}/2 \backslash \mathbb{R} = [0, \infty)$ (identify each orbit $\{x, -x\}$ with $|x|$) is a manifold *with boundary*, not a manifold, and $\pi$ ramifies over the boundary point rather than covering it. The strengthening that would restore (i) is exactly freeness together with properness, as Corollary 2 shows; freeness alone kills the fixed point, and (i) is the local shadow of freeness once the group is discrete.

The second clause,

> (ii) for every $p, q \in M$ lying in different orbits, $\Gamma \cdot p \neq \Gamma \cdot q$, there are neighbourhoods $U \ni p$ and $V \ni q$ with $g \cdot U \cap V = \varnothing$ for all $g \in \Gamma$,

is the global requirement, and it is what makes the quotient **Hausdorff**. Two points of $\Gamma \backslash M$ are the images $\pi(p)$ and $\pi(q)$ of points in different orbits; to separate them in the quotient topology one needs saturated open sets around the orbits that do not meet, and (ii) provides exactly the $\Gamma$-invariant neighbourhoods $\Gamma \cdot U$ and $\Gamma \cdot V$ that do the separating. If we drop (ii) but keep (i), the projection is still a local diffeomorphism but the quotient can be non-Hausdorff. Consider $\Gamma = \mathbb{Z}$ acting on $M = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ by the hyperbolic map $n \cdot (x, y) = (2^n x,\, 2^{-n} y)$. This action is free — if $2^n x = x$ and $2^{-n} y = y$ with $(x, y) \neq (0, 0)$, then whichever coordinate is non-zero forces $2^n = 1$, hence $n = 0$ — and it satisfies (i), because each orbit is a discrete subset of the level set $\{xy = \text{const}\}$ escaping to infinity in both directions, so a small enough neighbourhood of any point misses all of its non-trivial translates. But a point $p$ on the positive $x$-axis and a point $q$ on the positive $y$-axis lie in different orbits (their invariant $xy$ vanishes on neither, yet no power of the map sends one to the other, since the orbit of $p$ has $y$-coordinate tending to $0^+$ and $x$-coordinate to $+\infty$, while $q$ has the reverse), and their orbits both accumulate onto the deleted origin; consequently no neighbourhoods $U \ni p$, $V \ni q$ can satisfy (ii), and $\pi(p)$, $\pi(q)$ are distinct points of the quotient with no disjoint neighbourhoods. The quotient is a non-Hausdorff surface. What (ii) rules out is precisely this accumulation of one orbit onto another, and Corollary 2 identifies it as the failure of properness.

A reader who has internalised that (i) is "local injectivity, i.e. freeness made local" and (ii) is "orbit-separation, i.e. properness made explicit" could reconstruct the definition unaided: it is the smallest pair of conditions guaranteeing that $\pi : M \to \Gamma \backslash M$ is a Hausdorff covering. The equivalence with "free and proper" (Corollary 2) is the theorem that certifies this reading.

# The Definition

We give the two interlocking notions the page is responsible for — a discrete group, and a properly discontinuous action of one — followed by the equivalent formulation in terms of freeness and properness.

**Discrete group.** A **discrete group** is a $0$-dimensional Lie group $\Gamma$: a group that is also a $0$-dimensional smooth manifold, with smooth multiplication and inversion. A $0$-dimensional manifold is a space in which every point has a neighbourhood diffeomorphic to a point, that is, a discrete topological space; since the manifold is second countable, it is countable. Thus a discrete group is exactly a countable group carrying the discrete topology, and every group homomorphism out of or into it is automatically smooth.

**Properly discontinuous action.** Let $\Gamma$ be a discrete group acting smoothly (on the left) on a manifold $M$. The action is called **properly discontinuous** if the following two conditions hold.

1. **Local wandering.** For every $p \in M$ there exists an open neighbourhood $U$ of $p$ in $M$ such that
$$g \cdot U \cap U \neq \varnothing \ \Longrightarrow\ g = e \qquad (g \in \Gamma).$$

2. **Orbit separation.** For every pair $p, q \in M$ with $\Gamma \cdot p \neq \Gamma \cdot q$ there exist open neighbourhoods $U$ of $p$ and $V$ of $q$ in $M$ such that
$$g \cdot U \cap V = \varnothing \qquad \text{for all } g \in \Gamma.$$

**Equivalent formulation (proved in Corollary 2 below).** A smooth action of a discrete group $\Gamma$ on a manifold $M$ is properly discontinuous if and only if it is **free and proper**. In this form the notion is manifestly the discrete-group specialisation of the hypotheses of [[Thm - Quotient Manifold Theorem for Free Proper Actions|the quotient manifold theorem]], and it is this equivalence that lets the two strands of the literature be used interchangeably. The proof, which occupies most of the Examples / Corollaries section, rests on a single characterisation of properness for discrete groups (Lemma below): the action is proper exactly when, for every compact $K \subseteq M$, only finitely many group elements move $K$ so as to meet it again.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The true meaning of a properly discontinuous action lies one theorem downstream, on [[Thm - Quotient by a Properly Discontinuous Action is a Manifold and a Covering Map]]: it is **precisely the condition under which the quotient map $M \to \Gamma \backslash M$ is a smooth covering map with deck group $\Gamma$.** This places the definition at the meeting point of three subjects. In the topology of covering spaces it is the condition for $M$ to be a $\Gamma$-principal covering of $\Gamma \backslash M$; the whole theory of the [[Def - Covering Space|universal cover]] of a manifold is the statement that $\Gamma = \pi_1$ acts properly discontinuously on the universal cover $\widetilde{M}$ with quotient $M$, which is why chapter V can present the universal cover as a principal $\pi_1$-bundle. In the theory of principal bundles, developed from chapter III, a properly discontinuous free action is exactly a principal bundle with $0$-dimensional (discrete) structure group, so covering maps and principal bundles are one construction seen at two scales. In geometric group theory and the theory of locally symmetric spaces, discrete groups $\Gamma$ acting properly discontinuously on a model space — $\mathbb{Z}^n$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$, a crystallographic group on Euclidean space, a Fuchsian group on the hyperbolic plane — are the source of every flat, Euclidean, and hyperbolic manifold, the quotient inheriting the model's geometry because $\pi$ is a local isometry.

**True name.** The operational characterisation is "free and proper", not the pair (i)+(ii). Once Corollary 2 is available, one checks in practice that an action is properly discontinuous by verifying that no non-trivial element fixes a point (freeness) and that compact sets are moved off themselves by all but finitely many group elements (properness, in the form of the Lemma below); this is almost always faster than producing the neighbourhoods (i) and (ii) directly. When the group is finite, or more generally compact, properness is free of charge, and only freeness has to be checked — this is the observation that makes the antipodal action on the sphere properly discontinuous in one line, in the calibration check below.

# Examples / Corollaries

We first dispose of the elementary structural fact behind the whole discussion, then prove the central equivalence, and finally verify the standard examples and non-example clause by clause.

**Corollary 1 (a discrete group is compact if and only if it is finite; source R1.5.1).** Let $\Gamma$ be a discrete group. Then $\Gamma$ is compact if and only if $\Gamma$ is finite.

> [!note]- Full proof of Corollary 1
> We prove both directions. Throughout, $\Gamma$ carries the discrete topology, in which every subset is open.
>
> **Direction 1 ($\Leftarrow$): finite implies compact.** Assume $\Gamma$ is finite. Any topological space with finitely many points is compact, since any open cover has at most as many distinct members as there are points and is therefore already a finite subcover. Hence $\Gamma$ is compact.
>
> **Direction 2 ($\Rightarrow$): compact implies finite.** Assume $\Gamma$ is compact. Consider the open cover of $\Gamma$ by singletons, $\mathcal{U} = \{\{g\} : g \in \Gamma\}$, which is a cover because every point lies in its own singleton and each singleton is open in the discrete topology. By compactness $\mathcal{U}$ has a finite subcover $\{\{g_1\}, \dots, \{g_m\}\}$; but a subcover must still cover, so $\Gamma = \{g_1\} \cup \dots \cup \{g_m\} = \{g_1, \dots, g_m\}$ is finite.
>
> **Conclusion.** $\Gamma$ is compact if and only if it is finite. $\blacksquare$

The significance of Corollary 1 is exactly the one advertised in the Axiom Motivation: the quotient manifold theorem for compact groups is useless for an infinite discrete group, so a genuinely new criterion — proper discontinuity — is needed, and it is not vacuous.

**Corollary 2 (properly discontinuous $\iff$ free and proper).** Let $\Gamma$ be a discrete group acting smoothly on a manifold $M$. Then the action is properly discontinuous (conditions (i) and (ii)) if and only if it is free and proper.

The proof depends on one characterisation of properness that is special to discrete groups and is worth isolating.

> [!note]- Lemma (properness of a discrete action via compact sets)
> **Statement.** Let $\Gamma$ be a discrete group acting continuously on a manifold $M$. For a subset $K \subseteq M$ write $\Gamma_K = \{g \in \Gamma : g \cdot K \cap K \neq \varnothing\}$. Then the action is proper if and only if $\Gamma_K$ is finite for every compact set $K \subseteq M$.
>
> **Hint.** Properness is a statement about the map $\Phi(g, p) = (g \cdot p, p)$; test it against product compact sets $K \times K$ and remember that a compact subset of the discrete space $\Gamma$ is finite.
>
> **Why needed.** Both directions of Corollary 2 are cleanest when stated through $\Gamma_K$: proper discontinuity produces finite $\Gamma_K$ (the workhorse of the "$\Rightarrow$" direction), and finite $\Gamma_K$ produces the separating neighbourhoods (the workhorse of the "$\Leftarrow$" direction).
>
> > [!note]- Full proof of the Lemma
> > We prove the two implications. Recall $\Phi : \Gamma \times M \to M \times M$, $\Phi(g, p) = (g \cdot p, p)$, and that $\Gamma$ carries the discrete topology, so a subset of $\Gamma$ is compact if and only if it is finite.
> >
> > **Direction 1 ($\Rightarrow$): proper implies $\Gamma_K$ finite.** Assume $\Phi$ is proper and let $K \subseteq M$ be compact. Then $K \times K \subseteq M \times M$ is compact (a product of compact sets is compact, by Tychonoff's theorem for two factors), so $\Phi^{-1}(K \times K)$ is compact, since $\Phi$ is proper. Let $\mathrm{pr}_\Gamma : \Gamma \times M \to \Gamma$ be the projection, which is continuous. Its image $\mathrm{pr}_\Gamma\big(\Phi^{-1}(K \times K)\big)$ is compact (a continuous image of a compact set is compact), hence finite (a compact subset of the discrete space $\Gamma$ is finite). It remains to identify this image with $\Gamma_K$. A group element $g$ lies in the image if and only if there is a point $p \in M$ with $(g, p) \in \Phi^{-1}(K \times K)$, that is, with $p \in K$ and $g \cdot p \in K$; and such a $p$ exists if and only if $g \cdot K \cap K \neq \varnothing$ (take the witness $g \cdot p \in g \cdot K \cap K$, and conversely any point of $g \cdot K \cap K$ is $g \cdot p$ for some $p \in K$ with $g \cdot p \in K$). Therefore $\mathrm{pr}_\Gamma\big(\Phi^{-1}(K \times K)\big) = \Gamma_K$, and $\Gamma_K$ is finite.
> >
> > **Direction 2 ($\Leftarrow$): $\Gamma_K$ finite for all compact $K$ implies proper.** Assume $\Gamma_K$ is finite for every compact $K$, and let $L \subseteq M \times M$ be compact; we must show $\Phi^{-1}(L)$ is compact. Put $K := \mathrm{pr}_1(L) \cup \mathrm{pr}_2(L)$, where $\mathrm{pr}_1, \mathrm{pr}_2 : M \times M \to M$ are the two projections; $K$ is compact, being a union of two continuous images of the compact set $L$. **Confine the preimage to a compact box.** If $(g, p) \in \Phi^{-1}(L)$ then $(g \cdot p, p) \in L$, so $p \in \mathrm{pr}_2(L) \subseteq K$ and $g \cdot p \in \mathrm{pr}_1(L) \subseteq K$; the point $g \cdot p$ then lies in $g \cdot K \cap K$, whence $g \in \Gamma_K$. Thus $\Phi^{-1}(L) \subseteq \Gamma_K \times K$. **Compactness.** The set $\Gamma_K \times K$ is compact: $\Gamma_K$ is finite by hypothesis, so $\Gamma_K \times K$ is a finite union of the compact slices $\{g\} \times K$. Moreover $\Phi^{-1}(L)$ is closed in $\Gamma \times M$, because $\Phi$ is continuous and $L$ is closed in $M \times M$ (a compact subset of the Hausdorff space $M \times M$ is closed). A closed subset of a compact set is compact, so $\Phi^{-1}(L)$ is compact.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** The action is proper if and only if $\Gamma_K$ is finite for every compact $K \subseteq M$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Full proof of Corollary 2
> We prove the two directions separately. Write the (i)-neighbourhood of a point $p$, when one exists, as a $U$ satisfying $g \cdot U \cap U \neq \varnothing \Rightarrow g = e$. We use freely that $M$ is Hausdorff, locally compact, and metrizable (hence first countable, so limits and compactness may be tested with sequences), and the Lemma just proved.
>
> **Direction 1 ($\Rightarrow$): properly discontinuous implies free and proper.**
>
> *Freeness.* Suppose $g \cdot p = p$ for some $g \in \Gamma$ and $p \in M$; we must show $g = e$. Let $U$ be a neighbourhood of $p$ as in (i). Then $p = g \cdot p \in g \cdot U$ (since $p \in U$) and $p \in U$, so $p \in g \cdot U \cap U$, and this set is non-empty. By (i) it follows that $g = e$. Hence every stabiliser is trivial and the action is free.
>
> *Properness.* By the Lemma it suffices to show that $\Gamma_K$ is finite for every compact $K \subseteq M$. Suppose, for contradiction, that $\Gamma_K$ is infinite for some compact $K$. Then there are pairwise distinct elements $g_1, g_2, \dots \in \Gamma$ and points $p_n \in K$ with $g_n \cdot p_n \in K$. Since $K$ is compact and metrizable, we may pass to a subsequence (not relabelled) along which $p_n \to p \in K$ and $g_n \cdot p_n \to q \in K$. We split into the two possible relations between the limit points.
>
> **Case A: $\Gamma \cdot p \neq \Gamma \cdot q$.** The cases are exhaustive because either $p$ and $q$ lie in the same orbit or they do not; Case A is the "different orbit" alternative. Apply (ii) to $p$ and $q$: there are neighbourhoods $U \ni p$, $V \ni q$ with $g \cdot U \cap V = \varnothing$ for all $g$. For $n$ large enough, $p_n \in U$ (as $p_n \to p$) and $g_n \cdot p_n \in V$ (as $g_n \cdot p_n \to q$). Then $g_n \cdot p_n \in g_n \cdot U \cap V$, so this set is non-empty, contradicting (ii). Case A is impossible.
>
> **Case B: $\Gamma \cdot p = \Gamma \cdot q$.** Then $q = h \cdot p$ for some $h \in \Gamma$. Let $U$ be a neighbourhood of $p$ as in (i). Since $\theta_{h^{-1}}$ is continuous, $h^{-1} \cdot (g_n \cdot p_n) \to h^{-1} \cdot q = h^{-1} \cdot (h \cdot p) = p$. Thus for $n$ large enough both $p_n \in U$ (as $p_n \to p$) and $(h^{-1} g_n) \cdot p_n = h^{-1} \cdot (g_n \cdot p_n) \in U$. Writing $g := h^{-1} g_n$, we have $g \cdot p_n \in g \cdot U$ and $g \cdot p_n \in U$, so $g \cdot U \cap U \neq \varnothing$; by (i), $g = e$, that is $h^{-1} g_n = e$, so $g_n = h$. This holds for all sufficiently large $n$, contradicting the distinctness of the $g_n$ (only one of them can equal $h$). Case B is impossible.
>
> Both cases being impossible, $\Gamma_K$ is finite for every compact $K$, so the action is proper. This completes Direction 1.
>
> **Direction 2 ($\Leftarrow$): free and proper implies properly discontinuous.** Assume the action is free and proper; by the Lemma, $\Gamma_K$ is finite for every compact $K$.
>
> *Proof of (i).* Fix $p \in M$. Since $M$ is locally compact and Hausdorff, choose a compact neighbourhood $K$ of $p$, so that $p \in \operatorname{int}(K)$. By properness, $\Gamma_K = \{g : g \cdot K \cap K \neq \varnothing\}$ is finite; write $\Gamma_K = \{e, g_1, \dots, g_m\}$, which contains $e$ because $p \in e \cdot K \cap K$. For each $i \in \{1, \dots, m\}$ we have $g_i \neq e$, so by **freeness** $g_i \cdot p \neq p$; since $M$ is Hausdorff, choose disjoint open sets $A_i \ni p$ and $B_i \ni g_i \cdot p$. Now set
> $$U := \operatorname{int}(K) \cap \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} \big(A_i \cap g_i^{-1} \cdot B_i\big),$$
> which is open and contains $p$: indeed $p \in \operatorname{int}(K)$, $p \in A_i$, and $p \in g_i^{-1} \cdot B_i$ because $g_i \cdot p \in B_i$. We check that $U$ satisfies (i). Suppose $g \cdot U \cap U \neq \varnothing$ for some $g \in \Gamma$, and let $x \in g \cdot U \cap U$. Since $U \subseteq \operatorname{int}(K) \subseteq K$, the point $x$ lies in $g \cdot K \cap K$, so $g \in \Gamma_K$. If $g = g_i$ for some $i \in \{1, \dots, m\}$: then $x \in U \subseteq A_i$, while $x \in g_i \cdot U \subseteq g_i \cdot (g_i^{-1} \cdot B_i) = B_i$ (using $U \subseteq g_i^{-1} \cdot B_i$), so $x \in A_i \cap B_i = \varnothing$, a contradiction; hence no $g_i$ occurs, and the only remaining element of $\Gamma_K$ is $g = e$. Therefore $g \cdot U \cap U \neq \varnothing$ forces $g = e$, which is (i).
>
> *Proof of (ii).* Let $p, q \in M$ with $\Gamma \cdot p \neq \Gamma \cdot q$; we must produce the separating neighbourhoods. Suppose, for contradiction, that no such neighbourhoods exist: then for every pair of neighbourhoods $U \ni p$, $V \ni q$ there is some $g \in \Gamma$ with $g \cdot U \cap V \neq \varnothing$. Since $M$ is first countable and locally compact, choose nested neighbourhood bases $U_1 \supseteq U_2 \supseteq \cdots$ of $p$ and $V_1 \supseteq V_2 \supseteq \cdots$ of $q$ with $\overline{U_1}$ and $\overline{V_1}$ compact and with $\bigcap_n U_n = \{p\}$, $\bigcap_n V_n = \{q\}$. For each $n$ pick $g_n \in \Gamma$ and $x_n \in g_n \cdot U_n \cap V_n$; then $x_n \in V_n$, so $x_n \to q$, and $g_n^{-1} \cdot x_n \in U_n$, so $g_n^{-1} \cdot x_n \to p$. Put $K := \overline{U_1} \cup \overline{V_1}$, a compact set. For every $n$ we have $x_n \in V_n \subseteq K$ and $g_n^{-1} \cdot x_n \in U_n \subseteq K$, so $g_n^{-1} \cdot x_n \in g_n^{-1} \cdot K \cap K$, whence $g_n^{-1} \in \Gamma_K$. **Use properness to freeze the group element.** By properness $\Gamma_K$ is finite, so the sequence $(g_n^{-1})$, and hence $(g_n)$, takes only finitely many values; pass to a subsequence along which $g_n = g$ is constant. Then $g^{-1} \cdot x_n \to p$, while by continuity of $\theta_{g^{-1}}$ and $x_n \to q$ we also have $g^{-1} \cdot x_n \to g^{-1} \cdot q$. Limits in the Hausdorff space $M$ are unique, so $g^{-1} \cdot q = p$, that is $q = g \cdot p \in \Gamma \cdot p$. But then $\Gamma \cdot q = \Gamma \cdot p$, contradicting the hypothesis $\Gamma \cdot p \neq \Gamma \cdot q$. Therefore the separating neighbourhoods exist, which is (ii).
>
> **Conclusion.** A smooth action of a discrete group on a manifold is properly discontinuous if and only if it is free and proper. $\blacksquare$

**Is an instance — $\mathbb{Z}$ acting on $\mathbb{R}$ by translation (source E1.5.9).** Let $\Gamma = (\mathbb{Z}, +)$ act on $M = \mathbb{R}$ by $k \cdot t = k + t$. Each $\theta_k$ is the translation $t \mapsto k + t$, a diffeomorphism, so the action is smooth. We verify (i) and (ii) with Bär's explicit neighbourhoods.

For (i), fix $t \in \mathbb{R}$ and take $U := \big(t - \tfrac{1}{2},\, t + \tfrac{1}{2}\big)$, an interval of length $1$. Suppose $k \cdot U \cap U \neq \varnothing$, so there are $u, u' \in U$ with $k + u = u'$, giving $k = u' - u$. Since $u, u' \in U$ and $U$ has length $1$, we have $|u' - u| < 1$, so $|k| < 1$; as $k \in \mathbb{Z}$, this forces $k = 0 = e$. Thus (i) holds.

For (ii), let $s, t \in \mathbb{R}$ lie in different orbits, that is $t - s \notin \mathbb{Z}$ (the orbit of $s$ is $s + \mathbb{Z}$, so $t \in \Gamma \cdot s$ would mean $t - s \in \mathbb{Z}$). Set
$$\varepsilon := \min\{\,|t - (s + k)| : k \in \mathbb{Z}\,\},$$
the distance from $t$ to the orbit $s + \mathbb{Z}$. This minimum is attained and is strictly positive: the set $s + \mathbb{Z}$ is closed and discrete, only finitely many of its points lie within distance $1$ of $t$, and none equals $t$ because $t - s \notin \mathbb{Z}$, so the infimum is a positive minimum. Put $U := \big(s - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2},\, s + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)$ and $V := \big(t - \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2},\, t + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)$. For any $k \in \mathbb{Z}$, a point of $k \cdot U \cap V$ would be some $x$ with $|x - (s + k)| < \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}$ and $|x - t| < \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}$, whence by the triangle inequality $|t - (s + k)| < \varepsilon$, contradicting the definition of $\varepsilon$ as the minimum of exactly these distances. Hence $k \cdot U \cap V = \varnothing$ for all $k$, and (ii) holds. The action is properly discontinuous, and by Corollary 2 it is free and proper; the quotient $\mathbb{Z} \backslash \mathbb{R}$ is the circle $S^1$, as [[Thm - Quotient by a Properly Discontinuous Action is a Manifold and a Covering Map|the covering theorem]] and the exercise [[Ex - The Circle as the Quotient of R by the Integers]] make precise.

**Is an instance — the deck action of $\mathbb{Z}^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$.** Let $\Gamma = \mathbb{Z}^2$ act on $M = \mathbb{R}^2$ by $(m, n) \cdot (x, y) = (x + m,\, y + n)$, translation by the integer lattice. Each element acts by a diffeomorphism, so the action is smooth. For (i), fix $(x, y)$ and take the open square $U := \big(x - \tfrac{1}{2}, x + \tfrac{1}{2}\big) \times \big(y - \tfrac{1}{2}, y + \tfrac{1}{2}\big)$. If $(m, n) \cdot U \cap U \neq \varnothing$, there are points of $U$ differing by $(m, n)$, so each coordinate difference has absolute value $< 1$; since $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$, this gives $m = n = 0$, and (i) holds. For (ii), let $(x, y)$ and $(x', y')$ lie in different orbits, that is $(x - x', y - y') \notin \mathbb{Z}^2$. The orbit $(x', y') + \mathbb{Z}^2$ is a closed discrete subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ not containing $(x, y)$, so
$$\varepsilon := \min\big\{\,\big\|(x, y) - \big((x', y') + (m, n)\big)\big\| : (m, n) \in \mathbb{Z}^2\,\big\} > 0$$
is a positive minimum (again only finitely many lattice translates lie within distance $1$). Taking $U$ and $V$ to be the open $\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}$-balls about $(x, y)$ and $(x', y')$, the same triangle-inequality argument as before shows $(m, n) \cdot U \cap V = \varnothing$ for every $(m, n)$, so (ii) holds. The action is properly discontinuous; by Corollary 2 it is free (a translation by $(m,n) \neq (0,0)$ has no fixed point) and proper, and the quotient $\mathbb{Z}^2 \backslash \mathbb{R}^2$ is the $2$-torus $T^2$, the model example of a flat compact surface obtained as a covering quotient.

**Is NOT an instance — $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$ acting on $\mathbb{R}$ by translation (source E1.5.10).** Let $\Gamma = (\mathbb{Q}, +)$, taken with the discrete topology (it is a countable group, hence a legitimate discrete group, and by Corollary 1 it is not compact since it is infinite). Let it act on $M = \mathbb{R}$ by $q \cdot t = q + t$; each translation is a diffeomorphism, so the action is smooth. We show the action is **not** properly discontinuous by showing that (i) already fails at every point, and that the quotient is not Hausdorff.

The orbit of any $t \in \mathbb{R}$ is $\Gamma \cdot t = t + \mathbb{Q}$, which is **dense** in $\mathbb{R}$: for any $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and any $\delta > 0$ there is a rational $q$ with $|q - (x - t)| < \delta$, so $|(t + q) - x| < \delta$ and $t + q \in \Gamma \cdot t$ lies within $\delta$ of $x$. Density kills (i): given any neighbourhood $U$ of $t$, pick $\delta > 0$ with $(t - \delta, t + \delta) \subseteq U$; by density there is a non-zero rational $q$ with $|q| < \delta$, and then $t \in U$ while $q \cdot t = t + q \in (t - \delta, t + \delta) \subseteq U$, so $t + q \in q \cdot U \cap U$ is a point of the intersection with $q \neq 0 = e$. Thus $U$ is carried onto itself by a non-identity element, and (i) fails for every $U$; there is no local wandering.

Equivalently, in the free-and-proper language of Corollary 2, the action is free (if $q + t = t$ then $q = 0$) but **not proper**: taking $K = [0, 1]$, every rational $q \in [-1, 1]$ satisfies $q \cdot K \cap K \supseteq \{$a common point$\} \neq \varnothing$, so $\Gamma_K$ contains all rationals in $[-1,1]$ and is infinite, violating the Lemma's criterion for properness. Finally, the quotient $\mathbb{Q} \backslash \mathbb{R}$ is **not Hausdorff**: because every orbit is dense, every non-empty saturated open subset of $\mathbb{R}$ (a union of orbits that is open) is all of $\mathbb{R}$, so the only open sets in the quotient topology are $\varnothing$ and the whole space — the quotient carries the indiscrete topology, in which no two distinct points can be separated. This is the manifestation of the failed condition (ii): distinct orbits cannot be separated because each is dense.

**Calibration check.** Two actions to decide by hand from the criteria above.

First, the **antipodal action of $\mathbb{Z}/2$ on $S^n$**, $g \cdot x = -x$ for the non-trivial $g$, *is* properly discontinuous. The group is finite, hence compact (Corollary 1), and it acts freely, because $-x = x$ has no solution on $S^n$ (it would force $x = 0 \notin S^n$). A free action of a compact group is proper — the Lemma's criterion is immediate, since for compact $K$ the set $\Gamma_K \subseteq \Gamma$ is a subset of the finite group $\mathbb{Z}/2$ and is therefore finite regardless of $K$ — so by Corollary 2 the action is properly discontinuous. Its quotient is real projective space $\mathbb{RP}^n$, exhibited as a manifold and a double cover of $S^n$ by [[Thm - Quotient by a Properly Discontinuous Action is a Manifold and a Covering Map|the covering theorem]]. Concretely (i) holds by taking $U$ to be any open cap of angular radius less than $\pi/2$, which is disjoint from its antipode $-U$; and (ii) holds because the orbits $\{x, -x\}$ are finite and separable in the Hausdorff space $S^n$.

Second, $\mathbb{Z}$ acting on $S^1$ by an **irrational rotation**, $n \cdot z = e^{2\pi i n \alpha} z$ with $\alpha \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$, is *not* properly discontinuous, and the contrast with the sphere is instructive: this action is free but not proper. It is free, because $e^{2\pi i n \alpha} z = z$ forces $n \alpha \in \mathbb{Z}$, and with $\alpha$ irrational this holds only for $n = 0$. But the orbit $\{e^{2\pi i n \alpha} z : n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ is dense in $S^1$ — the classical equidistribution of the irrational-rotation orbit — so, exactly as for $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$ on $\mathbb{R}$, condition (i) fails at every point: any neighbourhood $U$ of $z$ contains $e^{2\pi i n \alpha} z$ for some $n \neq 0$, whence $n \cdot U \cap U \neq \varnothing$ with $n \neq e$. Taking $K = S^1$ (which is compact) shows $\Gamma_K = \mathbb{Z}$ is infinite, so the action is not proper, and by Corollary 2 not properly discontinuous. The quotient $\mathbb{Z} \backslash S^1$ carries the indiscrete topology and is not Hausdorff. The two calibration examples together isolate the roles of the hypotheses: freeness is common to both, and it is **properness** — automatic for the finite group, absent for the dense $\mathbb{Z}$-orbit — that decides proper discontinuity.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Quotient by a Properly Discontinuous Action is a Manifold and a Covering Map *(from this section)*
> The immediate payoff is [[Thm - Quotient by a Properly Discontinuous Action is a Manifold and a Covering Map]]: when $\Gamma$ acts smoothly and properly discontinuously on $M$, the orbit space $\Gamma \backslash M$ has a unique smooth structure making $\pi : M \to \Gamma \backslash M$ a smooth covering map, and it satisfies the universal property of the quotient. The proof runs through Corollary 2 — proper discontinuity delivers the free-and-proper hypotheses of [[Thm - Quotient Manifold Theorem for Free Proper Actions|the quotient manifold theorem]] — and then upgrades "submersion" to "covering" using the evenly covered neighbourhoods furnished by condition (i).

> [!tip] The Torus and Flat Manifolds as Covering Quotients *(from this section)*
> With the criterion in hand, $T^n = \mathbb{R}^n / \mathbb{Z}^n$ is a manifold and $\mathbb{R}^n \to T^n$ a covering, and more generally every quotient of a model geometry by a discrete group acting properly discontinuously and freely is a manifold locally isometric to the model. This is the source of flat tori, Klein bottles, and mapping tori, the last of which appear in chapter III.

> [!tip] The Universal Cover as a Principal Bundle *(from Gauge Theory V)*
> Chapter V presents the **universal cover** $\widetilde{M} \to M$ of a connected manifold as the quotient of $\widetilde{M}$ by the properly discontinuous free action of the fundamental group $\pi_1(M)$, exhibiting it as a principal bundle with discrete structure group $\pi_1(M)$ — the first bridge between covering-space theory and the principal-bundle theory of the later chapters.
