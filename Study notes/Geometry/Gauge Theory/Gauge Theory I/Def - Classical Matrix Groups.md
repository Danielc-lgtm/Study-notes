# Notation

Throughout the series, manifolds are smooth, Hausdorff, and second countable, and "smooth" means $C^\infty$. A **[[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]]** is a smooth manifold $G$ that is also a group, such that the multiplication map $G \times G \to G$, $(g_1, g_2) \mapsto g_1 \cdot g_2$, and the inversion map $G \to G$, $g \mapsto g^{-1}$, are smooth. The identity element of a group is written $e$, or $1_n$ when the group consists of $n \times n$ matrices. This page depends on no sign or action convention; it fixes the names of the groups that every later chapter uses as structure groups.

The letter $\mathbb{K}$ stands for one of the two fields $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$. For a positive integer $n$, $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ denotes the set of $n \times n$ matrices with entries in $\mathbb{K}$; it is a $\mathbb{K}$-vector space of dimension $n^2$ under entrywise addition and scalar multiplication, and we identify it with $\mathbb{K}^{n^2}$ by reading off the entries $A_{jk}$ ($1 \le j, k \le n$) in a fixed order. In the complex case we identify further $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}^{n^2} = \mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$ by splitting every entry into its real and imaginary parts, $A_{jk} = x_{jk} + i y_{jk}$ with $x_{jk}, y_{jk} \in \mathbb{R}$; this makes $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C})$ a smooth manifold of real dimension $2n^2$. (Bär prints $\mathbb{R}^{(2n)^2}$ at this point; the correct exponent is $2n^2$, since $n^2$ complex entries carry $2n^2$ real coordinates.) For $A \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$: $A^t$ is the transpose, $(A^t)_{jk} = A_{kj}$; $\bar{A}$ is the entrywise complex conjugate, $(\bar{A})_{jk} = \overline{A_{jk}}$; and $A^* := (\bar{A})^t$ is the conjugate transpose (the adjoint of $A$ with respect to the standard Hermitian inner product). For real matrices $A^* = A^t$. The **[[Def - Determinant|determinant]]** $\det A \in \mathbb{K}$ is given by the Leibniz formula $\det A = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, A_{\sigma(1),1} \cdots A_{\sigma(n),n}$, where $S_n$ is the symmetric group on $\{1, \dots, n\}$; it is a polynomial of degree $n$ in the $n^2$ entries. We use two facts about it, both proved in the linked pages: $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$ ([[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative]]), so that $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det A \neq 0$, and $\det(A^t) = \det A$ (the transpose corollary on [[Def - Determinant]]).

The standard inner products are $\langle x, y \rangle = x^t y = \sum_{j=1}^n x_j y_j$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\langle x, y \rangle = x^* y = \sum_{j=1}^n \overline{x_j} y_j$ on $\mathbb{C}^n$ (conjugate-linear in the first slot, linear in the second), with $x, y$ regarded as column vectors.

The **[[Def - Quaternions|quaternions]]** $\mathbb{H}$ form the four-dimensional real associative algebra with basis $1, i, j, k$ and relations $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$; a quaternion is $q = a + bi + cj + dk$ with $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}$, its conjugate is $\bar{q} = a - bi - cj - dk$, and its norm is $|q| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2}$. We identify $\mathbb{H}$ with $\mathbb{R}^4$ through the coordinates $(a, b, c, d)$ and write $\mathbb{H}^\times = \mathbb{H} \setminus \{0\}$ for the nonzero quaternions.

For two Lie groups $G$ and $G'$, $G \times G'$ is their Cartesian product; $\pi_1 : G \times G' \to G$ and $\pi_2 : G \times G' \to G'$ are the two projections.

This is a compound page: it defines nine interlocking notions — the general linear groups $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, the orthogonal, special linear, special orthogonal, unitary, and special unitary groups $O(n)$, $SL(n; \mathbb{K})$, $SO(n)$, $U(n)$, $SU(n)$, the group $Sp(1)$ of unit quaternions, and the product $G \times G'$ of two Lie groups — because they are all instances of one mechanism (a closed subgroup of an open subset of a vector space of matrices, or a product of two such), because they are verified to be Lie groups by one and the same argument, and because every later chapter of the series draws its structure groups from this single list.

> [!warning] Convention: where the sources differ
> Bär (§1.1, Examples 1.1.2 and 1.1.4) defines $O(n)$ as a subset of $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ but $SL(n; \mathbb{R})$, $U(n)$, and $SL(n; \mathbb{C})$ as subsets of $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$. The two habits agree, because every element of each of these groups has nonzero determinant (this is proved in Block B below), so each group is a subset of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ in either reading; we define all of them inside $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ so that the closed subgroup theorem applies verbatim. Bär's item 5 of Example 1.1.4 prints "$SL(n; \mathbb{R}) := \{A \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C}) \mid \det(A) = 1\}$"; the left-hand side is a misprint for $SL(n; \mathbb{C})$, as the ambient set $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C})$ and item 6 ($SU(n) := U(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{C})$) both show, and we use the corrected form. The group $Sp(1)$ of unit quaternions is not in Bär's §1.1 at all; it is taken from Haydys §2.4, where it appears as the total space of the quaternionic Hopf bundle and is identified with $SU(2)$. The identification $Sp(1) \cong SU(2)$ is proved on [[Ex - SU(2) is the Group of Unit Quaternions]]; this page only establishes that $Sp(1)$ is a Lie group.

# Axiom Motivation

The abstract definition of a Lie group — a smooth manifold whose group operations are smooth — says nothing about where such objects come from. The first task after stating it is therefore to exhibit a supply of examples large enough to carry the whole subject, and in gauge theory that supply is remarkably small: the electromagnetic field has structure group $U(1)$, the weak interaction $SU(2)$, the strong interaction $SU(3)$, the frame bundle of an oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold has structure group $SO(n)$, and the Seiberg–Witten equations of chapter XI are written on a bundle with structure group $U(1) \times SU(2)$ glued along a common centre. Every one of these is a group of matrices. The question this page answers is: what is the uniform reason that these sets of matrices are Lie groups, and which features of their definitions are essential to that reason?

The organising principle is that each classical group is the group of invertible linear maps of $\mathbb{K}^n$ that preserve some additional structure. The group $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ preserves nothing beyond linearity; $SL(n; \mathbb{K})$ preserves the determinant, that is, oriented volume; $O(n)$ preserves the Euclidean inner product; $U(n)$ preserves the Hermitian inner product; $SO(n)$ and $SU(n)$ preserve both an inner product and the volume form; $Sp(1)$ is the group of quaternions of norm one, which are exactly the left multiplications preserving the quaternionic norm. The reason the defining equations look the way they do is the following computation, which we record once because it explains both the transpose in $A^t A = 1_n$ and the conjugate transpose in $A^* A = 1_n$. For $A \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R})$ and column vectors $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$,
$$\langle Ax, Ay \rangle = (Ax)^t (Ay) = x^t A^t A\, y \qquad \text{(definition of } \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle \text{ and } (Ax)^t = x^t A^t \text{)},$$
so $\langle Ax, Ay \rangle = \langle x, y \rangle$ for all $x, y$ if and only if $x^t (A^t A - 1_n) y = 0$ for all $x, y$, which — taking $x = e_j$ and $y = e_k$ to be standard basis vectors — holds if and only if every entry of $A^t A - 1_n$ vanishes, that is, if and only if $A^t A = 1_n$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ the same computation with $(Ax)^* = x^* A^*$ shows that $A$ preserves the Hermitian inner product if and only if $A^* A = 1_n$. So the conditions $A^t A = 1_n$ and $A^* A = 1_n$ are not arbitrary matrix identities: they are the statement "$A$ is an isometry", written in coordinates. This is the single idea from which the reader should be able to regenerate every definition below.

We now examine what each clause of the definitions is doing, by removing it. **The condition $\det A \neq 0$ in $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$** is what makes the set a group rather than merely a monoid: $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ is closed under multiplication and contains $1_n$, but the zero matrix has no inverse, and more generally a matrix has a multiplicative inverse if and only if its determinant is nonzero (the corollary of [[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative]], restated in Block A below). The condition is also what makes $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ a manifold in the cheapest possible way: it is an *open* condition, so $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ inherits charts from the vector space $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ with no work. Had we instead asked for a closed condition, such as $\det A = 0$, we would have obtained a set that is neither a group nor, near the zero matrix, a manifold.

**The transpose versus the conjugate transpose.** Over $\mathbb{C}$ one might be tempted to define the "orthogonal" group by the same equation $A^t A = 1_n$ that works over $\mathbb{R}$. This does define a group — the complex orthogonal group $O(n; \mathbb{C})$, a perfectly good Lie group by the same closed-subgroup argument — but it is the wrong group for our purposes, because it does not preserve the Hermitian inner product and it is not compact. The smallest case already shows the divergence: for $n = 1$ the equation $a^2 = 1$ has the two solutions $a = \pm 1$, so $O(1; \mathbb{C}) = \{\pm 1\}$, whereas $\bar{a} a = |a|^2 = 1$ has the whole unit circle as its solution set, so $U(1) = \{a \in \mathbb{C} : |a| = 1\}$. For $n = 2$ the matrices
$$A_t = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh t & i \sinh t \\ -i \sinh t & \cosh t \end{pmatrix}, \qquad t \in \mathbb{R},$$
satisfy $A_t^t A_t = 1_2$: the $(1,1)$ entry of $A_t^t A_t$ is $\cosh^2 t + (-i \sinh t)^2 = \cosh^2 t - \sinh^2 t = 1$, the $(2,2)$ entry is $(i \sinh t)^2 + \cosh^2 t = 1$, and the off-diagonal entries are $\cosh t \cdot i \sinh t + (-i \sinh t) \cosh t = 0$ and its transpose partner; yet the entries of $A_t$ are unbounded in $t$, so $O(2; \mathbb{C})$ is not compact. Compactness of the structure group is what later gives us an $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant inner product on the Lie algebra (chapter VII uses $-\operatorname{tr}(XY)$ on $\mathfrak{su}(n)$) and hence a positive Yang–Mills functional; the conjugate transpose is the clause that secures it, and it is exactly the clause that the Hermitian isometry computation above demands.

**The condition $\det A = 1$.** For $A \in O(n)$ the identity $A^t A = 1_n$ forces $(\det A)^2 = \det(A^t) \det A = \det(1_n) = 1$ (by multiplicativity and the transpose corollary), so $\det A = \pm 1$ automatically, and the "special" condition selects the half with $\det A = +1$, the transformations that preserve orientation as well as length. For $n = 1$ this is the difference between $O(1) = \{+1, -1\}$ and $SO(1) = \{+1\}$: dropping the clause keeps the reflection $x \mapsto -x$. For $U(n)$ the same two facts, together with $\det(\bar{A}) = \overline{\det A}$, give $|\det A|^2 = \overline{\det A} \det A = \det(A^*) \det A = \det(A^* A) = 1$, so $\det A$ lies on the unit circle, and $\det A = 1$ cuts $U(n)$ down to $SU(n)$; for $n = 1$ it cuts the circle $U(1)$ down to the single point $SU(1) = \{1\}$. The alternative clause $|\det A| = 1$ would be no restriction on $O(n)$ or $U(n)$ at all, while on $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ it would produce the group $\{\det A = \pm 1\}$, which is the union of $SL(n; \mathbb{R})$ with a second copy of it and is again the wrong group for orientation-sensitive purposes. The clause $\det A = 1$ is the minimal condition that fixes the volume form itself rather than its absolute value.

**Closedness.** Every group above is cut out of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ by equations, $A^t A = 1_n$, $\det A = 1$, $A^* A = 1_n$, and equations in continuous functions define closed sets. This is the clause that the [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem|closed subgroup theorem]] needs, and it cannot be dropped: a subgroup of a Lie group that is not closed need not be a Lie group in the induced topology, and need not be a submanifold at all. The concrete witness is
$$H_{\mathbb{Q}} := \{A \in GL(2; \mathbb{R}) : \det A \in \mathbb{Q}\},$$
which is a subgroup (by multiplicativity of the determinant, since the nonzero rationals form a group under multiplication) but not closed: the matrices $\operatorname{diag}(q_m, 1)$ with $q_m \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $q_m \to \sqrt{2}$ lie in $H_{\mathbb{Q}}$ and converge in $GL(2; \mathbb{R})$ to $\operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{2}, 1) \notin H_{\mathbb{Q}}$. This example is verified in full in the Examples section. The strengthening that would exclude it is exactly the closedness hypothesis of the theorem, and there is no weaker hypothesis that suffices in general: the theorem's converse (an embedded Lie subgroup is closed) shows that closedness is necessary as well as sufficient.

**The product.** For two Lie groups $G$ and $G'$ we equip $G \times G'$ with the componentwise operations $(g_1, g_1') \cdot (g_2, g_2') = (g_1 g_2, g_1' g_2')$ and $(g, g')^{-1} = (g^{-1}, g'^{-1})$. The alternative one might consider is a *twisted* product in which the second factor acts on the first before multiplying (a semidirect product); the direct product is the case of trivial action, and it is singled out by the property that both projections $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ are group homomorphisms and that $G \times \{e'\}$ and $\{e\} \times G'$ commute elementwise. The smoothness of the componentwise operations is not an additional axiom but a consequence of how the product manifold is built: its charts are products of charts, so a map into $G \times G'$ is smooth exactly when its two components are. The torus $T^n = U(1)^n$, the structure group of an $n$-fold product of line bundles, is the first payoff.

Could a reader invent these definitions from the desiderata alone? We believe so. Given the wish for "all invertible linear maps of $\mathbb{K}^n$ preserving structure $S$", one writes down $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, verifies that it is a Lie group because invertibility is an open polynomial condition and the operations are polynomial and rational, and then observes that "preserving $S$" is an equation in the entries of $A$, hence a closed condition, hence — by the closed subgroup theorem — the set of solutions is a Lie group. The list of classical groups is then simply the list of structures on $\mathbb{K}^n$ that one cares about: a volume form, a Euclidean or Hermitian inner product, both, or the quaternionic norm.

# The Definition

Let $n$ be a positive integer and $\mathbb{K} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$.

**The general linear group.**
$$GL(n; \mathbb{K}) := \{A \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) : \det A \neq 0\},$$
with matrix multiplication as the group operation, $1_n$ as the identity, and matrix inversion as the inverse. It is an open subset of $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) = \mathbb{K}^{n^2}$ and is given the smooth structure of an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ (real case) or $\mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$ (complex case).

**The classical subgroups.** Each of the following is a subset of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ with the induced group operations.

1. The **orthogonal group** $O(n) := \{A \in GL(n; \mathbb{R}) : A^t A = 1_n\}$.
2. The **special linear group** $SL(n; \mathbb{K}) := \{A \in GL(n; \mathbb{K}) : \det A = 1\}$, over $\mathbb{R}$ or over $\mathbb{C}$.
3. The **special orthogonal group** $SO(n) := O(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{R})$.
4. The **unitary group** $U(n) := \{A \in GL(n; \mathbb{C}) : A^* A = 1_n\}$, where $A^* = (\bar{A})^t$.
5. The **special unitary group** $SU(n) := U(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{C})$.

**The group of unit quaternions.**
$$Sp(1) := \{q \in \mathbb{H} : |q| = 1\},$$
with quaternion multiplication as the group operation, $1$ as the identity, and $q^{-1} = \bar{q}$ as the inverse. As a set, $Sp(1)$ is the unit sphere $S^3 = \{(a, b, c, d) \in \mathbb{R}^4 : a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 = 1\}$.

**The product of two Lie groups.** For Lie groups $G$ and $G'$, the **product Lie group** is the product manifold $G \times G'$ with the componentwise operations
$$(g_1, g_1') \cdot (g_2, g_2') := (g_1 \cdot g_2,\ g_1' \cdot g_2'), \qquad (g, g')^{-1} := (g^{-1}, g'^{-1}),$$
and identity $(e, e')$.

Each of these sets, with the operations named, is a Lie group. This is the content of the following proposition, which is the substance of the page; its proof verifies Bär's Examples 1.1.2 and 1.1.4 in full for every group in the list and Example 1.1.5 for the product.

> **Proposition (the classical matrix groups are Lie groups).**
> (A) $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ and $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$ are Lie groups, of dimensions $n^2$ and $2n^2$ respectively.
> (B) $O(n)$, $SL(n; \mathbb{R})$, $SO(n)$, $U(n)$, $SL(n; \mathbb{C})$, and $SU(n)$ are closed subgroups of $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ (the first three) or of $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$ (the last three), hence embedded Lie subgroups and Lie groups in their own right.
> (C) $Sp(1)$ is a closed subgroup of the Lie group $\mathbb{H}^\times$, hence a Lie group.
> (D) If $G$ and $G'$ are Lie groups, then $G \times G'$ with the componentwise operations is a Lie group.

The proof uses two results from elsewhere in the vault, which we restate here so that the argument can be checked without leaving the page.

> **Theorem (closed subgroup theorem; [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]]).** Let $G$ be a Lie group and $H \subset G$ a subgroup that is a closed subset of $G$ in the manifold topology. Then $H$ is an embedded submanifold of $G$, and with the induced smooth structure and the restricted group operations it is a Lie group.

> **Theorem (products of smooth manifolds; [[Thm - Product of Smooth Manifolds is a Smooth Manifold]]).** If $M_1$ and $M_2$ are smooth manifolds of dimensions $n_1$ and $n_2$, with maximal atlases $\mathcal{A}_1$ and $\mathcal{A}_2$, then $M_1 \times M_2$ with the product topology is a topological manifold of dimension $n_1 + n_2$, and the product atlas $\{(U_1 \times U_2, \varphi_1 \times \varphi_2) : (U_i, \varphi_i) \in \mathcal{A}_i\}$ is a smooth atlas on it, where $(\varphi_1 \times \varphi_2)(p_1, p_2) = (\varphi_1(p_1), \varphi_2(p_2)) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_1} \times \mathbb{R}^{n_2} = \mathbb{R}^{n_1 + n_2}$.

> [!note]- Proof of the Proposition
> We must show four things: that $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ satisfies the three clauses of the definition of a Lie group (smooth manifold, smooth multiplication, smooth inversion); that each of the six classical subgroups is a subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ and closed in it; that $\mathbb{H}^\times$ is a Lie group and $Sp(1)$ a closed subgroup of it; and that $G \times G'$ is a group whose operations are smooth. Two elementary facts from topology and linear algebra are used repeatedly and we name them once. **(Fact T)** If $f : X \to Y$ is a continuous map between topological spaces and $C \subset Y$ is closed, then $f^{-1}(C)$ is closed in $X$: indeed $X \setminus f^{-1}(C) = f^{-1}(Y \setminus C)$ is the preimage of an open set, hence open by the definition of continuity. If $X_0 \subset X$ carries the subspace topology, then $f|_{X_0}$ is continuous (open sets of $X_0$ are the traces of open sets of $X$), so $f^{-1}(C) \cap X_0$ is closed in $X_0$. **(Fact P)** A polynomial function $\mathbb{R}^N \to \mathbb{R}$ is smooth, and a quotient of two polynomial functions is smooth on the open set where the denominator does not vanish (partial derivatives of every order exist and are again such quotients, by the product and quotient rules). A map $\mathbb{R}^N \supset U \to \mathbb{R}^M$ is smooth if and only if each of its $M$ component functions is smooth, because partial derivatives are computed componentwise.
>
> **Block A: $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a Lie group.** We treat $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ first and then say precisely what changes for $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{C}$.
>
> **Step A.0 (the set is a group).** We need to show that $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ is closed under multiplication, contains $1_n$, and contains inverses. By [[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative]] — for $A, B \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$, $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B$ — we have, for $A, B \in GL(n; \mathbb{R})$,
> $$\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B \neq 0 \qquad \text{(multiplicativity; a product of two nonzero scalars in a field is nonzero)},$$
> so $AB \in GL(n; \mathbb{R})$. Next $\det 1_n = 1 \neq 0$ (Leibniz formula: only the identity permutation contributes), so $1_n \in GL(n; \mathbb{R})$. Finally, the corollary of the same theorem states that $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det A \neq 0$, and then $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A \neq 0$; so every $A \in GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ has an inverse $A^{-1}$ that again lies in $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$. Associativity of matrix multiplication and the identity law hold in $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R})$ and are inherited. Hence $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ is a group.
>
> **Step A.1 (the set is an open subset, hence a smooth manifold of dimension $n^2$).** The determinant $\det : \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}^{n^2} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a polynomial in the entries (Leibniz formula), hence continuous (Fact P; smooth functions are continuous). The set $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ is open in $\mathbb{R}$, so
> $$GL(n; \mathbb{R}) = \det{}^{-1}(\mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}) \text{ is open in } \mathbb{R}^{n^2} \qquad \text{(preimage of an open set under a continuous map)}.$$
> An open subset $U$ of $\mathbb{R}^{N}$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $N$ with the single chart $(U, \mathrm{id}_U)$: it is Hausdorff and second countable as a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^N$, locally Euclidean via the identity, and the one-chart atlas has no transition maps to check. (This is the content of [[Ex - The General Linear Group is a Smooth Manifold]], which we have just re-derived.) So $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $n^2$, and a map into or out of it is smooth if and only if it is smooth as a map between open subsets of Euclidean spaces.
>
> **Step A.2 (multiplication is smooth).** The multiplication map is $\mu : GL(n; \mathbb{R}) \times GL(n; \mathbb{R}) \to GL(n; \mathbb{R})$, $(A, B) \mapsto AB$. Its domain is an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n^2} \times \mathbb{R}^{n^2} = \mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$ and each component of its value is
> $$(AB)_{jk} = \sum_{l=1}^n A_{jl} B_{lk} \qquad \text{(definition of matrix multiplication)},$$
> a polynomial of degree two in the $2n^2$ coordinates $(A_{jl}, B_{lk})$. By Fact P each component is smooth, hence $\mu$ is smooth.
>
> **Step A.3 (inversion is smooth).** The inversion map is $\iota : GL(n; \mathbb{R}) \to GL(n; \mathbb{R})$, $A \mapsto A^{-1}$. By the adjugate formula of [[Thm - Cofactor Expansion and Cramer's Rule]] — for every $n \times n$ matrix $A$, $A \cdot \operatorname{adj}(A) = (\det A) \cdot 1_n$, where $\operatorname{adj}(A)_{jk} = (-1)^{j+k} M_{kj}$ and $M_{kj}$ is the determinant of the $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ matrix obtained by deleting row $k$ and column $j$ of $A$ — we have, for $\det A \neq 0$,
> $$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A} \operatorname{adj}(A), \qquad \text{so} \qquad (A^{-1})_{jk} = \frac{(-1)^{j+k} M_{kj}}{\det A} \qquad \text{(adjugate formula divided by } \det A \neq 0 \text{)}.$$
> Each minor $M_{kj}$ is a polynomial in the entries of $A$ (Leibniz formula for an $(n-1) \times (n-1)$ determinant), and $\det A$ is a polynomial in the entries that does not vanish on $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$. By Fact P each component of $\iota$ is a quotient of polynomials with non-vanishing denominator on the open set $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$, hence smooth; so $\iota$ is smooth.
>
> **Step A.4 (the complex case).** Identify $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C})$ with $\mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$ through $A_{jk} = x_{jk} + i y_{jk}$. A complex polynomial $p(A)$ in the entries $A_{jk}$ becomes, after substituting $A_{jk} = x_{jk} + i y_{jk}$ and expanding, an expression $p_1(x, y) + i\, p_2(x, y)$ with $p_1, p_2$ real polynomials in the $2n^2$ real coordinates, because sums and products of complex numbers have real and imaginary parts that are polynomial in the real and imaginary parts of the factors. Applying this to $\det$: $\det A = p_1 + i p_2$ with $p_1, p_2$ continuous, and
> $$GL(n; \mathbb{C}) = \{(x, y) : (p_1(x, y), p_2(x, y)) \neq (0, 0)\} = (p_1, p_2)^{-1}\big(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}\big) \qquad \text{(since } \det A \neq 0 \iff (\operatorname{Re} \det A, \operatorname{Im} \det A) \neq (0,0) \text{)},$$
> the preimage of an open set under a continuous map, hence open in $\mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$; so $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$ is a smooth manifold of real dimension $2n^2$ by the argument of Step A.1. Step A.0 goes through verbatim, since [[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative]] holds over any field. In Step A.2, the real and imaginary parts of $(AB)_{jk}$ are real polynomials in $(x, y)$ by the same expansion, so $\mu$ is smooth. In Step A.3, the real and imaginary parts of $(-1)^{j+k} M_{kj} / \det A$ are, writing $\det A = p_1 + i p_2$ and $(-1)^{j+k} M_{kj} = r_1 + i r_2$ with real polynomials $r_1, r_2$,
> $$\frac{r_1 + i r_2}{p_1 + i p_2} = \frac{(r_1 + i r_2)(p_1 - i p_2)}{p_1^2 + p_2^2} = \frac{r_1 p_1 + r_2 p_2}{p_1^2 + p_2^2} + i\, \frac{r_2 p_1 - r_1 p_2}{p_1^2 + p_2^2} \qquad \text{(multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate)},$$
> quotients of real polynomials whose denominator $p_1^2 + p_2^2 = |\det A|^2$ does not vanish on $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$; by Fact P they are smooth, so $\iota$ is smooth. This proves (A).
>
> **Block B: the six classical subgroups are closed subgroups.** Throughout this block we use the following elementary identities for matrices $A, B$ over $\mathbb{K}$: $(AB)^t = B^t A^t$ (both sides have $(j,k)$ entry $\sum_l A_{kl} B_{lj}$), $\overline{AB} = \bar{A} \bar{B}$ (complex conjugation is a ring homomorphism of $\mathbb{C}$, so it commutes with the sums and products in $\sum_l A_{jl} B_{lk}$), hence $(AB)^* = (\overline{AB})^t = (\bar{A} \bar{B})^t = \bar{B}^t \bar{A}^t = B^* A^*$; also $(A^t)^t = A$, $(A^*)^* = A$, $1_n^t = 1_n^* = 1_n$, and $\det(\bar{A}) = \overline{\det A}$ (apply conjugation to the Leibniz formula term by term). For each group $H$ we check: (i) $H \subset GL(n; \mathbb{K})$; (ii) $1_n \in H$; (iii) $A, B \in H \Rightarrow AB \in H$; (iv) $A \in H \Rightarrow A^{-1} \in H$; (v) $H$ is closed in $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$. Then the closed subgroup theorem, restated above, makes $H$ an embedded Lie subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ and a Lie group.
>
> **Step B.1: $O(n)$.** (i) By definition $O(n) \subset GL(n; \mathbb{R})$; we note in passing that the equation alone forces invertibility, since $A^t A = 1_n$ gives $(\det A)^2 = \det(A^t) \det A = \det 1_n = 1$ (transpose corollary of [[Def - Determinant]], multiplicativity), so $\det A = \pm 1 \neq 0$. (ii) $1_n^t 1_n = 1_n$, so $1_n \in O(n)$. (iii) For $A, B \in O(n)$,
> $$(AB)^t (AB) = B^t A^t A B = B^t 1_n B = B^t B = 1_n \qquad \text{(} (AB)^t = B^t A^t\text{; } A^t A = 1_n \text{ since } A \in O(n)\text{; } B^t B = 1_n \text{ since } B \in O(n) \text{)},$$
> so $AB \in O(n)$. (iv) For $A \in O(n)$, the equation $A^t A = 1_n$ says that $A^t$ is a left inverse of $A$; since $A$ is invertible by (i), multiplying $A^t A = 1_n$ on the right by $A^{-1}$ gives $A^t = A^{-1}$. Therefore
> $$(A^{-1})^t A^{-1} = (A^t)^t A^t = A A^t = A A^{-1} = 1_n \qquad \text{(} A^{-1} = A^t\text{; } (A^t)^t = A\text{; } A^t = A^{-1} \text{)},$$
> so $A^{-1} \in O(n)$. (v) The map $\Phi : GL(n; \mathbb{R}) \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R})$, $\Phi(A) = A^t A$, has components $\sum_l A_{lj} A_{lk}$, polynomials in the entries, hence is continuous (Fact P). The singleton $\{1_n\}$ is closed in $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ (points of a metric space are closed). Hence $O(n) = \Phi^{-1}(\{1_n\})$ is closed in $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ by Fact T. So $O(n)$ is a closed subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$.
>
> **Step B.2: $SL(n; \mathbb{R})$.** (i) Every $A$ with $\det A = 1$ has $\det A \neq 0$, so $SL(n; \mathbb{R}) \subset GL(n; \mathbb{R})$. (ii) $\det 1_n = 1$. (iii) For $A, B \in SL(n; \mathbb{R})$, $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$ (multiplicativity). (iv) For $A \in SL(n; \mathbb{R})$, $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A = 1$ (corollary of multiplicativity). (v) $\det : GL(n; \mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$ is the restriction of a polynomial, hence continuous, and $\{1\}$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}$; so $SL(n; \mathbb{R}) = \det^{-1}(\{1\}) \cap GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ is closed in $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ by Fact T.
>
> **Step B.3: $SO(n)$.** We show generally that the intersection $H_1 \cap H_2$ of two closed subgroups of a group $G$ is a closed subgroup, and apply it to $H_1 = O(n)$, $H_2 = SL(n; \mathbb{R})$. (i) $H_1 \cap H_2 \subset H_1 \subset G$. (ii) $e \in H_1$ and $e \in H_2$, so $e \in H_1 \cap H_2$. (iii) If $A, B \in H_1 \cap H_2$ then $AB \in H_1$ (as $H_1$ is a subgroup) and $AB \in H_2$ (as $H_2$ is a subgroup), so $AB \in H_1 \cap H_2$. (iv) If $A \in H_1 \cap H_2$ then $A^{-1} \in H_1$ and $A^{-1} \in H_2$, so $A^{-1} \in H_1 \cap H_2$. (v) The intersection of two closed sets is closed (its complement is the union of two open sets). By Steps B.1 and B.2, $SO(n) = O(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{R})$ is a closed subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$.
>
> **Step B.4: $U(n)$.** (i) Suppose $A \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C})$ satisfies $A^* A = 1_n$. Then
> $$1 = \det(1_n) = \det(A^* A) = \det(A^*) \det(A) = \det\big((\bar{A})^t\big) \det A = \det(\bar{A}) \det A = \overline{\det A} \cdot \det A = |\det A|^2 \qquad \text{(multiplicativity; transpose corollary; } \det \bar{A} = \overline{\det A} \text{)},$$
> so $\det A \neq 0$ and $A \in GL(n; \mathbb{C})$; in particular Bär's definition of $U(n)$ inside $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C})$ agrees with ours inside $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$. (ii) $1_n^* 1_n = 1_n$. (iii) For $A, B \in U(n)$,
> $$(AB)^* (AB) = B^* A^* A B = B^* 1_n B = B^* B = 1_n \qquad \text{(} (AB)^* = B^* A^*\text{; } A^* A = 1_n\text{; } B^* B = 1_n \text{)},$$
> so $AB \in U(n)$. (iv) For $A \in U(n)$, multiplying $A^* A = 1_n$ on the right by $A^{-1}$ (which exists by (i)) gives $A^* = A^{-1}$, and then
> $$(A^{-1})^* A^{-1} = (A^*)^* A^* = A A^* = A A^{-1} = 1_n \qquad \text{(} A^{-1} = A^*\text{; } (A^*)^* = A\text{; } A^* = A^{-1} \text{)},$$
> so $A^{-1} \in U(n)$. (v) The map $\Psi : GL(n; \mathbb{C}) \to \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C})$, $\Psi(A) = A^* A$, has components $\sum_l \overline{A_{lj}} A_{lk}$, whose real and imaginary parts are real polynomials in the real coordinates $(x, y)$ (conjugation changes the sign of $y_{lj}$), so $\Psi$ is continuous; $\{1_n\}$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$; hence $U(n) = \Psi^{-1}(\{1_n\})$ is closed in $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$ by Fact T.
>
> **Step B.5: $SL(n; \mathbb{C})$.** This is Bär's item 5 with the misprint $SL(n; \mathbb{R})$ corrected to $SL(n; \mathbb{C})$. Clauses (i)–(iv) are word for word those of Step B.2 with $\mathbb{R}$ replaced by $\mathbb{C}$, every step surviving the substitution because multiplicativity of the determinant and its corollary hold over every field. (v) $\det : GL(n; \mathbb{C}) \to \mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^2$ has real and imaginary parts $p_1, p_2$ that are real polynomials (Step A.4), hence is continuous, and $\{1\} = \{(1, 0)\}$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}^2$; so $SL(n; \mathbb{C}) = \det^{-1}(\{1\}) \cap GL(n; \mathbb{C})$ is closed in $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$ by Fact T.
>
> **Step B.6: $SU(n)$.** By the general intersection argument of Step B.3 applied to the closed subgroups $U(n)$ (Step B.4) and $SL(n; \mathbb{C})$ (Step B.5) of $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$, $SU(n) = U(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{C})$ is a closed subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$.
>
> **Conclusion of Block B.** Each of the six sets is a subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ that is closed in the manifold topology of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$; $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a Lie group by Block A; therefore, by the closed subgroup theorem, each is an embedded submanifold of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ and a Lie group with the induced structure. This proves (B).
>
> **Block C: $Sp(1)$ is a Lie group.** The argument is Blocks A and B for the quaternions in place of matrices, and we need three quaternion identities. For $p = a + bi + cj + dk$ and $q = a' + b'i + c'j + d'k$, the relations $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = -1$, $ij = k = -ji$, $jk = i = -kj$, $ki = j = -ik$ (which follow from $ijk = -1$ and the squares) give, by distributing,
> $$pq = (aa' - bb' - cc' - dd') + (ab' + ba' + cd' - dc')\, i + (ac' - bd' + ca' + db')\, j + (ad' + bc' - cb' + da')\, k. \tag{Q1}$$
> **(Identity 1: $\overline{pq} = \bar{q} \bar{p}$.)** Substituting $(a', -b', -c', -d')$ for $p$'s coefficients and $(a, -b, -c, -d)$ for $q$'s in (Q1) computes $\bar{q} \bar{p}$: its real part is $a'a - b'b - c'c - d'd$, equal to the real part of $pq$; its $i$-coefficient is $a'(-b) + (-b')a + (-c')(-d) - (-d')(-c) = -(ab' + ba' + cd' - dc')$, the negative of the $i$-coefficient of $pq$; the $j$-coefficient is $a'(-c) - (-b')(-d) + (-c')a + (-d')(-b) = -(ac' - bd' + ca' + db')$, and the $k$-coefficient is $a'(-d) + (-b')(-c) - (-c')(-b) + (-d')a = -(ad' + bc' - cb' + da')$; so $\bar{q}\bar{p} = \overline{pq}$ coefficient by coefficient. **(Identity 2: $q \bar{q} = \bar{q} q = |q|^2$.)** Substituting $(a, -b, -c, -d)$ for the second factor in (Q1) gives real part $a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2$ and $i$-coefficient $a(-b) + ba + c(-d) - d(-c) = 0$, $j$-coefficient $a(-c) - b(-d) + ca + d(-b) = 0$, $k$-coefficient $a(-d) + b(-c) - c(-b) + da = 0$; the same substitution in the first factor gives the same result, since the real part of (Q1) is symmetric in $p$ and $q$ and the three imaginary coefficients vanish by the same cancellations. **(Identity 3: $|pq| = |p|\,|q|$.)** Using associativity of $\mathbb{H}$ and that a real scalar commutes with every quaternion,
> $$|pq|^2 = (pq)\overline{(pq)} = p q \bar{q} \bar{p} = p\, |q|^2\, \bar{p} = |q|^2\, p \bar{p} = |q|^2 |p|^2 \qquad \text{(Identity 2; Identity 1; Identity 2; real scalars are central; Identity 2)},$$
> and taking nonnegative square roots gives Identity 3.
>
> **Step C.0 ($\mathbb{H}^\times$ is a Lie group).** *Group:* $\mathbb{H}^\times = \{q : |q| \neq 0\}$ is closed under multiplication by Identity 3, contains $1$, and every $q \neq 0$ has the two-sided inverse $q^{-1} = \bar{q} / |q|^2$, since $q \cdot \bar{q}/|q|^2 = |q|^2/|q|^2 = 1 = \bar{q} q / |q|^2$ by Identity 2; associativity is that of the algebra $\mathbb{H}$. *Manifold:* $|q|^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2$ is a polynomial on $\mathbb{R}^4$, hence continuous, and $\mathbb{H}^\times$ is the preimage of the open set $(0, \infty)$, hence an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^4$ and a smooth manifold of dimension $4$ by the argument of Step A.1. *Multiplication smooth:* by (Q1), the four coordinates of $pq$ are polynomials of degree two in the eight coordinates of $(p, q)$, so multiplication is smooth by Fact P. *Inversion smooth:* the coordinates of $q^{-1} = \bar{q}/|q|^2$ are $(a, -b, -c, -d)/(a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2)$, quotients of polynomials with non-vanishing denominator on $\mathbb{H}^\times$, hence smooth by Fact P. So $\mathbb{H}^\times$ is a Lie group.
>
> **Step C.1 ($Sp(1)$ is a closed subgroup).** (i) $Sp(1) \subset \mathbb{H}^\times$ since $|q| = 1 \neq 0$. (ii) $|1| = 1$. (iii) For $p, q \in Sp(1)$, $|pq| = |p|\,|q| = 1$ by Identity 3. (iv) For $q \in Sp(1)$, $q^{-1} = \bar{q}/|q|^2 = \bar{q}$ and $|\bar{q}| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2} = |q| = 1$. (v) $|\cdot|^2 : \mathbb{H}^\times \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous and $\{1\}$ is closed in $\mathbb{R}$, so $Sp(1) = (|\cdot|^2)^{-1}(\{1\})$ is closed in $\mathbb{H}^\times$ by Fact T. By the closed subgroup theorem, $Sp(1)$ is an embedded Lie subgroup of $\mathbb{H}^\times$, hence a Lie group. This proves (C).
>
> **Block D: $G \times G'$ is a Lie group.** Let $G$ and $G'$ be Lie groups with multiplications $\mu, \mu'$, inversions $\iota, \iota'$, and identities $e, e'$.
>
> **Step D.0 ($G \times G'$ is a group).** We check the three group axioms for the componentwise operation. *Associativity:* for $(g_1, g_1'), (g_2, g_2'), (g_3, g_3') \in G \times G'$,
> $$\big((g_1, g_1')(g_2, g_2')\big)(g_3, g_3') = (g_1 g_2, g_1' g_2')(g_3, g_3') = \big((g_1 g_2) g_3, (g_1' g_2') g_3'\big) = \big(g_1 (g_2 g_3), g_1' (g_2' g_3')\big) = (g_1, g_1')\big((g_2, g_2')(g_3, g_3')\big) \qquad \text{(definition twice; associativity in } G \text{ and in } G'\text{; definition twice)}.$$
> *Identity:* $(e, e')(g, g') = (eg, e'g') = (g, g')$ and $(g, g')(e, e') = (ge, g'e') = (g, g')$ (identity laws in $G$ and $G'$). *Inverses:* $(g, g')(g^{-1}, g'^{-1}) = (gg^{-1}, g'g'^{-1}) = (e, e')$ and $(g^{-1}, g'^{-1})(g, g') = (e, e')$ (inverse laws in $G$ and $G'$). So $G \times G'$ is a group with identity $(e, e')$ and $(g, g')^{-1} = (g^{-1}, g'^{-1})$.
>
> **Step D.1 ($G \times G'$ is a smooth manifold).** By the product theorem restated above, $G \times G'$ with the product topology is a topological manifold — in particular Hausdorff and second countable — of dimension $\dim G + \dim G'$, and the product atlas is a smooth atlas; we give $G \times G'$ the maximal atlas containing it.
>
> **Step D.2 (the component criterion).** We claim: for a smooth manifold $N$ and a map $F = (F_1, F_2) : N \to G \times G'$, $F$ is smooth if and only if $F_1 : N \to G$ and $F_2 : N \to G'$ are smooth; and the projections $\pi_1, \pi_2$ are smooth. Take $p \in N$, a chart $(W, \psi)$ of $N$ around $p$, and product charts $(U \times U', \varphi \times \varphi')$ around $F(p)$ with $(U, \varphi)$ a chart of $G$ and $(U', \varphi')$ a chart of $G'$. Shrinking $W$ so that $F(W) \subset U \times U'$ (possible because $F$ is continuous when $F_1, F_2$ are, the product topology being the coarsest for which both projections are continuous, and conversely $F_1 = \pi_1 \circ F$, $F_2 = \pi_2 \circ F$ are continuous when $F$ is), the coordinate representation is
> $$(\varphi \times \varphi') \circ F \circ \psi^{-1} = \big(\varphi \circ F_1 \circ \psi^{-1},\ \varphi' \circ F_2 \circ \psi^{-1}\big) : \psi(W) \to \mathbb{R}^{\dim G} \times \mathbb{R}^{\dim G'} \qquad \text{(definition of } \varphi \times \varphi' \text{)},$$
> and by Fact P a map into $\mathbb{R}^{\dim G} \times \mathbb{R}^{\dim G'} = \mathbb{R}^{\dim G + \dim G'}$ is smooth if and only if both blocks of components are smooth; the left block is the coordinate representation of $F_1$ and the right block that of $F_2$. Since smoothness of a map between manifolds means smoothness of its coordinate representations in every pair of charts of the maximal atlases, and it suffices to check it in one pair of charts around each point ([[Def - Smooth Map between Manifolds]]: transition maps are smooth, so smoothness in one chart pair implies it in all), the claim follows. For the projections, $\varphi \circ \pi_1 \circ (\varphi \times \varphi')^{-1}(u, u') = u$ is the restriction of a linear map, hence smooth; likewise for $\pi_2$.
>
> **Step D.3 (multiplication is smooth).** The multiplication of $G \times G'$ is the map $M : (G \times G') \times (G \times G') \to G \times G'$, $M\big((g_1, g_1'), (g_2, g_2')\big) = (g_1 g_2, g_1' g_2')$. Write $\mathrm{pr}_1, \mathrm{pr}_2 : (G \times G') \times (G \times G') \to G \times G'$ for the two projections of the outer product (smooth by Step D.2 applied to the product of the manifolds $G \times G'$ and $G \times G'$). The first component of $M$ is
> $$M_1 = \mu \circ \big(\pi_1 \circ \mathrm{pr}_1,\ \pi_1 \circ \mathrm{pr}_2\big) : (G \times G') \times (G \times G') \to G \times G \to G,$$
> where $(\pi_1 \circ \mathrm{pr}_1, \pi_1 \circ \mathrm{pr}_2)$ is smooth as a map into $G \times G$ by Step D.2 (both components are compositions of smooth projections, and compositions of smooth maps are smooth by [[Def - Smooth Map between Manifolds]]), and $\mu$ is smooth because $G$ is a Lie group; hence $M_1$ is smooth. In the same way $M_2 = \mu' \circ (\pi_2 \circ \mathrm{pr}_1, \pi_2 \circ \mathrm{pr}_2)$ is smooth because $\mu'$ is smooth. By Step D.2, $M = (M_1, M_2)$ is smooth.
>
> **Step D.4 (inversion is smooth).** The inversion of $G \times G'$ is $I : G \times G' \to G \times G'$, $I(g, g') = (g^{-1}, g'^{-1}) = (\iota \circ \pi_1, \iota' \circ \pi_2)(g, g')$. Each component is a composition of a smooth projection with a smooth inversion map ($\iota$ and $\iota'$ are smooth because $G$ and $G'$ are Lie groups), hence smooth; by Step D.2, $I$ is smooth. This proves (D).
>
> **Conclusion.** $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a Lie group by Block A; the six classical subgroups and $Sp(1)$ are closed subgroups of Lie groups by Blocks B and C and hence Lie groups by the closed subgroup theorem; and the product of two Lie groups is a Lie group by Block D. Therefore every group named in the definition is a Lie group. $\blacksquare$

**The smallest cases, written out.** For $n = 1$ the matrices are scalars and every group in the list can be seen whole. $GL(1; \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}^\times = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ and $GL(1; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{C}^\times$, under multiplication. $O(1) = \{a \in \mathbb{R}^\times : a^2 = 1\} = \{+1, -1\}$, a group of order two, and a $0$-dimensional Lie group (a discrete set is a $0$-manifold, and any map out of a discrete space is smooth). $SL(1; \mathbb{K}) = \{1\}$ and $SO(1) = SU(1) = \{1\}$, the trivial group. $U(1) = \{a \in \mathbb{C}^\times : \bar{a} a = 1\} = \{a \in \mathbb{C} : |a| = 1\}$, the unit circle $S^1 \subset \mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^2$, under multiplication of complex numbers; it is the structure group of every Hermitian line bundle and the gauge group of electromagnetism. The first case in which all the groups are non-trivial is $n = 2$: $SO(2)$ is the group of rotations of the plane, and [[Thm - SO(2) is Isomorphic to U(1)]] proves that $SO(2) = \left\{\begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix} : \varphi \in \mathbb{R}\right\}$ and that $\begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix} \mapsto e^{i\varphi}$ is an isomorphism of Lie groups onto $U(1)$; and $SU(2)$, the structure group of the weak interaction and of every $\operatorname{Spin}(3)$-structure, is identified with $Sp(1) = S^3$ on [[Ex - SU(2) is the Group of Unit Quaternions]].

# Categorical / Structural Definition

Two structural readings make the list above less arbitrary.

**Each classical group is an automorphism group.** A group of the form $\{A \in GL(V) : A \text{ preserves } S\}$, for a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ and a structure $S$ on $V$, is the automorphism group $\operatorname{Aut}(V, S)$ of the pair $(V, S)$ in the category whose objects are vector spaces with such a structure and whose morphisms are linear maps preserving it. With $V = \mathbb{K}^n$: $GL(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{K}^n)$; $SL(n; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{K}^n, \omega)$ for the volume form $\omega = e_1^* \wedge \cdots \wedge e_n^*$, since a linear map $A$ satisfies $A^* \omega = (\det A)\, \omega$ by the defining property of the determinant on [[Def - Determinant]] (an alternating $n$-linear form pulled back by $A$ is $\det A$ times itself); $O(n) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{R}^n, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ and $U(n) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^n, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle)$ by the isometry computation in the Axiom Motivation (an isometry of a finite-dimensional inner product space is injective, hence invertible, so the invertibility clause is automatic); $SO(n) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{R}^n, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle, \omega)$ and $SU(n) = \operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{C}^n, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle, \omega)$. This reading is the one that chapter III uses: a principal $G$-bundle for one of these $G$ is the bundle of frames of a vector bundle adapted to the corresponding structure ([[Def - Frame Bundle of a Vector Bundle]], [[Def - Reduction and Extension of the Structure Group]]).

**The product is the categorical product of Lie groups.** The product Lie group $G \times G'$, together with the projections $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$, satisfies the universal property of a product in the category of Lie groups and Lie group homomorphisms: for every Lie group $H$ and every pair of Lie group homomorphisms $f : H \to G$ and $f' : H \to G'$ there is exactly one Lie group homomorphism $(f, f') : H \to G \times G'$ with $\pi_1 \circ (f, f') = f$ and $\pi_2 \circ (f, f') = f'$. We verify this. *Existence:* define $(f, f')(h) := (f(h), f'(h))$. It is smooth by the component criterion of Step D.2, because $f$ and $f'$ are smooth. It is a group homomorphism because
$$(f, f')(h_1 h_2) = \big(f(h_1 h_2), f'(h_1 h_2)\big) = \big(f(h_1) f(h_2), f'(h_1) f'(h_2)\big) = (f, f')(h_1) \cdot (f, f')(h_2) \qquad \text{(} f, f' \text{ homomorphisms; componentwise product)},$$
and $\pi_1 \circ (f, f') = f$, $\pi_2 \circ (f, f') = f'$ by construction. The projections are smooth by Step D.2 and are homomorphisms because $\pi_1\big((g_1, g_1')(g_2, g_2')\big) = \pi_1(g_1 g_2, g_1' g_2') = g_1 g_2 = \pi_1(g_1, g_1')\, \pi_1(g_2, g_2')$, and likewise for $\pi_2$. *Uniqueness:* if $\Phi : H \to G \times G'$ satisfies $\pi_1 \circ \Phi = f$ and $\pi_2 \circ \Phi = f'$, then for every $h$, $\Phi(h) = (\pi_1(\Phi(h)), \pi_2(\Phi(h))) = (f(h), f'(h))$, so $\Phi = (f, f')$. Therefore $G \times G'$ is the product in the category of Lie groups, and in particular it is determined up to a unique isomorphism by $G$ and $G'$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** The operational content of the whole page is one sentence: *a closed subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ is a Lie group*. A group presented this way is called a **closed matrix group** or a **linear Lie group**, and every group in the list above is one — $Sp(1)$ included, since $\mathbb{H}^\times$ embeds in $GL(4; \mathbb{R})$ by left multiplication, or since $Sp(1) \cong SU(2) \subset GL(2; \mathbb{C})$ ([[Ex - SU(2) is the Group of Unit Quaternions]]). The converse fails in both directions in ways worth knowing: not every Lie group is a closed matrix group (the universal covering group of $SL(2; \mathbb{R})$ has no faithful finite-dimensional representation), while every *compact* Lie group is one (a consequence of the **Peter–Weyl theorem**); both facts are stated here for orientation only, are not proved, and are not used in this series. For gauge theory the restriction to closed matrix groups costs nothing, because the structure groups that occur — $U(1)$, $SU(2)$, $SO(n)$, $U(n)$, $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ — are all compact.

**Linear algebra.** $O(n)$ and $U(n)$ are the groups of matrices of orthogonal and [[Def - Unitary Operator|unitary operators]] of $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbb{C}^n$ in the standard basis: the characterisation $T^* T = 1$ of a unitary operator is the equation $A^* A = 1_n$ for its matrix. The normal-form theorems of linear algebra — every orthogonal matrix is conjugate in $O(n)$ to a block-diagonal matrix of planar rotations and $\pm 1$ entries, every unitary matrix is diagonalisable by a unitary matrix with eigenvalues on the unit circle — are the reason these groups are tractable: their maximal tori (products of copies of $SO(2)$ or $U(1)$) meet every conjugacy class.

**Topology: Hilbert's fifth problem.** Bär's Remark 1.1.6 records a historical fact that puts the smoothness clause of the definition of a Lie group in perspective. At the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris in 1900, Hilbert asked, as the fifth of his problems, whether "smooth" in the definition of a Lie group can be replaced by "continuous": is every [[Def - Topological Group|topological group]] whose underlying space is a topological manifold already a Lie group for some smooth structure? The answer, found in the early 1950s, is yes — every locally Euclidean topological group admits a unique compatible smooth (indeed real-analytic) structure making it a Lie group — and the theorem is due to **Gleason, Montgomery, and Zippin**. For the classical groups this is visible directly: their group operations are polynomial and rational, so no separate continuity-versus-smoothness question arises. The general theorem is **not proved and not used in this series**; we mention it only so that the reader does not mistake the smoothness hypothesis for a substantive restriction on which topological groups qualify.

**Physics.** The classical groups are the structure groups of the gauge theories of chapter VII: $U(1)$ for electrodynamics ([[Def - U(1) Gauge Field and Electromagnetic Connection]]), $SU(n)$ for Yang–Mills theory ([[Def - Yang-Mills Lagrangian and Action Functional]]). The orthogonal groups are the structure groups of the frame bundles of Riemannian manifolds, and the unit quaternions $Sp(1) = SU(2)$ are simultaneously the fibre of the quaternionic Hopf bundle $S^7 \to S^4$ and the group $\operatorname{Spin}(3)$ of chapter VIII. The condition $A^* A = 1_n$ is the coordinate form of "conserves the Hermitian inner product", that is, of "conserves probability" in quantum mechanics, which is why unitary rather than complex-orthogonal groups appear.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — $G = \mathbb{R}^n$ with addition (Bär, Example 1.1.2, item 1).** We verify the three clauses. $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a smooth manifold with the single chart $(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathrm{id})$. It is a group under addition with identity $0$ and inverse $x \mapsto -x$ (associativity and commutativity of addition of real numbers, applied coordinatewise). The multiplication map $\mathbb{R}^n \times \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$, $(x, y) \mapsto x + y$, has components $x_j + y_j$, polynomials of degree one in the $2n$ coordinates, hence is smooth (Fact P); the inversion $x \mapsto -x$ has components $-x_j$, linear, hence smooth. So $(\mathbb{R}^n, +)$ is a Lie group of dimension $n$. It is also a closed matrix group: the map $x \mapsto \begin{pmatrix} 1_n & x \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \in GL(n+1; \mathbb{R})$ (with $x$ as a column) satisfies $\begin{pmatrix} 1_n & x \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1_n & y \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1_n & x + y \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (block multiplication), so it is an injective group homomorphism, and its image $\{A : A_{jj} = 1, A_{jk} = 0 \text{ for } j \neq k \text{ except } k = n+1\}$ is cut out by equations in continuous functions, hence closed.

**Is an instance — the torus $T^n = U(1)^n$.** By Step B.4, $U(1) = \{a \in \mathbb{C} : |a| = 1\}$ is a Lie group; by Block D applied $n - 1$ times (the product of Lie groups is again a Lie group, so the construction iterates), $T^n := U(1) \times \cdots \times U(1)$ ($n$ factors) is a Lie group of dimension $n$, with the componentwise operations $(a_1, \dots, a_n)(b_1, \dots, b_n) = (a_1 b_1, \dots, a_n b_n)$. It is abelian because $\mathbb{C}$ is commutative, and as a set it is the product of $n$ circles, $S^1 \times \cdots \times S^1 \subset \mathbb{C}^n$. The exercise [[Ex - Products of Lie Groups and the Torus]] shows that $x \mapsto (e^{2\pi i x_1}, \dots, e^{2\pi i x_n})$ is a surjective Lie group homomorphism $\mathbb{R}^n \to T^n$ with kernel $\mathbb{Z}^n$. The torus is the structure group of a direct sum of $n$ Hermitian line bundles and, in chapter III, of the reductions of $U(n)$-bundles to their maximal torus.

**Is an instance — the matrix form of $SU(2)$.** Every matrix of the form $A = \begin{pmatrix} z & w \\ -\bar{w} & \bar{z} \end{pmatrix}$ with $z, w \in \mathbb{C}$ and $|z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1$ lies in $SU(2)$. Verification: $A^* = \begin{pmatrix} \bar{z} & -w \\ \bar{w} & z \end{pmatrix}$ (conjugate every entry, then transpose), so
$$A^* A = \begin{pmatrix} \bar{z} z + w \bar{w} & \bar{z} w - w \bar{z} \\ \bar{w} z - z \bar{w} & \bar{w} w + z \bar{z} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} |z|^2 + |w|^2 & 0 \\ 0 & |z|^2 + |w|^2 \end{pmatrix} = 1_2 \qquad \text{(matrix multiplication; } \bar{z} w = w \bar{z} \text{ as } \mathbb{C} \text{ is commutative; } |z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1 \text{)},$$
and $\det A = z \bar{z} - (w)(-\bar{w}) = |z|^2 + |w|^2 = 1$ (the $2 \times 2$ determinant formula). So $A \in U(2) \cap SL(2; \mathbb{C}) = SU(2)$. The converse — that every element of $SU(2)$ has this form, so that $SU(2) \to Sp(1)$, $A \mapsto z + wj$, is an isomorphism of Lie groups onto the unit quaternions — is proved on [[Ex - SU(2) is the Group of Unit Quaternions]], and the diffeomorphism $SU(2) \cong S^3$ on [[Ex - SU(2) is Diffeomorphic to S^3]].

**Is NOT an instance of a closed subgroup — $H_{\mathbb{Q}} = \{A \in GL(2; \mathbb{R}) : \det A \in \mathbb{Q}\}$.** *It is a subgroup:* $\det 1_2 = 1 \in \mathbb{Q}$; for $A, B \in H_{\mathbb{Q}}$, $\det(AB) = \det A \cdot \det B \in \mathbb{Q}$ (multiplicativity; $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under multiplication); for $A \in H_{\mathbb{Q}}$, $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det A \in \mathbb{Q}$ (corollary of multiplicativity; $\det A$ is a nonzero rational, and the reciprocal of a nonzero rational is rational). *It is not closed in $GL(2; \mathbb{R})$:* choose rationals $q_m$ with $q_m \to \sqrt{2}$ (for instance the decimal truncations $q_m = \lfloor 10^m \sqrt{2} \rfloor / 10^m$, which satisfy $|q_m - \sqrt{2}| \le 10^{-m}$) and put $A_m = \operatorname{diag}(q_m, 1)$. Then $\det A_m = q_m \in \mathbb{Q} \setminus \{0\}$ for $m \ge 1$, so $A_m \in H_{\mathbb{Q}}$; and $A_m \to A_\infty := \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{2}, 1)$ in $\operatorname{Mat}(2 \times 2; \mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}^4$, since the entries converge. The limit lies in $GL(2; \mathbb{R})$, because $\det A_\infty = \sqrt{2} \neq 0$, but not in $H_{\mathbb{Q}}$, because $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational (if $\sqrt{2} = p/q$ in lowest terms then $p^2 = 2q^2$, so $p$ is even, $p = 2p'$, so $q^2 = 2p'^2$ and $q$ is even, contradicting lowest terms). A closed subset contains the limits of its convergent sequences (in a metric space, closedness is equivalent to sequential closedness), so $H_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is not closed in $GL(2; \mathbb{R})$. In fact $H_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is dense in $GL(2; \mathbb{R})$: given any $A \in GL(2; \mathbb{R})$, choose nonzero rationals $t_m \to \det A$ and put $B_m := \operatorname{diag}(t_m / \det A, 1)\, A$; then $\det B_m = (t_m / \det A) \det A = t_m \in \mathbb{Q}$ (multiplicativity), so $B_m \in H_{\mathbb{Q}}$, and $B_m \to \operatorname{diag}(1, 1) A = A$ because the entries of $B_m$ are the entries of $A$ multiplied by $t_m/\det A \to 1$ or by $1$. Hence every element of $GL(2; \mathbb{R})$ is a limit of elements of $H_{\mathbb{Q}}$, and a proper dense subset is as far from closed as a subset can be. The hypothesis of the closed subgroup theorem therefore fails for $H_{\mathbb{Q}}$, and the theorem's converse (an embedded Lie subgroup is closed) shows that $H_{\mathbb{Q}}$ is not an embedded Lie subgroup of $GL(2; \mathbb{R})$. This is the example that shows the closedness clause cannot be dropped.

**Corollary — $O(n)$, $SO(n)$, $U(n)$, $SU(n)$, and $Sp(1)$ are compact.** By the [[Thm - Heine–Borel Theorem|Heine–Borel theorem]] — a subset of $\mathbb{R}^N$ is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded — it suffices to show each group is closed and bounded in the ambient Euclidean space $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K}) = \mathbb{R}^{n^2}$ or $\mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$, respectively $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}^4$. *Closed in the ambient space:* $O(n) = \{A \in \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R}) : A^t A = 1_n\}$, because (Step B.1 (i)) the equation already forces $\det A \neq 0$; so $O(n)$ is the preimage of the closed set $\{1_n\}$ under the continuous map $A \mapsto A^t A$ defined on all of $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R})$, hence closed there (Fact T). The same argument with Step B.4 (i) shows $U(n)$ is closed in $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C})$; $SO(n)$ and $SU(n)$ are the intersections of these closed sets with the closed sets $\det^{-1}(\{1\})$; and $Sp(1) = (|\cdot|^2)^{-1}(\{1\})$ is closed in $\mathbb{H}$. *Bounded:* for $A \in O(n)$, the $(k,k)$ entry of $A^t A = 1_n$ reads $\sum_{j=1}^n A_{jk}^2 = 1$, so $|A_{jk}| \le 1$ for all $j, k$; for $A \in U(n)$, the $(k,k)$ entry of $A^* A = 1_n$ reads $\sum_{j=1}^n |A_{jk}|^2 = 1$, so $|A_{jk}| \le 1$; for $q \in Sp(1)$, $|q| = 1$. Subsets of these groups are bounded by the same bound. Hence all five groups are closed and bounded, and therefore compact. This compactness is what chapters VI and VII use to average inner products over the group and to make the Yang–Mills functional nonnegative; $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ and $SL(n; \mathbb{K})$ for $n \ge 2$ are not compact, since $\operatorname{diag}(t, 1/t, 1, \dots, 1) \in SL(n; \mathbb{K})$ has unbounded entries as $t \to \infty$.

**Corollary — $SO(n)$ is a normal subgroup of $O(n)$ of index at most two.** By Step B.1 (i), $\det A \in \{+1, -1\}$ for every $A \in O(n)$, so $\det$ restricts to a group homomorphism $O(n) \to \{\pm 1\}$ (multiplicativity), whose kernel is $\{A \in O(n) : \det A = 1\} = O(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{R}) = SO(n)$. The kernel of a homomorphism is a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] (for $A \in SO(n)$ and $B \in O(n)$, $\det(B A B^{-1}) = \det B \det A (\det B)^{-1} = \det A = 1$), and the image is a subgroup of $\{\pm 1\}$, so $O(n)/SO(n)$ has order one or two. For $n \ge 1$ the reflection $\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, \dots, 1)$ lies in $O(n)$ (its transpose is itself and its square is $1_n$) and has determinant $-1$, so the index is exactly two: $O(n)$ is the disjoint union of $SO(n)$ and the coset $\operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, \dots, 1) \cdot SO(n)$. The same argument with $\det : U(n) \to U(1)$ shows that $SU(n)$ is a normal subgroup of $U(n)$, now with quotient the whole circle $U(1)$, since $\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\varphi}, 1, \dots, 1) \in U(n)$ has determinant $e^{i\varphi}$.

**Calibration check.** First, verify from the page that $SO(n) = O(n) \cap SL(n; \mathbb{R})$ is closed in $GL(n; \mathbb{R})$ without invoking Step B.3: $SO(n)$ is the preimage of the closed set $\{(1_n, 1)\} \subset \operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{R}) \times \mathbb{R}$ under the continuous map $A \mapsto (A^t A, \det A)$, whose components are polynomial. Second, verify that $U(1) \subset GL(1; \mathbb{C})$ is the unit circle: a $1 \times 1$ complex matrix is a scalar $a$, $a^* = \bar{a}$, so $a^* a = 1$ reads $|a|^2 = 1$, and the set of solutions is $\{a \in \mathbb{C} : |a| = 1\} = \{e^{i\varphi} : \varphi \in \mathbb{R}\}$, the unit circle in $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^2$. Third, verify that the intersection of $O(n)$ with the diagonal matrices is the group $\{\pm 1\}^n$ of sign changes: a diagonal matrix $D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \dots, d_n)$ has $D^t D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1^2, \dots, d_n^2)$, which equals $1_n$ if and only if every $d_j = \pm 1$. We deliberately compute no dimensions here: the dimensions $\dim O(n) = n(n-1)/2$, $\dim U(n) = n^2$, $\dim SU(n) = n^2 - 1$, and the identification of the tangent spaces at $1_n$ with the antisymmetric, anti-Hermitian, and traceless anti-Hermitian matrices, belong to §1.2 and are proved on [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]].

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Lie algebras of the classical groups *(from §1.2 of this chapter)*
> Once the classical groups are known to be embedded submanifolds of $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$, their tangent spaces at $1_n$ can be computed inside $\operatorname{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{K})$ by differentiating the defining equations along curves: $\mathfrak{o}(n) = \{X : X^t + X = 0\}$, $\mathfrak{u}(n) = \{X : X^* + X = 0\}$, $\mathfrak{sl}(n; \mathbb{K}) = \{X : \operatorname{tr} X = 0\}$, with the commutator as bracket. This is [[Thm - Lie Algebras and Dimensions of the Classical Matrix Groups]].

> [!tip] $Sp(1) \cong SU(2) \cong S^3$ *(from §1.1 and Haydys §2.4)*
> The unit quaternions and the special unitary group of rank two are the same Lie group, and both are the three-sphere; [[Ex - SU(2) is the Group of Unit Quaternions]] proves the isomorphism and [[Ex - SU(2) is Diffeomorphic to S^3]] the diffeomorphism. This single group is the structure group of the Hopf bundle ([[Def - The Hopf Bundle]]), the fibre of the quaternionic Hopf fibration, and the group whose bundles over four-manifolds carry the instantons of chapter VII.

> [!tip] Structure groups of principal bundles *(from chapter III)*
> A [[Def - Principal G-Bundle|principal $G$-bundle]] needs a Lie group $G$ to act on its fibres, and the reduction of the structure group of a vector bundle from $GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ to $O(n)$, $SO(n)$, $U(n)$, or $SU(n)$ ([[Def - Reduction and Extension of the Structure Group]]) is exactly the choice of a metric, an orientation, a Hermitian metric, or a Hermitian metric together with a trivialisation of the determinant line bundle — the structures $S$ of the Categorical Definition, now varying over the base.

> [!tip] Representations *(from §1.3 of this chapter)*
> The defining inclusion $G \subset GL(n; \mathbb{K})$ of each classical group is a faithful representation on $\mathbb{K}^n$ ([[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]]); tensor, dual, and symmetric powers of it produce the representations classified for $U(1)$ and $SU(2)$ on [[Thm - Complex Representations of U(1) and SU(2)]].
