# Notation

This page depends on the standing conventions of the series: manifolds are smooth, Hausdorff and second countable; "smooth" means $C^\infty$; Lie groups act on manifolds on the left and on the total spaces of bundles on the right. The group $U(1) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$ is abelian, so for it the two sides coincide, and we write the orbit space of its scalar action as $S^{2n-1}/U(1)$ (the bundle-side notation of the series) rather than Bär's $U(1)\backslash S^{2n-1}$.

This is a compound page: it defines three interlocking notions — the complex projective space $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ as the quotient manifold $S^{2n-1}/U(1)$, the homogeneous coordinates $[w_1 : \dots : w_n]$ with their affine charts (which identify the quotient with the set of complex lines in $\mathbb{C}^n$ and with $(\mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\})/\mathbb{C}^*$), and the Hopf map $\mathrm{Hopf} : S^3 \to S^2$ — because the three are one object seen from three sides, and the proofs that they agree are the content of the page.

Throughout, $n \geq 1$ is a fixed integer. We write $\mathbb{C}^n$ for complex $n$-space with coordinates $w = (w_1, \dots, w_n)$ and identify it with $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ by $w_j = x_{2j-1} + i x_{2j}$; the norm is $|w|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^n |w_j|^2 = \sum_{k=1}^{2n} x_k^2$. The unit sphere is $S^{2n-1} = \{w \in \mathbb{C}^n : |w| = 1\}$, and more generally $S^m = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^{m+1} : |x| = 1\}$ with its north pole $N = (0, \dots, 0, 1)$ and south pole $S = (0, \dots, 0, -1)$; $S^m$ carries the smooth structure of the stereographic atlas of [[Ex - The Sphere as a Smooth Manifold via Stereographic Projection]], with charts $\sigma_N(x) = (x^1, \dots, x^m)/(1 - x^{m+1})$ on $S^m \setminus \{N\}$ and $\sigma_S(x) = (x^1, \dots, x^m)/(1 + x^{m+1})$ on $S^m \setminus \{S\}$. The group $U(1)$ is the classical matrix group of [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]] for $n = 1$; concretely it is the unit circle $S^1 \subset \mathbb{C}$, and Lemma A below verifies that with the stereographic structure of $S^1$ it is a Lie group of dimension $1$ (this is the same smooth structure as the closed-subgroup one, because both make $U(1)$ an embedded submanifold of $\mathbb{C}$ and such a structure is unique, [[Def - Embedded Submanifold]]). We write $\mathbb{C}^* = \mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$ for the multiplicative group of nonzero complex numbers and $\hat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C} \cup \{\infty\}$ for the extended complex plane (as a set).

The action is $\theta : U(1) \times S^{2n-1} \to S^{2n-1}$, $\theta(z, w) = z w := (z w_1, \dots, z w_n)$. The orbit of $w$ is $U(1) \cdot w = \{zw : z \in U(1)\}$, written $[w]$; the orbit space is $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1} := S^{2n-1}/U(1)$ with quotient map $\pi : S^{2n-1} \to \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, $\pi(w) = [w]$. For $v \in \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\}$ the homogeneous coordinates $[v_1 : \dots : v_n]$ denote $\pi(v/|v|)$ (Definition 2). For $j \in \{1, \dots, n\}$ we write $W_j = \{w \in S^{2n-1} : w_j \neq 0\}$, $V_j = \pi(W_j) = \{[w] : w_j \neq 0\}$, and $\varphi_j : V_j \to \mathbb{C}^{n-1}$ for the $j$-th affine chart, $\varphi_j([w]) = (w_1/w_j, \dots, \widehat{w_j/w_j}, \dots, w_n/w_j)$, the hat marking the omitted entry; $\iota_j : \mathbb{C}^{n-1} \to \mathbb{C}^n$ inserts a $1$ in the $j$-th slot. The Hopf map is $\mathrm{Hopf} : S^3 \to S^2$ (Definition 3); $\tau^{-1} : \hat{\mathbb{C}} \to S^2$ is Bär's stereographic parametrisation $u \mapsto (4u, 4 - |u|^2)/(4 + |u|^2)$, $\infty \mapsto S$, where a point of $S^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3 = \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{R}$ is written $(\zeta, t)$ with $\zeta \in \mathbb{C}$, $t \in \mathbb{R}$, $|\zeta|^2 + t^2 = 1$; $\eta : S^3 \to S^2$ is the Hopf map of [[Def - The Hopf Map]] (chapter Algebraic Topology III), $\eta(w) = (2 w_1 \bar w_2, |w_1|^2 - |w_2|^2)$; and $F : S^2 \to S^2$, $F(\zeta, t) = (4\zeta, 3 - 5t)/(5 - 3t)$, is the diffeomorphism that converts one into the other (Lemma E). Finally, $\sigma : S^3 \setminus \{p_0\} \to \mathbb{R}^3$ is stereographic projection of $S^3$ from the point $p_0 = (0, 1) \in \mathbb{C}^2$, $\sigma(x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4) = (x_1, x_2, x_4)/(1 - x_3)$ (Proposition 5).

> [!warning] Convention: left versus right quotient notation
> Bär writes the orbit space of any action as $G \backslash M$ and defines $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1} := U(1) \backslash S^{2n-1}$ (his Definition 1.5.13). The series writes $P/G$ for right actions on bundles, and in chapter III the sphere $S^{2n-1}$ becomes the total space of a principal $U(1)$-bundle over $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ with $U(1)$ acting on the right by $w \cdot z := zw$. Since $U(1)$ is abelian, $(w \cdot z) \cdot z' = z' z w = (zz') w = w \cdot (zz')$, so the right-action axiom holds for the very same map, and the two orbit spaces are the same set with the same topology. Conversion recipe: read every $U(1) \backslash S^{2n-1}$ of Bär as $S^{2n-1}/U(1)$.

> [!warning] Convention: two normalisations of the Hopf map
> Bär's Hopf map is $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) = \big(4 w_1 \bar w_2,\ 4|w_2|^2 - |w_1|^2\big)/\big(|w_1|^2 + 4|w_2|^2\big)$, obtained from the ratio $u = w_1/w_2$ through the stereographic parametrisation $\tau^{-1}(u) = (4u, 4 - |u|^2)/(4 + |u|^2)$, which is projection from the south pole onto the plane $\{t = 1\}$ tangent at the north pole. The vault's page [[Def - The Hopf Map]] (and [[Ex - The Hopf Map is a Submersion]]) uses $\eta(w) = (2 w_1 \bar w_2, |w_1|^2 - |w_2|^2)$, obtained from the same ratio through projection from the north pole onto the equatorial plane. Haydys's form of the Hopf map is the quotient map itself, $w \mapsto [w] \in \mathbb{CP}^1$. Lemma E proves that $\mathrm{Hopf} = F \circ \eta$ for the explicit smooth involution $F(\zeta, t) = (4\zeta, 3 - 5t)/(5 - 3t)$ of $S^2$; in particular the two maps have the same fibres — the $U(1)$-orbits — and differ only by a fixed diffeomorphism of the target, which exchanges the poles. Conversion recipe: $\mathrm{Hopf} = F \circ \eta$ and $\eta = F \circ \mathrm{Hopf}$.

The source's Example 1.5.12 contains two misprints, which we correct silently below: "for some $Z \in \mathbb{C}$ with $|z| = 1$" reads "for some $z \in \mathbb{C}$ with $|z| = 1$", and "the complex lines panned by $w, w$ coincide" reads "the complex lines spanned by $w, w'$ coincide".

# Axiom Motivation

What space parametrises the one-dimensional complex subspaces of $\mathbb{C}^n$? The question arises in several forms. In quantum mechanics a state of an $n$-level system is a nonzero vector $\psi \in \mathbb{C}^n$, and two vectors describe the same physical state exactly when they differ by a nonzero complex scalar (normalisation and overall phase are unobservable), so the space of physical states is the space of lines. In algebraic geometry the zero set of a homogeneous polynomial in $n$ variables is a union of lines through the origin, so it is naturally a subset of the space of lines. In this series the space of lines in $\mathbb{C}^2$ is the base of the first non-trivial principal bundle we shall meet, the Hopf bundle of chapter III, and the space of lines in $\mathbb{C}^{n}$ is the base of its higher-dimensional relatives. In every case the set of lines is easy to write down; the difficulty is to make it a smooth manifold in a way that is canonical, that is computable in coordinates, and that produces the maps we need (the quotient map from the sphere, the affine charts, the Hopf map) as smooth maps.

There are three candidate descriptions, and the definition on this page takes the one to which a general theorem applies and then proves that the other two agree with it. The first candidate is the bare set of lines, $\{L \subset \mathbb{C}^n : L \text{ is a complex subspace of dimension } 1\}$. It has no topology until we give it one, and any topology we impose by hand must afterwards be shown to make the natural maps continuous. The second candidate is the orbit space $(\mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\})/\mathbb{C}^*$ of the scalar action of $\mathbb{C}^*$, with the quotient topology, and with charts given by dividing through by a nonzero coordinate; this is the route taken for real projective space in [[Ex - Real Projective Space is a Smooth Manifold]], and it works, but each of the manifold axioms — that the charts are homeomorphisms, that the transition maps are smooth, that the quotient is Hausdorff and second countable — has to be verified by hand, because $\mathbb{C}^*$ is not compact and no general quotient theorem is available without first proving that the action is proper. The third candidate is the orbit space $S^{2n-1}/U(1)$ of the scalar action of the *unit* scalars on the *unit* sphere. Here every hypothesis of the [[Thm - Quotient Manifold Theorem for Free Proper Actions|quotient manifold theorem]] is verified in one line each: the action is smooth, it is free because a unit scalar fixing a nonzero vector is $1$, and it is proper because $U(1)$ is compact. The theorem then delivers, all at once, a smooth structure, its dimension $2n - 2$, the Hausdorff and second countability properties, the fact that $\pi$ is a submersion, smooth local sections, and a universal property that makes every orbit-invariant smooth map descend. This is why the series defines $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ as $S^{2n-1}/U(1)$.

Each clause of the definition is load-bearing. Suppose we drop the restriction to nonzero vectors and try to quotient all of $\mathbb{C}^n$ by $\mathbb{C}^*$. The orbit $\{0\}$ then lies in the closure of every other orbit, because $\lambda v \to 0$ as $\lambda \to 0$, and the quotient topology fails to be Hausdorff: every open set containing the class of $0$ is the whole quotient (this is verified in the non-instance below). Suppose instead we keep the nonzero vectors but quotient by a non-compact group without first proving properness; then the quotient theorem does not apply, and indeed for other non-compact actions the orbit space can be non-Hausdorff — the action of $(\mathbb{Q}, +)$ on $\mathbb{R}$ by translation, treated in this chapter on [[Def - Discrete Group and Properly Discontinuous Action]], has dense orbits and an indiscrete quotient. Suppose we keep a compact group but drop freeness: the rotation action of $U(1)$ on $S^2$ about the $z$-axis has two fixed points, its orbit space is the closed interval $[-1, 1]$ (see [[Ex - Rotation of the Sphere about an Axis]]), which is not a manifold, because the endpoint $1$ has no neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open interval — removing $1$ from any of its neighbourhoods $(1 - \varepsilon, 1]$ leaves a connected set, whereas removing a point from an open interval disconnects it. Freeness is exactly what rules out such exceptional orbits. Suppose finally that we replace $U(1)$ by its subgroup $\{\pm 1\}$: the quotient $S^{2n-1}/\{\pm 1\}$ is the real projective space $\mathbb{RP}^{2n-1}$, a different manifold of dimension $2n - 1$, which parametrises *real* lines in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$; the choice of the full circle of phases is what identifies vectors that span the same *complex* line.

The second definition on the page, the homogeneous coordinates and affine charts, exists because the quotient theorem is an existence theorem: it says that a smooth structure exists and is unique, but it does not hand us coordinates in which to compute. The affine chart $\varphi_j$ records a line by the point where it meets the affine hyperplane $\{w_j = 1\}$, and Proposition 3 proves that each $\varphi_j$ is a diffeomorphism from an open subset of the quotient onto $\mathbb{C}^{n-1}$. This is the statement that "the quotient smooth structure" and "the smooth structure with the affine atlas" are the same structure, and it is what allows the vault's other pages — which define $\mathbb{CP}^1$ by affine charts — to be used here without a translation step.

The third definition, the Hopf map, exists because for $n = 2$ the quotient is a familiar manifold in disguise. Two unit vectors in $\mathbb{C}^2$ span the same line if and only if the ratios $w_1/w_2$ agree in $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$, so the orbit space is in bijection with the extended plane, which stereographic projection identifies with $S^2$. The Hopf map is the composite $S^3 \to \hat{\mathbb{C}} \to S^2$ written out as a single explicit formula. The formula depends on which stereographic projection is used, which is why two normalisations coexist in the vault, and why the Convention callout above reconciles them by an explicit diffeomorphism of $S^2$. What does not depend on the choice is the fibre structure: the fibres of any version of the Hopf map are the $U(1)$-orbits, which are great circles of $S^3$, and Proposition 5 shows what these circles look like after stereographic projection of $S^3$ to $\mathbb{R}^3$ — a decomposition of $\mathbb{R}^3$ into round circles and one straight line. A reader who has followed this paragraph could reconstruct all three definitions: quotient the unit sphere by unit scalars so that the quotient theorem applies; coordinatise by dehomogenising; and for $n = 2$ compose with the ratio and a stereographic projection.

# The Definition

We first record a small lemma about maps into spheres that we shall use repeatedly, then give the three definitions with their verifications.

> [!note]- Lemma A: smooth maps into spheres; $U(1)$ is a Lie group
> **Statement.** Let $m \geq 1$ and give $S^m \subset \mathbb{R}^{m+1}$ the smooth structure of the stereographic atlas $\{(S^m \setminus \{N\}, \sigma_N), (S^m \setminus \{S\}, \sigma_S)\}$ of [[Ex - The Sphere as a Smooth Manifold via Stereographic Projection]], whose chart inverses are $\sigma_N^{-1}(u) = (2u, |u|^2 - 1)/(|u|^2 + 1)$ and $\sigma_S^{-1}(u) = (2u, 1 - |u|^2)/(|u|^2 + 1)$ for $u \in \mathbb{R}^m$.
> (a) The inclusion $S^m \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^{m+1}$ is smooth.
> (b) If $M$ is a smooth manifold and $f : M \to \mathbb{R}^{m+1}$ is smooth with $f(M) \subset S^m$, then $f$ is smooth as a map $M \to S^m$.
> (c) $U(1) = S^1 \subset \mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}^2$, with this smooth structure and with complex multiplication, is a compact Lie group of dimension $1$; and $S^{2n-1} \subset \mathbb{C}^n = \mathbb{R}^{2n}$ is a compact smooth manifold of dimension $2n - 1$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Part (a).** We need to show that the inclusion is smooth in each chart. In the chart $\sigma_N$ the inclusion reads $u \mapsto \sigma_N^{-1}(u) = (2u, |u|^2 - 1)/(|u|^2 + 1)$, each component of which is a quotient of polynomials in $u^1, \dots, u^m$ with denominator $|u|^2 + 1 \geq 1$ (by the definition of $\sigma_N^{-1}$ and since a sum of squares is non-negative); a quotient of polynomials with nowhere-vanishing denominator is smooth on $\mathbb{R}^m$ (by the quotient rule, iterated). The same argument applies to $\sigma_S^{-1}$. Since smoothness of a map is checked chart by chart on a cover of the domain, the inclusion is smooth.
> >
> > **Part (b).** Write $f = (f^1, \dots, f^{m+1})$ with each $f^k : M \to \mathbb{R}$ smooth (components of a smooth map into $\mathbb{R}^{m+1}$ are smooth). Set $M_N := \{p \in M : f^{m+1}(p) \neq 1\}$ and $M_S := \{p \in M : f^{m+1}(p) \neq -1\}$; both are open in $M$ (preimages of the open sets $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$, $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{-1\}$ under the continuous map $f^{m+1}$), and $M = M_N \cup M_S$ (since no point has $f^{m+1}(p) = 1$ and $f^{m+1}(p) = -1$ simultaneously). On $M_N$ we have $f(p) \in S^m \setminus \{N\}$ (because $f(p) \in S^m$ by hypothesis and $f^{m+1}(p) \neq 1$), and
> > $$\sigma_N \circ f = \frac{(f^1, \dots, f^m)}{1 - f^{m+1}} \quad \text{on } M_N \qquad \text{(by the formula for } \sigma_N\text{)},$$
> > a quotient of smooth functions with denominator nowhere zero on $M_N$, hence smooth. Likewise $\sigma_S \circ f = (f^1, \dots, f^m)/(1 + f^{m+1})$ is smooth on $M_S$. Since $\{M_N, M_S\}$ is an open cover of $M$ and $f$ is smooth in a chart of the target on each member, $f : M \to S^m$ is smooth.
> >
> > **Part (c), the sphere.** $S^{2n-1}$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $2n - 1$ by the stereographic atlas (the cited exercise, with $m = 2n - 1$). It is closed in $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ (the preimage of $\{1\}$ under the continuous map $x \mapsto |x|^2$) and bounded (contained in the ball of radius $1$), hence compact by the [[Thm - Heine–Borel Theorem|Heine–Borel theorem]] (a subset of $\mathbb{R}^k$ is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded).
> >
> > **Part (c), the circle.** The same applies to $S^1 \subset \mathbb{R}^2$, so $U(1)$ is a compact smooth manifold of dimension $1$. Complex multiplication $\mu : \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$, $(z, z') \mapsto zz'$, is real-bilinear, hence polynomial in real coordinates, hence smooth; its restriction to $S^1 \times S^1$ is the composite of $\mu$ with the product of the two inclusions $S^1 \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}$, which are smooth by (a), so $\mu|_{S^1 \times S^1}$ is smooth into $\mathbb{C}$; its image lies in $S^1$ (since $|zz'| = |z||z'| = 1$), so by (b) it is smooth into $S^1$. Inversion on $U(1)$ is $z \mapsto z^{-1} = \bar z$ (since $z \bar z = |z|^2 = 1$), the restriction of the real-linear map of complex conjugation, smooth into $\mathbb{C}$ by the same argument and smooth into $S^1$ by (b). The group axioms hold because $U(1)$ is a subgroup of the group $\mathbb{C}^*$ (closed under products and inverses as just computed, and containing $1$). Hence $U(1)$ is a Lie group ([[Def - Lie Group]]): a group that is a smooth manifold with smooth multiplication and inversion. Therefore all three parts hold. $\blacksquare$

## Definition 1: the quotient $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1} = S^{2n-1}/U(1)$

**The action.** Define $\theta : U(1) \times S^{2n-1} \to S^{2n-1}$ by $\theta(z, w) = zw = (zw_1, \dots, zw_n)$. Proposition 1 verifies that this is a smooth, free and proper action.

**The definition.** The **$(n-1)$-dimensional complex projective space** is the orbit space
$$\mathbb{CP}^{n-1} := S^{2n-1}/U(1) = \{[w] : w \in S^{2n-1}\}, \qquad [w] = U(1) \cdot w = \{zw : z \in U(1)\},$$
with the quotient topology induced by $\pi : S^{2n-1} \to \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, $\pi(w) = [w]$ (this is the orbit space of [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions]]), and with the unique smooth structure making $\pi$ a smooth submersion, supplied by the quotient manifold theorem, whose statement we transclude:

![[Thm - Quotient Manifold Theorem for Free Proper Actions#Statement]]

In words: if a Lie group $G$ acts smoothly, freely and properly on a manifold $M$, then $M/G$ with the quotient topology carries a unique smooth manifold structure of dimension $\dim M - \dim G$ for which the quotient map $\pi$ is a smooth submersion; the quotient is Hausdorff and second countable; $\pi$ admits smooth local sections through every point; and every smooth map $f : M \to N$ that is constant on orbits factors uniquely as $f = \tilde f \circ \pi$ with $\tilde f : M/G \to N$ smooth. The hypotheses are verified in Proposition 1, and the conclusions are collected in Proposition 1 as well.

> [!note]- Proposition 1: the hypotheses of the quotient theorem hold, and what follows
> **Statement.** (i) $\theta$ is a well-defined smooth left action of $U(1)$ on $S^{2n-1}$, and $w \cdot z := zw$ is a smooth right action with the same orbits. (ii) The action is free. (iii) The action is proper. (iv) Consequently $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ is a smooth manifold of real dimension $2n - 2$; it is Hausdorff and second countable; $\pi$ is a surjective smooth submersion, an open map, and a topological quotient map; through every point of $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ there is a smooth local section of $\pi$; every smooth map $f : S^{2n-1} \to Q$ into a manifold $Q$ with $f(zw) = f(w)$ for all $z \in U(1)$ factors uniquely as $f = \tilde f \circ \pi$ with $\tilde f$ smooth; and $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ is compact.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Part (i): well-definedness and the action axioms.** Fix $z \in U(1)$ and $w \in S^{2n-1}$. We need to show $zw \in S^{2n-1}$:
> > $$|zw|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^n |z w_j|^2 = \sum_{j=1}^n |z|^2 |w_j|^2 = |z|^2 |w|^2 = 1 \qquad \text{(multiplicativity of the modulus; } |z| = 1 \text{ and } |w| = 1\text{)}.$$
> > The identity axiom holds because $1 \cdot w = (1 \cdot w_1, \dots, 1 \cdot w_n) = w$. The compatibility axiom holds because, for $z, z' \in U(1)$, $z(z'w) = (z(z'w_1), \dots, z(z'w_n)) = ((zz')w_1, \dots, (zz')w_n) = (zz')w$ (associativity of complex multiplication in each coordinate). For the right-action reading, $(w \cdot z) \cdot z' = z'(zw) = (z'z)w = (zz')w = w \cdot (zz')$ (compatibility just shown and commutativity of $U(1)$), and $w \cdot 1 = w$; the orbits $\{zw\}$ are the same sets. This is the conversion of the sibling page [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions]] between left and right actions, which for an abelian group requires no inverse.
> >
> > **Smoothness.** The map $\hat\theta : \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}^n \to \mathbb{C}^n$, $(z, w) \mapsto zw$, is real-bilinear, hence polynomial in the real coordinates, hence smooth. The inclusion $U(1) \times S^{2n-1} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{C}^n$ is the product of two smooth inclusions (Lemma A(a)), hence smooth. Therefore $\theta = \hat\theta \circ (\text{inclusion})$ is smooth as a map into $\mathbb{C}^n$, and its image lies in $S^{2n-1}$ by the computation above, so $\theta$ is smooth as a map into $S^{2n-1}$ by Lemma A(b). Thus $\theta$ is a smooth action in the sense of [[Def - Smooth Action of a Lie Group]].
> >
> > **Part (ii): freeness.** Assume $z \in U(1)$ and $w \in S^{2n-1}$ satisfy $zw = w$. We need to show $z = 1$. Since $|w| = 1$, $w \neq 0$, so some coordinate $w_k$ is nonzero. From $zw = w$ we read off the $k$-th coordinate, $z w_k = w_k$, and dividing by $w_k \neq 0$ gives $z = 1$. Hence the stabiliser of every point is $\{1\}$: the action is free. (This is Bär's Example 1.5.7(2); it is drilled on [[Ex - The Scalar Action of U(1) on Odd Spheres is Free]].)
> >
> > **Part (iii): properness.** By Lemma A(c), $U(1)$ is compact. The sibling page [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions]] proves that every smooth action of a compact Lie group is proper — that is, the map $U(1) \times S^{2n-1} \to S^{2n-1} \times S^{2n-1}$, $(z, w) \mapsto (zw, w)$, has compact preimages of compact sets. We invoke that result with $G = U(1)$: the action $\theta$ is proper.
> >
> > **Part (iv): the conclusions.** By (i)–(iii), the quotient manifold theorem applies with $G = U(1)$ (dimension $1$ by Lemma A(c)) and $M = S^{2n-1}$ (dimension $2n - 1$ by Lemma A(c)). It yields: a unique smooth structure on $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ of dimension $(2n - 1) - 1 = 2n - 2$ making $\pi$ a smooth submersion; the Hausdorff and second countability properties; smooth local sections; and the universal property. Surjectivity of $\pi$ is by definition of the orbit space. Since $\pi$ is a surjective smooth submersion, it is an open map and a topological quotient map by [[Thm - Submersions are Open Maps]] (every smooth submersion is open, and a surjective submersion is a quotient map). Finally, $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1} = \pi(S^{2n-1})$ is the continuous image of the compact space $S^{2n-1}$ (Lemma A(c)), hence compact by Lemma B below. Therefore all the assertions of (iv) hold. $\blacksquare$

The three point-set lemmas that the page uses are proved here in full, since the vault's Topology II pages carrying them are statement-only.

> [!note]- Lemma B: the continuous image of a compact space is compact
> **Statement.** Let $f : X \to Y$ be continuous and $X$ compact. Then $f(X)$, with the subspace topology from $Y$, is compact.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}$ be an open cover of $f(X)$ in the subspace topology. We need to show that finitely many $U_\alpha$ already cover $f(X)$. **Lift the cover.** By definition of the subspace topology, $U_\alpha = f(X) \cap O_\alpha$ for some open $O_\alpha \subset Y$. Each $f^{-1}(O_\alpha)$ is open in $X$ (continuity of $f$), and $\bigcup_\alpha f^{-1}(O_\alpha) = f^{-1}(\bigcup_\alpha O_\alpha) \supset f^{-1}(f(X)) = X$ (since the $U_\alpha$ cover $f(X)$), so $\{f^{-1}(O_\alpha)\}$ is an open cover of $X$. **Extract and push forward.** By compactness of $X$ there are $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r$ with $X = \bigcup_{i=1}^r f^{-1}(O_{\alpha_i})$. Then for every $y = f(x) \in f(X)$, $x \in f^{-1}(O_{\alpha_i})$ for some $i$, so $y \in O_{\alpha_i} \cap f(X) = U_{\alpha_i}$. Hence $\{U_{\alpha_1}, \dots, U_{\alpha_r}\}$ covers $f(X)$. Therefore $f(X)$ is compact. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma C: a compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed
> **Statement.** Let $Y$ be Hausdorff and $A \subset Y$ compact. Then $A$ is closed in $Y$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We need to show that $Y \setminus A$ is open. Fix $y \in Y \setminus A$. **Separate $y$ from each point of $A$.** For each $a \in A$ we have $a \neq y$, so by the Hausdorff property there are disjoint open sets $U_a \ni a$ and $W_a \ni y$. **Extract a finite subcover.** The sets $U_a \cap A$, $a \in A$, form an open cover of $A$ in the subspace topology, so by compactness there are $a_1, \dots, a_r \in A$ with $A \subset \bigcup_{i=1}^r U_{a_i}$. **Intersect the neighbourhoods of $y$.** The set $W := \bigcap_{i=1}^r W_{a_i}$ is open (a finite intersection of open sets) and contains $y$. It is disjoint from $A$: if $b \in W \cap A$, then $b \in U_{a_i}$ for some $i$ (since the $U_{a_i}$ cover $A$) and $b \in W \subset W_{a_i}$, contradicting $U_{a_i} \cap W_{a_i} = \emptyset$. Hence $W \subset Y \setminus A$ is an open neighbourhood of $y$ inside $Y \setminus A$. As $y$ was arbitrary, $Y \setminus A$ is open, and $A$ is closed. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma D: a continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism
> **Statement.** Let $f : X \to Y$ be a continuous bijection with $X$ compact and $Y$ Hausdorff. Then $f^{-1} : Y \to X$ is continuous, so $f$ is a homeomorphism.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We need to show that for every closed $C \subset X$ the set $(f^{-1})^{-1}(C) = f(C)$ is closed in $Y$; this is the closed-set criterion for continuity of $f^{-1}$, and the equality $(f^{-1})^{-1}(C) = f(C)$ holds because $f$ is a bijection. **$C$ is compact.** By [[Thm - Closed Subset of Compact is Compact]] — a closed subset of a compact space is compact; the proof adjoins $X \setminus C$ to any open cover of $C$, extracts a finite subcover of $X$, and discards $X \setminus C$ — the set $C$ is compact. **$f(C)$ is compact.** By Lemma B applied to $f|_C$, $f(C)$ is compact. **$f(C)$ is closed.** By Lemma C, since $Y$ is Hausdorff, $f(C)$ is closed. Therefore $f^{-1}$ is continuous and $f$ is a homeomorphism. $\blacksquare$

**The smallest cases.** For $n = 1$ the sphere is $S^1 = U(1)$ itself and the action is left multiplication, which is transitive (given $w, w' \in U(1)$, $z := w' w^{-1} \in U(1)$ satisfies $zw = w'$); hence there is exactly one orbit, $\mathbb{CP}^0$ is a single point, and its dimension $2 \cdot 1 - 2 = 0$ agrees. For $n = 2$ the quotient $\mathbb{CP}^1 = S^3/U(1)$ is a compact surface, which the next page, [[Thm - CP^1 is Diffeomorphic to S^2 via the Hopf Map]], identifies with $S^2$; Definition 3 below constructs the map that does so.

## Definition 2: homogeneous coordinates and the affine charts

**The definition.** For $v \in \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\}$ the **homogeneous coordinates** of the line through $v$ are
$$[v_1 : \dots : v_n] := \pi\!\left(\frac{v}{|v|}\right) \in \mathbb{CP}^{n-1};$$
Proposition 2 shows that $[v_1 : \dots : v_n] = [\lambda v_1 : \dots : \lambda v_n]$ for every $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}^*$, that $[w_1 : \dots : w_n] = [w]$ for $w \in S^{2n-1}$, and that $[v] \mapsto \mathbb{C} v$ is a bijection from $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ onto the set of one-dimensional complex subspaces of $\mathbb{C}^n$. For $j \in \{1, \dots, n\}$ the **$j$-th affine chart** is
$$V_j := \{[w] \in \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} : w_j \neq 0\}, \qquad \varphi_j : V_j \to \mathbb{C}^{n-1}, \quad \varphi_j([w]) := \left(\frac{w_1}{w_j}, \dots, \widehat{\frac{w_j}{w_j}}, \dots, \frac{w_n}{w_j}\right),$$
and its inverse is $\psi_j : \mathbb{C}^{n-1} \to V_j$, $\psi_j(u) := [\iota_j(u)] = [u_1 : \dots : u_{j-1} : 1 : u_j : \dots : u_{n-1}]$. Proposition 3 shows that the $V_j$ are open, that they cover $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, that each $\varphi_j$ is a diffeomorphism onto $\mathbb{C}^{n-1} = \mathbb{R}^{2n-2}$, and that $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ is homeomorphic to $(\mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\})/\mathbb{C}^*$ with its quotient topology, the affine charts corresponding to the dehomogenisation maps there. Thus the quotient smooth structure of Definition 1 is the smooth structure with the atlas $\{(V_j, \varphi_j)\}_{j=1}^n$, which is how [[Def - The Hopf Map]] and [[Ex - The Hopf Map is a Submersion]] describe $\mathbb{CP}^1$ and how [[Ex - Real Projective Space is a Smooth Manifold]] describes the real analogue.

> [!note]- Proposition 2: orbits are complex lines
> **Statement.** (i) For $w, w' \in S^{2n-1}$: $[w] = [w']$ if and only if $w' = zw$ for some $z \in U(1)$, if and only if $w$ and $w'$ are linearly dependent over $\mathbb{C}$, if and only if $\mathbb{C} w = \mathbb{C} w'$. (ii) For $v \in \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\}$ and $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}^*$, $[\lambda v_1 : \dots : \lambda v_n] = [v_1 : \dots : v_n]$, and for $w \in S^{2n-1}$, $[w_1 : \dots : w_n] = [w]$. (iii) The map $\Lambda : \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \to \{L \subset \mathbb{C}^n : L \text{ a complex subspace},\ \dim_{\mathbb{C}} L = 1\}$, $\Lambda([w]) := \mathbb{C} w$, is well defined and bijective. (iv) For $n = 2$: $[w] = [w']$ if and only if $w_1/w_2 = w_1'/w_2'$ in $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$, where a ratio with zero denominator is read as $\infty$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Part (i), first equivalence.** $[w] = [w']$ means $U(1) \cdot w = U(1) \cdot w'$ as sets. If $w' = zw$ with $z \in U(1)$, then for every $z' \in U(1)$, $z'w' = (z'z)w \in U(1) \cdot w$ and $z'w = (z'z^{-1})w' \in U(1) \cdot w'$, so the orbits coincide. Conversely if the orbits coincide then $w' = 1 \cdot w' \in U(1) \cdot w$, so $w' = zw$ for some $z \in U(1)$.
> >
> > **Part (i), second equivalence.** If $w' = zw$ with $z \in U(1)$, then $w' - zw = 0$ is a non-trivial linear relation (the coefficient of $w'$ is $1$), so $w, w'$ are linearly dependent. Conversely, assume $w, w'$ are linearly dependent: $\alpha w + \beta w' = 0$ with $(\alpha, \beta) \neq (0, 0)$. If $\beta = 0$ then $\alpha \neq 0$ and $\alpha w = 0$ forces $w = 0$, contradicting $|w| = 1$; so $\beta \neq 0$ and $w' = \lambda w$ with $\lambda := -\alpha/\beta$. Taking norms, $1 = |w'| = |\lambda||w| = |\lambda|$ (multiplicativity of the modulus and $|w| = |w'| = 1$), so $\lambda \in U(1)$, and $w' = \lambda w$ is the required relation.
> >
> > **Part (i), third equivalence.** If $w' = \lambda w$ with $\lambda \neq 0$ then $\mathbb{C} w' = \{\mu \lambda w\} = \mathbb{C} w$ (as $\mu \mapsto \mu\lambda$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{C}$). Conversely if $\mathbb{C} w = \mathbb{C} w'$ then $w' \in \mathbb{C} w$, so $w' = \lambda w$ for some $\lambda$, and $\lambda \neq 0$ because $w' \neq 0$; thus $w, w'$ are linearly dependent, and by the second equivalence $[w] = [w']$.
> >
> > **Part (ii).** For $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}^*$ write $\lambda = |\lambda| z$ with $z := \lambda/|\lambda| \in U(1)$. Then
> > $$\frac{\lambda v}{|\lambda v|} = \frac{|\lambda| z v}{|\lambda| |v|} = z \cdot \frac{v}{|v|} \qquad \text{(since } |\lambda v| = |\lambda||v| \text{ and } |\lambda| > 0\text{)},$$
> > so $[\lambda v_1 : \dots : \lambda v_n] = \pi(z \cdot v/|v|) = \pi(v/|v|) = [v_1 : \dots : v_n]$ by Part (i). For $w \in S^{2n-1}$, $w/|w| = w$, so $[w_1 : \dots : w_n] = \pi(w) = [w]$.
> >
> > **Part (iii): well defined.** If $[w] = [w']$ then $\mathbb{C} w = \mathbb{C} w'$ by Part (i), so $\Lambda$ does not depend on the representative; and $\mathbb{C} w$ is a one-dimensional subspace because $w \neq 0$. **Injective.** If $\mathbb{C} w = \mathbb{C} w'$ then $[w] = [w']$ by Part (i). **Surjective.** Let $L$ be a one-dimensional complex subspace and $v \in L \setminus \{0\}$. Then $w := v/|v| \in S^{2n-1} \cap L$ (since $L$ is closed under scalars) and $\mathbb{C} w = L$ (a one-dimensional space is spanned by any of its nonzero vectors), so $\Lambda([w]) = L$.
> >
> > **Part (iv).** Let $n = 2$. Assume first $[w] = [w']$, so $w' = zw$ with $z \in U(1)$ by Part (i). If $w_2 = 0$ then $w_2' = z w_2 = 0$ and both ratios are $\infty$. If $w_2 \neq 0$ then $w_2' = zw_2 \neq 0$ and $w_1'/w_2' = (zw_1)/(zw_2) = w_1/w_2$. Conversely assume the ratios agree. **Case 1: both are $\infty$.** Then $w_2 = w_2' = 0$, so $|w_1| = |w_1'| = 1$ (unit norm), and $z := w_1'/w_1 \in U(1)$ satisfies $zw = (w_1', 0) = w'$. **Case 2: both are finite and equal.** Then $w_2, w_2' \neq 0$ and $w_1' w_2 = w_1 w_2'$. Set $z := w_2'/w_2 \in \mathbb{C}^*$. Then $z w_2 = w_2'$ and $z w_1 = w_1 w_2'/w_2 = w_1'$ (by the cross-multiplied relation), so $w' = zw$; and $1 = |w'|^2 = |z|^2 |w|^2 = |z|^2$, so $z \in U(1)$. The two cases are exhaustive because the ratios are equal, so both are $\infty$ or both are finite. In either case $[w] = [w']$ by Part (i). Therefore all four parts hold. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Proposition 3: the affine charts are diffeomorphisms; identification with $(\mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\})/\mathbb{C}^*$
> **Statement.** (i) Each $V_j$ is a well-defined open subset of $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ with $\pi^{-1}(V_j) = W_j$, and $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1} = \bigcup_{j=1}^n V_j$. (ii) $\varphi_j$ is well defined and is a diffeomorphism $V_j \to \mathbb{C}^{n-1}$ with inverse $\psi_j$. (iii) For $j \neq k$ the transition map is $\varphi_k \circ \varphi_j^{-1}(u) = \tilde\varphi_k(\iota_j(u))$ on $\varphi_j(V_j \cap V_k) = \{u \in \mathbb{C}^{n-1} : (\iota_j(u))_k \neq 0\}$, where $\tilde\varphi_k(v) := (v_l/v_k)_{l \neq k}$; for $n = 2$ this is $u \mapsto 1/u$ on $\mathbb{C}^*$. (iv) Let $P := (\mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\})/\mathbb{C}^*$ be the set of orbits of the scalar action of $\mathbb{C}^*$, with the quotient topology of $q : \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\} \to P$, $q(v) = \mathbb{C}^* v$. The map $\Phi : \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \to P$, $\Phi([w]) := \mathbb{C}^* w$, is a homeomorphism, and $\varphi_j \circ \Phi^{-1}(\mathbb{C}^* v) = (v_l/v_j)_{l \neq j}$ for $v_j \neq 0$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Part (i).** The set $W_j = \{w \in S^{2n-1} : w_j \neq 0\}$ is open in $S^{2n-1}$, being the preimage of the open set $\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}$ under the continuous coordinate function $w \mapsto w_j$ (continuous as the composite of the inclusion with a linear map). It is $U(1)$-invariant: $(zw)_j = z w_j \neq 0$ if and only if $w_j \neq 0$, since $z \neq 0$. Hence the condition "$w_j \neq 0$" depends only on the orbit $[w]$, so $V_j = \{[w] : w_j \neq 0\} = \pi(W_j)$ is well defined, and $\pi^{-1}(V_j) = W_j$ (if $\pi(w) \in \pi(W_j)$ then $w$ lies in the orbit of some element of $W_j$, hence in $W_j$ by invariance). Since $\pi$ is an open map (Proposition 1(iv)), $V_j = \pi(W_j)$ is open. Every $w \in S^{2n-1}$ has some $w_j \neq 0$ (as $|w| = 1$), so $[w] \in V_j$; hence the $V_j$ cover $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$.
> >
> > **Part (ii): $\varphi_j$ is well defined.** Define $\tilde\varphi_j : W_j \to \mathbb{C}^{n-1}$ by $\tilde\varphi_j(w) = (w_l/w_j)_{l \neq j}$. For $z \in U(1)$, $\tilde\varphi_j(zw) = ((zw_l)/(zw_j))_{l \neq j} = (w_l/w_j)_{l \neq j} = \tilde\varphi_j(w)$, so $\tilde\varphi_j$ is constant on orbits, and $\varphi_j([w]) := \tilde\varphi_j(w)$ is independent of the representative.
> >
> > **$\varphi_j$ is smooth.** $\tilde\varphi_j$ is smooth: it is the restriction to the open subset $W_j$ of $S^{2n-1}$ of the composite of the smooth inclusion $S^{2n-1} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}^n$ (Lemma A(a)) with the map $\{v \in \mathbb{C}^n : v_j \neq 0\} \to \mathbb{C}^{n-1}$, $v \mapsto (v_l/v_j)_{l \neq j}$, whose real components are quotients of polynomials with denominator $|v_j|^2 > 0$ (write $v_l/v_j = v_l \bar v_j/|v_j|^2$), hence smooth. Now fix $p \in V_j$. By Proposition 1(iv) there is an open neighbourhood $U$ of $p$ and a smooth local section $s : U \to S^{2n-1}$ with $\pi \circ s = \mathrm{id}_U$; shrinking $U$ we may assume $U \subset V_j$. For $x \in U$ we have $\pi(s(x)) = x \in V_j$, so $s(x) \in \pi^{-1}(V_j) = W_j$ by Part (i), and $\varphi_j(x) = \varphi_j(\pi(s(x))) = \tilde\varphi_j(s(x))$ (by the definition of $\varphi_j$ on the representative $s(x)$). Hence $\varphi_j|_U = \tilde\varphi_j \circ s$ is a composite of smooth maps, hence smooth. Since smoothness is local and $p$ was arbitrary, $\varphi_j$ is smooth on $V_j$.
> >
> > **$\psi_j$ is smooth and lands in $V_j$.** The map $\hat\psi_j : \mathbb{C}^{n-1} \to \mathbb{C}^n$, $u \mapsto \iota_j(u)/\sqrt{1 + |u|^2}$, is smooth (the components of $\iota_j(u)$ are linear, $1 + |u|^2 \geq 1$ is a positive polynomial, and $t \mapsto t^{-1/2}$ is smooth on $(0, \infty)$), and $|\hat\psi_j(u)|^2 = (|u|^2 + 1)/(1 + |u|^2) = 1$ (since $|\iota_j(u)|^2 = |u|^2 + 1^2$), so $\hat\psi_j$ is smooth into $S^{2n-1}$ by Lemma A(b). Its $j$-th coordinate is $1/\sqrt{1 + |u|^2} \neq 0$, so $\hat\psi_j(u) \in W_j$ and $\psi_j = \pi \circ \hat\psi_j$ is a smooth map into $V_j$ (composite of smooth maps; note $\psi_j(u) = [\iota_j(u)]$ by Definition 2, since $\iota_j(u)/|\iota_j(u)| = \hat\psi_j(u)$).
> >
> > **$\varphi_j \circ \psi_j = \mathrm{id}$.** For $u \in \mathbb{C}^{n-1}$,
> > $$\varphi_j(\psi_j(u)) = \tilde\varphi_j(\hat\psi_j(u)) = \left(\frac{(\iota_j(u))_l/\sqrt{1 + |u|^2}}{1/\sqrt{1 + |u|^2}}\right)_{l \neq j} = \big((\iota_j(u))_l\big)_{l \neq j} = u \qquad \text{(definition of } \tilde\varphi_j\text{; the normalising factors cancel; } \iota_j \text{ inserts } 1 \text{ in slot } j\text{)}.$$
> >
> > **$\psi_j \circ \varphi_j = \mathrm{id}$.** For $[w] \in V_j$ let $u := \tilde\varphi_j(w) = (w_l/w_j)_{l \neq j}$, so that $\iota_j(u) = w/w_j$ (its $j$-th entry is $1 = w_j/w_j$). Then $|\iota_j(u)| = |w|/|w_j| = 1/|w_j|$, and
> > $$\hat\psi_j(u) = \frac{\iota_j(u)}{|\iota_j(u)|} = \frac{w/w_j}{1/|w_j|} = \frac{|w_j|}{w_j}\, w = z^{-1} w, \qquad z := \frac{w_j}{|w_j|} \in U(1) \qquad \text{(since } \sqrt{1 + |u|^2} = |\iota_j(u)|\text{)}.$$
> > Hence $\psi_j(\varphi_j([w])) = \pi(z^{-1} w) = [w]$ by Proposition 2(i). Therefore $\varphi_j$ is a bijection $V_j \to \mathbb{C}^{n-1}$ with smooth inverse $\psi_j$: a diffeomorphism ([[Def - Diffeomorphism]]).
> >
> > **Part (iii).** For $u \in \varphi_j(V_j \cap V_k)$ we have $\varphi_j^{-1}(u) = \psi_j(u) = [\iota_j(u)]$, and this lies in $V_k$ if and only if $(\iota_j(u))_k \neq 0$ (the normalising factor being positive). Then $\varphi_k([\iota_j(u)]) = \tilde\varphi_k(\hat\psi_j(u)) = \tilde\varphi_k(\iota_j(u))$, because $\tilde\varphi_k$ is a ratio of coordinates and the positive normalising factor cancels. For $n = 2$, $j = 1$, $k = 2$: $\iota_1(u) = (1, u)$, $V_1 \cap V_2$ corresponds to $u \neq 0$, and $\varphi_2 \circ \varphi_1^{-1}(u) = \tilde\varphi_2(1, u) = 1/u$; by the same computation $\varphi_1 \circ \varphi_2^{-1}(u) = 1/u$ as well. The map $u \mapsto 1/u = \bar u/|u|^2$ is smooth on $\mathbb{C}^*$ (rational with nonvanishing denominator), as it must be, since it is a transition map between two charts of the same smooth structure.
> >
> > **Part (iv): $\Phi$ is well defined and bijective.** If $[w] = [w']$ then $w' = zw$ with $z \in U(1) \subset \mathbb{C}^*$, so $\mathbb{C}^* w = \mathbb{C}^* w'$; thus $\Phi$ is well defined. Injective: if $\mathbb{C}^* w = \mathbb{C}^* w'$ with $w, w' \in S^{2n-1}$, then $w' = \lambda w$ with $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}^*$ and $|\lambda| = 1$ by taking norms, so $[w] = [w']$ by Proposition 2(i). Surjective: for $v \in \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\}$, $\mathbb{C}^* v = \mathbb{C}^*(v/|v|) = \Phi([v/|v|])$ (since $|v| \in \mathbb{C}^*$).
> >
> > **$\Phi$ is continuous.** We have $\Phi \circ \pi = q \circ \mathrm{incl}$ on $S^{2n-1}$, where $\mathrm{incl} : S^{2n-1} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\}$ is the (continuous) inclusion: indeed $\Phi(\pi(w)) = \mathbb{C}^* w = q(w)$. The right-hand side is continuous ($q$ is continuous by the definition of the quotient topology, [[Def - Quotient Topology and Identification Map]]). Since $\pi$ is a topological quotient map (Proposition 1(iv)), the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Quotient|universal property of the quotient]] — a function out of a quotient is continuous if and only if its composite with the quotient map is continuous — gives that $\Phi$ is continuous.
> >
> > **$\Phi^{-1}$ is continuous.** Define $\nu : \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\} \to S^{2n-1}$, $\nu(v) = v/|v|$; it is continuous (a quotient of continuous maps with positive denominator, landing in the subspace $S^{2n-1}$). For $v \in \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\}$, $\Phi([\nu(v)]) = \mathbb{C}^* \nu(v) = \mathbb{C}^* v = q(v)$, so $\Phi^{-1} \circ q = \pi \circ \nu$, which is continuous. Since $q$ is a quotient map by construction, the universal property of the quotient gives that $\Phi^{-1}$ is continuous. Hence $\Phi$ is a homeomorphism.
> >
> > **The charts correspond.** For $v$ with $v_j \neq 0$, $\Phi^{-1}(\mathbb{C}^* v) = [\nu(v)]$ and $\varphi_j([\nu(v)]) = \tilde\varphi_j(v/|v|) = (v_l/v_j)_{l \neq j}$, the positive factor $1/|v|$ cancelling in each ratio. Therefore transporting the smooth structure of $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ to $P$ along $\Phi$ makes the dehomogenisation maps $\mathbb{C}^* v \mapsto (v_l/v_j)_{l \neq j}$ the charts of $P$, exactly as in the affine-chart construction of [[Ex - Real Projective Space is a Smooth Manifold]] with $\mathbb{R}$ replaced by $\mathbb{C}$. $\blacksquare$

**Unpacking for $n = 2$.** $\mathbb{CP}^1$ is covered by $V_1 = \{[w] : w_1 \neq 0\}$ with $\varphi_1([w]) = w_2/w_1$ and $V_2 = \{[w] : w_2 \neq 0\}$ with $\varphi_2([w]) = w_1/w_2$; the single point outside $V_1$ is $[0 : 1]$ and the single point outside $V_2$ is $[1 : 0]$; the transition map on $\mathbb{C}^*$ is $u \mapsto 1/u$. This is the Riemann sphere presented as two copies of $\mathbb{C}$ glued along $\mathbb{C}^*$ by inversion, which is why the next definition can identify $\mathbb{CP}^1$ with $S^2$.

## Definition 3: the Hopf map $S^3 \to S^2$

**The definition.** Let $n = 2$, so $S^3 = \{(w_1, w_2) \in \mathbb{C}^2 : |w_1|^2 + |w_2|^2 = 1\}$, and write points of $S^2 \subset \mathbb{C} \times \mathbb{R}$ as $(\zeta, t)$. The **Hopf map** (in Bär's normalisation) is
$$\mathrm{Hopf} : S^3 \to S^2, \qquad \mathrm{Hopf}(w_1, w_2) := \frac{1}{|w_1|^2 + 4|w_2|^2}\Big(4 w_1 \bar w_2,\ 4|w_2|^2 - |w_1|^2\Big).$$
It arises as follows. By Proposition 2(iv) the orbit of $w$ is determined by the ratio $u = w_1/w_2 \in \hat{\mathbb{C}}$, so the orbit space is in bijection with $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$; the stereographic parametrisation
$$\tau^{-1} : \hat{\mathbb{C}} \to S^2, \qquad \tau^{-1}(u) := \frac{1}{4 + |u|^2}\big(4u,\ 4 - |u|^2\big) \ \ (u \in \mathbb{C}), \qquad \tau^{-1}(\infty) := S = (0, -1),$$
identifies $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$ with $S^2$ (it is the inverse of projection from the south pole $S$ onto the plane $\{t = 1\}$: for $(\zeta, t) \in S^2 \setminus \{S\}$ the inverse is $\tau(\zeta, t) = 2\zeta/(1 + t)$); and substituting $u = w_1/w_2$ into $\tau^{-1}$ gives the displayed formula. Proposition 4 verifies all of this. In Haydys's form the Hopf map is simply the quotient map $\pi : S^3 \to \mathbb{CP}^1$, $w \mapsto [w]$, and the Convention callout in the Notation section, made precise in Lemma E, relates $\mathrm{Hopf}$ to the vault's $\eta$.

> [!note]- Proposition 4: the Hopf map is well defined and smooth, and its fibres are the $U(1)$-orbits
> **Statement.** (i) The denominator $D(w) := |w_1|^2 + 4|w_2|^2$ satisfies $D(w) \geq 1$ on $S^3$, and $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) \in S^2$ for every $w \in S^3$. (ii) $\mathrm{Hopf}$ is smooth. (iii) $\tau^{-1} : \hat{\mathbb{C}} \to S^2$ is a bijection with $\tau \circ \tau^{-1} = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{C}}$ and $\tau^{-1} \circ \tau = \mathrm{id}$ on $S^2 \setminus \{S\}$, and $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) = \tau^{-1}(w_1/w_2)$ with $w_1/w_2 := \infty$ when $w_2 = 0$. (iv) For $w, w' \in S^3$: $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) = \mathrm{Hopf}(w')$ if and only if $[w] = [w']$; that is, $\mathrm{Hopf}^{-1}(\mathrm{Hopf}(w)) = U(1) \cdot w$. (v) $\mathrm{Hopf}$ is surjective and descends to a smooth bijection $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}} : \mathbb{CP}^1 \to S^2$ with $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}} \circ \pi = \mathrm{Hopf}$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Part (i).** For $w \in S^3$, $D(w) = |w_1|^2 + 4|w_2|^2 \geq |w_1|^2 + |w_2|^2 = 1$ (since $4|w_2|^2 \geq |w_2|^2$). We compute the squared norm of the image:
> > $$|4 w_1 \bar w_2|^2 + \big(4|w_2|^2 - |w_1|^2\big)^2 = 16|w_1|^2|w_2|^2 + 16|w_2|^4 - 8|w_1|^2|w_2|^2 + |w_1|^4 \qquad \text{(expand; } |\bar w_2| = |w_2|\text{)}$$
> > $$= 16|w_2|^4 + 8|w_1|^2|w_2|^2 + |w_1|^4 = \big(4|w_2|^2 + |w_1|^2\big)^2 = D(w)^2 \qquad \text{(collect; recognise the square)}.$$
> > Dividing by $D(w)^2$ shows $|\mathrm{Hopf}(w)|^2 = 1$, so $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) \in S^2$.
> >
> > **Part (ii).** In the real coordinates $w_1 = x_1 + ix_2$, $w_2 = x_3 + ix_4$, the three real components of $(4w_1\bar w_2, 4|w_2|^2 - |w_1|^2)$ are the polynomials $4(x_1x_3 + x_2x_4)$, $4(x_2x_3 - x_1x_4)$ and $4(x_3^2 + x_4^2) - (x_1^2 + x_2^2)$ (expand $w_1\bar w_2 = (x_1 + ix_2)(x_3 - ix_4)$), and $D = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + 4(x_3^2 + x_4^2)$ is a polynomial vanishing only at $x = 0$. Hence the formula defines a smooth map $\mathbb{R}^4 \setminus \{0\} \to \mathbb{R}^3$ (rational, nonvanishing denominator), whose composite with the smooth inclusion $S^3 \hookrightarrow \mathbb{R}^4 \setminus \{0\}$ (Lemma A(a)) is smooth into $\mathbb{R}^3$, with image in $S^2$ by Part (i); by Lemma A(b) it is smooth into $S^2$.
> >
> > **Part (iii): $\tau^{-1}$ lands in $S^2 \setminus \{S\}$ on $\mathbb{C}$.** For $u \in \mathbb{C}$, $|4u|^2 + (4 - |u|^2)^2 = 16|u|^2 + 16 - 8|u|^2 + |u|^4 = (4 + |u|^2)^2$, so $\tau^{-1}(u) \in S^2$; its last coordinate $(4 - |u|^2)/(4 + |u|^2) > -1$ (since $4 - |u|^2 > -4 - |u|^2$), so $\tau^{-1}(u) \neq S$. **$\tau \circ \tau^{-1} = \mathrm{id}$.** With $(\zeta, t) = \tau^{-1}(u)$ we have $1 + t = (4 + |u|^2 + 4 - |u|^2)/(4 + |u|^2) = 8/(4 + |u|^2)$, hence $\tau(\zeta, t) = 2\zeta/(1 + t) = 2 \cdot \frac{4u}{4 + |u|^2} \cdot \frac{4 + |u|^2}{8} = u$. In particular $\tau^{-1}$ is injective on $\mathbb{C}$. **$\tau^{-1} \circ \tau = \mathrm{id}$ on $S^2 \setminus \{S\}$.** For $(\zeta, t) \in S^2$ with $t \neq -1$ put $u = 2\zeta/(1 + t)$. Then $|u|^2 = 4|\zeta|^2/(1 + t)^2 = 4(1 - t^2)/(1 + t)^2 = 4(1 - t)/(1 + t)$ (using $|\zeta|^2 = 1 - t^2$), so $4 + |u|^2 = 4 \cdot \frac{(1 + t) + (1 - t)}{1 + t} = \frac{8}{1 + t}$ and $4 - |u|^2 = 4 \cdot \frac{(1 + t) - (1 - t)}{1 + t} = \frac{8t}{1 + t}$; therefore $\tau^{-1}(u) = \frac{1 + t}{8}\big(\frac{8\zeta}{1 + t}, \frac{8t}{1 + t}\big) = (\zeta, t)$. Hence $\tau^{-1}$ maps $\mathbb{C}$ bijectively onto $S^2 \setminus \{S\}$, and with $\tau^{-1}(\infty) = S$ it is a bijection $\hat{\mathbb{C}} \to S^2$. **The ratio formula.** If $w_2 \neq 0$, put $u = w_1/w_2$; then $|u|^2 = |w_1|^2/|w_2|^2$, so
> > $$\tau^{-1}(u) = \frac{|w_2|^2}{4|w_2|^2 + |w_1|^2}\left(\frac{4w_1}{w_2},\ \frac{4|w_2|^2 - |w_1|^2}{|w_2|^2}\right) = \frac{1}{4|w_2|^2 + |w_1|^2}\big(4 w_1 \bar w_2,\ 4|w_2|^2 - |w_1|^2\big) = \mathrm{Hopf}(w) \qquad \text{(multiply numerator and denominator by } |w_2|^2\text{; } w_2^{-1}|w_2|^2 = \bar w_2\text{)}.$$
> > If $w_2 = 0$ then $|w_1| = 1$ and $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) = (0, -1)/1 = S = \tau^{-1}(\infty)$. This is Bär's computation on p. 24, written out.
> >
> > **Part (iv).** By Part (iii), $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) = \mathrm{Hopf}(w')$ if and only if $\tau^{-1}(w_1/w_2) = \tau^{-1}(w_1'/w_2')$, if and only if $w_1/w_2 = w_1'/w_2'$ in $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$ (injectivity of $\tau^{-1}$ on $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$), if and only if $[w] = [w']$ (Proposition 2(iv)). Hence the fibre of $\mathrm{Hopf}$ through $w$ is exactly the orbit $U(1) \cdot w$.
> >
> > **Part (v).** Surjectivity: given $(\zeta, t) \in S^2$, if $t \neq -1$ let $u = \tau(\zeta, t)$ and $w = (u, 1)/\sqrt{1 + |u|^2} \in S^3$; then $w_1/w_2 = u$ and $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) = \tau^{-1}(u) = (\zeta, t)$ by Part (iii); if $(\zeta, t) = S$ take $w = (1, 0)$. Descent: $\mathrm{Hopf}$ is smooth (Part (ii)) and constant on orbits (Part (iv)), so by the universal property in Proposition 1(iv) there is a unique smooth $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}} : \mathbb{CP}^1 \to S^2$ with $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}} \circ \pi = \mathrm{Hopf}$. It is surjective because $\mathrm{Hopf}$ is, and injective because $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}}([w]) = \widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}}([w'])$ means $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) = \mathrm{Hopf}(w')$, which forces $[w] = [w']$ by Part (iv). Therefore all five parts hold. $\blacksquare$

That $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}}$ is moreover a diffeomorphism — Bär's Remark 1.5.14 — is the content of the next page, [[Thm - CP^1 is Diffeomorphic to S^2 via the Hopf Map]], which computes the differential of $\mathrm{Hopf}$ in the two affine charts; that computation is not needed for anything on this page.

> [!note]- Lemma E: reconciling the two normalisations, $\mathrm{Hopf} = F \circ \eta$
> **Statement.** Define $F : S^2 \to S^2$ by $F(\zeta, t) := \dfrac{1}{5 - 3t}\big(4\zeta,\ 3 - 5t\big)$. Then (i) $F$ is well defined and smooth, and $F \circ F = \mathrm{id}_{S^2}$, so $F$ is a diffeomorphism with $F^{-1} = F$; $F$ exchanges the poles, $F(N) = S$ and $F(S) = N$. (ii) With $\eta(w) = (2 w_1 \bar w_2, |w_1|^2 - |w_2|^2)$ the Hopf map of [[Def - The Hopf Map]], we have $\mathrm{Hopf} = F \circ \eta$ on $S^3$, and consequently $\eta = F \circ \mathrm{Hopf}$; in particular $\eta$ and $\mathrm{Hopf}$ have the same fibres, the $U(1)$-orbits. (iii) The map $h : S^3 \to \mathbb{CP}^1$, $h(w) = [w]$, of [[Ex - The Hopf Map is a Submersion]] is the quotient map $\pi$, and $\eta = (F \circ \widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}}) \circ h$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Part (i): well defined.** For $(\zeta, t) \in S^2$ we have $t \in [-1, 1]$, so $5 - 3t \geq 2 > 0$, and the formula makes sense; each real component is a quotient of polynomials in $(\zeta, t) \in \mathbb{R}^3$ with denominator nonvanishing on the open set $\{t < 5/3\} \supset S^2$, so $F$ is smooth as a map into $\mathbb{R}^3$ (composite with the smooth inclusion of Lemma A(a)). **Lands in $S^2$.** Using $|\zeta|^2 = 1 - t^2$,
> > $$|4\zeta|^2 + (3 - 5t)^2 = 16(1 - t^2) + 9 - 30t + 25t^2 = 25 - 30t + 9t^2 = (5 - 3t)^2 \qquad \text{(expand and collect)},$$
> > so $|F(\zeta, t)| = 1$ and, by Lemma A(b), $F$ is smooth into $S^2$. **Involution.** Let $(\zeta', t') = F(\zeta, t)$, so $\zeta' = 4\zeta/(5 - 3t)$ and $t' = (3 - 5t)/(5 - 3t)$. Then
> > $$5 - 3t' = \frac{5(5 - 3t) - 3(3 - 5t)}{5 - 3t} = \frac{16}{5 - 3t}, \qquad 3 - 5t' = \frac{3(5 - 3t) - 5(3 - 5t)}{5 - 3t} = \frac{16t}{5 - 3t} \qquad \text{(common denominators)},$$
> > hence $F(\zeta', t') = \frac{5 - 3t}{16}\big(\frac{16\zeta}{5 - 3t}, \frac{16t}{5 - 3t}\big) = (\zeta, t)$. So $F \circ F = \mathrm{id}$, $F$ is bijective with inverse $F$, and both $F$ and $F^{-1}$ are smooth: $F$ is a diffeomorphism. **Poles.** $F(0, 1) = (0, -2)/2 = (0, -1) = S$ and $F(0, -1) = (0, 8)/8 = N$.
> >
> > **Part (ii).** Fix $w \in S^3$ and put $(\zeta, t) := \eta(w) = (2w_1\bar w_2, |w_1|^2 - |w_2|^2)$; this lies in $S^2$ because $4|w_1|^2|w_2|^2 + (|w_1|^2 - |w_2|^2)^2 = (|w_1|^2 + |w_2|^2)^2 = 1$. Using $|w_1|^2 + |w_2|^2 = 1$ to homogenise the constants,
> > $$5 - 3t = 5(|w_1|^2 + |w_2|^2) - 3(|w_1|^2 - |w_2|^2) = 2|w_1|^2 + 8|w_2|^2 = 2D(w) \qquad \text{(since } |w| = 1\text{)},$$
> > $$3 - 5t = 3(|w_1|^2 + |w_2|^2) - 5(|w_1|^2 - |w_2|^2) = 8|w_2|^2 - 2|w_1|^2 = 2\big(4|w_2|^2 - |w_1|^2\big) \qquad \text{(since } |w| = 1\text{)},$$
> > $$4\zeta = 8 w_1 \bar w_2 = 2 \cdot 4 w_1 \bar w_2 \qquad \text{(definition of } \zeta\text{)}.$$
> > Therefore $F(\eta(w)) = \frac{1}{2D(w)}\big(2 \cdot 4w_1\bar w_2,\ 2(4|w_2|^2 - |w_1|^2)\big) = \mathrm{Hopf}(w)$, the factor $2$ cancelling. Applying $F$ to both sides and using $F \circ F = \mathrm{id}$ gives $\eta = F \circ \mathrm{Hopf}$. Since $F$ is injective, $\eta(w) = \eta(w')$ if and only if $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) = \mathrm{Hopf}(w')$, if and only if $[w] = [w']$ (Proposition 4(iv)); so the fibres of $\eta$ are the $U(1)$-orbits.
> >
> > **Part (iii).** The exercise defines $h(w) = [w : w']$ as the class of $w$ under the scalar action; by Proposition 2(ii) this is $\pi(w)$, so $h = \pi$. Then $\eta = F \circ \mathrm{Hopf} = F \circ \widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}} \circ \pi = (F \circ \widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}}) \circ h$ by Proposition 4(v). Therefore all three parts hold. $\blacksquare$

Geometrically, $F$ is the composite of stereographic projection from the north pole onto the equatorial plane, the dilation $u \mapsto u/2$ of that plane, the inverse of stereographic projection from the south pole onto the plane $\{t = 1\}$, and the reflection $t \mapsto -t$; the reader may verify from the formulas $\sigma_N(\zeta, t) = \zeta/(1 - t)$ and $\tau^{-1}$ that this composite has exactly the formula of $F$, but the proof above uses only the formula.

**A worked point.** Take $v = (1, i) \in \mathbb{C}^2$, so $[1 : i] = \pi(w)$ with $w = (1, i)/\sqrt{2}$. Its affine coordinates are $\varphi_1([1 : i]) = i/1 = i$ and $\varphi_2([1 : i]) = 1/i = -i$, consistent with the transition $u \mapsto 1/u$. For the Hopf map, $|w_1|^2 = |w_2|^2 = 1/2$, so $D(w) = 1/2 + 2 = 5/2$, and $4w_1\bar w_2 = 4 \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt2} \cdot \frac{-i}{\sqrt2} = -2i$, $4|w_2|^2 - |w_1|^2 = 2 - 1/2 = 3/2$; hence $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) = \frac{2}{5}(-2i, 3/2) = (-4i/5, 3/5)$, whose norm is $\sqrt{16/25 + 9/25} = 1$. Through the ratio: $u = w_1/w_2 = 1/i = -i$, $|u|^2 = 1$, $\tau^{-1}(-i) = \frac{1}{5}(-4i, 3)$, the same point. Through $\eta$: $\eta(w) = (2w_1\bar w_2, 0) = (-i, 0)$, and $F(-i, 0) = \frac{1}{5}(-4i, 3)$, again the same point, as Lemma E requires. The whole fibre through $w$ is $\{(e^{i\vartheta}, ie^{i\vartheta})/\sqrt2 : \vartheta \in \mathbb{R}\}$, a great circle of $S^3$, all of whose points have the same ratio $-i$.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

**As a coequaliser.** The universal property in Proposition 1(iv) characterises $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ up to unique diffeomorphism: it is the *coequaliser*, in the category of smooth manifolds, of the pair of maps $\theta, \mathrm{pr}_2 : U(1) \times S^{2n-1} \rightrightarrows S^{2n-1}$. Explicitly, $\pi \circ \theta = \pi \circ \mathrm{pr}_2$ (both send $(z, w)$ to $[zw] = [w]$), and every smooth $f : S^{2n-1} \to Q$ with $f \circ \theta = f \circ \mathrm{pr}_2$ — that is, $f(zw) = f(w)$ — factors uniquely through $\pi$. We verify that this determines the manifold. Suppose $\pi' : S^{2n-1} \to Q'$ is another smooth map, constant on orbits, with the same universal property. Applying the property of $\pi$ to $\pi'$ gives a smooth $a : \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \to Q'$ with $a \circ \pi = \pi'$; applying the property of $\pi'$ to $\pi$ gives a smooth $b : Q' \to \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ with $b \circ \pi' = \pi$. Then $(b \circ a) \circ \pi = b \circ \pi' = \pi = \mathrm{id} \circ \pi$, and by the *uniqueness* clause of the property of $\pi$ (applied to the orbit-invariant map $\pi$ itself), $b \circ a = \mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}}$; symmetrically $a \circ b = \mathrm{id}_{Q'}$. Hence $a$ is a diffeomorphism, and it is the unique one compatible with the two quotient maps. This is the precise sense in which Definition 1 is canonical.

**As a homogeneous space.** $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ is also the orbit space of a transitive action of the unitary group $U(n)$, with stabiliser $U(1) \times U(n-1)$; we prove the set-level statement here and record how the smooth statement follows from the vault's orbit–stabiliser theorem.

> [!note]- Proposition 6: $U(n)$ acts smoothly and transitively on $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ with stabiliser $U(1) \times U(n-1)$
> **Statement.** (i) The map $U(n) \times S^{2n-1} \to S^{2n-1}$, $(A, w) \mapsto Aw$, is a smooth transitive action. (ii) It descends to a smooth transitive action $U(n) \times \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \to \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, $A \cdot [w] := [Aw]$. (iii) The stabiliser of $[e_1]$ is $\{\operatorname{diag}(z, B) : z \in U(1),\ B \in U(n-1)\} \cong U(1) \times U(n-1)$. (iv) Consequently, by [[Thm - Orbit-Stabilizer for Lie Group Actions]], $U(n)/(U(1) \times U(n-1)) \to \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, $A \cdot \mathrm{Stab} \mapsto [Ae_1]$, is a $U(n)$-equivariant diffeomorphism.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Part (i): action and smoothness.** $U(n) = \{A \in \mathrm{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C}) : A^*A = 1\}$ is a Lie group by [[Def - Classical Matrix Groups]] (a closed subgroup of $GL(n; \mathbb{C})$), with inclusion into $\mathrm{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C}) = \mathbb{R}^{2n^2}$ smooth. For $A \in U(n)$ and $w \in S^{2n-1}$, $|Aw|^2 = (Aw)^*(Aw) = w^* A^* A w = w^* w = 1$, so $Aw \in S^{2n-1}$; the identity and compatibility axioms are $1 \cdot w = w$ and $A(Bw) = (AB)w$ (matrix multiplication). The map $(A, w) \mapsto Aw$ is real-bilinear on $\mathrm{Mat}(n \times n; \mathbb{C}) \times \mathbb{C}^n$, hence smooth, and composing with the smooth inclusions and applying Lemma A(b) shows the action is smooth into $S^{2n-1}$. **Transitivity.** Fix $w \in S^{2n-1}$; we construct $A \in U(n)$ with $Ae_1 = w$. Since $w \neq 0$, some $w_k \neq 0$, and the list $(w, e_1, \dots, \widehat{e_k}, \dots, e_n)$ of $n$ vectors spans $\mathbb{C}^n$, because $e_k = w_k^{-1}\big(w - \sum_{l \neq k} w_l e_l\big)$ lies in its span and the $e_l$ span; a spanning list of $n$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space is a basis. Apply the [[Thm - Gram-Schmidt Procedure|Gram–Schmidt procedure]] to this basis, in this order: it produces an orthonormal basis $(f_1, \dots, f_n)$ with $f_1 = w/|w| = w$ (the first output vector is the normalised first input vector, and $|w| = 1$). Let $A$ be the matrix with columns $f_1, \dots, f_n$. Then $(A^*A)_{kl} = f_k^* f_l = \delta_{kl}$ (orthonormality), so $A \in U(n)$, and $Ae_1 = f_1 = w$. Given $w, w' \in S^{2n-1}$, choose $A, A'$ with $Ae_1 = w$, $A'e_1 = w'$; then $A'A^{-1} w = A' e_1 = w'$ (using $A^{-1} = A^* \in U(n)$). Hence the action is transitive.
> >
> > **Part (ii): descent.** Consider the free, proper, smooth action of $U(1)$ on the manifold $U(n) \times S^{2n-1}$ by $z \cdot (A, w) := (A, zw)$: it is smooth (product of the identity with $\theta$), free (freeness of $\theta$, Proposition 1(ii)), and proper ($U(1)$ is compact; the compact-group result of [[Def - Free, Transitive, Effective, and Proper Group Actions]] applies). Its orbit space is $U(n) \times \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ as a set, and the map $\mathrm{id} \times \pi : U(n) \times S^{2n-1} \to U(n) \times \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ is a surjective smooth submersion (the product of the identity with the submersion $\pi$; its differential is the block sum of an isomorphism and a surjection) whose fibres are exactly the orbits. By the uniqueness clause of the quotient manifold theorem — the smooth structure making the quotient map a submersion is unique — the product manifold $U(n) \times \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ *is* the quotient manifold of this action, and the theorem's universal property holds for $\mathrm{id} \times \pi$. The map $f : U(n) \times S^{2n-1} \to \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, $f(A, w) := [Aw] = \pi(Aw)$, is smooth (Part (i) composed with $\pi$) and constant on orbits ($A(zw) = z(Aw)$, so $[A(zw)] = [Aw]$ by Proposition 2(i)); hence it factors through a unique smooth map $U(n) \times \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \to \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, which is $(A, [w]) \mapsto [Aw]$. The action axioms descend from Part (i), and transitivity descends from transitivity on $S^{2n-1}$ (given $[w], [w']$, pick $A$ with $Aw = w'$).
> >
> > **Part (iii): the stabiliser.** $A \cdot [e_1] = [e_1]$ means $[Ae_1] = [e_1]$, that is (Proposition 2(i)) $Ae_1 = ze_1$ for some $z \in U(1)$: the first column of $A$ is $ze_1$. Since the columns of a unitary matrix are orthonormal, every other column $f_l$ ($l \geq 2$) satisfies $f_l^* (z e_1) = 0$, that is, its first entry vanishes. Hence $A = \begin{pmatrix} z & 0 \\ 0 & B \end{pmatrix}$ with $B \in \mathrm{Mat}((n-1) \times (n-1); \mathbb{C})$, and $A^*A = \begin{pmatrix} |z|^2 & 0 \\ 0 & B^*B \end{pmatrix} = 1$ forces $B^*B = 1$, so $B \in U(n-1)$. Conversely every $\operatorname{diag}(z, B)$ with $z \in U(1)$, $B \in U(n-1)$ is unitary and sends $e_1$ to $ze_1$, hence fixes $[e_1]$. The map $(z, B) \mapsto \operatorname{diag}(z, B)$ is an injective group homomorphism $U(1) \times U(n-1) \to U(n)$ (block multiplication), so the stabiliser is isomorphic to $U(1) \times U(n-1)$.
> >
> > **Part (iv).** The orbit–stabiliser theorem of the vault states: if a Lie group $G$ acts smoothly and transitively on a smooth manifold $M$ and $p \in M$, then the orbit map $g \mapsto g \cdot p$ descends to a $G$-equivariant diffeomorphism $G/G_p \to M$. By Parts (ii) and (iii) its hypotheses hold with $G = U(n)$, $M = \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, $p = [e_1]$, and $G_p = U(1) \times U(n-1)$. Therefore all four parts hold. $\blacksquare$

For $n = 2$ this reads $\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong U(2)/(U(1) \times U(1))$; restricting to $SU(2)$ gives $\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong SU(2)/U(1)$ with $U(1)$ the diagonal subgroup $\{\operatorname{diag}(z, \bar z)\}$, which is the description of the Hopf fibration as the coset projection $SU(2) \to SU(2)/U(1)$ used on [[Def - The Hopf Map]] and taken up in chapter III on [[Def - The Hopf Bundle]].

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name:** $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ is *the space of complex lines through the origin of $\mathbb{C}^n$, topologised and smoothed so that "the line through $v$" depends smoothly on $v \in \mathbb{C}^n \setminus \{0\}$ and so that the coordinates of $v$ up to a common nonzero factor are smooth coordinates on it*. Proposition 2 makes the first clause literal and Proposition 3 the second; Definition 1's quotient of the sphere by phases is the shortest rigorous route to that object, because it is the route on which a general theorem does the work. Operationally, one never computes with orbits: one computes with a representative $v$ and with the affine coordinates $(v_l/v_j)_{l \neq j}$, and Proposition 3 guarantees that every such computation is a computation on the manifold.

**Real projective space and Grassmannians.** The real analogue $\mathbb{RP}^n = (\mathbb{R}^{n+1} \setminus \{0\})/\mathbb{R}^* = S^n/\{\pm 1\}$ is constructed by affine charts in [[Ex - Real Projective Space is a Smooth Manifold]], and the argument of Proposition 3(iv) shows that the same two descriptions agree there. Both are the $k = 1$ cases of the Grassmannians $\mathrm{Gr}_k(\mathbb{K}^n)$ of $k$-dimensional subspaces, constructed in [[Ex - The Grassmannian is a Smooth Manifold]]; the affine chart $\varphi_j$ is the $k = 1$ case of the chart that records a subspace as the graph of a linear map. The difference between the real and complex cases is visible in the quotient description: $\{\pm 1\}$ is a discrete group, so $S^n \to \mathbb{RP}^n$ is a two-sheeted covering ([[Thm - Quotient by a Properly Discontinuous Action is a Manifold and a Covering Map]]), whereas $U(1)$ is one-dimensional, so $S^{2n-1} \to \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ has circle fibres and drops the dimension by one.

**Quantum mechanics.** A pure state of an $n$-level quantum system is a unit vector $\psi \in \mathbb{C}^n$ modulo phase, that is, a point of $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$; the projection $\pi : S^{2n-1} \to \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ is the passage from state vectors to physical states. For $n = 2$ the space of states of a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle is $\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2$, the Bloch sphere, and the Hopf map is precisely the map that assigns to a normalised spinor $(w_1, w_2)$ its Bloch vector; in the normalisation of $\eta$ the Bloch vector is $(2\,\mathrm{Re}(w_1 \bar w_2), 2\,\mathrm{Im}(w_1 \bar w_2), |w_1|^2 - |w_2|^2)$, the expectation values of the three Pauli matrices.

**Algebraic geometry.** Homogeneous coordinates are the language in which projective varieties are written: the zero locus in $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ of a homogeneous polynomial $p(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ is well defined because $p(\lambda v) = \lambda^{\deg p} p(v)$ vanishes if and only if $p(v)$ does (Proposition 2(ii) is what makes the point $[v]$ well defined). The decomposition $\mathbb{CP}^n = \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \sqcup \mathbb{C}^n$ proved in Proposition 7 below is the statement that projective space is affine space with a "hyperplane at infinity" adjoined.

**The tautological line bundle.** Proposition 2(iii) attaches to each point $[w] \in \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ the line $\mathbb{C} w \subset \mathbb{C}^n$; the union of these lines, $\{([w], v) : v \in \mathbb{C} w\} \subset \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \times \mathbb{C}^n$, is the tautological line bundle, and $S^{2n-1}$ is its unit-circle bundle, with $\pi$ as the bundle projection. This is the principal $U(1)$-bundle of chapter III, [[Def - The Hopf Bundle]], and the reason the present page belongs to a gauge theory series.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Instance — $n = 1$.** $S^1/U(1)$ is a point. Verified: the action is transitive (shown under "The smallest cases"), so there is one orbit; the quotient theorem gives dimension $1 - 1 = 0$, and a connected $0$-manifold is a point. The single affine chart $\varphi_1 : V_1 \to \mathbb{C}^0 = \{0\}$ is the unique map, trivially a diffeomorphism.

**Instance — $\mathbb{CP}^1$ with its two charts and the Hopf map.** For $n = 2$, Proposition 3 gives the atlas $\{(V_1, \varphi_1), (V_2, \varphi_2)\}$ with transition $u \mapsto 1/u$ on $\mathbb{C}^*$, verified in Proposition 3(iii). Proposition 4 gives the smooth bijection $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}} : \mathbb{CP}^1 \to S^2$; the next page proves it is a diffeomorphism. Its values at the two chart-complements are $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}}([1 : 0]) = \mathrm{Hopf}(1, 0) = (0, -1) = S$ and $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}}([0 : 1]) = \mathrm{Hopf}(0, 1) = (0, 4)/4 = N$; under $\eta$ the roles are exchanged, $\eta(1, 0) = N$ and $\eta(0, 1) = S$, as Lemma E's $F(N) = S$ predicts.

**Instance — the Hopf circles in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Bär's Example 1.5.10 and Remark on p. 24).** The fibres of the Hopf map are the orbits $U(1) \cdot w$, great circles of $S^3$; to visualise them, one projects $S^3$ minus a point stereographically to $\mathbb{R}^3$. The result, stated by Bär with figures and proved here by explicit parametrisation, is that $\mathbb{R}^3$ becomes a disjoint union of round circles and one straight line.

> [!note]- Proposition 5: stereographic projection of the Hopf circles
> **Statement.** Write points of $S^3 \subset \mathbb{C}^2$ in real coordinates $(x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4)$ with $w_1 = x_1 + ix_2$, $w_2 = x_3 + ix_4$, let $p_0 := (0, 1) \in \mathbb{C}^2$, that is, $(0, 0, 1, 0)$, and define
> $$\sigma : S^3 \setminus \{p_0\} \to \mathbb{R}^3, \qquad \sigma(x) := \frac{1}{1 - x_3}(x_1, x_2, x_4).$$
> Let $C_0 := U(1) \cdot p_0 = \{(0, e^{i\vartheta}) : \vartheta \in \mathbb{R}\}$; this is the fibre $\mathrm{Hopf}^{-1}(N) = \eta^{-1}(S)$. Call a subset of $\mathbb{R}^3$ a **round circle** if it has the form $\{c + r(\cos\beta\, e + \sin\beta\, v) : \beta \in \mathbb{R}\}$ with $c \in \mathbb{R}^3$, $r > 0$, and $(e, v)$ an orthonormal pair.
> (i) $\sigma$ is a bijection with inverse $\sigma^{-1}(y) = \frac{1}{1 + |y|^2}\big(2y_1, 2y_2, |y|^2 - 1, 2y_3\big)$.
> (ii) $\sigma(C_0 \setminus \{p_0\}) = \{(0, 0, s) : s \in \mathbb{R}\}$, the $y_3$-axis.
> (iii) Let $C = U(1) \cdot w$ be an orbit with $C \neq C_0$, and put $a := |w_1| \in (0, 1]$, $b := |w_2| = \sqrt{1 - a^2} \in [0, 1)$, and choose $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$ with $w_1 \bar w_2 = ab\, e^{i\gamma}$ (any $\gamma$ if $b = 0$). Then $\sigma(C)$ is the round circle with centre $c = \frac{b}{a}(\cos\gamma, \sin\gamma, 0)$, radius $r = \frac{1}{a}$, and orthonormal pair $e = (\cos\gamma, \sin\gamma, 0)$, $v = (-a\sin\gamma, a\cos\gamma, b)$.
> (iv) The line $\sigma(C_0 \setminus \{p_0\})$ together with the circles $\sigma(C)$, $C$ ranging over the orbits other than $C_0$, form a partition of $\mathbb{R}^3$ into pairwise disjoint sets.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Part (i).** The map $\sigma$ is defined on $S^3 \setminus \{p_0\}$ because $x_3 = 1$ together with $|x| = 1$ forces $x = p_0$. Write $\rho(y) := \frac{1}{1 + |y|^2}(2y_1, 2y_2, |y|^2 - 1, 2y_3)$ for $y \in \mathbb{R}^3$. **$\rho$ lands in $S^3 \setminus \{p_0\}$:** $|\rho(y)|^2 (1 + |y|^2)^2 = 4|y|^2 + (|y|^2 - 1)^2 = (|y|^2 + 1)^2$, so $|\rho(y)| = 1$; and its third coordinate $(|y|^2 - 1)/(|y|^2 + 1) < 1$, so $\rho(y) \neq p_0$. **$\sigma \circ \rho = \mathrm{id}$:** with $x = \rho(y)$, $1 - x_3 = \frac{(|y|^2 + 1) - (|y|^2 - 1)}{|y|^2 + 1} = \frac{2}{|y|^2 + 1}$, so $\sigma(x) = \frac{|y|^2 + 1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + |y|^2}(2y_1, 2y_2, 2y_3) = y$. **$\rho \circ \sigma = \mathrm{id}$:** for $x \in S^3 \setminus \{p_0\}$ and $y = \sigma(x)$, $|y|^2 = \frac{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_4^2}{(1 - x_3)^2} = \frac{1 - x_3^2}{(1 - x_3)^2} = \frac{1 + x_3}{1 - x_3}$ (using $|x| = 1$), hence $1 + |y|^2 = \frac{2}{1 - x_3}$ and $|y|^2 - 1 = \frac{2x_3}{1 - x_3}$, and
> > $$\rho(y) = \frac{1 - x_3}{2}\left(\frac{2x_1}{1 - x_3}, \frac{2x_2}{1 - x_3}, \frac{2x_3}{1 - x_3}, \frac{2x_4}{1 - x_3}\right) = x \qquad \text{(substituting the three identities just derived)}.$$
> > So $\sigma$ is a bijection with inverse $\rho$. (This is the stereographic projection of [[Ex - The Sphere as a Smooth Manifold via Stereographic Projection]] with the pole coordinate placed in slot $3$.)
> >
> > **Part (ii).** A point of $C_0 \setminus \{p_0\}$ is $(0, e^{i\vartheta})$ with $\vartheta \in (0, 2\pi)$, that is, $x = (0, 0, \cos\vartheta, \sin\vartheta)$, and
> > $$\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1 - \cos\vartheta}(0, 0, \sin\vartheta) = \big(0, 0, \cot(\vartheta/2)\big) \qquad \text{(since } \sin\vartheta = 2\sin(\vartheta/2)\cos(\vartheta/2) \text{ and } 1 - \cos\vartheta = 2\sin^2(\vartheta/2)\text{)}.$$
> > As $\vartheta$ ranges over $(0, 2\pi)$, $\vartheta/2$ ranges over $(0, \pi)$, on which $\cot$ is continuous and strictly decreasing (its derivative is $-1/\sin^2 < 0$) with $\cot(s) \to +\infty$ as $s \to 0^+$ and $\cot(s) \to -\infty$ as $s \to \pi^-$; by the intermediate value theorem it takes every real value, and by strict monotonicity each exactly once. Hence $\sigma(C_0 \setminus \{p_0\})$ is the whole $y_3$-axis.
> >
> > **Part (iii), Step 1: parametrise the orbit.** Write $w_1 = ae^{i\phi}$, $w_2 = be^{i\psi}$ with $a = |w_1|$, $b = |w_2|$, so $a^2 + b^2 = 1$ and $w_1\bar w_2 = ab\, e^{i(\phi - \psi)}$; when $b > 0$ the hypothesis forces $\gamma \equiv \phi - \psi \pmod{2\pi}$, and when $b = 0$ we may take $\psi := \phi - \gamma$. Also $a > 0$, because $a = 0$ would mean $w = (0, w_2)$ with $|w_2| = 1$, so $w \in C_0$ and $C = C_0$, excluded. The orbit is
> > $$C = \{(zw_1, zw_2) : z = e^{it}\} = \{(ae^{i(\theta + \gamma)}, be^{i\theta}) : \theta \in \mathbb{R}\} \qquad \text{(substituting } \theta := \psi + t \text{, so } \phi + t = \theta + \gamma\text{)}.$$
> > Define the rotation $R_\gamma : \mathbb{C}^2 \to \mathbb{C}^2$, $R_\gamma(w_1, w_2) = (e^{i\gamma}w_1, w_2)$, and $\bar R_\gamma : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$, $\bar R_\gamma(y_1, y_2, y_3) = (y_1\cos\gamma - y_2\sin\gamma,\ y_1\sin\gamma + y_2\cos\gamma,\ y_3)$, the rotation by $\gamma$ about the $y_3$-axis. Then $C = R_\gamma(C')$ with $C' := \{(ae^{i\theta}, be^{i\theta}) : \theta \in \mathbb{R}\}$, $R_\gamma$ preserves $S^3$ and fixes $p_0$ (it preserves norms and $w_2$), and
> > $$\sigma(R_\gamma x) = \frac{1}{1 - x_3}\big(x_1\cos\gamma - x_2\sin\gamma,\ x_1\sin\gamma + x_2\cos\gamma,\ x_4\big) = \bar R_\gamma(\sigma(x)) \qquad \text{(} R_\gamma \text{ rotates } (x_1, x_2) \text{ and fixes } x_3, x_4\text{)}.$$
> > Hence $\sigma(C) = \bar R_\gamma(\sigma(C'))$.
> >
> > **Step 2: project $C'$.** A point of $C'$ is $x(\theta) = (a\cos\theta, a\sin\theta, b\cos\theta, b\sin\theta)$, and $1 - b\cos\theta \geq 1 - b > 0$ (as $b < 1$), so
> > $$\sigma(x(\theta)) = \frac{1}{1 - b\cos\theta}\big(a\cos\theta,\ a\sin\theta,\ b\sin\theta\big) \qquad \text{(definition of } \sigma\text{)}.$$
> > Put $c' := (b/a, 0, 0)$, $e_1 := (1, 0, 0)$, $v' := (0, a, b)$; then $|e_1| = 1$, $|v'|^2 = a^2 + b^2 = 1$, and $e_1 \cdot v' = 0$, so $(e_1, v')$ is orthonormal. We claim
> > $$\sigma(x(\theta)) = c' + \frac{1}{a}\big(\cos\beta(\theta)\, e_1 + \sin\beta(\theta)\, v'\big), \qquad \cos\beta(\theta) := \frac{\cos\theta - b}{1 - b\cos\theta}, \quad \sin\beta(\theta) := \frac{a\sin\theta}{1 - b\cos\theta}.$$
> > First, the two proposed values are a legitimate cosine–sine pair: $(\cos\theta - b)^2 + a^2\sin^2\theta = \cos^2\theta - 2b\cos\theta + b^2 + (1 - b^2)\sin^2\theta = 1 - 2b\cos\theta + b^2\cos^2\theta = (1 - b\cos\theta)^2$ (using $a^2 = 1 - b^2$ and $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$), so $\cos\beta(\theta)^2 + \sin\beta(\theta)^2 = 1$ and $\beta(\theta)$ exists. Second, the first coordinate of the right-hand side is
> > $$\frac{b}{a} + \frac{\cos\theta - b}{a(1 - b\cos\theta)} = \frac{b(1 - b\cos\theta) + \cos\theta - b}{a(1 - b\cos\theta)} = \frac{(1 - b^2)\cos\theta}{a(1 - b\cos\theta)} = \frac{a\cos\theta}{1 - b\cos\theta} \qquad \text{(common denominator; } 1 - b^2 = a^2\text{)},$$
> > and the second and third coordinates are $\frac{1}{a}\sin\beta(\theta)\,(0, a, b) = \frac{\sin\theta}{1 - b\cos\theta}(0, a, b)$, matching $\sigma(x(\theta))$. So $\sigma(C') \subset \Gamma' := \{c' + \frac{1}{a}(\cos\beta\, e_1 + \sin\beta\, v') : \beta \in \mathbb{R}\}$, a round circle of centre $c'$ and radius $1/a$.
> >
> > **Step 3: $\sigma(C')$ is all of $\Gamma'$.** Given $\beta \in \mathbb{R}$ we must find $\theta$ with $\cos\beta(\theta) = \cos\beta$ and $\sin\beta(\theta) = \sin\beta$. Consider $g : [-1, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$, $g(s) = (s - b)/(1 - bs)$; it is well defined ($1 - bs \geq 1 - b > 0$), satisfies $g(-1) = -1$ and $g(1) = 1$, and is strictly increasing, since $g'(s) = \frac{(1 - bs) + b(s - b)}{(1 - bs)^2} = \frac{1 - b^2}{(1 - bs)^2} > 0$; by the intermediate value theorem and monotonicity it is a bijection $[-1, 1] \to [-1, 1]$. Let $s_0 := g^{-1}(\cos\beta)$ and $\theta_0 := \arccos s_0 \in [0, \pi]$. Then $\cos\beta(\theta_0) = g(\cos\theta_0) = \cos\beta$, and $\sin\beta(\theta_0) = a\sin\theta_0/(1 - b\cos\theta_0) \geq 0$ with $\sin\beta(\theta_0)^2 = 1 - \cos^2\beta = \sin^2\beta$. If $\sin\beta \geq 0$ take $\theta := \theta_0$; if $\sin\beta < 0$ take $\theta := -\theta_0$, which leaves $\cos\beta(\theta)$ unchanged and changes the sign of $\sin\beta(\theta)$. In both cases $\sigma(x(\theta)) = c' + \frac{1}{a}(\cos\beta\, e_1 + \sin\beta\, v')$. Hence $\sigma(C') = \Gamma'$.
> >
> > **Step 4: rotate back.** By Step 1, $\sigma(C) = \bar R_\gamma(\Gamma')$. Since $\bar R_\gamma$ is linear and orthogonal, $\bar R_\gamma\big(c' + \frac{1}{a}(\cos\beta\, e_1 + \sin\beta\, v')\big) = \bar R_\gamma c' + \frac{1}{a}\big(\cos\beta\, \bar R_\gamma e_1 + \sin\beta\, \bar R_\gamma v'\big)$, with $\bar R_\gamma c' = \frac{b}{a}(\cos\gamma, \sin\gamma, 0) = c$, $\bar R_\gamma e_1 = (\cos\gamma, \sin\gamma, 0) = e$, $\bar R_\gamma v' = (-a\sin\gamma, a\cos\gamma, b) = v$, and $(e, v)$ orthonormal because $\bar R_\gamma$ preserves inner products. Therefore $\sigma(C)$ is the round circle with centre $c$, radius $1/a$ and orthonormal pair $(e, v)$, as claimed.
> >
> > **Part (iv).** The orbits of a group action partition $S^3$ (each point lies in its own orbit; two orbits that share a point coincide, by Proposition 2(i)). Removing $p_0$, the sets $C_0 \setminus \{p_0\}$ and $C$ ($C \neq C_0$) partition $S^3 \setminus \{p_0\}$, and since $\sigma$ is a bijection onto $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Part (i)), their images partition $\mathbb{R}^3$. By Parts (ii) and (iii) these images are the $y_3$-axis and round circles. Therefore $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the disjoint union of one straight line and the family of round circles $\sigma(C)$, $C \neq C_0$. $\blacksquare$

Two features of the picture are worth reading off the formulas. The fibre $C_1 := \{(e^{i\vartheta}, 0)\}$ over the other pole ($a = 1$, $b = 0$) projects to the unit circle in the plane $y_3 = 0$ centred at the origin: $c = 0$, $r = 1$, $e = (\cos\gamma, \sin\gamma, 0)$, $v = (-\sin\gamma, \cos\gamma, 0)$. Every other circle $\sigma(C)$ meets the plane $y_3 = 0$ in exactly the two points $c \pm \frac{1}{a} e = \frac{b \pm 1}{a}(\cos\gamma, \sin\gamma, 0)$ (since $v$ has $y_3$-component $b > 0$, the parameter $\beta$ must satisfy $\sin\beta = 0$ there), one at distance $\frac{1 + b}{a} > 1$ from the axis and one at distance $\frac{1 - b}{a} = \frac{a}{1 + b} < 1$ on the opposite side; so each circle passes once inside and once outside the unit circle $\sigma(C_1)$, and winds once around the axis $\sigma(C_0 \setminus \{p_0\})$. That any two Hopf circles are linked — the Hopf link, Bär's remark on p. 25 — is proved from this description on [[Ex - Any Two Hopf Circles are Linked]].

**Non-instance — quotienting all of $\mathbb{C}^n$ by $\mathbb{C}^*$.** Let $n \geq 1$, let $Q := \mathbb{C}^n/\mathbb{C}^*$ be the set of orbits of the scalar action of $\mathbb{C}^*$ on all of $\mathbb{C}^n$ (the orbits are $\{0\}$ and the punctured lines $\mathbb{C}^* v$), with the quotient topology of $q_0 : \mathbb{C}^n \to Q$. We verify that $Q$ is not Hausdorff, hence not a manifold, so that removing the origin (or passing to the sphere) is necessary. Let $U \subset Q$ be open with $[0] \in U$. Then $q_0^{-1}(U)$ is open in $\mathbb{C}^n$ (definition of the quotient topology) and contains $0$, so it contains a ball $B_\varepsilon(0) = \{v : |v| < \varepsilon\}$ for some $\varepsilon > 0$. For any $v \neq 0$ put $\lambda := \varepsilon/(2|v|) \in \mathbb{C}^*$; then $|\lambda v| = \varepsilon/2 < \varepsilon$, so $\lambda v \in q_0^{-1}(U)$; since $q_0^{-1}(U)$ is a union of orbits and $v$ lies in the orbit of $\lambda v$, also $v \in q_0^{-1}(U)$, that is, $[v] \in U$. Hence $U = Q$. Now $[0] \neq [e_1]$ (the orbits $\{0\}$ and $\mathbb{C}^* e_1$ are disjoint), but every open set containing $[0]$ contains $[e_1]$, so no disjoint open sets separate them: $Q$ is not Hausdorff. Manifolds are Hausdorff by the standing convention, so $Q$ is not a manifold.

**Non-instance — a non-free action of a compact group.** The action of $U(1)$ on $S^2$ by rotation about the $z$-axis is smooth and proper ($U(1)$ is compact) but not free: the poles are fixed by every rotation. Its orbit space is identified with $[-1, 1]$ by the $z$-coordinate on [[Ex - Rotation of the Sphere about an Axis]]. We verify that $[-1, 1]$ is not a topological manifold of dimension $1$ at the point $1$, so the conclusion of the quotient theorem genuinely fails. Suppose $U$ were an open neighbourhood of $1$ in $[-1, 1]$ homeomorphic to an open interval $(\alpha, \beta)$ via $h$, with $h(1) = s_0$. Shrinking, we may take $U = (1 - \varepsilon, 1]$ and correspondingly $h(U) = (\alpha', \beta')$ an open interval containing $s_0$ (open subsets of open intervals containing $s_0$ contain an open interval around $s_0$; replace $U$ by the preimage of one). The set $U \setminus \{1\} = (1 - \varepsilon, 1)$ is connected (an interval), while $h(U) \setminus \{s_0\} = (\alpha', s_0) \cup (s_0, \beta')$ is disconnected (two disjoint nonempty open sets); a homeomorphism $U \setminus \{1\} \to h(U) \setminus \{s_0\}$ would preserve connectedness. This contradiction shows no such $U$ exists. Freeness is thus not a technical convenience: without it, exceptional orbits produce boundary points.

**Corollary — the decomposition $\mathbb{CP}^n = \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \sqcup \mathbb{C}^n$ (Bär's Remark 1.5.15).** Bär observes that the natural inclusions $\mathbb{C} \subset \mathbb{C}^2 \subset \dots$ induce inclusions $S^1 \subset S^3 \subset \dots$ and $\{*\} = \mathbb{CP}^0 \subset \mathbb{CP}^1 \subset \mathbb{CP}^2 \subset \dots$, and that $\mathbb{CP}^n$ is obtained from $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ by adjoining a copy of $\mathbb{C}^n$. Chapter III uses this decomposition for the Mayer–Vietoris computation of the cohomology of $\mathbb{CP}^n$; here we prove it. To keep indices readable we state it for $\mathbb{CP}^n = S^{2n+1}/U(1)$ with $n \geq 1$, writing $\pi_{n-1}$ and $\pi_n$ for the two quotient maps, and $V_j^{(n)}, \varphi_j^{(n)}$ for the charts of $\mathbb{CP}^n$.

> [!note]- Proposition 7: the hyperplane at infinity and the affine cell
> **Statement.** Let $n \geq 1$ and let $i : \mathbb{C}^n \to \mathbb{C}^{n+1}$, $i(v) = (v, 0)$, be the linear inclusion.
> (i) $i$ restricts to a smooth map $S^{2n-1} \to S^{2n+1}$, and there is a unique map $j : \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \to \mathbb{CP}^n$ with $j \circ \pi_{n-1} = \pi_n \circ i$; explicitly $j([w_1 : \dots : w_n]) = [w_1 : \dots : w_n : 0]$.
> (ii) $j$ is a smooth embedding ([[Def - Immersion, Submersion, and Embedding]]): an injective immersion that is a homeomorphism onto its image. Its image is $H := \{[w] \in \mathbb{CP}^n : w_{n+1} = 0\} = \mathbb{CP}^n \setminus V_{n+1}^{(n)}$, which is closed; so $H$ is an embedded submanifold of $\mathbb{CP}^n$ of codimension $2$, diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$.
> (iii) The complement $\mathbb{CP}^n \setminus H = V_{n+1}^{(n)}$ is open, and $\psi_{n+1}^{(n)} : \mathbb{C}^n \to \mathbb{CP}^n \setminus H$, $u \mapsto [u_1 : \dots : u_n : 1]$, is a diffeomorphism.
> (iv) In terms of lines: a one-dimensional subspace $L \subset \mathbb{C}^{n+1} = \mathbb{C}^n \oplus \mathbb{C}$ either lies in the hyperplane $\mathbb{C}^n \times \{0\}$, or meets it only in $0$; in the second case $L$ meets the affine hyperplane $A := \{x \in \mathbb{C}^{n+1} : x_{n+1} = 1\} = \mathbb{C}^n \times \{0\} + e_{n+1}$ in exactly one point, namely $(\varphi_{n+1}^{(n)}([L]), 1)$, where $[L] := \Lambda^{-1}(L)$.
> Consequently $\mathbb{CP}^n = j(\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}) \sqcup \psi_{n+1}^{(n)}(\mathbb{C}^n)$, a disjoint union of a closed copy of $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ and an open copy of $\mathbb{C}^n$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Part (i).** For $w \in S^{2n-1}$, $|i(w)|^2 = |w|^2 + 0 = 1$, so $i(w) \in S^{2n+1}$; $i$ is linear, hence smooth into $\mathbb{C}^{n+1}$, and smooth into $S^{2n+1}$ by Lemma A(b) after composing with the smooth inclusion of $S^{2n-1}$ (Lemma A(a)). The map $\pi_n \circ i : S^{2n-1} \to \mathbb{CP}^n$ is smooth and constant on $U(1)$-orbits, because $i(zw) = (zw, 0) = z\, i(w)$ and so $[i(zw)] = [i(w)]$ (Proposition 2(i)). By the universal property (Proposition 1(iv) for $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$) there is a unique smooth $j$ with $j \circ \pi_{n-1} = \pi_n \circ i$, and $j([w]) = [i(w)] = [w_1 : \dots : w_n : 0]$ by Proposition 2(ii).
> >
> > **Part (ii): injective.** If $j([w]) = j([w'])$ then $[(w, 0)] = [(w', 0)]$, so $(w', 0) = z(w, 0)$ for some $z \in U(1)$ (Proposition 2(i)), whence $w' = zw$ and $[w] = [w']$.
> >
> > **Immersion.** Fix $[w] \in \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ and choose $k \leq n$ with $w_k \neq 0$, so $[w] \in V_k^{(n-1)}$. Then $j([w]) = [w_1 : \dots : w_n : 0]$ has $k$-th coordinate $w_k \neq 0$, so $j(V_k^{(n-1)}) \subset V_k^{(n)}$, and in the charts $\varphi_k^{(n-1)}$, $\varphi_k^{(n)}$ (diffeomorphisms by Proposition 3(ii)) the map $j$ reads
> > $$\varphi_k^{(n)} \circ j \circ (\varphi_k^{(n-1)})^{-1}(u) = \varphi_k^{(n)}\big([\iota_k(u) : 0]\big) = (u, 0) \in \mathbb{C}^{n-1} \times \mathbb{C} = \mathbb{C}^n \qquad \text{(the } k\text{-th entry of } \iota_k(u) \text{ is } 1\text{, so the ratios are the entries of } \iota_k(u) \text{ other than the } k\text{-th, followed by } 0/1\text{)}.$$
> > This is the linear inclusion $\mathbb{C}^{n-1} \to \mathbb{C}^n$, $u \mapsto (u, 0)$, whose differential (itself) is injective. Hence $dj$ is injective at $[w]$; as $[w]$ was arbitrary, $j$ is an immersion.
> >
> > **Image.** If $[w] \in j(\mathbb{CP}^{n-1})$ then $w_{n+1} = 0$ for the representative $(w', 0)$, and this condition is orbit-invariant, so $[w] \in H$. Conversely if $[w] \in H$ with $w \in S^{2n+1}$ and $w_{n+1} = 0$, then $w' := (w_1, \dots, w_n) \in S^{2n-1}$ (same norm) and $j([w']) = [i(w')] = [w]$. So $j(\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}) = H$, and $H = \mathbb{CP}^n \setminus V_{n+1}^{(n)}$ by the definition of $V_{n+1}^{(n)}$; since $V_{n+1}^{(n)}$ is open (Proposition 3(i)), $H$ is closed.
> >
> > **Homeomorphism onto the image.** $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ is compact (Proposition 1(iv)) and $H$, as a subspace of the Hausdorff space $\mathbb{CP}^n$, is Hausdorff (restrict separating open sets). The corestriction $j : \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \to H$ is a continuous bijection (injective, image $H$, continuous because $j$ is smooth). By Lemma D it is a homeomorphism. Hence $j$ is an injective immersion and a topological embedding, that is, a smooth embedding; the manifold structure on $H$ transported along $j$ makes $H$ an embedded submanifold ([[Def - Embedded Submanifold]]: the inclusion $H \hookrightarrow \mathbb{CP}^n$ is $j \circ (\text{transport})$, a smooth embedding, and the topology is the subspace topology), of dimension $2n - 2$ inside the $2n$-dimensional $\mathbb{CP}^n$, hence of codimension $2$, and diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ via $j$.
> >
> > **Part (iii).** $V_{n+1}^{(n)} = \mathbb{CP}^n \setminus H$ is open, and $\psi_{n+1}^{(n)} : \mathbb{C}^n \to V_{n+1}^{(n)}$ is a diffeomorphism by Proposition 3(ii) (it is $\varphi_{n+1}^{(n)}$'s inverse), with $\psi_{n+1}^{(n)}(u) = [\iota_{n+1}(u)] = [u_1 : \dots : u_n : 1]$.
> >
> > **Part (iv).** Let $L = \mathbb{C} v$ with $v \in \mathbb{C}^{n+1} \setminus \{0\}$ (Proposition 2(iii)). **Case 1: $v_{n+1} = 0$.** Then every $\lambda v$ has last coordinate $0$, so $L \subset \mathbb{C}^n \times \{0\}$, and $L \cap A = \emptyset$ (points of $A$ have last coordinate $1$). **Case 2: $v_{n+1} \neq 0$.** A point $\lambda v$ lies in $\mathbb{C}^n \times \{0\}$ if and only if $\lambda v_{n+1} = 0$, if and only if $\lambda = 0$; so $L \cap (\mathbb{C}^n \times \{0\}) = \{0\}$. And $\lambda v \in A$ if and only if $\lambda v_{n+1} = 1$, if and only if $\lambda = 1/v_{n+1}$; so $L \cap A$ is the single point $v/v_{n+1} = \big((v_l/v_{n+1})_{l \leq n}, 1\big) = (\varphi_{n+1}^{(n)}([v]), 1)$ (definition of $\varphi_{n+1}^{(n)}$, which is independent of the representative by Proposition 3(ii)). The two cases are exhaustive and mutually exclusive, and they correspond under $\Lambda$ exactly to $[v] \in H$ and $[v] \in V_{n+1}^{(n)}$.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** $\mathbb{CP}^n = H \sqcup V_{n+1}^{(n)}$ with $H = j(\mathbb{CP}^{n-1})$ closed and diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$, and $V_{n+1}^{(n)} = \psi_{n+1}^{(n)}(\mathbb{C}^n)$ open and diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{C}^n$. Therefore $\mathbb{CP}^n = \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \sqcup \mathbb{C}^n$ in the stated sense. $\blacksquare$

Iterating Proposition 7 gives the tower $\{*\} = \mathbb{CP}^0 \subset \mathbb{CP}^1 \subset \dots \subset \mathbb{CP}^n$, each term a closed embedded submanifold of the next with complement a copy of $\mathbb{C}^k$, and as a set $\mathbb{CP}^n = \mathbb{C}^0 \sqcup \mathbb{C}^1 \sqcup \dots \sqcup \mathbb{C}^n$. This is the **cell decomposition** of $\mathbb{CP}^n$ with one cell in each even dimension; that the cells assemble into a **CW structure** is not proved and not used in this series, whereas the open–closed decomposition $\mathbb{CP}^n = H \sqcup \mathbb{C}^n$ of Proposition 7 is exactly what the Mayer–Vietoris argument of chapter III consumes. For $n = 1$ it reads $\mathbb{CP}^1 = \{[1 : 0]\} \sqcup \{[u : 1] : u \in \mathbb{C}\}$: the Riemann sphere is the plane plus one point at infinity, matching the chart picture under Definition 2.

**Calibration check.** First, verify directly from Definition 1 and Proposition 2 that $[1 : i] = [i : -1] = [1/\sqrt2 : i/\sqrt2]$ but $[1 : i] \neq [1 : -i]$ (compute the ratios $w_1/w_2$: $-i$, $-i$, $-i$, and $i$). Second, check that the point $(0, 0, 3)$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$ lies on the line $\sigma(C_0 \setminus \{p_0\})$ and find its preimage: $\cot(\vartheta/2) = 3$ gives $\vartheta = 2\operatorname{arccot} 3$, and $\sigma^{-1}(0, 0, 3) = \frac{1}{10}(0, 0, 8, 6) = (0, \tfrac{4}{5} + \tfrac{3}{5}i)$, which indeed has $w_1 = 0$ and $|w_2| = 1$. Third, using Lemma E, compute $\eta$ and $\mathrm{Hopf}$ at $w = (1, 1)/\sqrt2$ and confirm $F$ carries one to the other: $\eta(w) = (1, 0)$, $\mathrm{Hopf}(w) = \frac{1}{5/2}(2, \tfrac32) = (\tfrac45, \tfrac35)$, and $F(1, 0) = \frac{1}{5}(4, 3)$. A reader who can do these three, and can say in one sentence why each of the three hypotheses of the quotient theorem was needed, has understood the page.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] $\mathbb{CP}^1 \cong S^2$ *(from this chapter)*
> Proposition 4 produced a smooth bijection $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}} : \mathbb{CP}^1 \to S^2$. The next page, [[Thm - CP^1 is Diffeomorphic to S^2 via the Hopf Map]], shows that $\mathrm{Hopf}$ has maximal rank everywhere and deduces that $\widetilde{\mathrm{Hopf}}$ is a diffeomorphism, so that $\mathbb{CP}^1$ is the $2$-sphere and the Hopf map is a smooth submersion $S^3 \to S^2$ with circle fibres — the same conclusion that [[Ex - The Hopf Map is a Submersion]] reaches for $\eta$ by a direct kernel computation.

> [!tip] The Hopf bundle and the tautological line bundle *(from Gauge Theory III)*
> The quotient map $\pi : S^{2n-1} \to \mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ with its free $U(1)$-action is the first non-trivial principal bundle of the series, [[Def - The Hopf Bundle]]; the local sections of Proposition 1(iv) are its local trivialisations, and the lines $\Lambda([w]) = \mathbb{C} w$ of Proposition 2 are the fibres of the associated tautological line bundle. Its first Chern class, computed in chapter VI, generates the classification of $U(1)$-bundles.

> [!tip] Linking of the Hopf circles *(from this chapter)*
> Proposition 5's explicit circles are the input to [[Ex - Any Two Hopf Circles are Linked]], which shows that every circle $\sigma(C)$ meets the flat disc bounded by $\sigma(C_1)$ in exactly one point, transversally — the Hopf link, and the geometric shadow of the non-triviality of the Hopf bundle.

> [!tip] Cohomology of $\mathbb{CP}^n$ *(from Gauge Theory III)*
> The decomposition $\mathbb{CP}^n = \mathbb{CP}^{n-1} \sqcup \mathbb{C}^n$ of Proposition 7, with $\mathbb{C}^n$ open and $\mathbb{CP}^{n-1}$ a closed submanifold with a tubular neighbourhood, feeds a **Mayer–Vietoris computation of the de Rham cohomology of complex projective space** in chapter III, which in turn is what classifies line bundles over $\mathbb{CP}^n$ by their degree.
