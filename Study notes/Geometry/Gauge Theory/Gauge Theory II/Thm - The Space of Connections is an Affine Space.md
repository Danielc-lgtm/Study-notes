# Notation

Throughout, manifolds are smooth ($C^\infty$), Hausdorff, and second countable; $M$ is a manifold of dimension $n$ and $\pi \colon E \to M$ is a smooth real [[Def - Vector Bundle|vector bundle]] of rank $k$ with fibre $E_m := \pi^{-1}(m)$ over $m \in M$. $\Gamma(E) = \Gamma(M; E)$ is the space of smooth [[Def - Section of a Vector Bundle|sections]] of $E$, a module over the ring $C^\infty(M)$ of smooth real-valued functions on $M$, and $\Gamma(U; E)$ is the space of smooth sections of the restricted bundle $E|_U := \pi^{-1}(U) \to U$ over an open subset $U \subseteq M$; for $s \in \Gamma(E)$ we write $s|_U \in \Gamma(U; E)$ for its restriction. As on [[Def - Bundle-Valued Differential Forms]], $\Omega^p(M; E) := \Gamma(\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E)$ is the space of $E$-valued $p$-forms, so $\Omega^0(M; E) = \Gamma(E)$ and $\Omega^1(M; E) = \Gamma(T^*M \otimes E)$; an element $\omega \in \Omega^1(M; E)$ has at each $m$ a value $\omega_m \in T^*_mM \otimes E_m = \operatorname{Hom}(T_mM, E_m)$. The endomorphism bundle $\operatorname{End}E = \operatorname{Hom}(E, E)$ of [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles]] has fibre $\operatorname{End}(E_m)$, and $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E) = \Gamma(T^*M \otimes \operatorname{End}E)$ is the space of $\operatorname{End}E$-valued 1-forms. Such a form $a$ acts on sections by the contraction $\operatorname{End}E \otimes E \to E$ of [[Def - Bundle-Valued Differential Forms]]:
$$(a \cdot s)(m) := a_m\big(s(m)\big) \in T^*_mM \otimes E_m, \qquad \text{determined on decomposables by } (\alpha \otimes \phi) \cdot s = \alpha \otimes \phi(s),$$
for $\alpha \in \Omega^1(M)$ and $\phi \in \Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$; the map $(a, s) \mapsto a \cdot s$ is $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear, because the contraction is bilinear in each fibre. For $f \in C^\infty(M)$, $df \in \Omega^1(M)$ is the [[Def - The Differential of a Function as a 1-Form|differential]] of $f$, $df_m(v) = v(f)$ for a tangent vector $v \in T_mM$ regarded as a [[Def - Derivation at a Point|derivation]] at $m$; the differential obeys the product rule $d(fg) = g\,df + f\,dg$ (the Leibniz rule for derivations, paired with $v$), and it is local: $(df)|_U = d(f|_U)$ for every open $U$, because $v(f)$ depends only on the values of $f$ near the base point of $v$ (the locality corollary on [[Def - Derivation at a Point]]), and this same locality identifies $T_mU$ with $T_mM$.

A **connection** (equivalently a **covariant derivative**) on $E$, [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle]], is an $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\nabla \colon \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ satisfying the Leibniz rule $\nabla(fs) = df \otimes s + f\,\nabla s$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$; the set of all connections on $E$ is $\mathcal{A}(E)$, and $\mathcal{A}(E|_U)$ is the set of connections on the restricted bundle over the open set $U$. Because $\Omega^1(M; E)$ is a vector space, the set of all maps $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ is a vector space under pointwise operations, and $\nabla - \hat\nabla$, $t\nabla$, and $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha \nabla_\alpha$ (for functions $\rho_\alpha$, acting by pointwise multiplication on the values) are formed in it; the theorem below is about which of these combinations land back in $\mathcal{A}(E)$. A [[Def - Local Frame|local frame]] of $E$ over $U$ is a $k$-tuple $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$ of sections $e_j \in \Gamma(U; E)$ whose values form a basis of $E_m$ at every $m \in U$; it corresponds to the [[Def - Local Trivialization|local trivialisation]] $\psi_U \colon E|_U \to U \times \mathbb{R}^k$ with $\psi_U^{-1}(m, x) = e(m) \cdot x := \sum_{j=1}^k x_j e_j(m)$, and conversely a trivialisation $\psi_U$ gives the frame $e_j := \psi_U^{-1}(\cdot, \epsilon_j)$, where $\epsilon_1, \dots, \epsilon_k$ is the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^k$ ([[Ex - Local Frames Correspond to Local Trivialisations]]). Every section $s \in \Gamma(U; E)$ is $s = e\sigma := \sum_j \sigma_j e_j$ for a unique smooth **component column** $\sigma = (\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_k)^{\mathsf{T}} \colon U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ ([[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]]). The **connection matrix** of $\nabla$ in the frame $e$, [[Def - Connection Matrix and Local Form of a Connection]], is the matrix $A = A(\nabla, e) \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ of 1-forms with $\nabla e = e \cdot A$, that is, $\nabla e_j = \sum_i A_{ij} \otimes e_i$; locally $\nabla(e\sigma) = e(d\sigma + A\sigma)$, written $\nabla = d + A$, and under a change of frame $e' = eg$ with $g \colon U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$, $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$.

For a function $\rho \in C^\infty(M)$, $\operatorname{supp}\rho := \overline{\{m \in M : \rho(m) \neq 0\}}$ is its [[Def - Support of a Function|support]], a closed set. A **smooth partition of unity subordinate to an open cover $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}$** of $M$ ([[Def - Partition of Unity on a Manifold]]) is a family $\{\rho_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}$ of smooth functions $\rho_\alpha \colon M \to [0, 1]$ with $\operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha \subseteq U_\alpha$ for every $\alpha$, with the family of supports locally finite (every point has a neighbourhood meeting only finitely many of them), and with $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha(m) = 1$ for every $m \in M$ — a finite sum at each point by local finiteness. Such a partition of unity exists for every open cover of every manifold, [[Thm - Existence of Smooth Partitions of Unity]]; this is where the standing hypotheses "Hausdorff and second countable" enter. If $\rho \in C^\infty(M)$ has $\operatorname{supp}\rho \subseteq U$ and $\omega$ is a smooth section over $U$ of a vector bundle $F \to M$ (for us $F = E$ or $F = T^*M \otimes E$), the **extension by zero** of $\rho\,\omega$ is the section
$$\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}(m) := \begin{cases} \rho(m)\,\omega(m), & m \in U, \\ 0 \in F_m, & m \notin U, \end{cases}$$
which is smooth on $M$ (Lemma 3 below). Indices: $\alpha, \beta \in I$ label the members of a cover, $i, j \in \{1, \dots, k\}$ label frame entries, and $t \in \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar. The symbols $\nabla, \hat\nabla, \nabla_\alpha, \nabla_U, \nabla_0$ always denote connections, $a, b$ always denote elements of $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, and $s, u$ denote sections of $E$.

Finally, the conclusion of the theorem uses the following standard notion from linear algebra, for which the vault has no separate page. An **affine space modelled on a real vector space $V$** is a non-empty set $\mathcal{P}$ together with a map $\mathcal{P} \times V \to \mathcal{P}$, $(P, v) \mapsto P + v$, such that (A1) $P + 0 = P$ for all $P$; (A2) $(P + v) + w = P + (v + w)$ for all $P \in \mathcal{P}$ and $v, w \in V$; and (A3) for all $P, Q \in \mathcal{P}$ there is exactly one $v \in V$ with $Q = P + v$, written $v = Q - P$. Conditions (A1)–(A2) say that the additive group of $V$ acts on $\mathcal{P}$, and (A3) says that the action is free and transitive: an affine space is a vector space that has forgotten its origin, and choosing any base point $P_0$ identifies $\mathcal{P}$ with $V$ through $v \mapsto P_0 + v$. The full symbol registry of the chapter is on [[Gauge Theory II — Vector Bundles, Covariant Derivatives, and Curvature]].

> [!warning] Convention: names and notation in the source
> Haydys (Theorem 11, p. 7) writes $\Omega^1(\operatorname{End}E)$ where this series writes $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, and uses "connection" and "covariant derivative" interchangeably, as does this series. He states the convexity of $\mathcal{A}(E)$ for $t \in [0, 1]$; the same computation gives every $t \in \mathbb{R}$, and we prove the stronger form because the gluing needs coefficients that are functions rather than constants. For the partition-of-unity step he refers to the proof of the existence of Riemannian metrics in a reference he cites as [BT03, Theorem 3.3.7]; the vault's version of that argument is [[Thm - Existence of Riemannian Metrics via Partitions of Unity]], and the argument is written out in full here rather than by analogy. Bär's notes treat connections on principal bundles, where the corresponding statement is [[Thm - Existence of Connections on Principal Bundles]] (chapter IV); there is no conflict of conventions to reconcile on this page.

# Statement

> **Theorem (the space of connections is an affine space; Haydys, Theorem 11).** Let $\pi \colon E \to M$ be a smooth real vector bundle of rank $k$ over a manifold $M$. Then:
>
> **(a) Existence.** $\mathcal{A}(E) \neq \varnothing$: the bundle $E$ admits a connection.
>
> **(b) Differences are tensors.** For any two connections $\nabla, \hat\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$, the map $\nabla - \hat\nabla \colon \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$, $s \mapsto \nabla s - \hat\nabla s$, is $C^\infty(M)$-linear; consequently there is a unique $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ with
> $$\nabla s - \hat\nabla s = a \cdot s \qquad \text{for all } s \in \Gamma(E),$$
> and we write $a = \nabla - \hat\nabla$.
>
> **(c) Shifts by tensors are connections.** For any $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and any $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, the map $\nabla + a$ defined by $(\nabla + a)s := \nabla s + a \cdot s$ is a connection on $E$.
>
> **Consequently**, the set $\mathcal{A}(E)$ with the map $\mathcal{A}(E) \times \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E) \to \mathcal{A}(E)$, $(\nabla, a) \mapsto \nabla + a$, is an affine space modelled on the vector space $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$.

> **Corollary (coordinates on the space of connections; affine combinations).** (i) For any fixed $\nabla_0 \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ the map $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E) \to \mathcal{A}(E)$, $a \mapsto \nabla_0 + a$, is a bijection, with inverse $\nabla \mapsto \nabla - \nabla_0$. (ii) If $\nabla_1, \dots, \nabla_N \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N \in C^\infty(M)$ satisfy $\sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i = 1$, then $\sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i \nabla_i \in \mathcal{A}(E)$; in particular $t\nabla + (1 - t)\hat\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}$, so $\mathcal{A}(E)$ is convex.

The theorem and its corollary say the same thing from two sides. Parts (b) and (c) say that once one connection is known, every other connection is obtained from it by adding a tensor, and every tensor may be added; part (a) says that at least one connection exists. Together they say that $\mathcal{A}(E)$ looks exactly like the vector space $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, except that no connection is distinguished as the zero — the origin can be chosen at will, and the corollary records that any choice works.

**The smallest concrete case.** Let $E = M \times \mathbb{R}$ be the product line bundle, so that $\Gamma(E) = C^\infty(M)$, $\Omega^1(M; E) = \Omega^1(M)$, and, since $\operatorname{End}(E_m) = \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$, also $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E) = \Omega^1(M)$ with $a \cdot s = a\,s$ the product of a 1-form by a function. The exterior derivative $d$ is a connection (it is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and $d(fs) = s\,df + f\,ds$ by the product rule), which is part (a). If $\nabla$ is any connection, then $\nabla - d$ is $C^\infty(M)$-linear, so $\theta := (\nabla - d)(\mathbb{1}) \in \Omega^1(M)$, where $\mathbb{1}$ is the constant function $1$, satisfies $(\nabla - d)(s) = (\nabla - d)(s \cdot \mathbb{1}) = s\,\theta$ for every $s$; that is, $\nabla = d + \theta$, which is part (b) with $a = \theta$ (here the tensoriality lemma is not even needed, because the bundle has a global frame). Conversely $d + \theta$ is a connection for every $\theta \in \Omega^1(M)$, by the computation $(d + \theta)(fs) = s\,df + f\,ds + f\theta s = df \otimes s + f\,(d + \theta)s$, which is part (c). So $\mathcal{A}(M \times \mathbb{R}) = \{d + \theta : \theta \in \Omega^1(M)\}$, an affine space over $\Omega^1(M)$ with the natural base point $d$. For the product bundle $M \times \mathbb{R}^k$ the same reasoning gives $\mathcal{A}(M \times \mathbb{R}^k) = \{d + A : A \in \Omega^1(M; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))\}$ (the converse proposition on [[Def - Connection Matrix and Local Form of a Connection]]), an affine space over the $k \times k$ matrices of 1-forms. The content of the theorem beyond these cases is that the same picture holds when $E$ has no global frame: then there is no preferred base point $d$, part (a) has to be proved by gluing, and part (b) has to be proved without a global frame, which is what the tensoriality lemma does.

# Motivation

The definition of a connection on [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle]] is axiomatic: a connection is any $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ obeying the Leibniz rule. An axiomatic definition raises two questions immediately. Does an object satisfying the axioms exist at all, and, if it does, how many are there? For a vector bundle that admits a global frame both questions have easy answers, as the smallest concrete case above shows: the coefficientwise derivative $d$ exists, and every connection is $d$ plus a matrix of 1-forms. But most vector bundles of interest — the tangent bundle of the sphere, the Möbius bundle, the bundles carrying the fields of gauge theory — have no global frame, and the non-example on [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle]] shows that "differentiate the coefficients" then makes no sense globally, because different local frames give different answers. It is therefore not obvious that any connection exists on such a bundle, and it is not obvious what the totality of connections looks like.

The theorem answers both questions completely, and the answer is as simple as it could be. Connections always exist, and the set of all of them is an affine space modelled on the vector space $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ of $\operatorname{End}E$-valued 1-forms. In words: any two connections differ by a tensor, and any connection can be shifted by any tensor. The reason is visible in the Leibniz rule itself. The rule $\nabla(fs) = df \otimes s + f\,\nabla s$ is a linear condition on $\nabla$ with an inhomogeneous term $df \otimes s$ that does not involve $\nabla$; the solution set of an inhomogeneous linear equation is, whenever it is non-empty, a translate of the solution set of the associated homogeneous equation. The homogeneous equation here is $T(fs) = f\,T(s)$, whose solutions are the $C^\infty(M)$-linear maps, and by the tensoriality lemma those are exactly the maps $s \mapsto a \cdot s$ with $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$. This is why the model vector space is $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ and not something more complicated.

The theorem is the foundation for everything that follows in the series. Curvature, the object studied from §2.3 onward, becomes a map $F \colon \mathcal{A}(E) \to \Omega^2(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ on an affine space, and the formula $F_{\nabla + a} = F_\nabla + d^\nabla a + a \wedge a$ of [[Thm - Curvature of a Shifted Connection]] shows that it is a quadratic polynomial in the affine coordinate $a$. The gauge group of §2.4, [[Def - Gauge Group of a Vector Bundle]], acts on $\mathcal{A}(E)$, and its orbits are subsets of this affine space; the moduli spaces of gauge theory are quotients of an affine space by a group. The Yang–Mills functional of chapter VII is a function on $\mathcal{A}(E)$, and its variational theory — the derivation of the Yang–Mills equation, the second variation, the Sobolev completions of chapter IX on which the analysis is done — all rest on the fact that $\mathcal{A}(E)$ can be identified with a vector space by choosing a base point, and that nothing depends on which base point is chosen. Even the elementary observation that any two connections can be joined by the segment $\nabla + t a$, $t \in [0, 1]$, which is the starting point of the Chern–Weil theorem of chapter VI, is a consequence of this page.

The proof of existence is the standard partition-of-unity gluing that every reader has seen for Riemannian metrics, [[Thm - Existence of Riemannian Metrics via Partitions of Unity]], and it is worth seeing why the same argument works: the gluing needs that the objects being glued form a convex set, positive-definite inner products in the Riemannian case and connections here. In the Riemannian case convexity comes from the convexity of the cone of positive-definite forms; here it comes from the Leibniz rule being affine in $\nabla$. The source writes this step only by analogy; we write it out, including the bookkeeping of extending locally defined objects by zero, which is where the hypothesis $\operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha \subseteq U_\alpha$ on a partition of unity does its work.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The hypothesis of the theorem is minimal — any smooth real vector bundle over any manifold — so the source question is which objects, not presented as vector bundles, nonetheless are vector bundles and therefore carry an affine space of connections.

The first disguised source is **a bundle presented by transition functions**. If a collection of open sets $U_\alpha$ covering $M$ and smooth maps $g_{\alpha\beta} \colon U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ satisfying the cocycle condition $g_{\alpha\beta}\,g_{\beta\gamma} = g_{\alpha\gamma}$ on triple overlaps are given, then the [[Thm - Vector Bundle Construction Lemma|vector bundle construction lemma]] produces a vector bundle $E$ with these [[Def - Transition Function of a Vector Bundle|transition functions]], and the theorem then says that $E$ carries connections. The bridge is that the construction lemma delivers local trivialisations $\psi_\alpha$ over the $U_\alpha$, and local trivialisations are all that the existence proof consumes. This is non-obvious because the bundle so constructed may be very far from trivial — every bundle arises this way — and the theorem nonetheless guarantees a connection, and indeed produces one with an explicit formula in terms of the $g_{\alpha\beta}$ (see the first Target below). *Example problem:* the tangent bundle of $S^2$ presented by the two stereographic charts; the transition function is the Jacobian of the change of charts, and the gluing formula produces a connection on $TS^2$ without any reference to a metric.

The second disguised source is **a pull-back or a subbundle of a bundle already carrying a connection**. If $f \colon N \to M$ is smooth and $E \to M$ is a vector bundle, the pull-back $f^*E \to N$ of [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles]] is a vector bundle over $N$, so by the theorem it carries connections, and every one of them is $f^*\nabla + a$ once one pull-back connection $f^*\nabla$ is constructed. Likewise a subbundle $E' \subseteq E$ (for instance $TM \subseteq M \times \mathbb{R}^N$ for an embedded submanifold) is a vector bundle, so it carries connections, and once a single one is found — the projected connection $\operatorname{pr} \circ d$ of [[Ex - The Projected Connection on an Embedded Submanifold]] — every other connection on $TM$ is the projected connection plus a tensor $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}TM)$. The bridge is that the pull-back and subbundle constructions are proved on their pages to yield vector bundles in the sense of the definition. The non-obvious point is that the theorem turns the search for *all* connections on a bundle like $TM$ into the search for *one* connection together with a description of $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}TM)$: for the Levi-Civita connection of a Riemannian metric on an embedded submanifold, the "one connection" is the projected one, and the tensor $a$ is what one has to compute.

The third disguised source is **an associated bundle of a principal bundle**. In chapter III a principal $G$-bundle $P \to M$ and a representation $\rho \colon G \to GL(V)$ give the [[Def - Associated Bundle|associated vector bundle]] $P \times_G V$; that this is a vector bundle is proved there, so the theorem applies and $P \times_G V$ carries an affine space of connections. The bridge is that local sections of $P$ give local trivialisations of $P \times_G V$. The non-obvious content is the comparison with chapter IV: a connection on the principal bundle $P$ induces a connection on every associated bundle ([[Thm - Principal Connections Induce Covariant Differentiation on Associated Bundles|Thm - Principal Connections Induce Covariant Derivatives on Associated Bundles]]), and the induced connections form an affine subspace of $\mathcal{A}(P \times_G V)$ modelled on the subspace $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{ad}P) \subseteq \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}(P \times_G V))$ of forms with values in the image of the Lie algebra of $G$ under the representation — a strictly smaller affine space when $G$ is smaller than $GL(V)$, which is the precise sense in which a principal connection is "a connection with a reduced structure group". *Example problem:* for $G = SO(k)$ and the defining representation, identify the induced connections with the metric connections of the next Target.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

Combine the theorem with **the transformation law for connection matrices** to obtain an explicit connection from a cocycle. Let $\{U_\alpha\}$ be a trivialising cover of $E$ with frames $e_\alpha$ and transition functions $g_{\alpha\beta}$ defined by $e_\beta = e_\alpha\,g_{\alpha\beta}$ on $U_\alpha \cap U_\beta$, and let $\{\rho_\beta\}$ be a subordinate partition of unity. The connection $\nabla$ built in the existence proof is $\nabla = \sum_\beta \rho_\beta \nabla_\beta$ with $\nabla_\beta$ the product connection of the frame $e_\beta$, which satisfies $\nabla_\beta e_\beta = 0$ (the components of $e_\beta$ in its own frame are constant, so their differentials vanish). On $U_\alpha \cap U_\beta$ one has $e_\alpha = e_\beta\,g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}$, and the Leibniz rule applied entrywise gives $\nabla_\beta e_\alpha = \nabla_\beta(e_\beta\,g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}) = e_\beta\,d(g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}) = e_\alpha\,g_{\alpha\beta}\,d(g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1})$, so the connection matrix of $\nabla_\beta$ in the frame $e_\alpha$ is $g_{\alpha\beta}\,d(g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1})$ (this agrees with the transformation law $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$ of [[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Connection 1-Forms]] applied with $A' = 0$, $g = g_{\alpha\beta}$, which gives $A = -dg\,g^{-1} = g\,d(g^{-1})$). Since the connection matrix of an affine combination of connections is the same affine combination of their matrices (apply $\sum_\beta \rho_\beta \nabla_\beta$ to $e_\alpha$, using the restriction of the glued connection to $U_\alpha$ from Lemma A on [[Def - Connection Matrix and Local Form of a Connection]]), the glued connection has, in the frame $e_\alpha$, the connection matrix
$$A_\alpha = \sum_\beta \rho_\beta\; g_{\alpha\beta}\, d\big(g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}\big) \qquad \text{(each summand extended by zero outside } U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \text{, using } \operatorname{supp}\rho_\beta \subseteq U_\beta\text{)},$$
where the summand with $\beta = \alpha$ vanishes because $g_{\alpha\alpha} = 1$. This is a formula for a connection in terms of nothing but the cocycle and the partition of unity, and it is the form in which the existence of connections is used in computations.

Combine the theorem with **the local formula for curvature** to see that curvature is a quadratic function on an affine space. By [[Thm - Curvature of a Shifted Connection]], for $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ the curvature of the shifted connection is $F_{\nabla + a} = F_\nabla + d^\nabla a + a \wedge a$, where $d^\nabla$ is the exterior covariant derivative on $\operatorname{End}E$-valued forms induced by $\nabla$. The theorem is what makes the left-hand side meaningful for every $a$ — part (c) says $\nabla + a$ is a connection, so it has a curvature — and what makes the formula exhaustive — part (b) says every connection is of the form $\nabla + a$. The payoff is that after choosing a base point $\nabla$, the curvature map $\mathcal{A}(E) \to \Omega^2(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ is a polynomial of degree two on the vector space $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, with constant term $F_\nabla$, linear term $d^\nabla a$, and quadratic term $a \wedge a$; the Yang–Mills functional $\tfrac12 \int_M |F_{\nabla + a}|^2$ of chapter VII, [[Def - Yang-Mills Lagrangian and Action Functional]], is then a quartic polynomial on that vector space, and its critical points are found by differentiating in $a$.

Combine the theorem with **a Euclidean structure on $E$** to obtain the affine space of metric connections. Suppose $E$ carries a Euclidean structure $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$, a smooth inner product on each fibre, and call $\nabla$ metric if $d\langle s, u \rangle = \langle \nabla s, u \rangle + \langle s, \nabla u \rangle$ for all sections $s, u$ ([[Def - Euclidean Vector Bundle and Metric Connection]]). If $\nabla$ is metric and $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, then
$$\langle (\nabla + a)s, u \rangle + \langle s, (\nabla + a)u \rangle - d\langle s, u \rangle = \langle a \cdot s, u \rangle + \langle s, a \cdot u \rangle \qquad \text{(since } \nabla \text{ is metric; the terms in } \nabla \text{ cancel against } d\langle s, u \rangle\text{)},$$
so $\nabla + a$ is metric if and only if $\langle a_m(v)\,x, y \rangle + \langle x, a_m(v)\,y \rangle = 0$ for all $m$, all $v \in T_mM$, and all $x, y \in E_m$ — that is, if and only if $a$ takes values in the subbundle $\mathfrak{so}(E) \subseteq \operatorname{End}E$ of skew-adjoint endomorphisms (the "only if" uses that every fibre vector is the value of a global section, Lemma 7 below). Hence, once one metric connection exists, the metric connections form an affine subspace of $\mathcal{A}(E)$ modelled on $\Omega^1(M; \mathfrak{so}(E))$. And one does exist: the existence proof below goes through verbatim with orthonormal frames in place of arbitrary frames — an orthonormal frame exists locally by applying the Gram–Schmidt process pointwise to any local frame, and the product connection of an orthonormal frame is metric because in that frame the inner product has constant coefficients — since a convex combination of metric connections is again metric (the metric condition is affine in $\nabla$, by the same computation). This is the vector-bundle form of the statement that connections on an $O(k)$-bundle form an affine space over $\Omega^1(M; \mathfrak{o}(k))$-valued forms.

Combine the theorem with **the gauge action** to see gauge orbits as subsets of an affine space. The gauge group $\mathcal{G}(E)$ of [[Def - Gauge Group of a Vector Bundle]] acts on $\mathcal{A}(E)$ by $\nabla^g s := g^{-1}\nabla(gs)$, and by [[Thm - Gauge Action on Connection Matrices]] the connection matrices transform by $A \mapsto g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$. The difference $\nabla^g - \nabla$ is, by part (b), a tensor, namely $s \mapsto g^{-1}(\nabla^{\operatorname{End}}g) \cdot s$ where $\nabla^{\operatorname{End}}$ is the induced connection on $\operatorname{End}E$ of [[Def - Induced Connections on Dual, Hom, and Endomorphism Bundles]]; so the gauge group acts on the affine space $\mathcal{A}(E)$ by affine maps, each orbit is a subset of an affine space, and the quotient $\mathcal{A}(E)/\mathcal{G}(E)$ — the space on which the invariants of chapters XI and XIII live — is the quotient of an affine space by a group of affine transformations. For a Hermitian line bundle with the unitary gauge group this is completely explicit: connections form an affine space over $i\Omega^1(M)$, and $g = e^{if}$ acts by $A \mapsto A + i\,df$, so the quotient is an affine space over $i\Omega^1(M)/i\,d C^\infty(M)$, whose "size" is measured by the first de Rham cohomology ([[Ex - The Gauge Group of a Trivial Line Bundle and its Action on Connections]]).

# Why Is It True

The theorem has an existence half and a structure half, and they rest on two different mechanisms.

The structure half rests on a single observation about the shape of the Leibniz rule. Write the defining condition of a connection as an equation in the unknown $\nabla$:
$$\nabla(fs) - f\,\nabla s = df \otimes s \qquad \text{for all } f, s.$$
The left-hand side is linear in $\nabla$, and the right-hand side does not involve $\nabla$ at all. So the condition is an inhomogeneous linear equation, and the elementary principle of linear algebra applies: if $\nabla$ and $\hat\nabla$ both solve it, their difference solves the homogeneous equation $T(fs) - f\,T(s) = 0$, and if $\nabla$ solves it and $T$ solves the homogeneous equation, then $\nabla + T$ solves it again. **The mechanism, in one sentence: the Leibniz rule is an inhomogeneous linear condition on $\nabla$, so its solution set is a translate of the solution set of the homogeneous condition, and the homogeneous solutions — the $C^\infty(M)$-linear maps — are exactly the tensors $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$.** The only genuinely geometric input in this half is the identification of the homogeneous solutions with tensors, which is the [[Thm - Tensoriality Lemma for C-Infinity-Linear Maps|tensoriality lemma]]: a $C^\infty(M)$-linear operator cannot see derivatives of its argument, because multiplying by a function that vanishes at $m$ but has non-zero differential there kills the output at $m$, so the operator depends only on the value of the section at each point, and a map that depends only on the value at each point is a bundle map. The two $df \otimes s$ terms cancel in a difference of connections precisely because both connections carry the same inhomogeneous term with the same coefficient $1$; had the definition allowed different normalisations for different connections, the differences would not be tensors.

The existence half rests on a different observation: the solution set of the Leibniz equation, being affine, is convex, and convexity is exactly what a partition of unity needs. Locally a connection is easy to find — in a local frame, differentiate the components. The difficulty is that the local candidates on overlapping trivialising sets disagree, by the non-example on [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle]], and there is no way to pick one of them. But one does not have to pick: a partition of unity $\{\rho_\alpha\}$ produces at each point a weighted average $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha(m)\,\nabla_\alpha$ of the local candidates that are defined near $m$, with weights that are non-negative, sum to one, and vary smoothly; and a weighted average of solutions of an affine equation is again a solution, because $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha\,(df \otimes s) = df \otimes s$ when $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha = 1$. **The mechanism, in one sentence: local solutions of the Leibniz equation exist, weighted averages of solutions with weights summing to one are solutions, and a partition of unity is the manifold's device for forming such smoothly varying weighted averages from locally defined pieces.** The role of the hypothesis $\operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha \subseteq U_\alpha$ is to make each weighted piece $\rho_\alpha \nabla_\alpha s$ a smooth object on all of $M$ — it is smooth on $U_\alpha$, and it is identically zero on the open set $M \setminus \operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha$, and these two open sets cover $M$ — so that pieces defined on different open sets can be added as global objects; the role of local finiteness is to make the sum finite near every point, so that it is smooth.

This is the same pair of mechanisms that proves the existence of Riemannian metrics, [[Thm - Existence of Riemannian Metrics via Partitions of Unity]], with one difference worth noting: positive-definite inner products form a convex *cone*, not an affine space, so metrics can be averaged with non-negative weights but not with arbitrary real weights, and differences of metrics are not metrics; connections form an affine space, so they can be combined with any weights summing to one, and differences of connections are tensors. The two theorems are the two most common instances of the general principle that a smoothly varying choice from a convex set of local objects can always be made global.

# What Makes This Hard

The proof has no difficult step, but it has two places where a careless argument is incomplete. The first is the gluing: the local connections $\nabla_\alpha$ are defined only on $E|_{U_\alpha}$, so the expression $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha \nabla_\alpha s$ has no meaning until each term $\rho_\alpha\,\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha})$, which is a 1-form on $U_\alpha$, has been extended by zero to a smooth 1-form on $M$ and the sum has been shown to be locally finite; the source writes "sewed into a global covariant derivative just like for Riemannian metrics" and leaves both to the reader. The extension by zero is smooth only because $\operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha$ is a closed subset of $U_\alpha$, so that $M \setminus \operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha$ is an open set on which the extended form vanishes; if one only knew that $\rho_\alpha$ vanished outside $U_\alpha$, the extension could fail to be smooth (or even continuous) at the boundary of $U_\alpha$. The second is the affine structure: the theorem asserts that the difference $a = \nabla - \hat\nabla$ is *unique*, and uniqueness of an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued form that is only known through its contractions $a \cdot s$ with sections requires that the global sections of $E$ take every value in every fibre — a bump-function argument that is easy but must be made. The common error is to treat the theorem as a triviality and to skip exactly these two verifications.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** Treat the Leibniz rule as an inhomogeneous linear equation in $\nabla$. For the structure statements (b) and (c), subtract two solutions to get a homogeneous solution, identify homogeneous solutions with tensors by the tensoriality lemma, and add a tensor to a solution to get a solution. For existence (a), produce a solution on each set of a trivialising cover (differentiate components in a frame), and average the local solutions with a partition of unity, using that the Leibniz equation is affine so that averages of solutions are solutions. Then verify the three axioms of an affine space, using a bump-function argument for uniqueness.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Local existence.** For a local trivialisation $\psi_U$ with frame $e$, show that $\nabla_U(e\sigma) := \sum_j d\sigma_j \otimes e_j$ is a connection on $E|_U$.
   - *Hint:* Components of $fs$ are $f\sigma_j$; apply the product rule $d(f\sigma_j) = \sigma_j\,df + f\,d\sigma_j$ and regroup.
   - *Why needed:* These are the local pieces to be glued; nothing else about $\nabla_U$ matters.

2. **Extension by zero.** For $\rho \in C^\infty(M)$ with $\operatorname{supp}\rho \subseteq U$ and a smooth section $\omega$ over $U$, show that $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}$ (equal to $\rho\omega$ on $U$ and to $0$ off $U$) is smooth on $M$.
   - *Hint:* $U$ and $M \setminus \operatorname{supp}\rho$ are open, cover $M$, and $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}$ is smooth on each.
   - *Why needed:* Without it the terms of the glued sum are not global objects.

3. **Gluing.** With a trivialising cover $\{U_\alpha\}$, local connections $\nabla_\alpha$ from subgoal 1, and a subordinate partition of unity $\{\rho_\alpha\}$, define $\nabla s := \sum_\alpha \widetilde{\rho_\alpha\,\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha})}$; show that the sum is locally finite, hence smooth, and that $\nabla$ is a connection.
   - *Hint:* Leibniz for each $\nabla_\alpha$ gives $\rho_\alpha\,df \otimes s + f\,\rho_\alpha \nabla_\alpha s$ for the $\alpha$-th term; sum and use $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha = 1$.
   - *Why needed:* This is part (a).

4. **Differences are $C^\infty(M)$-linear.** Show $(\nabla - \hat\nabla)(fs) = f\,(\nabla - \hat\nabla)(s)$.
   - *Hint:* Write out both Leibniz rules and watch the $df \otimes s$ terms cancel.
   - *Why needed:* It is the hypothesis of the tensoriality lemma, which then gives part (b).

5. **Shifts are connections.** Show $(\nabla + a)(fs) = df \otimes s + f\,(\nabla + a)s$.
   - *Hint:* $a \cdot (fs) = f\,(a \cdot s)$ because $a_m$ is linear on $E_m$.
   - *Why needed:* This is part (c).

6. **Every fibre vector is a section value; uniqueness of the difference.** Show that for $m \in M$ and $v \in E_m$ there is $s \in \Gamma(E)$ with $s(m) = v$, and deduce that $a \cdot s = 0$ for all $s$ forces $a = 0$.
   - *Hint:* Expand $v$ in a local frame, multiply by a bump function equal to $1$ at $m$, extend by zero.
   - *Why needed:* It gives the uniqueness in (b) and the freeness (A3) of the affine structure.

7. **Affine axioms.** Verify (A1) $\nabla + 0 = \nabla$, (A2) $(\nabla + a) + b = \nabla + (a + b)$, and (A3) existence and uniqueness of $a$ with $\hat\nabla = \nabla + a$.
   - *Hint:* (A1), (A2) are pointwise linearity of the contraction in $a$; (A3) is subgoals 4 and 6.
   - *Why needed:* This is the "consequently" of the theorem.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: affine combinations of connections with smooth coefficients summing to one are connections
> **Statement:** Let $\nabla_1, \dots, \nabla_N \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and let $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N \in C^\infty(M)$ satisfy $\sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i = 1$ on $M$. Then the map $\nabla := \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i \nabla_i$, defined by $\nabla s := \sum_i \lambda_i\,\nabla_i s$ (pointwise multiplication of the $E$-valued 1-form $\nabla_i s$ by the function $\lambda_i$), is a connection on $E$. In particular, for $\nabla, \hat\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and $t \in \mathbb{R}$, $t\nabla + (1 - t)\hat\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$; this is the convexity of $\mathcal{A}(E)$ asserted at the start of the source's proof.
>
> **Hint:** Apply the Leibniz rule to each $\nabla_i(fs)$, collect the terms $\lambda_i\,df \otimes s$, and use $\sum_i \lambda_i = 1$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the source's first step and the finite, global prototype of the gluing lemma (Lemma 4); it is also part (ii) of the Corollary. The gluing lemma repeats this computation with the additional bookkeeping that the $\nabla_i$ are only locally defined.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We are given connections $\nabla_1, \dots, \nabla_N$ and smooth functions $\lambda_i$ with $\sum_i \lambda_i = 1$. We need to show that $\nabla = \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla_i$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and satisfies the Leibniz rule.
> >
> > **Step 0 — $\nabla$ is a well-defined map into $\Omega^1(M; E)$.** For $s \in \Gamma(E)$ each $\nabla_i s$ lies in $\Omega^1(M; E)$ (definition of a connection), $\lambda_i\,\nabla_i s$ lies in $\Omega^1(M; E)$ because $\Omega^1(M; E) = \Gamma(T^*M \otimes E)$ is a $C^\infty(M)$-module under pointwise multiplication ([[Def - Bundle-Valued Differential Forms]]), and a finite sum of elements of $\Omega^1(M; E)$ lies in $\Omega^1(M; E)$. So $\nabla s \in \Omega^1(M; E)$.
> >
> > **$\mathbb{R}$-linearity.** For $s, u \in \Gamma(E)$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$,
> > $$\nabla(s + cu) = \sum_i \lambda_i\,\nabla_i(s + cu) = \sum_i \lambda_i\,(\nabla_i s + c\,\nabla_i u) = \nabla s + c\,\nabla u \qquad \text{(definition of } \nabla \text{; } \mathbb{R}\text{-linearity of each } \nabla_i \text{; distributivity of the pointwise operations).}$$
> >
> > **Leibniz rule.** For $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$,
> > $$\nabla(fs) = \sum_i \lambda_i\,\nabla_i(fs) = \sum_i \lambda_i\,\big(df \otimes s + f\,\nabla_i s\big) \qquad \text{(definition of } \nabla \text{; Leibniz rule for each } \nabla_i)$$
> > $$= \Big(\sum_i \lambda_i\Big)\,df \otimes s + f \sum_i \lambda_i\,\nabla_i s = df \otimes s + f\,\nabla s \qquad \text{(distributivity and commutativity of pointwise multiplication by functions; } \textstyle\sum_i \lambda_i = 1 \text{; definition of } \nabla).$$
> >
> > **The two-term case.** Taking $N = 2$, $\nabla_1 = \nabla$, $\nabla_2 = \hat\nabla$, $\lambda_1 \equiv t$, $\lambda_2 \equiv 1 - t$ (constant functions, whose sum is the constant $1$) gives $t\nabla + (1 - t)\hat\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ for every real $t$, and in particular for $t \in [0, 1]$.
> >
> > Therefore every affine combination of connections with smooth coefficients summing to one is a connection, and $\mathcal{A}(E)$ is convex. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: the product connection of a local trivialisation is a connection on the restricted bundle
> **Statement:** Let $\psi_U \colon E|_U \to U \times \mathbb{R}^k$ be a local trivialisation of $E$ over the open set $U \subseteq M$, and let $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$, $e_j := \psi_U^{-1}(\cdot, \epsilon_j)$, be the corresponding local frame. For $s \in \Gamma(U; E)$ let $\sigma = (\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_k)^{\mathsf{T}} \colon U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ be its component column, $s = e\sigma$, and define
> $$\nabla_U s := e\,d\sigma := \sum_{j=1}^k d\sigma_j \otimes e_j \in \Omega^1(U; E).$$
> Then $\nabla_U \in \mathcal{A}(E|_U)$. In Haydys's notation this is $\nabla_U s = \psi_U^{-1}\,d(\psi_U(s))$, where $\psi_U(s) := \sigma$ is $s$ read through the trivialisation as an $\mathbb{R}^k$-valued function, $d\sigma$ is its componentwise differential, an $\mathbb{R}^k$-valued 1-form, and $\psi_U^{-1}$ is applied fibrewise through $\operatorname{id} \otimes \psi_{U, m}^{-1} \colon T^*_mM \otimes \mathbb{R}^k \to T^*_mM \otimes E_m$. Its connection matrix in the frame $e$ is $A(\nabla_U, e) = 0$.
>
> **Hint:** The component column of $fs$ is $f\sigma$; apply the product rule for $d$ and regroup.
>
> **Why needed:** These are the local pieces that the partition of unity glues; existence of a connection on each trivialising set is the input to Lemma 4.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We are given the trivialisation $\psi_U$ and its frame $e$. We must show that $\nabla_U$ is well defined, $\mathbb{R}$-linear, and satisfies the Leibniz rule on $E|_U$, and we then identify it with $\psi_U^{-1} \circ d \circ \psi_U$ and compute its connection matrix.
> >
> > **Step 0 — well-definedness.** By [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]] — for a local frame $e$ over $U$, every $s \in \Gamma(U; E)$ has a unique expression $s = \sum_j \sigma_j e_j$ with $\sigma_j \in C^\infty(U)$ — the component column $\sigma$ of $s$ exists, is unique, and is smooth. Each $d\sigma_j$ is a smooth 1-form on $U$, each $d\sigma_j \otimes e_j$ is a smooth section of $T^*M \otimes E$ over $U$ (a decomposable $E$-valued 1-form, [[Def - Bundle-Valued Differential Forms]]), and the finite sum is smooth. So $\nabla_U s \in \Omega^1(U; E)$ is well defined.
> >
> > **$\mathbb{R}$-linearity.** If $s = e\sigma$ and $u = e\tau$ then $s + cu = e(\sigma + c\tau)$ for $c \in \mathbb{R}$, and by uniqueness of components, $\sigma + c\tau$ is the component column of $s + cu$. Hence
> > $$\nabla_U(s + cu) = \sum_j d(\sigma_j + c\tau_j) \otimes e_j = \sum_j (d\sigma_j + c\,d\tau_j) \otimes e_j = \nabla_U s + c\,\nabla_U u \qquad \text{(definition of } \nabla_U \text{; } \mathbb{R}\text{-linearity of } d \text{ on functions; bilinearity of } \otimes).$$
> >
> > **Leibniz rule.** Let $f \in C^\infty(U)$ and $s = e\sigma$. Then $fs = \sum_j (f\sigma_j) e_j = e(f\sigma)$, so by uniqueness of components the component column of $fs$ is $f\sigma$. Therefore
> > $$\nabla_U(fs) = \sum_j d(f\sigma_j) \otimes e_j = \sum_j \big(\sigma_j\,df + f\,d\sigma_j\big) \otimes e_j \qquad \text{(definition of } \nabla_U \text{; product rule } d(f\sigma_j) = \sigma_j\,df + f\,d\sigma_j \text{ for the differential of a product of functions)}$$
> > $$= df \otimes \Big(\sum_j \sigma_j e_j\Big) + f \sum_j d\sigma_j \otimes e_j = df \otimes s + f\,\nabla_U s \qquad \text{(bilinearity of } \otimes \text{; } s = e\sigma \text{; definition of } \nabla_U).$$
> > This is the Leibniz rule for $\nabla_U$ on $E|_U$, with $U$ regarded as a manifold in its own right (an open subset of $M$, with $T_mU = T_mM$ and $d(f)$ computed on $U$).
> >
> > **Identification with $\psi_U^{-1} \circ d \circ \psi_U$.** By the definition of the frame associated with $\psi_U$, $\psi_U(s(m)) = (m, \sigma(m))$, since $\psi_U^{-1}(m, \sigma(m)) = \sum_j \sigma_j(m)\,\psi_U^{-1}(m, \epsilon_j) = \sum_j \sigma_j(m)\,e_j(m) = s(m)$ (fibrewise linearity of $\psi_U^{-1}$; definition of $e_j$). So "$\psi_U(s)$" is the $\mathbb{R}^k$-valued function $\sigma$, its differential is the $\mathbb{R}^k$-valued 1-form $d\sigma = \sum_j d\sigma_j \otimes \epsilon_j$, and applying $\operatorname{id} \otimes \psi_{U,m}^{-1}$ at each $m$ sends $d\sigma_j \otimes \epsilon_j$ to $d\sigma_j \otimes e_j(m)$ (definition of $e_j$); summing over $j$ gives $\nabla_U s$.
> >
> > **Connection matrix.** The component column of $e_j$ in the frame $e$ is the constant column $\epsilon_j$, whose differential is zero; hence $\nabla_U e_j = \sum_i d(\delta_{ij}) \otimes e_i = 0$ for each $j$, so $\nabla_U e = e \cdot 0$ and $A(\nabla_U, e) = 0$ by the uniqueness of the connection matrix ([[Def - Connection Matrix and Local Form of a Connection]]).
> >
> > Therefore $\nabla_U$ is a connection on $E|_U$: it is the coefficientwise derivative in the frame $e$, transported from the product bundle $U \times \mathbb{R}^k$ through the trivialisation. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: extension by zero of a section cut off by a function supported in an open set is smooth
> **Statement:** Let $F \to M$ be a smooth vector bundle, $U \subseteq M$ open, $\rho \in C^\infty(M)$ with $\operatorname{supp}\rho \subseteq U$, and $\omega \in \Gamma(U; F)$. Define $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega} \colon M \to F$ by $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}(m) := \rho(m)\,\omega(m)$ for $m \in U$ and $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}(m) := 0 \in F_m$ for $m \notin U$. Then $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega} \in \Gamma(F)$, it vanishes identically on the open set $M \setminus \operatorname{supp}\rho$, and the assignment $\omega \mapsto \widetilde{\rho\,\omega}$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and satisfies $\widetilde{\rho\,(f|_U\,\omega)} = f\,\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}$ for every $f \in C^\infty(M)$.
>
> **Hint:** The open sets $U$ and $M \setminus \operatorname{supp}\rho$ cover $M$; the candidate is smooth on each.
>
> **Why needed:** The terms $\rho_\alpha\,\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha})$ of the glued connection live on $U_\alpha$; this lemma makes them global smooth $E$-valued 1-forms, which is what allows them to be summed. It is also used with $F = E$ in Lemma 7.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We are given the data of the statement; we must show that $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}$ is a smooth section of $F$, identify where it vanishes, and check the two algebraic properties.
> >
> > **It is a section.** For every $m \in M$, $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}(m) \in F_m$: for $m \in U$ because $\omega(m) \in F_m$ and $F_m$ is a vector space, for $m \notin U$ by definition. So $\pi_F \circ \widetilde{\rho\,\omega} = \operatorname{id}_M$.
> >
> > **Two open sets that cover $M$.** The support $\operatorname{supp}\rho$ is closed in $M$ (it is a closure, [[Def - Support of a Function]]), so $W := M \setminus \operatorname{supp}\rho$ is open; $U$ is open by hypothesis. They cover $M$: if $m \notin U$ then $m \notin \operatorname{supp}\rho$ (because $\operatorname{supp}\rho \subseteq U$), so $m \in W$.
> >
> > **Smoothness on $U$.** On $U$, $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}|_U = \rho|_U \cdot \omega$, the product of the smooth function $\rho|_U \in C^\infty(U)$ and the smooth section $\omega \in \Gamma(U; F)$, which is smooth because $\Gamma(U; F)$ is a $C^\infty(U)$-module under pointwise multiplication ([[Def - Section of a Vector Bundle]]: in a local trivialisation of $F$ the components of $\rho\omega$ are the products of $\rho$ with the components of $\omega$).
> >
> > **Smoothness on $W$.** For $m \in W$ we have $\rho(m) = 0$ (since $m \notin \operatorname{supp}\rho \supseteq \{\rho \neq 0\}$). If moreover $m \in U$, then $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}(m) = \rho(m)\,\omega(m) = 0$; if $m \notin U$, then $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}(m) = 0$ by definition. So $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}|_W$ is the zero section of $F|_W$, which is smooth.
> >
> > **Smoothness on $M$.** Smoothness of a map is a local property, and $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}$ is smooth on each of the two open sets $U$ and $W$ whose union is $M$; hence $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}$ is smooth on $M$, that is, $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega} \in \Gamma(F)$. The vanishing on $W = M \setminus \operatorname{supp}\rho$ was shown in the previous paragraph.
> >
> > **Linearity and the module property.** For $\omega, \eta \in \Gamma(U; F)$, $c \in \mathbb{R}$, and $f \in C^\infty(M)$, at a point $m \in U$ both $\widetilde{\rho\,(\omega + c\eta)}(m)$ and $\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}(m) + c\,\widetilde{\rho\,\eta}(m)$ equal $\rho(m)\omega(m) + c\rho(m)\eta(m)$, and both $\widetilde{\rho\,(f|_U\,\omega)}(m)$ and $f(m)\,\widetilde{\rho\,\omega}(m)$ equal $\rho(m) f(m) \omega(m)$ (bilinearity of scalar multiplication in $F_m$; commutativity of real multiplication); at a point $m \notin U$ all of these expressions are $0$ by definition of the extension. Since the two sides agree at every point, they are equal as sections.
> >
> > Therefore cutting off by a function supported in $U$ and extending by zero produces a global smooth section, linearly in the local section and compatibly with multiplication by global functions. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 4: gluing local connections with a partition of unity gives a global connection
> **Statement:** Let $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}$ be an open cover of $M$, let $\nabla_\alpha \in \mathcal{A}(E|_{U_\alpha})$ be a connection on the restricted bundle for each $\alpha \in I$, and let $\{\rho_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}$ be a smooth partition of unity subordinate to $\{U_\alpha\}$. For $s \in \Gamma(E)$ define
> $$\nabla s := \sum_{\alpha \in I} \widetilde{\rho_\alpha\,\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha})},$$
> the sum of the extensions by zero (Lemma 3, with $F = T^*M \otimes E$) of the $E$-valued 1-forms $\rho_\alpha\,\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha}) \in \Omega^1(U_\alpha; E)$. Then (i) for every $m \in M$ there is an open neighbourhood of $m$ on which all but finitely many summands vanish identically, so that $\nabla s$ is a well-defined element of $\Omega^1(M; E)$; and (ii) $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$. This is the meaning of the source's "$\nabla := \sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha \nabla_{U_\alpha}$".
>
> **Hint:** Local finiteness of the supports makes the sum finite near each point; the Leibniz rule for each $\nabla_\alpha$ produces $\rho_\alpha\,df \otimes s$, and these add up to $df \otimes s$ because $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha = 1$.
>
> **Why needed:** This is the existence part (a) of the theorem, given the local connections of Lemma 2.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We are given the cover, the local connections, and the partition of unity. We must show that the sum defining $\nabla s$ is locally finite and smooth, and that $\nabla$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and satisfies the Leibniz rule.
> >
> > **Step 0 — each summand is a global smooth $E$-valued 1-form.** Fix $\alpha$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$. The restriction $s|_{U_\alpha}$ lies in $\Gamma(U_\alpha; E)$ (restriction of a smooth map to an open subset is smooth), so $\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha}) \in \Omega^1(U_\alpha; E) = \Gamma(U_\alpha; T^*M \otimes E)$ (definition of a connection on $E|_{U_\alpha}$; the cotangent bundle of the open subset $U_\alpha$ is $T^*M|_{U_\alpha}$). Since $\operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha \subseteq U_\alpha$ (partition of unity subordinate to the cover), Lemma 3 with $F = T^*M \otimes E$, $U = U_\alpha$, $\rho = \rho_\alpha$, $\omega = \nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha})$ shows that $\omega_\alpha(s) := \widetilde{\rho_\alpha\,\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha})}$ is a smooth section of $T^*M \otimes E$ over $M$, that is, an element of $\Omega^1(M; E)$, vanishing identically on $M \setminus \operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha$.
> >
> > **Part (i) — local finiteness and smoothness of the sum.** Let $m \in M$. By the local finiteness clause of the partition of unity there is an open neighbourhood $W$ of $m$ meeting $\operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha$ for only finitely many $\alpha$, say for $\alpha \in \{\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_N\}$. For every other $\alpha$, $W \subseteq M \setminus \operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha$, so $\omega_\alpha(s)$ vanishes identically on $W$ by Step 0. Hence on $W$,
> > $$\sum_{\alpha \in I} \omega_\alpha(s)\Big|_W = \sum_{r=1}^N \omega_{\alpha_r}(s)\Big|_W \qquad \text{(all other summands are zero on } W),$$
> > a finite sum of smooth sections of $T^*M \otimes E$ over $W$, hence smooth on $W$. In particular the sum at each point $m$ has only finitely many non-zero terms, so $(\nabla s)(m) \in T^*_mM \otimes E_m$ is a well-defined finite sum, and $\nabla s$ is smooth near every point, hence $\nabla s \in \Omega^1(M; E)$. (The value of a finite sum does not depend on the order of summation, so no ordering of $I$ is needed.)
> >
> > **Part (ii), $\mathbb{R}$-linearity.** For $s, u \in \Gamma(E)$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$, and every $\alpha$,
> > $$\omega_\alpha(s + cu) = \widetilde{\rho_\alpha\,\nabla_\alpha\big((s + cu)|_{U_\alpha}\big)} = \widetilde{\rho_\alpha\,\big(\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha}) + c\,\nabla_\alpha(u|_{U_\alpha})\big)} = \omega_\alpha(s) + c\,\omega_\alpha(u) \qquad \text{(restriction is linear; } \mathbb{R}\text{-linearity of } \nabla_\alpha \text{; linearity of the extension by zero, Lemma 3).}$$
> > Summing over $\alpha$ — at each point a finite sum, by part (i) — gives $\nabla(s + cu) = \nabla s + c\,\nabla u$.
> >
> > **Part (ii), Leibniz rule.** Let $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$. Fix $\alpha$. On $U_\alpha$, $(fs)|_{U_\alpha} = f|_{U_\alpha} \cdot s|_{U_\alpha}$ with $f|_{U_\alpha} \in C^\infty(U_\alpha)$, so
> > $$\nabla_\alpha\big((fs)|_{U_\alpha}\big) = d(f|_{U_\alpha}) \otimes s|_{U_\alpha} + f|_{U_\alpha}\,\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha}) = (df)|_{U_\alpha} \otimes s|_{U_\alpha} + f|_{U_\alpha}\,\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha}) \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule for } \nabla_\alpha \text{ on } E|_{U_\alpha} \text{; locality of the differential, } d(f|_U) = (df)|_U).$$
> > Multiply by $\rho_\alpha$ and extend by zero:
> > $$\omega_\alpha(fs) = \widetilde{\rho_\alpha\,\big((df)|_{U_\alpha} \otimes s|_{U_\alpha}\big)} + \widetilde{\rho_\alpha\,\big(f|_{U_\alpha}\,\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha})\big)} = \rho_\alpha\,df \otimes s + f\,\omega_\alpha(s) \qquad \text{(linearity of the extension by zero; the module property of Lemma 3 applied to } f \text{; and, for the first term, the pointwise identity below).}$$
> > For the first term, at $m \in U_\alpha$ both $\widetilde{\rho_\alpha\,((df)|_{U_\alpha} \otimes s|_{U_\alpha})}(m)$ and $(\rho_\alpha\,df \otimes s)(m)$ equal $\rho_\alpha(m)\,df_m \otimes s(m)$; at $m \notin U_\alpha$ the extension is $0$ by definition, and $\rho_\alpha(m) = 0$ because $m \notin \operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha \subseteq U_\alpha$, so $(\rho_\alpha\,df \otimes s)(m) = 0$ as well. Now sum over $\alpha$, a finite sum at each point by part (i):
> > $$\nabla(fs) = \sum_\alpha \omega_\alpha(fs) = \sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha\,df \otimes s + f \sum_\alpha \omega_\alpha(s) = \Big(\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha\Big)\,df \otimes s + f\,\nabla s = df \otimes s + f\,\nabla s \qquad \text{(definition of } \nabla \text{; the identity just proved for each } \alpha \text{; rearrangement of a finite sum; } \textstyle\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha = 1 \text{ pointwise, the normalisation clause of the partition of unity).}$$
> >
> > Therefore $\nabla$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ satisfying the Leibniz rule, that is, $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$: the locally defined connections have been sewn into a global one, with the partition of unity supplying at each point a weighted average, with weights summing to one, of the finitely many local connections defined there. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 5: the difference of two connections is $C^\infty(M)$-linear
> **Statement:** Let $\nabla, \hat\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and define $T := \nabla - \hat\nabla \colon \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$, $T(s) := \nabla s - \hat\nabla s$. Then $T$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and $C^\infty(M)$-linear: $T(fs) = f\,T(s)$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$.
>
> **Hint:** Subtract the two Leibniz rules.
>
> **Why needed:** It is exactly the hypothesis of the tensoriality lemma, which converts $T$ into an element of $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$; that is part (b). (This is also Corollary 4(i) on [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle]]; it is reproved here because it is part of this page's theorem.)
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We are given two connections; we need to show that their difference commutes with multiplication by smooth functions.
> >
> > **$\mathbb{R}$-linearity.** $T$ is the difference of two $\mathbb{R}$-linear maps into the vector space $\Omega^1(M; E)$, hence $\mathbb{R}$-linear: $T(s + cu) = \nabla(s + cu) - \hat\nabla(s + cu) = (\nabla s + c\nabla u) - (\hat\nabla s + c\hat\nabla u) = T(s) + c\,T(u)$ ($\mathbb{R}$-linearity of $\nabla$ and $\hat\nabla$; regrouping).
> >
> > **$C^\infty(M)$-linearity.** For $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$,
> > $$T(fs) = \nabla(fs) - \hat\nabla(fs) = \big(df \otimes s + f\,\nabla s\big) - \big(df \otimes s + f\,\hat\nabla s\big) \qquad \text{(definition of } T \text{; Leibniz rule for } \nabla \text{ and for } \hat\nabla)$$
> > $$= f\,\nabla s - f\,\hat\nabla s = f\,(\nabla s - \hat\nabla s) = f\,T(s) \qquad \text{(the two terms } df \otimes s \text{ cancel; distributivity of pointwise multiplication; definition of } T).$$
> >
> > Therefore the difference of two connections is $C^\infty(M)$-linear; the inhomogeneous terms of the two Leibniz rules, being identical, cancel. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 6: shifting a connection by an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued 1-form gives a connection, and the shift is additive in the form
> **Statement:** Let $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and $a, b \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$. (i) The map $\nabla + a$, $(\nabla + a)s := \nabla s + a \cdot s$, is a connection on $E$. (ii) $\nabla + 0 = \nabla$, and $(\nabla + a) + b = \nabla + (a + b)$.
>
> **Hint:** For (i), $a \cdot (fs) = f\,(a \cdot s)$ because $a_m$ is a linear map on $E_m$; for (ii), the contraction is linear in $a$.
>
> **Why needed:** (i) is part (c) of the theorem; (ii) is the pair of axioms (A1), (A2) of an affine space.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We are given a connection and two $\operatorname{End}E$-valued 1-forms; we must verify that $\nabla + a$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and Leibniz, and the two identities of (ii).
> >
> > **Step 0 — $\nabla + a$ maps into $\Omega^1(M; E)$.** For $s \in \Gamma(E)$, $\nabla s \in \Omega^1(M; E)$ (definition of a connection) and $a \cdot s \in \Omega^1(M; E)$ (the contraction of [[Def - Bundle-Valued Differential Forms]] sends $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E) \times \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ and is smooth because, in a local frame, $a \cdot s$ has components $\sum_j a_{ij}\,\sigma_j$ where $a = (a_{ij})$ is the matrix of 1-forms of $a$ and $\sigma$ the component column of $s$). Their sum is in $\Omega^1(M; E)$.
> >
> > **(i), $\mathbb{R}$-linearity.** For $s, u \in \Gamma(E)$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$, $(\nabla + a)(s + cu) = \nabla s + c\,\nabla u + a \cdot s + c\,(a \cdot u) = (\nabla + a)s + c\,(\nabla + a)u$ ($\mathbb{R}$-linearity of $\nabla$; linearity of $a_m$ on $E_m$ at each point; regrouping).
> >
> > **(i), Leibniz rule.** For $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$, at each point $m$ we have $a_m\big(f(m)\,s(m)\big) = f(m)\,a_m\big(s(m)\big)$ because $a_m \in T^*_mM \otimes \operatorname{End}(E_m)$ acts linearly on $E_m$; that is, $a \cdot (fs) = f\,(a \cdot s)$. Hence
> > $$(\nabla + a)(fs) = \nabla(fs) + a \cdot (fs) = df \otimes s + f\,\nabla s + f\,(a \cdot s) = df \otimes s + f\,(\nabla + a)s \qquad \text{(definition of } \nabla + a \text{; Leibniz rule for } \nabla \text{ and } a \cdot (fs) = f\,(a \cdot s) \text{; distributivity; definition of } \nabla + a).$$
> > So $\nabla + a \in \mathcal{A}(E)$.
> >
> > **(ii).** At each point $m$ and for each $s$, $0_m(s(m)) = 0$, so $0 \cdot s = 0$ and $(\nabla + 0)s = \nabla s$; and $(a + b)_m(s(m)) = a_m(s(m)) + b_m(s(m))$ because the contraction $\operatorname{End}(E_m) \otimes E_m \to E_m$ is linear in its first argument, so $(a + b) \cdot s = a \cdot s + b \cdot s$ and
> > $$\big((\nabla + a) + b\big)s = (\nabla + a)s + b \cdot s = \nabla s + a \cdot s + b \cdot s = \nabla s + (a + b) \cdot s = \big(\nabla + (a + b)\big)s \qquad \text{(definition of the shift, twice; associativity of addition in } \Omega^1(M; E) \text{; linearity of the contraction in the form).}$$
> >
> > Therefore adding a tensor to a connection produces a connection — the added term is $C^\infty(M)$-linear and so contributes nothing to the inhomogeneous term of the Leibniz rule — and the additive group of $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ acts on $\mathcal{A}(E)$ by these shifts. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 7: every fibre vector is the value of a global section; an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued 1-form annihilating all sections is zero
> **Statement:** (i) For every $m \in M$ and $v \in E_m$ there is a global section $s \in \Gamma(E)$ with $s(m) = v$. (ii) If $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ satisfies $a \cdot s = 0$ for every $s \in \Gamma(E)$, then $a = 0$. Consequently, if $a, a' \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ satisfy $a \cdot s = a' \cdot s$ for every $s$, then $a = a'$.
>
> **Hint:** Expand $v$ in a local frame near $m$, multiply the resulting local section by a bump function equal to $1$ near $m$, and extend by zero (Lemma 3).
>
> **Why needed:** It supplies the uniqueness of the tensor $a$ in part (b), independently of the uniqueness clause of the tensoriality lemma, and hence the freeness of the action in axiom (A3).
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Part (i).** Let $m \in M$ and $v \in E_m$. By the definition of a vector bundle ([[Def - Vector Bundle]]) there is a local trivialisation over an open neighbourhood $U$ of $m$, hence a local frame $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$ over $U$ (Notation). Since $e(m)$ is a basis of $E_m$, there are real numbers $c_1, \dots, c_k$ with $v = \sum_j c_j\,e_j(m)$. The section $u := \sum_j c_j e_j \in \Gamma(U; E)$ (constant coefficients; smooth as a linear combination of smooth sections) has $u(m) = v$. By [[Thm - Existence of Smooth Bump Functions]] — for every point $m$ of an open set $U$ there is a smooth $\beta \colon M \to [0, 1]$ with $\beta \equiv 1$ on a neighbourhood of $m$ and $\operatorname{supp}\beta \subseteq U$ — choose such a $\beta$, and set $s := \widetilde{\beta\,u}$, the extension by zero of Lemma 3 with $F = E$. Then $s \in \Gamma(E)$ by Lemma 3, and $s(m) = \beta(m)\,u(m) = 1 \cdot v = v$ (definition of the extension at $m \in U$; $\beta(m) = 1$).
> >
> > **Part (ii).** Assume $a \cdot s = 0$ for all $s \in \Gamma(E)$; we need to show $a_m = 0$ for every $m$, that is, $a_m(v) = 0 \in T^*_mM \otimes E_m$ for every $v \in E_m$. Fix $m$ and $v$, and choose $s$ with $s(m) = v$ by part (i). Then
> > $$a_m(v) = a_m\big(s(m)\big) = (a \cdot s)(m) = 0 \qquad \text{(choice of } s \text{; definition of the contraction; hypothesis).}$$
> > As $m$ and $v$ were arbitrary, $a = 0$. For the consequence, apply this to $a - a'$, using $(a - a') \cdot s = a \cdot s - a' \cdot s$ (linearity of the contraction in the form, Lemma 6(ii)).
> >
> > Therefore global sections separate the fibres well enough to determine an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued form from its action on sections. $\blacksquare$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $\pi \colon E \to M$ be a smooth real vector bundle of rank $k$. We must prove (a) that $\mathcal{A}(E)$ is non-empty; (b) that for $\nabla, \hat\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ the difference is $C^\infty(M)$-linear and equals $s \mapsto a \cdot s$ for a unique $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$; (c) that $\nabla + a \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ for every $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$; and then that $\mathcal{A}(E)$ with the shift map is an affine space modelled on $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$. Finally we prove the Corollary.
>
> **Step 0 — preconditions.** By the definition of a vector bundle, every point of $M$ has an open neighbourhood over which $E$ is trivial; the trivialising open sets therefore form an open cover $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}$ of $M$, with local trivialisations $\psi_\alpha \colon E|_{U_\alpha} \to U_\alpha \times \mathbb{R}^k$. Because $M$ is Hausdorff and second countable (standing hypothesis), [[Thm - Existence of Smooth Partitions of Unity]] — for every open cover $\{U_\alpha\}$ of a smooth manifold there is a smooth partition of unity $\{\rho_\alpha\}$ subordinate to it, with $\operatorname{supp}\rho_\alpha \subseteq U_\alpha$, locally finite supports, and $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha = 1$ — provides such a partition of unity $\{\rho_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in I}$.
>
> **Part I — existence (a).**
>
> **Construct local connections.** For each $\alpha \in I$, Lemma 2 applied to the trivialisation $\psi_\alpha$ gives a connection $\nabla_\alpha := \nabla_{U_\alpha} \in \mathcal{A}(E|_{U_\alpha})$, the coefficientwise derivative in the frame of $\psi_\alpha$.
>
> **Glue.** Lemma 4 applied to the cover $\{U_\alpha\}$, the connections $\{\nabla_\alpha\}$, and the partition of unity $\{\rho_\alpha\}$ of Step 0 produces $\nabla := \sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha \nabla_\alpha \in \mathcal{A}(E)$, defined by $\nabla s = \sum_\alpha \widetilde{\rho_\alpha\,\nabla_\alpha(s|_{U_\alpha})}$. Hence $\mathcal{A}(E) \neq \varnothing$, which is (a).
>
> **Part II — differences are tensors (b).** Let $\nabla, \hat\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$. By Lemma 5, $T := \nabla - \hat\nabla$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-linear and $C^\infty(M)$-linear map $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$. By the [[Thm - Tensoriality Lemma for C-Infinity-Linear Maps|tensoriality lemma]] — if $A \colon \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^p(M; F)$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and satisfies $A(fs) = f\,A(s)$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$, then there is a unique $a \in \Omega^p(M; \operatorname{Hom}(E, F))$ with $A(s) = a \cdot s$ for all $s$ — applied with $p = 1$ and $F = E$, so that $\operatorname{Hom}(E, F) = \operatorname{End}E$, there is $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ with $\nabla s - \hat\nabla s = a \cdot s$ for all $s \in \Gamma(E)$. Uniqueness of $a$ is asserted by the tensoriality lemma and follows independently from Lemma 7(ii): if $a'$ also satisfies $\nabla s - \hat\nabla s = a' \cdot s$ for all $s$, then $a \cdot s = a' \cdot s$ for all $s$, so $a = a'$. This is (b).
>
> **Part III — shifts are connections (c).** Let $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$. By Lemma 6(i), $\nabla + a \in \mathcal{A}(E)$. This is (c).
>
> **Part IV — the affine structure.** We verify that the map $\mathcal{A}(E) \times \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E) \to \mathcal{A}(E)$, $(\nabla, a) \mapsto \nabla + a$ — which lands in $\mathcal{A}(E)$ by Part III — satisfies the three axioms of an affine space modelled on the vector space $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ (Notation). The set $\mathcal{A}(E)$ is non-empty by Part I.
>
> *(A1)* $\nabla + 0 = \nabla$ for every $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$: this is Lemma 6(ii).
>
> *(A2)* $(\nabla + a) + b = \nabla + (a + b)$ for all $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and $a, b \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$: this is Lemma 6(ii).
>
> *(A3), existence.* Given $\nabla, \hat\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$, Part II applied to the ordered pair $(\hat\nabla, \nabla)$ gives $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ with $\hat\nabla s - \nabla s = a \cdot s$ for all $s$, that is, $\hat\nabla s = \nabla s + a \cdot s = (\nabla + a)s$ for all $s$ (definition of the shift), so $\hat\nabla = \nabla + a$.
>
> *(A3), uniqueness.* If $\hat\nabla = \nabla + a = \nabla + a'$ with $a, a' \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, then for every $s$, $\nabla s + a \cdot s = \nabla s + a' \cdot s$ (definition of the shift), hence $a \cdot s = a' \cdot s$ (cancel $\nabla s$ in the vector space $\Omega^1(M; E)$), hence $a = a'$ by Lemma 7(ii).
>
> Therefore $\mathcal{A}(E)$, with the shift action of $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, is an affine space modelled on $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, and the notation $\nabla - \hat\nabla$ for the unique $a$ with $\nabla = \hat\nabla + a$ is justified. This proves the theorem.
>
> **Part V — the Corollary.** *(i)* Fix $\nabla_0 \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and let $\Phi(a) := \nabla_0 + a$, a map $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E) \to \mathcal{A}(E)$ by Part III. It is surjective by (A3)-existence (every $\nabla$ is $\nabla_0 + a$ for some $a$) and injective by (A3)-uniqueness ($\nabla_0 + a = \nabla_0 + a'$ forces $a = a'$); its inverse sends $\nabla$ to the unique $a$ with $\nabla = \nabla_0 + a$, which is $\nabla - \nabla_0$ by definition of the difference. *(ii)* This is Lemma 1.
>
> **Conclusion.** Connections on $E$ exist, any two differ by a unique $\operatorname{End}E$-valued 1-form, any $\operatorname{End}E$-valued 1-form may be added to a connection, and these facts make $\mathcal{A}(E)$ an affine space modelled on $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$; choosing any base connection identifies $\mathcal{A}(E)$ with that vector space, and no such choice is canonical unless $E$ carries a preferred connection. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Riemannian geometry: the contorsion tensor.** On the tangent bundle $TM$ of a Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$, the theorem says that every connection is $\nabla^{LC} + a$ for the [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita connection]] $\nabla^{LC}$ and a unique $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}TM)$, that is, a $(1, 2)$-tensor $a(X)Y$. Show that $\nabla^{LC} + a$ is metric if and only if $g(a(X)Y, Z) + g(Y, a(X)Z) = 0$ (the third Target), and that it is torsion-free ([[Def - Torsion Tensor]]) if and only if $a(X)Y = a(Y)X$; conclude that the Levi-Civita connection is the unique point of the affine subspace of metric connections at which the alternation of $a$ vanishes, and that a metric connection with torsion $T$ has $a$ equal to the contorsion tensor $a(X)Y = \tfrac12\big(T(X, Y) + \ldots\big)$, to be worked out from the Koszul-type formula. The theorem applies because $TM$ is a vector bundle; what is non-obvious is that the Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry ([[Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Full Proof)]]) becomes a statement about the intersection of two affine subspaces — the metric connections and the torsion-free connections — of the affine space $\mathcal{A}(TM)$, and that intersection is a single point.

**Electrodynamics: gauge potentials have no absolute meaning.** In chapter VII a $U(1)$ gauge field ([[Def - U(1) Gauge Field and Electromagnetic Connection]]) is a connection on a Hermitian line bundle $L$, and connections on $L$ compatible with the Hermitian structure form, by the complex analogue of this theorem, an affine space modelled on $i\Omega^1(M)$ — on a trivialised patch, the space of "vector potentials" $A$. Show directly from the theorem that a global vector potential exists as a 1-form only after a base connection is chosen, that the physically meaningful object is the difference $\nabla - \nabla_0 \in i\Omega^1(M)$ of two connections, and that the curvature $F = dA$ on a patch is independent of the base point because it is $F_{\nabla_0 + a} = F_{\nabla_0} + da$ (the quadratic term $a \wedge a$ vanishes for a line bundle, since $\operatorname{End}L$ is commutative). The theorem applies because $L$ is a vector bundle; the non-obvious point is that the familiar statement "the potential is defined only up to gauge" has, underneath it, the more basic statement "the potential is a coordinate on an affine space and coordinates require a choice of origin".

**Ordinary differential equations: linear systems on an interval as connections.** On the trivial bundle $[0, 1] \times \mathbb{R}^k \to [0, 1]$ the theorem identifies $\mathcal{A}$ with $\{d/dt + B(t)\,dt : B \colon [0, 1] \to \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}) \text{ smooth}\}$, an affine space over the matrix-valued functions $B$, and the parallel sections $\nabla s = 0$ are the solutions of the linear system $\dot\sigma + B\sigma = 0$. Show that the map $B \mapsto$ (fundamental solution matrix at $t = 1$) is a map from this affine space to $GL_k(\mathbb{R})$, that it is not affine, and that its derivative at $B = 0$ is $-\int_0^1 \delta B(t)\,dt$; this is the infinitesimal form of the path-ordered exponential of chapter V, [[Def - Path-Ordered Exponential]]. The theorem applies because the bundle is trivial; the non-obvious point is that the linearity of the ODE in $\sigma$ and the affineness of $\mathcal{A}$ in $B$ are different things, and that the holonomy, the object gauge theory cares about, is a non-linear function on the affine space.

**Global analysis: Sobolev completions of the space of connections.** Chapter IX defines Sobolev spaces of sections, [[Def - Sobolev Space of Sections]], and chapter XI does its analysis on the space of connections of Sobolev class $W^{k, 2}$, which is defined as $\nabla_0 + W^{k, 2}(T^*M \otimes \operatorname{End}E)$ for a smooth base connection $\nabla_0$. Show, using only this theorem and the fact that the difference of two smooth connections is a smooth section of $T^*M \otimes \operatorname{End}E$, that this definition does not depend on the choice of $\nabla_0$ among smooth connections, and that the resulting space is an affine Hilbert space — a Hilbert space that has forgotten its origin. The theorem applies to the smooth connections; the non-obvious point is that the affine structure is what allows a space of differential operators, which is not a vector space, to be completed in a norm at all.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Existence of Riemannian Metrics via Partitions of Unity|Existence of Riemannian metrics]]** — the same argument with a convex cone in place of an affine space. There the local objects are the pull-backs $\varphi_\alpha^*\bar g$ of the Euclidean metric through charts, glued by $g = \sum_\alpha \psi_\alpha\,\varphi_\alpha^*\bar g$; positive-definiteness survives because a sum of positive-definite forms with non-negative weights, at least one positive, is positive-definite. Here the local objects are the product connections $\nabla_\alpha$ of trivialisations, glued by $\nabla = \sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha \nabla_\alpha$; the Leibniz rule survives because it is affine in $\nabla$ and $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha = 1$. The difference between "cone" and "affine space" is visible in what fails: two metrics cannot be subtracted to give a metric, but two connections can be subtracted to give a tensor, and a connection can be combined with any real weights summing to one, not only non-negative ones.

- **[[Thm - Existence of Connections on Principal Bundles|Existence of connections on principal bundles]]** (chapter IV) — the same pattern one level up. A connection on a principal $G$-bundle $P$ is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form $\omega$ on $P$ with $\omega(\xi_P) = \xi$ and $R_g^*\omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\omega$; the first condition is inhomogeneous and the second is homogeneous, so again the solution set is affine, modelled on the horizontal $\operatorname{Ad}$-equivariant $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-forms, which are the same as $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{ad}P)$. Existence is again by gluing local connections (the pull-backs of the Maurer–Cartan form through local trivialisations) with a partition of unity pulled back from $M$, and the reason gluing works is again the affineness of the conditions. The vector-bundle theorem of this page is recovered from the principal one for the frame bundle $P = \operatorname{Fr}(E)$, with $\operatorname{ad}P = \operatorname{End}E$.

- **[[Def - Affine Connection on a Vector Bundle|Affine connections in the Riemannian-geometry chapter]]** — the same object, and the same affine structure, in the two-argument formulation $(X, s) \mapsto \nabla_X s$. In that formulation the theorem reads: the difference of two connections is a $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear map $(X, s) \mapsto a(X)s$, that is, a section of $T^*M \otimes \operatorname{End}E$, and every such section can be added; the difference tensor of two affine connections on $TM$ is the classical object that appears in the study of projectively and conformally equivalent connections, and its symmetric and antisymmetric parts control the change of geodesics and of torsion respectively.

- **[[Thm - Curvature of a Shifted Connection|Curvature of a shifted connection]]** — the first computation that uses the affine structure. Because every connection is $\nabla + a$, the curvature of every connection is determined by the curvature of one and the formula $F_{\nabla + a} = F_\nabla + d^\nabla a + a \wedge a$; this is the vector-bundle form of the statement that curvature is a quadratic polynomial on the affine space of connections, and it is the formula that chapter VII differentiates to obtain the Yang–Mills equation.

- **[[Def - Gauge Group of a Vector Bundle|The gauge group]] and [[Thm - Gauge Action on Connection Matrices|its action on connections]]** — the group acting on this affine space. The gauge group acts by affine transformations, $\nabla^g = \nabla + g^{-1}(\nabla^{\operatorname{End}}g)$, and the study of gauge theory is the study of the quotient of the affine space $\mathcal{A}(E)$ by this action. That $\mathcal{A}(E)$ is affine, hence contractible, is why all the topology of the quotient $\mathcal{A}(E)/\mathcal{G}(E)$ comes from the group and not from the space.

- **[[Ex - Convex Combinations of Connections are Connections|Convex combinations of connections]]** and **[[Ex - Every Matrix of 1-Forms is a Connection Matrix|every matrix of 1-forms is a connection matrix]]** — the two exercises of this section that drill the two halves of the proof: the first is Lemma 1 and the partition-of-unity version of it, the second is Lemma 2 together with parts (b) and (c) read in a local frame.

> [!tip] The Configuration Space of Yang–Mills Theory *(from Mathematical Physics)*
> Once $\mathcal{A}(E)$ is known to be an affine space, a functional on connections such as the Yang–Mills action $\tfrac12 \int_M |F_\nabla|^2\,\mathrm{vol}$ becomes, after choosing a base point, a function on the vector space $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, and its critical points can be found by the calculus of variations. See [[Def - Yang-Mills Lagrangian and Action Functional]] and [[Thm - Yang-Mills Equation from the Action Principle]].

> [!tip] Transgression and the Chern–Weil Theorem *(from Characteristic Classes)*
> Any two connections $\nabla_0, \nabla_1$ are joined by the segment $\nabla_t = \nabla_0 + t\,(\nabla_1 - \nabla_0)$, $t \in [0, 1]$, inside $\mathcal{A}(E)$; differentiating an invariant polynomial of the curvature along this segment produces the transgression form whose exterior derivative is the difference of the two Chern–Weil forms. > [!tip] Moduli Spaces as Quotients of an Affine Space *(from Gauge Theory)*
> The moduli spaces of chapters XI and XIII — the **Seiberg–Witten moduli space** and the **instanton moduli space** — are subsets of the quotient $\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{G}$ of an affine space (completed in a Sobolev norm) by the gauge group, cut out by a non-linear equation. The affine structure is what makes the linearisation of that equation at a solution a well-defined linear operator between fixed vector spaces, which is the starting point of the Fredholm theory of chapter X.
