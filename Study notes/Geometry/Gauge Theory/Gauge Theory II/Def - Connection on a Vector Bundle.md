# Notation

Throughout, manifolds are smooth ($C^\infty$), Hausdorff, and second countable, and $M$ denotes a manifold of dimension $n$. A [[Def - Vector Bundle|vector bundle]] $\pi \colon E \to M$ is real and smooth, of rank $k$, with fibre $E_m := \pi^{-1}(m)$ over $m \in M$; $\Gamma(E) = \Gamma(M; E)$ is the space of smooth [[Def - Section of a Vector Bundle|sections]] of $E$, a module over the ring $C^\infty(M)$ of smooth real-valued functions on $M$, and $\Gamma(U; E)$ is the space of smooth sections of $E$ over an open subset $U \subset M$. For $p \geq 0$ we write, as on [[Def - Bundle-Valued Differential Forms]],
$$\Omega^p(M; E) := \Gamma\big(\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E\big)$$
for the space of $E$-valued differential $p$-forms; in particular $\Omega^0(M; E) = \Gamma(E)$ and $\Omega^1(M; E) = \Gamma(T^*M \otimes E)$. An element $\omega \in \Omega^1(M; E)$ assigns to each $m \in M$ an element $\omega_m \in T^*_mM \otimes E_m = \operatorname{Hom}(T_mM, E_m)$, so $\omega_m(v) \in E_m$ for $v \in T_mM$; for $v \in T_mM$ the **interior product** (contraction, [[Def - Interior Product (Contraction with a Vector Field)]]) is $\iota_v \omega := \omega_m(v) \in E_m$, determined on decomposable elements by $\iota_v(\alpha \otimes u) = \alpha(v)\, u$ for $\alpha \in T^*_mM$ and $u \in E_m$. For a vector field $X \in \mathfrak{X}(M) := \Gamma(TM)$ ([[Def - Vector Field on a Manifold]]), $\iota_X \omega \in \Gamma(E)$ is the section $m \mapsto \omega_m(X(m))$. For $f \in C^\infty(M)$, $df \in \Omega^1(M)$ is its [[Def - The Differential of a Function as a 1-Form|differential]], $df(v) = v(f)$ for a tangent vector $v$ regarded as a [[Def - Derivation at a Point|derivation]], and $df \otimes s \in \Omega^1(M; E)$ for $s \in \Gamma(E)$ is the $E$-valued 1-form $m \mapsto df_m \otimes s(m)$.

A [[Def - Local Frame|local frame]] of $E$ over an open $U \subset M$ is a $k$-tuple $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$ of sections $e_j \in \Gamma(U; E)$ such that $e(m) = (e_1(m), \dots, e_k(m))$ is a basis of $E_m$ for every $m \in U$; the source calls a local frame a "pointwise base", and identifies it with a [[Def - Local Trivialization|local trivialisation]] $\psi_U$ via $\psi_U^{-1}(m, x) = e(m) \cdot x := \sum_{j=1}^k x_j e_j(m)$. Every section $s \in \Gamma(U; E)$ is then $s = e\sigma := \sum_{j=1}^k \sigma_j e_j$ for a unique smooth map $\sigma = (\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_k)^{\mathsf T} \colon U \to \mathbb{R}^k$, the **local representation** of $s$ ([[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]]); under a change of frame $e = e' \cdot g$ with $g \colon U \cap U' \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ the representations change by $\sigma' = g\sigma$. (The source prints the target of $g$ as $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ on p. 6; this is a typo for $GL_k(\mathbb{R})$, where $k$ is the rank, and we use the corrected form.) In a [[Def - Coordinate Chart and Atlas|chart]] $(U; x_1, \dots, x_n)$ of $M$, $\partial_i = \partial/\partial x_i$ are the coordinate vector fields and $dx_1, \dots, dx_n$ the coordinate 1-forms, with $dx_i(\partial_j) = \delta_{ij}$.

The symbol $\nabla$ (read "nabla") always denotes the object defined on this page; $\mathcal{A}(E)$ denotes the set of all of them. The letters $f, h$ denote smooth functions, $s, t, u$ sections of $E$, $X, Y$ vector fields, $v$ a tangent vector at a point, and $\theta, \alpha$ scalar 1-forms. The full symbol registry of the chapter is on [[Gauge Theory II — Vector Bundles, Covariant Derivatives, and Curvature]].

> [!warning] Convention: two names, two formulations, two notations for the same object
> Haydys (Definition 8, p. 7) defines a *covariant derivative* as a map $\nabla \colon \Gamma(E) \to \Gamma(T^*M \otimes E)$ and from then on uses the word *connection* interchangeably; he writes $\Omega^p(E)$ where this series writes $\Omega^p(M; E)$. The Riemannian-geometry chapter of this vault, [[Def - Affine Connection on a Vector Bundle]], defines the same notion as a two-argument operation $(X, s) \mapsto \nabla_X s$ with $C^\infty(M)$-linearity in $X$ and the Leibniz rule in $s$. The two formulations are equivalent — the dictionary is $\nabla_X s = \iota_X(\nabla s)$ — and the equivalence is proved in full on this page (Proposition 1 below). Both names, **connection** and **covariant derivative**, are used for the object of this page throughout the series.

# Axiom Motivation

We begin with the question the definition answers. The reader knows from analysis that the derivative is the single most useful operation one can perform on a function, and it is natural to ask whether sections of a vector bundle can be differentiated too. Sections generalise functions — a smooth function $f \colon M \to \mathbb{R}$ is a section of the product bundle $M \times \mathbb{R}$, and a vector field is a section of $TM$ — so the question is not idle: without some way of differentiating sections there is no calculus on bundles, no notion of a section being "constant along a curve", no equation of motion for a field with values in a bundle.

**The naive definition fails.** Recall how the derivative of a function $f \colon M \to \mathbb{R}$ at $m \in M$ in the direction $v \in T_mM$ is computed. One chooses a smooth curve $\gamma \colon (-\varepsilon, \varepsilon) \to M$ with $\gamma(0) = m$ and $\dot\gamma(0) = v$, and sets
$$df(v) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(\gamma(t)) - f(m)}{t}. \tag{1}$$
This limit makes sense because $f(\gamma(t))$ and $f(m)$ are both real numbers, and real numbers can be subtracted. Now try to replace $f$ by a section $s$ of a vector bundle $E$. The numerator becomes $s(\gamma(t)) - s(m)$, and here the construction breaks: $s(\gamma(t))$ lies in the fibre $E_{\gamma(t)}$ and $s(m)$ lies in the fibre $E_m$, and for $t \neq 0$ these are *different* vector spaces. The definition of a vector bundle provides no way to subtract a vector in one fibre from a vector in another. On a product bundle $M \times \mathbb{R}^k$ every fibre is the same copy of $\mathbb{R}^k$, so the subtraction can be performed and (1) gives the coefficientwise derivative; but even there the identification of fibres depends on the product structure, and a different trivialisation of the same bundle identifies the fibres differently and produces a different "derivative" (this frame dependence is exhibited explicitly in the non-example of the Examples section). On a bundle with no global trivialisation there is not even a preferred identification to start from. Formula (1) is therefore not available, and the derivative of a section has to be *defined*, not computed.

**What a derivative must do.** Since we cannot mimic (1), we ask instead what properties any reasonable derivative of sections must have, and we take those properties as the axioms. The derivative of a section $s$ should be an object that records, at each point $m$ and for each direction $v \in T_mM$, the "rate of change of $s$ at $m$ in the direction $v$", and that rate of change should be a vector in the fibre $E_m$ (as $df(v)$ is a number when $f$ is a function). An object assigning linearly to each $v \in T_mM$ a vector in $E_m$, smoothly in $m$, is exactly a section of $T^*M \otimes E$, that is, an element of $\Omega^1(M; E)$. So the derivative should be a map $\nabla \colon \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$, and $\nabla s$ plays for $s$ the role $df$ plays for $f$. Which properties of $d$ should be kept? The derivative of a sum should be the sum of the derivatives, so $\nabla$ should be additive. And the most basic property of any derivative — the property that encodes that differentiation is a *first-order* operation, seeing $s$ and its first variation but nothing else — is the product rule. Products of two sections are not defined, but the product of a function $f$ with a section $s$ is, and for it the product rule reads
$$\nabla(fs) = df \otimes s + f\, \nabla s. \tag{2}$$
Here $df \otimes s$ is the term recording the variation of the scalar factor $f$, and $f \nabla s$ records the variation of $s$ scaled by $f$. This is the Leibniz rule, and it is the whole axiom: a connection is an additive map $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ satisfying (2). Nothing else is imposed.

**Per-clause failure analysis.** The definition has two clauses, $\mathbb{R}$-linearity and the Leibniz rule (2). We examine what each contributes and what fails without it.

*The Leibniz rule (2), and specifically the term $df \otimes s$.* Suppose we dropped the first term on the right of (2) and asked only for $\nabla(fs) = f \nabla s$ for all smooth $f$. Then $\nabla$ would be $C^\infty(M)$-linear, and such a map is not a derivative at all but a tensor: it is given by a bundle map, $\nabla s = a \cdot s$ for a fixed $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ (this is the content of the tensoriality lemma, [[Thm - Tensoriality Lemma for C-Infinity-Linear Maps]], proved later in the chapter; the special case relevant here is checked directly in the non-example below). To see concretely why such a map cannot be called a derivative, consider the product line bundle $E = M \times \mathbb{R}$, whose sections are the functions $f \in C^\infty(M)$, each of the form $f = f \cdot \mathbb{1}$ for the constant section $\mathbb{1}$. A $C^\infty(M)$-linear map gives $\nabla f = \nabla(f \cdot \mathbb{1}) = f\, \nabla \mathbb{1}$, so $(\nabla f)(m)$ is determined by the single number $f(m)$ and the fixed 1-form $\nabla \mathbb{1}$; a function with $f(m) = 0$ but $df_m \neq 0$, such as a coordinate function centred at $m$, gets $(\nabla f)(m) = 0$. The map does not see the variation of $f$ at all. The term $df \otimes s$ is precisely what forces $\nabla$ to see first derivatives, and it is what makes the difference $\nabla - \hat\nabla$ of two connections a tensor (Corollary 4 below) while each connection separately is not: the derivative terms cancel in the difference. Replacing $df \otimes s$ by $c\, df \otimes s$ with a constant $c \neq 0$ changes nothing of substance — $c^{-1} \nabla$ then satisfies (2) — and $c = 0$ is the tensor case just discussed; the normalisation $c = 1$ is the one for which $\nabla = d$ on functions.

*$\mathbb{R}$-linearity.* Homogeneity is already a consequence of (2): for a constant function $f \equiv c$ we have $df = 0$, so (2) gives $\nabla(cs) = c\, \nabla s$. The genuine content of the linearity clause is therefore *additivity*, $\nabla(s + t) = \nabla s + \nabla t$. The Leibniz identity by itself relates $\nabla s$ only to $\nabla(fs)$, that is, to sections lying on the same $C^\infty(M)$-line as $s$; on a bundle of rank at least two, or on any bundle once we compare a section with one that is not a multiple of it, it says nothing about how $\nabla$ treats a sum, and so additivity has to be imposed separately. What is excluded by the two clauses together is any non-linear candidate: for instance, given a bundle metric $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ on $E$, a scalar 1-form $\theta$, a fixed section $u$, and a connection $\nabla$, the map $D s := \nabla s + \lVert s \rVert^2\, \theta \otimes u$ is a well-defined map $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ but it is not additive — $D(s + t) - Ds - Dt = 2\langle s, t \rangle\, \theta \otimes u$, which is non-zero as soon as $\langle s, t \rangle \theta \neq 0$ and $u \neq 0$ — and it also fails the Leibniz rule already for the constant function $f \equiv 2$, since $D(2s) = 2\nabla s + 4\lVert s \rVert^2 \theta \otimes u \neq 2 Ds$ whenever $\lVert s \rVert^2 \theta \otimes u \neq 0$. Additivity is what makes $\nabla$ a *linear* first-order differential operator, which is what every later construction uses: the local form $\nabla = d + A$ with a matrix of 1-forms $A$ ([[Def - Connection Matrix and Local Form of a Connection]]) depends on expanding $\nabla(\sum_j \sigma_j e_j)$ as a sum, and the affine structure of the space of connections ([[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]]) depends on the difference of two connections being additive.

*Why nothing more is imposed.* One might expect further axioms — that $\nabla$ be local (the value of $\nabla s$ near $m$ should depend only on $s$ near $m$), or that $\nabla$ be expressible in coordinates as "$d$ plus lower-order terms". Both are theorems, not axioms: locality is Lemma 2 below, a consequence of the Leibniz rule alone, and the local form $d + A$ is derived on the connection-matrix page. Any further condition one might add — flatness, compatibility with a metric, torsion-freeness on $TM$ — is a genuine restriction that singles out special connections rather than characterising what a derivative is, and each is introduced as its own definition when needed.

A reader who has followed this far could reconstruct the definition unaided: a derivative of sections is a map $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ that is additive and satisfies the product rule (2), and nothing about it is arbitrary except the name.

# The Definition

> **Definition (connection, covariant derivative).** Let $\pi \colon E \to M$ be a smooth real vector bundle. A **connection** on $E$ — equivalently, a **covariant derivative** on $E$; the two words are used interchangeably in this series, following Haydys — is a map
> $$\nabla \colon \Gamma(E) \longrightarrow \Omega^1(M; E) = \Gamma(T^*M \otimes E)$$
> such that
>
> 1. **($\mathbb{R}$-linearity)** $\nabla(s + t) = \nabla s + \nabla t$ and $\nabla(cs) = c\, \nabla s$ for all $s, t \in \Gamma(E)$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}$;
> 2. **(Leibniz rule)** $\nabla(fs) = df \otimes s + f\, \nabla s$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$.
>
> The $E$-valued 1-form $\nabla s$ is the **covariant derivative of $s$**. The set of all connections on $E$ is denoted $\mathcal{A}(E)$.

This is Haydys's Definition 8 (equation (9) there is the Leibniz rule). As shown in the Axiom Motivation, the homogeneity half of clause 1 follows from clause 2 applied to constant functions, so the definition could be stated with "additive" in place of "$\mathbb{R}$-linear"; we keep the usual wording.

**The smallest concrete case.** Let $E = M \times \mathbb{R}$ be the product line bundle. A section is a smooth function, $\Gamma(E) = C^\infty(M)$ (the section is $m \mapsto (m, f(m))$, which we identify with $f$), and an $E$-valued 1-form is an ordinary 1-form, $\Omega^1(M; E) = \Omega^1(M)$. The exterior derivative $d \colon C^\infty(M) \to \Omega^1(M)$ is a connection: it is $\mathbb{R}$-linear, and $d(fs) = s\, df + f\, ds = df \otimes s + f\, ds$ for functions $f, s$ by the product rule for the differential of a product of functions (at each $m$ and each $v \in T_mM$, $d(fs)(v) = v(fs) = f(m)\, v(s) + s(m)\, v(f)$ because a tangent vector is a derivation at $m$, [[Def - Derivation at a Point]]). More generally, for any fixed $\theta \in \Omega^1(M)$ the map $\nabla s := ds + \theta\, s$ is a connection: $\mathbb{R}$-linearity is inherited from $d$ and from multiplication by $\theta$, and
$$\nabla(fs) = d(fs) + \theta\, fs = s\, df + f\, ds + f\, \theta\, s = df \otimes s + f\,(ds + \theta s) = df \otimes s + f\, \nabla s \qquad \text{(product rule for } d \text{; regrouping).}$$
We will see on [[Def - Connection Matrix and Local Form of a Connection]] that every connection on the product line bundle is of this form, so on a trivial line bundle a connection is the same thing as a choice of 1-form $\theta$; for the trivial bundle of rank $k$ the 1-form is replaced by a $k \times k$ matrix of 1-forms.

## Directional and partial covariant derivatives

The value of $\nabla s$ at $m$ is a linear map $T_mM \to E_m$, and applying it to a tangent vector is the directional derivative.

> **Definition (covariant derivative in a direction; partial covariant derivatives).** Let $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$, $s \in \Gamma(E)$, $m \in M$, and $v \in T_mM$. The **covariant derivative of $s$ in the direction $v$** is
> $$\nabla_v s := \iota_v(\nabla s) = (\nabla s)_m(v) \in E_m.$$
> For a vector field $X \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$, $\nabla_X s := \iota_X(\nabla s) \in \Gamma(E)$ is the section $m \mapsto \nabla_{X(m)} s$. In a chart $(U; x_1, \dots, x_n)$ with coordinate vector fields $\partial_i$ — the tangent vector at $(x^0_1, \dots, x^0_n)$ of the curve $\gamma(t) = (x^0_1, \dots, x^0_{i-1}, t, x^0_{i+1}, \dots, x^0_n)$ — the sections
> $$\nabla_i s := \nabla_{\partial_i} s \in \Gamma(U; E)$$
> are the **partial covariant derivatives** of $s$.

The partial covariant derivatives reassemble $\nabla s$ exactly as the partial derivatives reassemble $df$.

> [!note]- Lemma 1 (coordinate expression): on a chart $U$, $\nabla s = \sum_{i=1}^n dx_i \otimes \nabla_i s$
> **Statement.** Let $(U; x_1, \dots, x_n)$ be a chart of $M$, $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$, and $s \in \Gamma(E)$. Then, as $E$-valued 1-forms on $U$,
> $$\nabla s\big|_U = \sum_{i=1}^n dx_i \otimes \nabla_i s .$$
>
> **Proof.** We are given the chart and the section; we must show that the restriction of $\nabla s$ to $U$ equals the displayed sum.
>
> **Expand $\nabla s$ in the coordinate coframe.** The 1-forms $dx_1, \dots, dx_n$ form a local frame of $T^*M$ over $U$ (they are pointwise the dual basis to $\partial_1, \dots, \partial_n$). By [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]] applied to the bundle $T^*M \otimes E$ over $U$ — whose local frame over a smaller open set $V \subset U$ on which $E$ has a frame $e$ is $(dx_i \otimes e_j)_{i, j}$ — every section of $T^*M \otimes E$ over $U$ can be written uniquely as $\sum_{i=1}^n dx_i \otimes \omega_i$ with $\omega_i \in \Gamma(U; E)$: on $V$ write the section as $\sum_{i,j} c_{ij}\, dx_i \otimes e_j$ with smooth coefficients $c_{ij}$ and set $\omega_i := \sum_j c_{ij} e_j$; the $\omega_i$ so defined on the various $V$ agree on overlaps because the expansion in the frame $(dx_i)$ with $E$-valued coefficients is unique pointwise (the $dx_i(m)$ are linearly independent in $T^*_mM$, so $\sum_i dx_i(m) \otimes w_i = 0$ with $w_i \in E_m$ forces every $w_i = 0$), hence they glue to sections $\omega_i \in \Gamma(U; E)$. Apply this to $\nabla s|_U$:
> $$\nabla s\big|_U = \sum_{i=1}^n dx_i \otimes \omega_i, \qquad \omega_i \in \Gamma(U; E) \qquad \text{(expansion in the coordinate coframe, just established).}$$
>
> **Identify the coefficients by contraction.** Contract both sides with $\partial_j$. By the definition of the interior product on decomposable elements, $\iota_{\partial_j}(dx_i \otimes \omega_i) = dx_i(\partial_j)\, \omega_i = \delta_{ij}\, \omega_i$, so
> $$\nabla_j s = \iota_{\partial_j}(\nabla s) = \sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{ij}\, \omega_i = \omega_j \qquad \text{(definition of } \nabla_j \text{; } \iota \text{ is additive; } dx_i(\partial_j) = \delta_{ij}).$$
> Substituting $\omega_j = \nabla_j s$ into the expansion gives $\nabla s|_U = \sum_i dx_i \otimes \nabla_i s$.
>
> Therefore the covariant derivative on a chart is the sum of the coordinate 1-forms tensored with the partial covariant derivatives, which is the bundle-valued form of the identity $df = \sum_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\, dx_i$. $\blacksquare$

On the product line bundle with $\nabla = d$, Lemma 1 is literally $df = \sum_i \partial_i f\, dx_i$: the partial covariant derivatives of a function are its partial derivatives. On the product line bundle with $\nabla = d + \theta$, $\theta = \sum_i \theta_i\, dx_i$, one gets $\nabla_i s = \partial_i s + \theta_i s$, the form in which a connection appears in physics as "$\partial_\mu + A_\mu$".

## Locality

The Leibniz rule alone forces $\nabla$ to be a local operator. This is what allows one to speak of the restriction of a connection to an open set, and hence of the connection matrix in a local frame on the next page.

> [!note]- Lemma 2 (locality and restriction): if $s$ vanishes on an open set $U$ then so does $\nabla s$; consequently $\nabla$ restricts to a connection $\nabla^U$ on $E|_U$
> **Statement.** Let $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and let $U \subset M$ be open.
>
> (a) If $s \in \Gamma(E)$ vanishes identically on $U$, then $\nabla s$ vanishes identically on $U$. Equivalently, if $s, t \in \Gamma(E)$ agree on $U$ then $\nabla s$ and $\nabla t$ agree on $U$.
>
> (b) There is a unique connection $\nabla^U \in \mathcal{A}(E|_U)$ such that $\nabla^U(s|_U) = (\nabla s)|_U$ for every $s \in \Gamma(E)$.
>
> **Proof.** We use the existence of smooth bump functions, [[Thm - Existence of Smooth Bump Functions]]: for every point $m$ of an open set $U \subset M$ there is a smooth $\beta \colon M \to [0, 1]$ with $\beta \equiv 1$ on a neighbourhood of $m$ and $\operatorname{supp} \beta \subset U$.
>
> **Part (a). Assume $s|_U = 0$; we must show $(\nabla s)(m) = 0$ for every $m \in U$.** Fix $m \in U$ and choose a bump function $\beta$ with $\beta \equiv 1$ near $m$ and $\operatorname{supp} \beta \subset U$. Because $s$ vanishes on $U \supset \operatorname{supp}\beta$, the section $\beta s$ vanishes on all of $M$: on $U$ because $s$ does, off $\operatorname{supp}\beta$ because $\beta$ does. Hence
> $$0 = \nabla(\beta s) = d\beta \otimes s + \beta\, \nabla s \qquad \text{(} \nabla 0 = 0 \text{ by } \mathbb{R}\text{-linearity; Leibniz rule).}$$
> Evaluate at $m$: $s(m) = 0$ because $m \in U$, and $\beta(m) = 1$, so $0 = d\beta_m \otimes 0 + 1 \cdot (\nabla s)(m) = (\nabla s)(m)$. As $m \in U$ was arbitrary, $(\nabla s)|_U = 0$. The equivalent formulation follows by applying this to $s - t$ and using $\mathbb{R}$-linearity: $(\nabla s - \nabla t)|_U = \nabla(s - t)|_U = 0$.
>
> **Part (b), Step 0 — extension of local sections.** Let $t \in \Gamma(U; E)$ and $m \in U$. Choose a bump function $\beta$ as above. The section $\beta t$ of $E|_U$ extends by zero to a smooth section $\widetilde{\beta t} \in \Gamma(E)$ (it is smooth on $U$ as a product of smooth objects, and smooth on the open set $M \setminus \operatorname{supp}\beta$ where it is zero; these two open sets cover $M$), and $\widetilde{\beta t} = t$ on the neighbourhood of $m$ where $\beta \equiv 1$.
>
> **Part (b), Step 1 — definition of $\nabla^U$ and independence of choices.** Define $(\nabla^U t)(m) := \nabla(\widetilde{\beta t})(m)$. If $\beta'$ is another bump function with the same properties, then $\widetilde{\beta t}$ and $\widetilde{\beta' t}$ agree on the open neighbourhood $W$ of $m$ where both are $\equiv 1$, so by part (a) $\nabla(\widetilde{\beta t})$ and $\nabla(\widetilde{\beta' t})$ agree on $W$, in particular at $m$. Thus $(\nabla^U t)(m)$ is well defined.
>
> **Part (b), Step 2 — $\nabla^U t$ is a smooth $E$-valued 1-form on $U$.** For a fixed bump $\beta$ centred at $m$, $\nabla^U t$ coincides on the open set $W$ where $\beta \equiv 1$ with the smooth form $\nabla(\widetilde{\beta t})|_W$ (by Step 1 applied at every point of $W$, using the same $\beta$). Smoothness is local, so $\nabla^U t \in \Omega^1(U; E|_U)$.
>
> **Part (b), Step 3 — $\nabla^U$ is a connection on $E|_U$.** Fix $m \in U$, $t, t' \in \Gamma(U; E)$, $c \in \mathbb{R}$, $h \in C^\infty(U)$, and one bump $\beta$ centred at $m$, with $W$ the neighbourhood where $\beta \equiv 1$. On $W$ we have $\widetilde{\beta(t + ct')} = \widetilde{\beta t} + c\, \widetilde{\beta t'}$ and $\widetilde{\beta(ht)} = \widetilde{\beta h} \cdot \widetilde{\beta t}$ where $\widetilde{\beta h} \in C^\infty(M)$ is the extension by zero of $\beta h$ (both identities hold on $W$, because $\beta \equiv 1$ there, and agreement on the neighbourhood $W$ of $m$ is all that part (a) requires). Hence, evaluating at $m$,
> $$(\nabla^U(t + ct'))(m) = \nabla\big(\widetilde{\beta t} + c\,\widetilde{\beta t'}\big)(m) = (\nabla^U t)(m) + c\,(\nabla^U t')(m) \qquad \text{(part (a) for the first equality, since the two global sections agree near } m \text{; } \mathbb{R}\text{-linearity of } \nabla),$$
> $$(\nabla^U(ht))(m) = \nabla\big(\widetilde{\beta h}\, \widetilde{\beta t}\big)(m) = d(\widetilde{\beta h})_m \otimes t(m) + h(m)\, (\nabla^U t)(m) = dh_m \otimes t(m) + h(m)\, (\nabla^U t)(m) \qquad \text{(part (a); Leibniz rule for } \nabla \text{; } \widetilde{\beta h} = h \text{ near } m \text{ so their differentials at } m \text{ agree).}$$
> These are $\mathbb{R}$-linearity and the Leibniz rule for $\nabla^U$.
>
> **Part (b), Step 4 — compatibility and uniqueness.** For $s \in \Gamma(E)$ and $m \in U$, $\widetilde{\beta (s|_U)} = \beta s$ agrees with $s$ near $m$, so $(\nabla^U(s|_U))(m) = \nabla(\beta s)(m) = (\nabla s)(m)$ by part (a). If $\nabla'$ is any connection on $E|_U$ with $\nabla'(s|_U) = (\nabla s)|_U$ for all global $s$, then for $t \in \Gamma(U; E)$ and $m \in U$, $t$ agrees near $m$ with the restriction of the global section $\widetilde{\beta t}$, so by part (a) applied to $\nabla'$ on $E|_U$, $(\nabla' t)(m) = \nabla'(\widetilde{\beta t}|_U)(m) = \nabla(\widetilde{\beta t})(m) = (\nabla^U t)(m)$.
>
> Therefore $\nabla$ is a local operator, and it restricts uniquely to a connection on $E|_U$ for every open $U$. $\blacksquare$

Because of Lemma 2 we write $\nabla$ also for $\nabla^U$, and we apply $\nabla$ to local sections and to local frames without further comment. In particular $\nabla e_j \in \Omega^1(U; E)$ makes sense for a local frame $e$ over $U$, which is the starting point of [[Def - Connection Matrix and Local Form of a Connection]].

## The equivalent two-argument formulation

The Riemannian-geometry chapter defines a connection as an operation $(X, s) \mapsto \nabla_X s$, [[Def - Affine Connection on a Vector Bundle]]: an $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear map $D \colon \mathfrak{X}(M) \times \Gamma(E) \to \Gamma(E)$ with $D(fX, s) = f\, D(X, s)$ and $D(X, fs) = X(f)\, s + f\, D(X, s)$. We now prove that this is the same notion, in both directions. The only non-formal point is the converse direction: a $C^\infty(M)$-linear map $\mathfrak{X}(M) \to \Gamma(E)$ must be shown to come from a section of $T^*M \otimes E$, which is a bundle-valued instance of the tensor characterisation lemma [[Thm - Tensor Field is C-Infinity Multilinear over C-Infinity Functions]] (that page treats scalar-valued tensor fields; the $E$-valued case needed here is proved in full as Lemma 3 so that this page is self-contained).

> [!note]- Lemma 3 (tensoriality of $C^\infty$-linear maps on vector fields): a $C^\infty(M)$-linear map $\Phi \colon \mathfrak{X}(M) \to \Gamma(E)$ is evaluation of a unique $\omega \in \Omega^1(M; E)$
> **Statement.** Let $\Phi \colon \mathfrak{X}(M) \to \Gamma(E)$ be $\mathbb{R}$-linear and $C^\infty(M)$-linear, $\Phi(fX) = f\, \Phi(X)$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$, $X \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$. Then there is a unique $\omega \in \Omega^1(M; E)$ with $\Phi(X) = \iota_X \omega$ for all $X$. Conversely, for every $\omega \in \Omega^1(M; E)$ the map $X \mapsto \iota_X \omega$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and $C^\infty(M)$-linear.
>
> **Proof.** We are given $\Phi$ with the two linearity properties; we must construct $\omega$, show it is smooth, show $\Phi(X) = \iota_X \omega$, and show uniqueness; then prove the converse.
>
> **Step 1 — locality: if $X$ vanishes on an open set $U$ then $\Phi(X)$ vanishes on $U$.** Fix $m \in U$ and a bump function $\beta$ ([[Thm - Existence of Smooth Bump Functions]]) with $\beta \equiv 1$ near $m$ and $\operatorname{supp}\beta \subset U$. Then $\beta X = 0$ on $M$ (on $U$ since $X = 0$ there, off $\operatorname{supp}\beta$ since $\beta = 0$ there), so
> $$0 = \Phi(\beta X) = \beta\, \Phi(X) \qquad \text{(} \Phi(0) = 0 \text{ by } \mathbb{R}\text{-linearity; } C^\infty(M)\text{-linearity).}$$
> Evaluating at $m$ where $\beta(m) = 1$ gives $\Phi(X)(m) = 0$. Applying this to $X - X'$ shows that if $X$ and $X'$ agree on $U$ then $\Phi(X)$ and $\Phi(X')$ agree on $U$.
>
> **Step 2 — pointwise dependence: if $X(m) = 0$ then $\Phi(X)(m) = 0$.** Fix $m$ and a chart $(U; x_1, \dots, x_n)$ around $m$, so that on $U$, $X = \sum_{i=1}^n X^i \partial_i$ with $X^i \in C^\infty(U)$. Choose a bump $\beta$ with $\beta \equiv 1$ on a neighbourhood $W$ of $m$ and $\operatorname{supp}\beta \subset U$, and define global objects by extension by zero: $\widetilde{\partial_i} := \widetilde{\beta \partial_i} \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ and $\widetilde{X^i} := \widetilde{\beta X^i} \in C^\infty(M)$ (smooth by the two-open-set argument of Lemma 2, Step 0). On $W$ we have $\sum_i \widetilde{X^i}\, \widetilde{\partial_i} = \sum_i X^i \partial_i = X$ (because $\beta \equiv 1$ on $W$). By Step 1,
> $$\Phi(X)(m) = \Phi\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n \widetilde{X^i}\, \widetilde{\partial_i}\Big)(m) = \sum_{i=1}^n \widetilde{X^i}(m)\, \Phi(\widetilde{\partial_i})(m) = \sum_{i=1}^n X^i(m)\, \Phi(\widetilde{\partial_i})(m) \qquad \text{(Step 1, the two fields agree on } W \text{; } \mathbb{R}\text{- and } C^\infty(M)\text{-linearity; } \widetilde{X^i} = X^i \text{ at } m). \tag{3}$$
> If $X(m) = 0$ then every $X^i(m) = 0$ (the $\partial_i(m)$ are a basis of $T_mM$), so (3) gives $\Phi(X)(m) = 0$. Applying this to $X - X'$: if $X(m) = X'(m)$ then $\Phi(X)(m) = \Phi(X')(m)$.
>
> **Step 3 — construction of $\omega$.** For $m \in M$ and $v \in T_mM$ define $\omega_m(v) := \Phi(X)(m)$ for any $X \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ with $X(m) = v$. Such an $X$ exists: in a chart around $m$ write $v = \sum_i v^i \partial_i(m)$ and take $X := \sum_i v^i\, \widetilde{\partial_i}$ with the $\widetilde{\partial_i}$ of Step 2, which equals $\sum_i v^i \partial_i$ near $m$, hence has $X(m) = v$. The value $\omega_m(v)$ does not depend on the choice of $X$ by Step 2. It is linear in $v$: if $X(m) = v$ and $X'(m) = v'$ then $(aX + bX')(m) = av + bv'$ for $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, so $\omega_m(av + bv') = \Phi(aX + bX')(m) = a\,\Phi(X)(m) + b\,\Phi(X')(m) = a\,\omega_m(v) + b\,\omega_m(v')$ by $\mathbb{R}$-linearity of $\Phi$. Thus $\omega_m \in \operatorname{Hom}(T_mM, E_m) = T^*_mM \otimes E_m$.
>
> **Step 4 — smoothness of $\omega$.** On the chart $U$ of Step 2, formula (3) applied with $v = \sum_i v^i \partial_i(m')$ at any $m' \in W$ gives $\omega_{m'} = \sum_{i=1}^n dx_i(m') \otimes \Phi(\widetilde{\partial_i})(m')$, because both sides send $\partial_j(m')$ to $\Phi(\widetilde{\partial_j})(m')$ (the left side by (3) with $X = \widetilde{\partial_j}$, whose coefficients on $W$ are $X^i = \delta_{ij}$; the right side since $dx_i(\partial_j) = \delta_{ij}$). The right side is a smooth section of $T^*M \otimes E$ over $W$ (a finite sum of tensor products of smooth sections), so $\omega$ is smooth near every point, hence $\omega \in \Omega^1(M; E)$.
>
> **Step 5 — $\Phi(X) = \iota_X \omega$, and uniqueness.** For $X \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$ and $m \in M$, $(\iota_X \omega)(m) = \omega_m(X(m)) = \Phi(X)(m)$ by the definition of $\omega$ in Step 3 with the choice $X$ itself. If $\omega' \in \Omega^1(M; E)$ also satisfies $\iota_X \omega' = \Phi(X)$ for all $X$, then for any $m$ and $v \in T_mM$, choosing $X$ with $X(m) = v$ gives $\omega'_m(v) = \Phi(X)(m) = \omega_m(v)$; so $\omega' = \omega$.
>
> **Converse.** For $\omega \in \Omega^1(M; E)$, $f \in C^\infty(M)$, $X, X' \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$, $a \in \mathbb{R}$, and $m \in M$: $(\iota_{fX + aX'}\, \omega)(m) = \omega_m\big(f(m) X(m) + a X'(m)\big) = f(m)\, \omega_m(X(m)) + a\, \omega_m(X'(m))$ by linearity of $\omega_m$ on $T_mM$, which is $\big(f\, \iota_X \omega + a\, \iota_{X'} \omega\big)(m)$.
>
> Therefore $C^\infty(M)$-linear maps $\mathfrak{X}(M) \to \Gamma(E)$ are exactly the evaluations of $E$-valued 1-forms, and the 1-form is unique. $\blacksquare$

> **Proposition 1 (the two formulations of a connection are equivalent).** Let $E \to M$ be a vector bundle.
>
> **(a)** If $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$, then $D_\nabla(X, s) := \nabla_X s = \iota_X(\nabla s)$ defines a map $D_\nabla \colon \mathfrak{X}(M) \times \Gamma(E) \to \Gamma(E)$ which is $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear, $C^\infty(M)$-linear in $X$, and satisfies the Leibniz rule $D_\nabla(X, fs) = X(f)\, s + f\, D_\nabla(X, s)$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$.
>
> **(b)** Conversely, if $D \colon \mathfrak{X}(M) \times \Gamma(E) \to \Gamma(E)$ is $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear, $C^\infty(M)$-linear in $X$, and satisfies $D(X, fs) = X(f)\, s + f\, D(X, s)$, then there is a unique $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ with $\iota_X(\nabla s) = D(X, s)$ for all $X$ and $s$.
>
> **(c)** The assignments $\nabla \mapsto D_\nabla$ of (a) and $D \mapsto \nabla$ of (b) are mutually inverse bijections between $\mathcal{A}(E)$ and the set of maps $D$ as in (b).

> [!note]- Full proof of Proposition 1
> **Part (a). Assume $\nabla$ is a connection; we must verify the three properties of $D_\nabla$.** Fix $X, X' \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$, $s, s' \in \Gamma(E)$, $f \in C^\infty(M)$, $a \in \mathbb{R}$.
>
> **$\mathbb{R}$-linearity and $C^\infty(M)$-linearity in $X$:** by the converse half of Lemma 3 applied to $\omega = \nabla s$,
> $$D_\nabla(fX + aX', s) = \iota_{fX + aX'}(\nabla s) = f\, \iota_X(\nabla s) + a\, \iota_{X'}(\nabla s) = f\, D_\nabla(X, s) + a\, D_\nabla(X', s) \qquad \text{(Lemma 3, converse).}$$
>
> **$\mathbb{R}$-linearity in $s$:** $\nabla(s + as') = \nabla s + a\nabla s'$ by clause 1 of the definition, and $\iota_X$ is additive and $\mathbb{R}$-homogeneous on $\Omega^1(M; E)$ (it is evaluation of the pointwise linear maps $\omega_m$ at $X(m)$), so $D_\nabla(X, s + as') = \iota_X(\nabla s) + a\, \iota_X(\nabla s') = D_\nabla(X, s) + a\, D_\nabla(X, s')$.
>
> **Leibniz rule in $s$:** contracting the Leibniz rule for $\nabla$ with $X$,
> $$D_\nabla(X, fs) = \iota_X\big(df \otimes s + f\, \nabla s\big) = df(X)\, s + f\, \iota_X(\nabla s) = X(f)\, s + f\, D_\nabla(X, s) \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule for } \nabla \text{; } \iota_X(\alpha \otimes s) = \alpha(X)\, s \text{ and additivity of } \iota_X \text{; } df(X) = X(f)).$$
>
> **Part (b). Assume $D$ has the three properties; we must construct $\nabla$, verify that it is a connection, and show uniqueness.**
>
> **Step 1 — construction.** Fix $s \in \Gamma(E)$. The map $\Phi_s \colon \mathfrak{X}(M) \to \Gamma(E)$, $\Phi_s(X) := D(X, s)$, is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and $C^\infty(M)$-linear by the hypotheses on $D$ in the first argument. By Lemma 3 there is a unique $\omega_s \in \Omega^1(M; E)$ with $\iota_X \omega_s = D(X, s)$ for all $X$. Define $\nabla s := \omega_s$. By construction $\iota_X(\nabla s) = D(X, s)$.
>
> **Step 2 — $\mathbb{R}$-linearity of $\nabla$.** For $s, s' \in \Gamma(E)$ and $a \in \mathbb{R}$, the form $\nabla s + a\nabla s'$ satisfies, for every $X$,
> $$\iota_X(\nabla s + a \nabla s') = D(X, s) + a\, D(X, s') = D(X, s + as') \qquad \text{(Step 1 twice; } \mathbb{R}\text{-linearity of } D \text{ in the second argument).}$$
> By the uniqueness in Lemma 3 (applied to $\Phi_{s + as'}$), $\nabla(s + as') = \nabla s + a \nabla s'$.
>
> **Step 3 — Leibniz rule for $\nabla$.** For $f \in C^\infty(M)$, $s \in \Gamma(E)$, and every $X$,
> $$\iota_X\big(df \otimes s + f\, \nabla s\big) = X(f)\, s + f\, D(X, s) = D(X, fs) = \iota_X\big(\nabla(fs)\big) \qquad \text{(} \iota_X(df \otimes s) = df(X)\, s = X(f)\, s \text{ and Step 1; Leibniz rule for } D \text{; Step 1).}$$
> Two $E$-valued 1-forms whose contractions with every vector field agree are equal (uniqueness in Lemma 3, or directly: every $v \in T_mM$ is the value at $m$ of some vector field, by Lemma 3 Step 3). Hence $\nabla(fs) = df \otimes s + f\, \nabla s$.
>
> **Step 4 — uniqueness.** If $\nabla' \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ also satisfies $\iota_X(\nabla' s) = D(X, s)$ for all $X, s$, then for each $s$ both $\nabla s$ and $\nabla' s$ are the unique form of Lemma 3 for $\Phi_s$, so $\nabla' s = \nabla s$.
>
> **Part (c). We must show the two assignments compose to the identity in both orders.** Starting from $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$, form $D_\nabla$ and then the connection $\nabla'$ of part (b): $\nabla'$ is the unique connection with $\iota_X(\nabla' s) = D_\nabla(X, s) = \iota_X(\nabla s)$, and $\nabla$ itself has this property, so $\nabla' = \nabla$ by uniqueness. Starting from $D$, form $\nabla$ by (b) and then $D_\nabla$: $D_\nabla(X, s) = \iota_X(\nabla s) = D(X, s)$ by Step 1 of (b). Both composites are the identity.
>
> Therefore a connection in the sense of this page and a connection in the sense of [[Def - Affine Connection on a Vector Bundle]] are the same data, related by $\nabla_X s = \iota_X(\nabla s)$. $\blacksquare$

# Categorical Definition

The definition above is operational and this section records only its structural reading, without proof, as an orientation for later chapters. A connection is not a bundle map — it is a first-order differential operator, and the Leibniz rule is precisely the statement that its failure to be $C^\infty(M)$-linear is governed by $df \otimes \mathrm{id}$. This has a clean expression: **a connection on $E$ is the same thing as a $C^\infty(M)$-linear splitting of the Atiyah sequence of $E$**, the short exact sequence of vector bundles $0 \to \operatorname{End} E \to \mathcal{D}^1(E) \to TM \to 0$ in which $\mathcal{D}^1(E)$ is the bundle whose sections are the first-order differential operators $\Gamma(E) \to \Gamma(E)$ with scalar symbol, and the map to $TM$ reads off the symbol; the splitting sends $X$ to $\nabla_X$. This is stated here only as an outlook; the series does not construct $\mathcal{D}^1(E)$ and does not use this description. The reading that the series *does* use is the one of Proposition 1 together with [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]]: $\mathcal{A}(E)$ is a torsor for the vector space $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End} E)$, non-empty, and any two of its elements differ by a tensor.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name:** *a Leibniz-rule derivative in the base direction.* A connection is what you get by asking for an operator $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ that differentiates sections along the base manifold and obeys the product rule against functions, and for nothing else. Everything a connection does — its local form $d + A$, its directional derivatives, parallel transport, curvature — is unpacked from the single identity $\nabla(fs) = df \otimes s + f\nabla s$; a reader who remembers only that identity can regenerate the rest. The official definition is already operational; the true name records that the operator is "$d$ in the base direction, corrected so as to make sense in a bundle whose fibres have no fixed identification".

**Algebra: connections on modules.** The definition uses only the $C^\infty(M)$-module structure of $\Gamma(E)$ and the derivation $d \colon C^\infty(M) \to \Omega^1(M)$. In commutative algebra one defines, for a commutative ring $R$, an $R$-module $P$, and the module of Kähler differentials $\Omega_R$ with its universal derivation $d \colon R \to \Omega_R$, a *connection on $P$* to be an additive map $\nabla \colon P \to \Omega_R \otimes_R P$ with $\nabla(rp) = dr \otimes p + r\nabla p$. The definition of this page is exactly that definition with $R = C^\infty(M)$, $P = \Gamma(E)$, and $\Omega_R$ replaced by the geometric 1-forms (for smooth functions the geometric $\Omega^1(M)$ differs from the algebraic Kähler module, but the definition of a connection reads identically). This is the form in which connections appear in algebraic geometry and in the theory of $D$-modules, where a module with a flat connection is the algebraic incarnation of a local system.

**Physics: minimal coupling.** In field theory a charged field $\psi$ is a section of a vector bundle (a trivial bundle $M \times \mathbb{C}^k$ in the local description), and the requirement that the equations of motion be invariant under position-dependent changes of the fibre basis — a *gauge transformation* — forces one to replace the partial derivative $\partial_\mu \psi$ by the covariant derivative $D_\mu \psi = \partial_\mu \psi + A_\mu \psi$ with a matrix-valued *gauge potential* $A_\mu$. The gauge potential is a connection matrix, $A = \sum_\mu A_\mu\, dx^\mu$, and $D_\mu = \nabla_{\partial_\mu}$ is the partial covariant derivative $\nabla_\mu$ of this page in the coordinate expression of Lemma 1. The identification is made precise in chapter VII, **Def - U(1) Gauge Field and Electromagnetic Connection**.

**Riemannian geometry.** The Levi-Civita connection is the unique torsion-free metric connection on $TM$ ([[Def - Levi-Civita Connection]]); the general connection of this page is what remains when both extra conditions are removed and $TM$ is replaced by an arbitrary bundle. Chapter §2.5 of the topic page places the Levi-Civita connection inside this framework.

**Differential operators.** A connection is a first-order linear differential operator $\Gamma(E) \to \Gamma(T^*M \otimes E)$ whose principal symbol at a covector $\xi \in T^*_mM$ is $u \mapsto \xi \otimes u$, that is, the symbol of $d$ tensored with the identity of $E$. This is the reason the Leibniz rule takes the specific form (2): the symbol of a first-order operator $L$ is read off from $L(fs) - fL(s)$, and (2) says this equals $df \otimes s$. In chapter IX this observation is what makes the Dirac operator and the Laplacian built from a connection elliptic.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the product connection $d$ on the product bundle $M \times \mathbb{R}^k$.** Let $E = \underline{\mathbb{R}^k} := M \times \mathbb{R}^k$ with the constant frame $\epsilon = (\epsilon_1, \dots, \epsilon_k)$, $\epsilon_j(m) = (m, \mathrm{e}_j)$ where $\mathrm{e}_j$ is the $j$-th basis vector of $\mathbb{R}^k$. A section is $s = \sum_j \sigma_j \epsilon_j$ for a unique smooth map $\sigma = (\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_k) \colon M \to \mathbb{R}^k$, and we define
$$d s := \sum_{j=1}^k d\sigma_j \otimes \epsilon_j \in \Omega^1(M; E),$$
the coefficientwise derivative. We verify the two clauses. *$\mathbb{R}$-linearity:* if $s = \sum_j \sigma_j \epsilon_j$ and $t = \sum_j \tau_j \epsilon_j$ then $s + ct = \sum_j (\sigma_j + c\tau_j)\epsilon_j$ (uniqueness of the representation, [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]]), so $d(s + ct) = \sum_j d(\sigma_j + c\tau_j) \otimes \epsilon_j = \sum_j (d\sigma_j + c\, d\tau_j) \otimes \epsilon_j = ds + c\, dt$ by $\mathbb{R}$-linearity of $d$ on functions and bilinearity of $\otimes$. *Leibniz rule:* $fs = \sum_j (f\sigma_j)\epsilon_j$, so
$$d(fs) = \sum_{j=1}^k d(f\sigma_j) \otimes \epsilon_j = \sum_{j=1}^k \big(\sigma_j\, df + f\, d\sigma_j\big) \otimes \epsilon_j = df \otimes \sum_{j=1}^k \sigma_j \epsilon_j + f \sum_{j=1}^k d\sigma_j \otimes \epsilon_j = df \otimes s + f\, ds$$
(definition of $d$ on $E$; product rule $d(f\sigma_j) = \sigma_j\, df + f\, d\sigma_j$ for functions, from the derivation property of tangent vectors; bilinearity of $\otimes$ and the definition of $s$). Both clauses hold, so $d \in \mathcal{A}(\underline{\mathbb{R}^k})$. This is the connection Haydys writes $\nabla_U s = \psi_U^{-1} d(\psi_U(s))$ on any trivialised piece $E|_U$ of a general bundle, and it is the raw material from which [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]] builds a connection on an arbitrary bundle by a partition of unity.

**Is an instance — the projected connection on an embedded submanifold (Haydys, Example 10).** Let $M \subset \mathbb{R}^N$ be an [[Def - Embedded Submanifold|embedded submanifold]] of dimension $n$. For each $m \in M$ the tangent space $T_mM$ is a linear subspace of $T_m\mathbb{R}^N = \mathbb{R}^N$, and $TM = \{(m, w) : m \in M,\ w \in T_mM\}$ is a [[Def - Subbundle|subbundle]] of the product bundle $\underline{\mathbb{R}^N} = M \times \mathbb{R}^N$. A vector field $s \in \Gamma(TM)$ is therefore a smooth map $s \colon M \to \mathbb{R}^N$ with $s(m) \in T_mM$ for every $m$, and its coefficientwise derivative $ds = \sum_{a=1}^N ds_a \otimes \epsilon_a \in \Omega^1(M; \underline{\mathbb{R}^N})$ is defined by the previous example — but $ds$ takes values in $\mathbb{R}^N$, not in $TM$, so it is not yet a covariant derivative on $TM$. Let $\operatorname{pr}_m \colon \mathbb{R}^N \to T_mM$ be the orthogonal projection with respect to the Euclidean inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ of $\mathbb{R}^N$, and define
$$\nabla s := \operatorname{pr}(ds), \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad (\nabla s)_m(v) := \operatorname{pr}_m\big(ds_m(v)\big) \in T_mM \quad (v \in T_mM).$$
We verify, clause by clause, that $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(TM)$.

*Step 0 — $\operatorname{pr}$ is smooth, so $\nabla s$ is a smooth $TM$-valued 1-form.* Fix $m_0 \in M$ and a chart of $M$ around $m_0$ with coordinate vector fields $\partial_1, \dots, \partial_n$, which are smooth maps $U \to \mathbb{R}^N$ forming a basis of $T_mM$ at each $m \in U$. The Gram–Schmidt procedure $u_1 := \partial_1 / \lVert \partial_1 \rVert$, $u_i := w_i / \lVert w_i \rVert$ with $w_i := \partial_i - \sum_{l < i} \langle \partial_i, u_l \rangle u_l$, produces maps $u_1, \dots, u_n \colon U \to \mathbb{R}^N$ that are orthonormal at each point and smooth, because each formula involves only sums, inner products, and division by $\lVert w_i \rVert$, and $\lVert w_i \rVert$ never vanishes ($w_i(m)$ is the component of $\partial_i(m)$ orthogonal to the span of $\partial_1(m), \dots, \partial_{i-1}(m)$, and is non-zero by linear independence). Hence $\operatorname{pr}_m(w) = \sum_{i=1}^n \langle w, u_i(m) \rangle\, u_i(m)$ for $w \in \mathbb{R}^N$, and the $N \times N$ matrix of $\operatorname{pr}_m$ has entries $\sum_i u_i^a(m) u_i^b(m)$, smooth in $m$. Consequently $\nabla s = \sum_{a=1}^N ds_a \otimes \operatorname{pr}(\epsilon_a)$, where $\operatorname{pr}(\epsilon_a) \colon m \mapsto \operatorname{pr}_m(\mathrm{e}_a)$ is a smooth section of $TM$, is a smooth element of $\Omega^1(M; TM)$.

*$\mathbb{R}$-linearity:* $\nabla = \operatorname{pr} \circ d$ is a composition of the $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $d$ (previous example) with the map induced by the fibrewise linear $\operatorname{pr}$, hence $\mathbb{R}$-linear.

*Leibniz rule:* for $f \in C^\infty(M)$ and $s \in \Gamma(TM)$,
$$\nabla(fs) = \operatorname{pr}\big(d(fs)\big) = \operatorname{pr}\big(df \otimes s + f\, ds\big) = df \otimes \operatorname{pr}(s) + f\, \operatorname{pr}(ds) = df \otimes s + f\, \nabla s$$
(definition of $\nabla$; Leibniz rule for $d$ on $\underline{\mathbb{R}^N}$, previous example; $\operatorname{pr}$ acts on the $\mathbb{R}^N$-factor and is fibrewise linear, so it commutes with $df \otimes (\cdot)$ and with multiplication by $f$; $\operatorname{pr}_m(s(m)) = s(m)$ because $s(m) \in T_mM$ is already tangent). This is the computation the source leaves unwritten; the single geometric input is the last equality, $\operatorname{pr} s = s$. So $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(TM)$.

*The smallest concrete case, $M = S^1 \subset \mathbb{R}^2$.* Parametrise $S^1 = \{(\cos\vartheta, \sin\vartheta) : \vartheta \in \mathbb{R}\}$ and let $\tau(\vartheta) := (-\sin\vartheta, \cos\vartheta)$ be the unit tangent field, which frames $TS^1$. Then $d\tau = (-\cos\vartheta, -\sin\vartheta)\, d\vartheta$, and $(-\cos\vartheta, -\sin\vartheta)$ is the inward normal, orthogonal to $\tau$, so $\operatorname{pr}(d\tau) = 0$: the unit tangent field is covariantly constant, $\nabla \tau = 0$. For a general vector field $s = f\tau$ the Leibniz rule gives $\nabla s = df \otimes \tau + f \nabla\tau = df \otimes \tau$, so in the frame $\tau$ the projected connection is the coefficientwise derivative $d$ — the circle is flat. On $S^2 \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ this is no longer so: the projected connection is the Levi-Civita connection of the round metric, whose curvature is computed on [[Ex - The Curvature of the Projected Connection on the Sphere]]. The identification of the projected connection with the Levi-Civita connection of the induced metric is the subject of [[Ex - The Projected Connection on an Embedded Submanifold]].

**Corollary 4 — the difference of two connections is $C^\infty(M)$-linear; shifting a connection by a tensor gives a connection.** Let $\nabla, \hat\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and let $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End} E)$, acting on sections by $(a \cdot s)(m) := a_m(s(m)) \in T^*_mM \otimes E_m$ (the contraction $\operatorname{End} E \otimes E \to E$ of [[Def - Bundle-Valued Differential Forms]]).

*(i) $\nabla - \hat\nabla \colon \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ is $C^\infty(M)$-linear.* We must show $(\nabla - \hat\nabla)(fs) = f\,(\nabla - \hat\nabla)(s)$; $\mathbb{R}$-linearity is inherited from both connections. For $f \in C^\infty(M)$, $s \in \Gamma(E)$,
$$(\nabla - \hat\nabla)(fs) = \big(df \otimes s + f\, \nabla s\big) - \big(df \otimes s + f\, \hat\nabla s\big) = f\,(\nabla s - \hat\nabla s) = f\,(\nabla - \hat\nabla)(s) \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule for } \nabla \text{ and for } \hat\nabla \text{; the two } df \otimes s \text{ terms cancel).}$$
The derivative terms cancel exactly because both connections carry the same term $df \otimes s$ — this is the point made in the Axiom Motivation. By [[Thm - Tensoriality Lemma for C-Infinity-Linear Maps]] (statement: a $C^\infty(M)$-linear map $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; F)$ is $s \mapsto a \cdot s$ for a unique $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{Hom}(E, F))$), $\nabla - \hat\nabla = a \cdot (\,)$ for a unique $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End} E)$; this is part (b) of [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]].

*(ii) $\nabla + a$, defined by $(\nabla + a)s := \nabla s + a \cdot s$, is a connection.* $\mathbb{R}$-linearity: $\nabla$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and $s \mapsto a \cdot s$ is pointwise linear. Leibniz rule:
$$(\nabla + a)(fs) = df \otimes s + f\, \nabla s + a \cdot (fs) = df \otimes s + f\, \nabla s + f\,(a \cdot s) = df \otimes s + f\,(\nabla + a)s \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule for } \nabla \text{; } a_m(f(m)s(m)) = f(m)\, a_m(s(m)) \text{ by linearity of } a_m \text{).}$$
This is part (c) of the affine-space theorem, the step the source does not write out.

*(iii) Convex combinations.* For $t \in \mathbb{R}$ (not only $t \in [0, 1]$), $t\nabla + (1 - t)\hat\nabla$ is a connection: it is $\mathbb{R}$-linear as a linear combination of $\mathbb{R}$-linear maps, and
$$\big(t\nabla + (1 - t)\hat\nabla\big)(fs) = t\big(df \otimes s + f\nabla s\big) + (1 - t)\big(df \otimes s + f\hat\nabla s\big) = df \otimes s + f\big(t\nabla + (1 - t)\hat\nabla\big)s \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule twice; } t + (1 - t) = 1).$$
The same computation with a partition of unity $(\rho_\alpha)$ in place of $(t, 1 - t)$ shows $\sum_\alpha \rho_\alpha \nabla_\alpha$ is a connection whenever each $\nabla_\alpha$ is; this is the gluing step of the existence proof, written out on [[Ex - Convex Combinations of Connections are Connections]].

**Is NOT an instance — a tensorial map.** Let $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ with $a \neq 0$ and consider $T(s) := a \cdot s$. It is $\mathbb{R}$-linear, but the Leibniz rule fails: $T(fs) = f\, T(s)$ by pointwise linearity of $a_m$, so $T(fs) - f\, T(s) = 0$, whereas the Leibniz rule demands $df \otimes s$, which is non-zero whenever $df_m \neq 0$ and $s(m) \neq 0$ for some $m$ (choose $s$ non-vanishing at a point and $f$ a coordinate function there). Thus a $C^\infty(M)$-linear map is never a connection, on any bundle of positive rank over a manifold of positive dimension. (Even $T = 0$ fails, for the same reason.) The failure is exactly the absence of the $df \otimes s$ term.

**Is NOT an instance — the frame-wise coefficientwise derivative "$s \mapsto ds$" on a non-trivial bundle.** On the product bundle the coefficientwise derivative was defined using the constant frame $\epsilon$. On a general bundle one might try to define "$ds$" by choosing, over each open set $U$ of a trivialising cover, a frame $e$ and setting $\nabla^{(e)} s := e\, d\sigma := \sum_j d\sigma_j \otimes e_j$, where $s = e\sigma$ on $U$. This is a connection on $E|_U$ — it is the product connection transported through the trivialisation $\psi_U$, that is, Haydys's $\nabla_U s = \psi_U^{-1} d(\psi_U s)$ — but it is not an operation on $s$ alone: it depends on the frame. Let $e' = e \cdot g$ be another frame over $U$, $g \colon U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ (the source's $GL_n$ corrected to $GL_k$), so that $s = e\sigma = e'\sigma'$ with $\sigma' = g^{-1}\sigma$. Then
$$\nabla^{(e')} s = e'\, d\sigma' = e g\, d(g^{-1}\sigma) = e g\big(g^{-1} d\sigma + d(g^{-1})\, \sigma\big) = e\, d\sigma + e\, g\, d(g^{-1})\, \sigma = \nabla^{(e)} s - e\,(dg\, g^{-1})\, \sigma \qquad \text{(} \sigma' = g^{-1}\sigma \text{; product rule for } d \text{ on matrix-valued functions; } g\, d(g^{-1}) = -dg\, g^{-1} \text{, from differentiating } g g^{-1} = 1).$$
So $\nabla^{(e')} s = \nabla^{(e)} s$ for all $s$ if and only if $dg = 0$, that is, if and only if the frame change $g$ is locally constant. In rank one this reads: with $g = e^h$ for a function $h$, $\nabla^{(e')} s = \nabla^{(e)} s - dh\, \sigma\, e$, which differs from $\nabla^{(e)} s$ wherever $dh \neq 0$ and $\sigma \neq 0$. A bundle with no global frame — the [[Ex - The Möbius Bundle is Nontrivial|Möbius bundle]] $E = \mathbb{R}^2 / \big((x, y) \sim (x + 1, -y)\big) \to S^1 = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ is the smallest example — therefore has no candidate "$ds$" defined by one frame, and the local candidates $\nabla^{(e)}$ on the pieces of a trivialising cover glue to a global operator only when all the transition functions are locally constant. We record, for honesty, that on the Möbius bundle with the two natural frames $e_1([x]) := [x, 1]$ over $\{[x] : x \in (0, 1)\}$ and $e_2([x]) := [x, 1]$ over $\{[x] : x \in (\tfrac12, \tfrac32)\}$, the transition function *is* locally constant (it is $+1$ on the component $x \in (\tfrac12, 1)$ and $-1$ on the component $x \in (1, \tfrac32)$, since $[x, 1] = [x - 1, -1]$), so these two particular local candidates do glue to a connection on the Möbius bundle — a *flat* connection, in the language of chapter V, [[Def - Flat Connection]]. This does not rescue "$ds$" as a general construction: replacing $e_2$ by $e_2' := e_2 \cdot e^{h}$ for any smooth $h$ with $dh \neq 0$ on the overlap gives a different candidate on the second piece which no longer agrees with $\nabla^{(e_1)}$ there, and on a bundle whose transition functions cannot all be made locally constant (a bundle admitting no flat connection — for instance $TS^2$, whose non-vanishing curvature is computed on [[Ex - The Curvature of the Projected Connection on the Sphere]]) no choice of frames makes the local candidates glue. The lesson is the one that motivated the definition: differentiating coefficients is a frame-dependent operation, and a connection is precisely the extra datum — the matrix $A$ of [[Def - Connection Matrix and Local Form of a Connection]], transforming by $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}dg$ — that compensates for the frame dependence.

**Calibration check.** First, the connection matrix of the product connection is zero: for $\nabla = d$ on $\underline{\mathbb{R}^k}$ and the constant frame $\epsilon$, each $\epsilon_j$ has constant coefficients $\sigma = \mathrm{e}_j$, so $d\epsilon_j = \sum_l d(\delta_{lj}) \otimes \epsilon_l = 0$, and in the notation $\nabla e = e \cdot A$ of the next page, $A(d, \epsilon) = 0$; conversely, the computation in the non-example shows that in the rotated frame $\epsilon' = \epsilon\, g$ the same connection has matrix $g^{-1} dg \neq 0$, so "connection matrix zero" is a property of a pair (connection, frame), not of the connection. Second, the directional derivative of a constant section of the trivial bundle vanishes: if $s = \sum_j c_j \epsilon_j$ with $c_j \in \mathbb{R}$ then $ds = \sum_j dc_j \otimes \epsilon_j = 0$, hence $\nabla_v s = \iota_v(0) = 0$ for every tangent vector $v$; and by the Leibniz rule $\nabla_v(fs) = df(v)\, s + f \nabla_v s = v(f)\, s$, so on the trivial bundle with $\nabla = d$ the directional derivative is the ordinary directional derivative of the coefficients. Third, if $f$ is locally constant then $df = 0$ and the Leibniz rule reduces to $\nabla(fs) = f \nabla s$: a connection is linear over locally constant functions, and in particular over $\mathbb{R}$, which is the observation that homogeneity is a consequence of the Leibniz rule. A reader who can also explain why the two $df \otimes s$ terms cancel in Corollary 4(i) but not in either connection separately has understood the definition.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Connection Matrix and the Local Form $\nabla = d + A$ *(from this chapter)*
> Applying $\nabla$ to a local frame $e$ (legitimate by Lemma 2) and expanding $\nabla e_j$ in the frame produces the connection matrix $A \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ with $\nabla e = e \cdot A$, and hence the formula $\nabla(e\sigma) = e(d\sigma + A\sigma)$: locally every connection is "$d$ plus a matrix of 1-forms". See [[Def - Connection Matrix and Local Form of a Connection]].

> [!tip] The Space of Connections is an Affine Space *(from this chapter)*
> Corollary 4 is two of the three parts of [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]]; the third, non-emptiness, glues the product connections of trivialising pieces with a partition of unity using Corollary 4(iii). Once this is known, every connection is $\nabla_0 + a$ for a reference connection $\nabla_0$ and a tensor $a$, which is how the Yang–Mills functional of chapter VII becomes a function on a vector space.

> [!tip] Exterior Covariant Derivative and Curvature *(from this chapter)*
> The Leibniz rule extends $\nabla$ to $d^\nabla \colon \Omega^p(M; E) \to \Omega^{p+1}(M; E)$, and $d^\nabla \circ d^\nabla$, which vanishes for $d$ but not in general for $d^\nabla$, is the curvature $F_\nabla \in \Omega^2(M; \operatorname{End} E)$, measuring the failure of the partial covariant derivatives $\nabla_i, \nabla_j$ of this page to commute. See [[Def - Exterior Covariant Derivative on a Vector Bundle]] and [[Def - Curvature of a Vector-Bundle Connection]].

> [!tip] Parallel Transport *(from Riemannian Geometry I and chapter V)*
> The directional derivative $\nabla_{\dot\gamma} s$ along a curve $\gamma$ defines what it means for a section to be constant along $\gamma$, namely $\nabla_{\dot\gamma} s = 0$, and solving this linear ordinary differential equation transports fibres along curves: [[Def - Parallel Transport]] in the Riemannian chapter, and [[Def - Parallel Transport in a Principal Bundle]] in chapter V, where the holonomy of a connection is defined.

> [!tip] Connections on Principal Bundles *(from chapter IV)*
> The connection of this page is the associated-bundle shadow of a connection on the frame bundle of $E$: a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued 1-form on a principal bundle induces a covariant derivative on every associated vector bundle, and conversely. See [[Def - Connection on a Principal Bundle]] and [[Thm - Principal Connections Induce Covariant Derivatives on Associated Bundles]].
