# Notation

Throughout, $M$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $n$ (Hausdorff, second countable, "smooth" meaning $C^\infty$), and $\pi \colon E \to M$ is a smooth real [[Def - Vector Bundle|vector bundle]] of rank $k$ with fibre $E_m := \pi^{-1}(m)$ over $m \in M$. $\Gamma(E)$ denotes the $C^\infty(M)$-module of smooth [[Def - Section of a Vector Bundle|sections]] of $E$, and $\Gamma(U; E)$ the sections over an open subset $U \subseteq M$. $\mathfrak{X}(M)$ is the space of smooth vector fields on $M$. $\Lambda^p T^*M$ is the bundle of alternating $p$-tensors on $M$, whose sections are the [[Def - Differential k-Form on a Manifold|differential forms]] of degree $p$, $\Omega^p(M) = \Gamma(\Lambda^p T^*M)$; the [[Def - The Wedge Product on a Manifold|wedge product]] $\wedge$ is taken in the determinant convention of that page, so that for $\alpha \in \Omega^q(M)$ and $\beta \in \Omega^p(M)$ one has $\alpha \wedge \beta = \frac{(p+q)!}{p!\,q!}\operatorname{Alt}(\alpha \otimes \beta)$ with $\operatorname{Alt}$ the alternation projector $\operatorname{Alt}T(v_1, \dots, v_r) = \frac{1}{r!}\sum_{\sigma \in S_r} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, T(v_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, v_{\sigma(r)})$ of [[Def - Alternating Tensor and Lambda k V Dual]]. The derived bundles $E^*$, $E \otimes F$, $\operatorname{Hom}(E, F)$ and $\operatorname{End}E := \operatorname{Hom}(E, E)$ are those of [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles]], with fibres $(E \otimes F)_m = E_m \otimes F_m$ and $(\operatorname{End}E)_m = \operatorname{End}(E_m)$; the trivial line bundle is $\underline{\mathbb{R}} := M \times \mathbb{R}$.

The objects defined on this page are the spaces
$$\Omega^p(M; E) := \Gamma(\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E), \qquad \Omega^p(M; \operatorname{End}E) := \Gamma(\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes \operatorname{End}E),$$
of **$E$-valued** and **$\operatorname{End}E$-valued differential $p$-forms**, together with three products between them, written with the same symbol $\wedge$ throughout and distinguished by the types of their arguments. A [[Def - Local Frame|local frame]] of $E$ over $U$ is written as a row $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$ of sections $e_j \in \Gamma(U; E)$ whose values form a basis of $E_m$ at every $m \in U$; a column $\sigma = (\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_k)^{\mathsf{T}}$ of functions or of forms is multiplied against it as $e\sigma = \sum_j \sigma_j e_j$, respectively $e\sigma = \sum_j \sigma_j \otimes e_j$. A change of frame is written $e' = eg$ with $g \colon U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ smooth, meaning $e'_j = \sum_i e_i\, g_{ij}$. The local sections $E_{ij} \in \Gamma(U; \operatorname{End}E)$, $1 \le i, j \le k$, are the **matrix units of the frame $e$**, defined by $E_{ij}(e_l) = \delta_{jl}\, e_i$. For $\alpha \in \Omega^p(M)$ and $X_1, \dots, X_p \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$, $\alpha(X_1, \dots, X_p) \in C^\infty(M)$ is the evaluation of the form on the vector fields. $\operatorname{tr}$ is the [[Def - Trace|trace]] of an endomorphism of a finite-dimensional vector space.

This is a compound page: it defines four interlocking notions — the space $\Omega^p(M; E)$ with its evaluation on vector fields and its local description, the wedge of a scalar form with an $E$-valued form, the pairing of an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued form with an $E$-valued form, and the wedge-composition of two $\operatorname{End}E$-valued forms — because they are inseparable in use: the connection $\nabla$ of the next section takes values in $\Omega^1(M; E)$, its connection matrix lives in $\Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$, and its curvature is an element of $\Omega^2(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ whose defining property $d^\nabla \circ d^\nabla = F_\nabla \wedge \cdot$ is a statement about the pairing.

> [!warning] Convention: $\Omega^p(E)$ versus $\Omega^p(M; E)$, and the direction of a frame change
> Haydys writes $\Omega^p(E)$ for $\Gamma(\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E)$ and $\Omega^1(\operatorname{End}E)$ for $\Gamma(T^*M \otimes \operatorname{End}E)$ (pp. 7–8), suppressing the base. The series writes $\Omega^p(M; E)$ throughout, and $\Omega^p(U; E)$ for the forms over an open subset $U$; the two notations denote the same space, and Haydys's $\Omega^p(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ (p. 8) is the series' $\Omega^p(U; \underline{\mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R})})$, the $k \times k$ matrices of $p$-forms on $U$. For a change of frame the series follows Haydys's equation (14): $e' = eg$. Haydys's equation (6) on p. 6 writes the same relation the other way round, $e = e' g$, and consequently obtains $\sigma' = g\sigma$ for the coefficient column of a section; with the series convention $e' = eg$ the same computation gives $\sigma' = g^{-1}\sigma$, and the transformation law for the coefficient column of a bundle-valued form proved below is $\omega' = g^{-1}\omega$. To convert a formula written in the convention of (6), replace $g$ by $g^{-1}$. Haydys's $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ on p. 6 is a typo for $GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ (Appendix B of the content map), and is corrected here.

# Axiom Motivation

Where does the need for bundle-valued forms come from? The whole of this chapter is about differentiating sections of a vector bundle. The differential $df$ of a function $f \in C^\infty(M)$ is a $1$-form: it eats a tangent vector $v \in T_mM$ and returns the number $df(v)$, the rate of change of $f$ in the direction $v$. When $f$ is replaced by a section $s \in \Gamma(E)$, the rate of change of $s$ in the direction $v$ can no longer be a number — the values $s(m)$ lie in the fibre $E_m$, and a "rate of change" of $s$ at $m$ should again lie in $E_m$. So whatever the derivative of a section is, at each point $m$ it must be a linear map $T_mM \to E_m$, and as $m$ varies it must be a smooth family of such maps. A linear map $T_mM \to E_m$ is the same thing as an element of $T_m^*M \otimes E_m$ (the universal property of the [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces|tensor product]] identifies $\operatorname{Hom}(V, W)$ with $V^* \otimes W$), and a smooth family of such is a section of $T^*M \otimes E$. This is the object $\Omega^1(M; E)$, and it is forced on us before any connection has been defined. The higher-degree spaces $\Omega^p(M; E)$ are forced in turn by the second derivative: once we can differentiate sections, we can ask whether two derivatives commute, and the failure of commutation — the curvature — will be antisymmetric in the two directions, so it will be an alternating $2$-form, with values not in $E$ but in the endomorphisms of $E$, because at each point it sends a vector of $E_m$ to a vector of $E_m$. The three spaces $\Omega^1(M; E)$, $\Omega^2(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ and — for the second derivative of a section — $\Omega^2(M; E)$ are therefore the smallest collection of objects in which the differential calculus of sections can be written at all.

The desiderata are then the following. First, $\Omega^p(M; E)$ should reduce to the familiar $\Omega^p(M)$ when $E$ is the trivial line bundle $\underline{\mathbb{R}}$, so that the new calculus contains the old one. Second, an element of $\Omega^p(M; E)$ should be evaluable on $p$ vector fields to give a section of $E$, alternating and $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear in the vector fields, so that formulas such as "$F(X, Y)s = \nabla_X \nabla_Y s - \nabla_Y \nabla_X s - \nabla_{[X, Y]}s$" have a home. Third, in a local frame an $E$-valued $p$-form should be a column of ordinary $p$-forms, so that every local computation is a computation with ordinary forms and matrices, and the passage between frames should be governed by a transformation law that we can write down and check. Fourth, the scalar forms $\Omega^\bullet(M)$ should act on $\Omega^\bullet(M; E)$ by a wedge product, and the endomorphism-valued forms should act on $E$-valued forms and compose among themselves, in such a way that the local expressions are matrix multiplications with wedge products of entries; without these products the Leibniz rule of the exterior covariant derivative, $d^\nabla(\alpha \wedge \omega) = d\alpha \wedge \omega + (-1)^q \alpha \wedge d^\nabla \omega$, and the very statement $d^\nabla \circ d^\nabla = F_\nabla \wedge \cdot$ of [[Thm - Existence of the Curvature Form]] could not be written.

We now examine the clauses of the definition one at a time, asking what is lost if each is dropped. The first clause is that the form slot is *alternating*: we tensor $E$ with $\Lambda^p T^*M$ and not with the full tensor power $(T^*M)^{\otimes p}$. Suppose we dropped it and took sections of $(T^*M)^{\otimes 2} \otimes E$ as our "$E$-valued $2$-forms". The object $dx \otimes dy \otimes s$ on $M = \mathbb{R}^2$ would then be a legitimate element, and its evaluation on the ordered pair $(\partial_x, \partial_y)$ would be $s$ while its evaluation on $(\partial_y, \partial_x)$ would be $0$. But the quantity that a curvature is meant to measure, the failure of $\nabla_X \nabla_Y - \nabla_Y \nabla_X$ to vanish, is antisymmetric in $(X, Y)$ by construction, and an antisymmetric quantity stored in a non-alternating container carries redundant components that are never used and that transform in an uncontrolled way under coordinate changes (the full Jacobian rather than its minors, as explained on [[Def - Differential k-Form on a Manifold]]). Alternation is exactly the condition that makes the evaluation $\omega(X_1, \dots, X_p)$ change sign under a transposition of arguments, and it is what makes $\Omega^p(M; E)$ vanish for $p > n$ — the correct behaviour for a container of antisymmetric quantities on an $n$-manifold.

The second clause is *smoothness* of the section. If we allowed arbitrary set-theoretic assignments $m \mapsto \omega_m \in \Lambda^p T_m^*M \otimes E_m$, then in a local frame the coefficient forms $\omega_j$ would be arbitrary, and the exterior covariant derivative $d^\nabla$ of the next section, which differentiates those coefficients, would be undefined. Continuity would not suffice either: the curvature is defined through $d^\nabla \circ d^\nabla$, which differentiates twice, and its symmetry properties rest on the equality of mixed second partial derivatives of the coefficients, which is a $C^2$ statement. Requiring $C^\infty$ is the standing convention of the series and costs nothing here, since every object we construct from smooth data is smooth.

The third clause concerns *which products are defined*. We define a wedge of a scalar form with an $E$-valued form, a pairing of an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued form with an $E$-valued form, and a wedge-composition of two $\operatorname{End}E$-valued forms — and we do **not** define a product $\Omega^p(M; E) \times \Omega^q(M; E) \to \Omega^{p+q}(M; E)$. The reason is not laziness but impossibility: there is no bilinear map $E_m \times E_m \to E_m$ that is compatible with all changes of frame. The precise statement, proved below among the examples, is that a bilinear map $\mu \colon W \times W \to W$ on a real vector space $W$ satisfying $\mu(gv, gw) = g\mu(v, w)$ for all $g \in GL(W)$ is zero, because $g = 2\operatorname{id}_W$ forces $4\mu = 2\mu$. Each of the three products we do define comes from a fibrewise bilinear map that *is* compatible with every change of frame: scalar multiplication $\mathbb{R} \times W \to W$, evaluation $\operatorname{End}(W) \times W \to W$, and composition $\operatorname{End}(W) \times \operatorname{End}(W) \to \operatorname{End}(W)$. A reader who wants a product of two $E$-valued forms must first supply extra structure on $E$ — a metric, giving the scalar-valued pairing $\Omega^p(M; E) \times \Omega^q(M; E) \to \Omega^{p+q}(M)$; or a Lie bracket on the fibres, giving the bracket of Lie-algebra-valued forms of [[Def - Lie-Algebra-Valued Differential Forms and Their Bracket]] — and that extra structure is exactly what the bilinear map is compatible with.

The fourth clause is that all three products are defined *pointwise*, by the fibrewise linear algebra, and involve no differentiation. Dropping this clause — defining, say, the pairing $a \wedge \omega$ in a frame by a formula in the coefficient forms without checking that the formula is frame-independent — is the classical way to produce a non-object. The examples below exhibit the simplest failure: the rule "add the coefficient forms of $\omega$" produces, for $\omega = dx \otimes e_1 + x\,dy \otimes e_2$ on the trivial rank-two bundle over $\mathbb{R}^2$, the answer $dx + x\,dy$ in the frame $e$ and the answer $\tfrac12 dx + x\,dy$ in the frame $e' = e\operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$. A frame formula defines a global object only when it transforms correctly, and the transformation law $\omega' = g^{-1}\omega$ proved below is the tool with which every such check in this chapter is carried out.

The fifth clause is the *sign* in the right wedge, $\omega \wedge \alpha := (-1)^{pq}\alpha \wedge \omega$ for $\omega \in \Omega^p(M; E)$ and $\alpha \in \Omega^q(M)$. This is not a free choice: the decomposable elements $\beta \otimes s$ must satisfy $(\beta \otimes s) \wedge \alpha = (\beta \wedge \alpha) \otimes s$ if the wedge is to be the ordinary one when $E = \underline{\mathbb{R}}$, and graded anticommutativity of scalar forms then forces the sign. Had we set $\omega \wedge \alpha := \alpha \wedge \omega$, the two descriptions of $(dx \otimes s) \wedge dy$ would disagree, one giving $dx \wedge dy \otimes s$ and the other $dy \wedge dx \otimes s = -dx \wedge dy \otimes s$. A reader who has followed these five clauses can reconstruct the definition: take sections of $\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E$, evaluate them on vector fields through the fibrewise identification with alternating maps, describe them locally through a frame, and define exactly those products that fibrewise linear algebra supplies, with the signs dictated by the scalar case.

# The Definition

**The spaces.** Let $\pi \colon E \to M$ be a smooth real vector bundle of rank $k$ and $p \ge 0$ an integer. The **space of $E$-valued differential $p$-forms** on $M$ is
$$\Omega^p(M; E) := \Gamma\big(\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E\big),$$
the smooth sections of the tensor product bundle of [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles]], whose fibre over $m$ is $\Lambda^p T_m^*M \otimes E_m$. It is a real vector space and a $C^\infty(M)$-module under the pointwise operations, because $\Gamma$ of any vector bundle is ([[Def - Section of a Vector Bundle]]). For $p = 0$, $\Lambda^0 T^*M = \underline{\mathbb{R}}$ and $\underline{\mathbb{R}} \otimes E \cong E$ through $t \otimes v \mapsto tv$, so $\Omega^0(M; E) = \Gamma(E)$. For $p > n = \dim M$, $\Lambda^p T_m^*M = 0$ and hence $\Omega^p(M; E) = 0$. Applying the definition to the bundle $\operatorname{End}E$ in place of $E$ gives the **$\operatorname{End}E$-valued $p$-forms** $\Omega^p(M; \operatorname{End}E) = \Gamma(\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes \operatorname{End}E)$, with $\Omega^0(M; \operatorname{End}E) = \Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$. Over an open subset $U \subseteq M$ we write $\Omega^p(U; E) := \Gamma(U; \Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E)$. An element of the form $\beta \otimes s$ with $\beta \in \Omega^p(M)$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$ — the section $m \mapsto \beta_m \otimes s(m)$, smooth because it is the composite of the smooth map $m \mapsto (\beta_m, s(m))$ with the smooth fibrewise bilinear map $\Lambda^p T^*M \oplus E \to \Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E$ — is called **decomposable**.

**Evaluation on vector fields.** For $\omega \in \Omega^p(M; E)$ and vector fields $X_1, \dots, X_p \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$, the section $\omega(X_1, \dots, X_p) \in \Gamma(E)$ is defined pointwise through the linear isomorphism of Lemma 1 below: $\omega(X_1, \dots, X_p)(m) := \Phi(\omega_m)(X_1(m), \dots, X_p(m))$, where $\Phi \colon \Lambda^p T_m^*M \otimes E_m \to \operatorname{Alt}^p(T_mM; E_m)$ sends $\beta \otimes v$ to the map $(v_1, \dots, v_p) \mapsto \beta(v_1, \dots, v_p)\, v$. On decomposables, $(\beta \otimes s)(X_1, \dots, X_p) = \beta(X_1, \dots, X_p)\, s$. The map $(X_1, \dots, X_p) \mapsto \omega(X_1, \dots, X_p)$ is alternating and $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear (Lemma 1 and Corollary 3 below).

> [!note]- Lemma 1 (the fibrewise identification with alternating maps), with proof
> **Statement.** Let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional real vector spaces and $p \ge 0$. Write $\operatorname{Alt}^p(V; W)$ for the vector space of alternating $p$-linear maps $V^p \to W$ (for $p = 0$, $\operatorname{Alt}^0(V; W) := W$). The linear map $\Phi \colon \Lambda^p V^* \otimes W \to \operatorname{Alt}^p(V; W)$ determined on decomposables by $\Phi(\beta \otimes w)(v_1, \dots, v_p) = \beta(v_1, \dots, v_p)\, w$ is a linear isomorphism.
>
> **Proof.** We must show that $\Phi$ is well defined, injective and surjective.
>
> **Well-definedness of $\Phi$.** The assignment $(\beta, w) \mapsto \big[(v_1, \dots, v_p) \mapsto \beta(v_1, \dots, v_p)\, w\big]$ is bilinear in $(\beta, w)$, since $\beta(v_1, \dots, v_p)$ is linear in $\beta$ and the product of a scalar with $w$ is linear in $w$; its value is alternating and $p$-linear in $(v_1, \dots, v_p)$ because $\beta$ is (by the definition of $\Lambda^p V^*$ as the alternating $p$-tensors, [[Def - Alternating Tensor and Lambda k V Dual]]). By the universal property of the tensor product ([[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]]), a bilinear map $\Lambda^p V^* \times W \to \operatorname{Alt}^p(V; W)$ factors uniquely through a linear map $\Lambda^p V^* \otimes W \to \operatorname{Alt}^p(V; W)$. This is $\Phi$.
>
> **Injectivity and surjectivity.** Choose a basis $(w_1, \dots, w_k)$ of $W$, and let $(w^1, \dots, w^k)$ be the dual basis of $W^*$, so that $w^a(w_b) = \delta^a_b$. Every element of $\Lambda^p V^* \otimes W$ can be written uniquely as $\sum_{a=1}^k \beta_a \otimes w_a$ with $\beta_a \in \Lambda^p V^*$ (by the basis description of the tensor product, [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]]: if $(\epsilon_I)$ is a basis of $\Lambda^p V^*$ then $(\epsilon_I \otimes w_a)$ is a basis of $\Lambda^p V^* \otimes W$, and grouping the coefficients by $a$ gives the claim). Given $\Psi \in \operatorname{Alt}^p(V; W)$, define $\Psi_a := w^a \circ \Psi \colon V^p \to \mathbb{R}$; each $\Psi_a$ is alternating and $p$-linear (as the composite of the alternating $p$-linear $\Psi$ with the linear $w^a$), hence $\Psi_a \in \Lambda^p V^*$, and
> $$\Phi\Big(\sum_a \Psi_a \otimes w_a\Big)(v_1, \dots, v_p) = \sum_a \Psi_a(v_1, \dots, v_p)\, w_a = \sum_a w^a\big(\Psi(v_1, \dots, v_p)\big)\, w_a = \Psi(v_1, \dots, v_p) \qquad \text{(definition of } \Phi \text{; definition of } \Psi_a \text{; expansion of a vector in the basis } (w_a)\text{)},$$
> so $\Phi$ is surjective. If $\Phi(\sum_a \beta_a \otimes w_a) = 0$, then applying $w^b$ to the identity $\sum_a \beta_a(v_1, \dots, v_p)\, w_a = 0$ gives $\beta_b(v_1, \dots, v_p) = 0$ for all $v_1, \dots, v_p$ and all $b$ (since $w^b(w_a) = \delta^b_a$), so every $\beta_b = 0$ and the element was zero; thus $\Phi$ is injective.
>
> **Conclusion.** $\Phi$ is a well-defined linear bijection, hence a linear isomorphism, and its inverse sends $\Psi$ to $\sum_a (w^a \circ \Psi) \otimes w_a$. $\blacksquare$

**Local description.** Let $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$ be a local frame of $E$ over an open $U \subseteq M$. Every $\omega \in \Omega^p(M; E)$ has, over $U$, a unique expression
$$\omega|_U = \sum_{j=1}^k \omega_j \otimes e_j =: e\,\omega_e, \qquad \omega_j \in \Omega^p(U),$$
where $\omega_e := (\omega_1, \dots, \omega_k)^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the **coefficient column of $\omega$ in the frame $e$**; conversely every column of smooth $p$-forms on $U$ defines by this formula an element of $\Omega^p(U; E)$ (Lemma 2). Under a change of frame $e' = eg$, $g \colon U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ smooth, the coefficient columns are related by
$$\omega_{e'} = g^{-1}\,\omega_e, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \omega'_j = \sum_{i=1}^k (g^{-1})_{ji}\,\omega_i,$$
where the product of the function-valued matrix $g^{-1}$ with the column of forms is the entrywise product of functions and forms (Lemma 2). In particular an $E$-valued $p$-form on $U$ is the same thing as a column of $k$ scalar $p$-forms transforming by $g^{-1}$, and an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued $p$-form on $U$ is a $k \times k$ matrix $a = (a_{ij})$ of scalar $p$-forms, $a|_U = \sum_{i, j} a_{ij} \otimes E_{ij}$, transforming by conjugation, $a' = g^{-1} a g$ (Lemma 2, part (d)).

> [!note]- Lemma 2 (local frames of the tensor product bundle; the local description and its transformation law), with proof
> **Statement.** Let $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$ be a local frame of $E$ over an open $U \subseteq M$.
> (a) If moreover $(U, x^1, \dots, x^n)$ is a chart, then the sections $dx^I \otimes e_j$, with $I$ running over the increasing multi-indices of length $p$ and $j$ over $1, \dots, k$, form a local frame of $\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E$ over $U$.
> (b) For every $\omega \in \Omega^p(M; E)$ there are unique $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_k \in \Omega^p(U)$ with $\omega|_U = \sum_j \omega_j \otimes e_j$; conversely for any $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_k \in \Omega^p(U)$ the section $\sum_j \omega_j \otimes e_j$ lies in $\Omega^p(U; E)$.
> (c) If $e' = eg$ is a second frame over $U$, $g \colon U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ smooth, then the coefficient columns satisfy $\omega_e = g\,\omega_{e'}$, equivalently $\omega_{e'} = g^{-1}\omega_e$.
> (d) The matrix units $E_{ij}$ of the frame $e$ form a local frame of $\operatorname{End}E$ over $U$; every $a \in \Omega^p(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ has a unique expression $a|_U = \sum_{i, j} a_{ij} \otimes E_{ij}$ with $a_{ij} \in \Omega^p(U)$, and under $e' = eg$ the matrices of forms satisfy $a' = g^{-1} a g$, that is, $a'_{ij} = \sum_{k', l} (g^{-1})_{i k'}\, a_{k' l}\, g_{l j}$.
>
> **Proof.** We prove the four parts in order.
>
> **Part (a): a frame of the tensor product bundle.** We need to show that the $dx^I \otimes e_j$ are smooth sections of $\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E$ over $U$ whose values at each $m \in U$ form a basis of $\Lambda^p T_m^*M \otimes E_m$. **Pointwise basis:** at $m \in U$ the covectors $dx^I|_m$ form a basis of $\Lambda^p T_m^*M$ (the coordinate description of $\Lambda^p T^*M$ on [[Def - Differential k-Form on a Manifold]]) and the vectors $e_j(m)$ form a basis of $E_m$ (definition of a local frame, [[Def - Local Frame]]); by the basis description of the tensor product ([[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]]: tensor products of basis vectors of the two factors form a basis of the tensor product) the elements $dx^I|_m \otimes e_j(m)$ form a basis of $\Lambda^p T_m^*M \otimes E_m$. **Smoothness:** the local trivialisation of $\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E$ over $U$ constructed on [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles]] is $\tau_\Lambda \otimes \tau_E$, where $\tau_\Lambda$ sends $dx^I|_m$ to $(m, \epsilon_I)$ for the standard basis $(\epsilon_I)$ of $\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{p}}$ and $\tau_E$ sends $e_j(m)$ to $(m, \epsilon_j)$ for the standard basis $(\epsilon_j)$ of $\mathbb{R}^k$; under it the section $m \mapsto dx^I|_m \otimes e_j(m)$ becomes the map $m \mapsto (m, \epsilon_I \otimes \epsilon_j)$, which is smooth as a map $U \to U \times (\mathbb{R}^{\binom{n}{p}} \otimes \mathbb{R}^k)$. Since a local trivialisation is a diffeomorphism onto its image, the section is smooth (a section is smooth if and only if its composite with a local trivialisation is smooth, [[Def - Section of a Vector Bundle]]). This proves (a).
>
> **Part (b): existence, uniqueness and smoothness of the coefficient forms.** **Uniqueness and pointwise existence:** fix $m \in U$. The linear map $(\Lambda^p T_m^*M)^k \to \Lambda^p T_m^*M \otimes E_m$, $(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_k) \mapsto \sum_j \beta_j \otimes e_j(m)$, is a linear isomorphism, because expanding each $\beta_j$ in a basis $(\epsilon_I)$ of $\Lambda^p T_m^*M$ turns it into the map sending the coefficient array $(c_{I j})$ to $\sum_{I, j} c_{Ij}\, \epsilon_I \otimes e_j(m)$, and $(\epsilon_I \otimes e_j(m))$ is a basis of the target (basis description of the tensor product, as in (a)). Hence $\omega_m = \sum_j \omega_j(m) \otimes e_j(m)$ holds for a unique $k$-tuple $(\omega_1(m), \dots, \omega_k(m))$ of elements of $\Lambda^p T_m^*M$. **Smoothness of $m \mapsto \omega_j(m)$:** smoothness is a local property, so it suffices to check it on a neighbourhood of each point of $U$; shrink $U$ around a point to a chart domain $(U', x)$ with $U' \subseteq U$. By (a) and [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]] — for a local frame $(\sigma_\alpha)$ of a vector bundle over $U'$, every smooth section over $U'$ is uniquely $\sum_\alpha f^\alpha \sigma_\alpha$ with $f^\alpha \in C^\infty(U')$, and conversely every such combination of smooth functions is a smooth section — there are unique smooth functions $\omega_{Ij} \in C^\infty(U')$ with $\omega|_{U'} = \sum_{I, j} \omega_{Ij}\, dx^I \otimes e_j$. Grouping by $j$, $\omega|_{U'} = \sum_j \big(\sum_I \omega_{Ij}\, dx^I\big) \otimes e_j$ (bilinearity of the fibrewise tensor product), so by the pointwise uniqueness just proved $\omega_j|_{U'} = \sum_I \omega_{Ij}\, dx^I$, which is a smooth $p$-form on $U'$ because its coefficients in the coordinate coframe are smooth ([[Def - Differential k-Form on a Manifold]]: a form is smooth if and only if its coefficient functions in a chart are). **Converse:** given $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_k \in \Omega^p(U)$, the section $\sum_j \omega_j \otimes e_j$ is smooth on each chart domain $U' \subseteq U$ by the converse half of [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]] applied to the frame of (a) with the smooth coefficients $\omega_{Ij}$ of $\omega_j$, hence smooth on $U$. This proves (b).
>
> **Part (c): the transformation law.** Assume $e' = eg$, so that $e'_j = \sum_i e_i\, g_{ij}$ with $g_{ij} \in C^\infty(U)$ (smoothness of $g$). Let $\omega_e = (\omega_i)$ and $\omega_{e'} = (\omega'_j)$ be the two coefficient columns. We compute
> $$\omega|_U = \sum_j \omega'_j \otimes e'_j = \sum_j \omega'_j \otimes \Big(\sum_i g_{ij}\, e_i\Big) = \sum_i \Big(\sum_j g_{ij}\, \omega'_j\Big) \otimes e_i \qquad \text{(definition of } \omega_{e'} \text{; } e' = eg \text{; bilinearity of the fibrewise tensor product, which lets the function } g_{ij} \text{ pass from the } E \text{ factor to the form factor)}.$$
> By the uniqueness in (b) applied to the frame $e$, the coefficient of $e_i$ is $\omega_i$; hence $\omega_i = \sum_j g_{ij}\, \omega'_j$, that is, $\omega_e = g\,\omega_{e'}$. Multiplying on the left by the smooth matrix-valued function $g^{-1}$ (which exists pointwise since $g(m) \in GL_k(\mathbb{R})$, and is smooth because matrix inversion is a smooth map on $GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ by Cramer's rule) gives $\omega_{e'} = g^{-1}\omega_e$. This proves (c).
>
> **Part (d): the endomorphism case.** **The $E_{ij}$ are a frame:** at each $m \in U$ the endomorphism $E_{ij}(m)$ of $E_m$ has, in the basis $(e_l(m))$, the matrix with a single entry $1$ in row $i$ and column $j$ (since $E_{ij}(e_l) = \delta_{jl} e_i$), and the $k^2$ matrix units form a basis of the $k \times k$ matrices, hence the $E_{ij}(m)$ form a basis of $\operatorname{End}(E_m)$. They are smooth because under the local trivialisation of $\operatorname{Hom}(E, E)$ over $U$ built on [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles]] — $\phi \mapsto \tau_E \circ \phi \circ \tau_E^{-1}$, which sends an endomorphism of $E_m$ to its matrix in the basis $(e_l(m))$ — the section $E_{ij}$ becomes the constant matrix unit, a smooth map. So $(E_{ij})$ is a local frame of $\operatorname{End}E$ over $U$, and the unique expression $a|_U = \sum_{i, j} a_{ij} \otimes E_{ij}$ with $a_{ij} \in \Omega^p(U)$ follows from (b) applied to the bundle $\operatorname{End}E$ and this frame. **Transformation of the matrix units:** let $E'_{ij}$ be the matrix units of $e' = eg$, so $E'_{ij}(e'_l) = \delta_{jl}\, e'_i$. We express $E'_{ij}$ in the frame $(E_{k'l})$ by evaluating on $e_l$. Since $e' = eg$ gives $e = e' g^{-1}$, that is, $e_l = \sum_m e'_m (g^{-1})_{ml}$, we have
> $$E'_{ij}(e_l) = \sum_m (g^{-1})_{ml}\, E'_{ij}(e'_m) = (g^{-1})_{jl}\, e'_i = (g^{-1})_{jl} \sum_{k'} g_{k' i}\, e_{k'} \qquad \text{(linearity of } E'_{ij} \text{; } E'_{ij}(e'_m) = \delta_{jm} e'_i \text{; } e'_i = \sum_{k'} e_{k'} g_{k'i}\text{)},$$
> while $\big(\sum_{k', l'} c_{k'l'} E_{k'l'}\big)(e_l) = \sum_{k'} c_{k'l}\, e_{k'}$ (since $E_{k'l'}(e_l) = \delta_{l'l} e_{k'}$). Comparing coefficients of the basis $(e_{k'})$ gives $c_{k'l} = g_{k'i}\,(g^{-1})_{jl}$, that is,
> $$E'_{ij} = \sum_{k', l} g_{k' i}\,(g^{-1})_{j l}\, E_{k' l}.$$
> **Transformation of the matrix of forms:** substituting,
> $$a|_U = \sum_{i, j} a'_{ij} \otimes E'_{ij} = \sum_{i, j} a'_{ij} \otimes \sum_{k', l} g_{k'i}(g^{-1})_{jl}\, E_{k'l} = \sum_{k', l} \Big(\sum_{i, j} g_{k'i}\, a'_{ij}\, (g^{-1})_{jl}\Big) \otimes E_{k'l} \qquad \text{(expression of } a \text{ in the frame } (E'_{ij}) \text{; the formula for } E'_{ij} \text{; bilinearity of the fibrewise tensor product)},$$
> and by uniqueness in the frame $(E_{k'l})$, $a_{k'l} = \sum_{i,j} g_{k'i}\, a'_{ij}\, (g^{-1})_{jl}$, that is, $a = g\, a'\, g^{-1}$ as matrices of forms (the entries of $g$ and $g^{-1}$ are functions, which commute with forms). Multiplying on the left by $g^{-1}$ and on the right by $g$ gives $a' = g^{-1} a g$. This proves (d).
>
> **Conclusion.** A local frame of $E$ induces a local frame of $\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E$ and of $\operatorname{End}E$; an $E$-valued $p$-form is locally a column of scalar $p$-forms transforming by $\omega_{e'} = g^{-1}\omega_e$, and an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued $p$-form is locally a matrix of scalar $p$-forms transforming by $a' = g^{-1} a g$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Corollary 3 (evaluation on vector fields is alternating, $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear, and computed by coefficients), with proof
> **Statement.** For $\omega \in \Omega^p(M; E)$ and $X_1, \dots, X_p \in \mathfrak{X}(M)$, the section $\omega(X_1, \dots, X_p)$ is smooth, and in a local frame $e$ over $U$ with $\omega|_U = \sum_j \omega_j \otimes e_j$ one has $\omega(X_1, \dots, X_p)|_U = \sum_j \omega_j(X_1, \dots, X_p)\, e_j$. The map $(X_1, \dots, X_p) \mapsto \omega(X_1, \dots, X_p)$ is alternating and $C^\infty(M)$-linear in each argument.
>
> **Proof.** **The coefficient formula.** At $m \in U$, by the definition of the evaluation and the linearity of $\Phi$,
> $$\omega(X_1, \dots, X_p)(m) = \Phi\Big(\sum_j \omega_j(m) \otimes e_j(m)\Big)(X_1(m), \dots, X_p(m)) = \sum_j \omega_j(m)\big(X_1(m), \dots, X_p(m)\big)\, e_j(m) \qquad \text{(local description; linearity of } \Phi \text{ and its value on decomposables)}.$$
> **Smoothness.** The functions $\omega_j(X_1, \dots, X_p)$ are smooth on $U$ (evaluation of a smooth form on smooth vector fields is a smooth function, [[Def - Differential k-Form on a Manifold]]), so by the converse half of [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]] the right-hand side is a smooth section of $E$ over $U$; since every point of $M$ has a neighbourhood carrying a local frame ([[Def - Local Trivialization]] together with [[Def - Local Frame]]), $\omega(X_1, \dots, X_p)$ is smooth on $M$. **Alternation and multilinearity.** These hold pointwise because $\Phi(\omega_m)$ is an alternating $p$-linear map $T_mM^p \to E_m$ (Lemma 1); $C^\infty(M)$-linearity in each argument holds because, at each $m$, $\Phi(\omega_m)(\dots, f(m) X_i(m), \dots) = f(m)\, \Phi(\omega_m)(\dots, X_i(m), \dots)$ by linearity of $\Phi(\omega_m)$ in its $i$-th slot. **Conclusion.** The evaluation is a smooth, alternating, $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear map $\mathfrak{X}(M)^p \to \Gamma(E)$ computed in a frame coefficient by coefficient. $\blacksquare$

**The wedge of a scalar form with an $E$-valued form.** For $\alpha \in \Omega^q(M)$ and $\omega \in \Omega^p(M; E)$ define $\alpha \wedge \omega \in \Omega^{p+q}(M; E)$ pointwise by
$$(\alpha \wedge \omega)_m := \big((\alpha_m \wedge \cdot) \otimes \operatorname{id}_{E_m}\big)(\omega_m),$$
the image of $\omega_m$ under the tensor product of the linear map $\Lambda^p T_m^*M \to \Lambda^{p+q} T_m^*M$, $\beta \mapsto \alpha_m \wedge \beta$, with the identity of $E_m$; on decomposables this reads
$$\alpha \wedge (\beta \otimes s) = (\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes s.$$
The wedge on the right is defined by $\omega \wedge \alpha := (-1)^{pq}\, \alpha \wedge \omega$, so that on decomposables $(\beta \otimes s) \wedge \alpha = (\beta \wedge \alpha) \otimes s$ (Proposition 4). In a local frame, $(\alpha \wedge \omega)_e = \alpha \wedge \omega_e$ entrywise, that is, $(\alpha \wedge \omega)_j = \alpha \wedge \omega_j$.

**The pairing of an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued form with an $E$-valued form.** For $a \in \Omega^q(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ and $\omega \in \Omega^p(M; E)$ define $a \wedge \omega \in \Omega^{p+q}(M; E)$ pointwise as the image of $(a_m, \omega_m)$ under the bilinear map
$$\big(\Lambda^q T_m^*M \otimes \operatorname{End}(E_m)\big) \times \big(\Lambda^p T_m^*M \otimes E_m\big) \longrightarrow \Lambda^{p+q} T_m^*M \otimes E_m, \qquad (\alpha \otimes \phi,\ \beta \otimes v) \longmapsto (\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes \phi(v),$$
the tensor product of the wedge product of scalar forms with the natural contraction $\operatorname{End}(E_m) \otimes E_m \to E_m$, $\phi \otimes v \mapsto \phi(v)$ — this is the "combination of the wedge product and the natural contraction $\operatorname{End}(E) \otimes E \to E$" of Haydys's Proposition 15 (p. 9). On decomposables, $(\alpha \otimes \phi) \wedge (\beta \otimes s) = (\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes \phi(s)$. In a local frame, with $a|_U = \sum_{i,j} a_{ij} \otimes E_{ij}$ and $\omega|_U = \sum_j \omega_j \otimes e_j$,
$$(a \wedge \omega)_e = a \wedge \omega_e, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad (a \wedge \omega)_i = \sum_{j=1}^k a_{ij} \wedge \omega_j,$$
the matrix-times-column product with wedge of entries; and on vector fields
$$(a \wedge \omega)(X_1, \dots, X_{p+q}) = \frac{1}{p!\,q!} \sum_{\sigma \in S_{p+q}} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, a\big(X_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(q)}\big)\Big(\omega\big(X_{\sigma(q+1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(p+q)}\big)\Big),$$
so that for $q = p = 1$, $(a \wedge \omega)(X, Y) = a(X)\big(\omega(Y)\big) - a(Y)\big(\omega(X)\big)$ (Proposition 4). For $q = 0$ the pairing is the pointwise application of the endomorphism field $a \in \Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$ to the coefficients of $\omega$, and for $p = 0$ it is the evaluation $a \wedge s = a\,s$ of an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued form on a section, $a\,s := \sum_{i,j} a_{ij}\, \sigma_j \otimes e_i$ locally.

**The wedge-composition of two $\operatorname{End}E$-valued forms.** For $a \in \Omega^q(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ and $b \in \Omega^p(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ define $a \wedge b \in \Omega^{p+q}(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ pointwise as the image of $(a_m, b_m)$ under the bilinear map determined by
$$(\alpha \otimes \phi,\ \beta \otimes \psi) \longmapsto (\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes (\phi \circ \psi),$$
the tensor product of the wedge product with composition $\operatorname{End}(E_m) \otimes \operatorname{End}(E_m) \to \operatorname{End}(E_m)$. In a local frame,
$$(a \wedge b)_{ij} = \sum_{l=1}^k a_{il} \wedge b_{lj},$$
matrix multiplication with wedge of entries, and on vector fields the same alternating-sum formula as for the pairing holds with $a(\dots) \circ b(\dots)$ in place of $a(\dots)(\omega(\dots))$; for $q = p = 1$, $(a \wedge b)(X, Y) = a(X) b(Y) - a(Y) b(X)$, so that $(a \wedge a)(X, Y) = [a(X), a(Y)]$ is the commutator of endomorphisms (Proposition 4).

> [!note]- Proposition 4 (well-definedness, smoothness, local formulas, evaluation formulas, and the algebraic identities of the three products), with proof
> **Statement.** Let $\alpha \in \Omega^r(M)$, $\gamma \in \Omega^q(M)$, $a \in \Omega^q(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, $b \in \Omega^p(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, $c \in \Omega^{p'}(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, $\omega \in \Omega^p(M; E)$.
> (a) The three products are well defined and produce smooth sections; in a local frame $e$ they are given by $(\alpha \wedge \omega)_e = \alpha \wedge \omega_e$, $(a \wedge \omega)_e = a \wedge \omega_e$ and $(a \wedge b) = a \wedge b$ as matrices, entrywise wedge products understood.
> (b) On decomposables, $(\beta \otimes s) \wedge \alpha = (\beta \wedge \alpha) \otimes s$, where $\omega \wedge \alpha := (-1)^{pr}\alpha \wedge \omega$.
> (c) Evaluation formulas: $(a \wedge \omega)(X_1, \dots, X_{p+q}) = \frac{1}{p!\,q!}\sum_{\sigma \in S_{p+q}} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, a(X_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(q)})\big(\omega(X_{\sigma(q+1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(p+q)})\big)$, and the same with $\circ$ for $a \wedge b$; in particular for $p = q = 1$, $(a \wedge \omega)(X, Y) = a(X)(\omega(Y)) - a(Y)(\omega(X))$ and $(a \wedge b)(X, Y) = a(X) b(Y) - a(Y) b(X)$.
> (d) Associativity and compatibility: $(\gamma \wedge \alpha) \wedge \omega = \gamma \wedge (\alpha \wedge \omega)$; $(a \wedge b) \wedge \omega = a \wedge (b \wedge \omega)$; $(a \wedge b) \wedge c = a \wedge (b \wedge c)$; and $a \wedge (\alpha \wedge \omega) = (-1)^{qr}\, \alpha \wedge (a \wedge \omega)$, $a \wedge (\alpha \wedge b) = (-1)^{qr}\, \alpha \wedge (a \wedge b)$ (scalar forms pass through $\operatorname{End}E$-valued forms with the graded sign; in particular an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued form of even degree commutes with every scalar form).
> (e) All three products are $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear.
>
> **Proof.** **Step 0: the fibrewise bilinear maps exist and are unique.** Fix $m \in M$ and write $V := T_mM$, $W := E_m$. The assignment $(\alpha, \phi, \beta, v) \mapsto (\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes \phi(v)$ from $\Lambda^q V^* \times \operatorname{End}(W) \times \Lambda^p V^* \times W$ to $\Lambda^{p+q} V^* \otimes W$ is linear in each of its four arguments separately (the wedge product is bilinear, [[Thm - Wedge Product Properties]] (a); $\phi(v)$ is bilinear in $(\phi, v)$; the tensor product symbol is bilinear). By the universal property of the tensor product ([[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces]]), applied first to the pair $(\alpha, \phi)$ and then to the pair $(\beta, v)$, a $4$-linear map factors uniquely through a bilinear map $(\Lambda^q V^* \otimes \operatorname{End}W) \times (\Lambda^p V^* \otimes W) \to \Lambda^{p+q} V^* \otimes W$ taking the prescribed values on pairs of decomposables. Uniqueness holds because decomposables span each tensor product. The same argument with $(\alpha, \phi, \beta, \psi) \mapsto (\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes (\phi \circ \psi)$ (composition is bilinear) gives the wedge-composition, and with $(\alpha, \beta, v) \mapsto (\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes v$ gives the scalar wedge, which coincides with the map $(\alpha \wedge \cdot) \otimes \operatorname{id}_W$ because both are linear on $\Lambda^p V^* \otimes W$ and agree on decomposables, which span.
>
> **Step 1: the local formulas, part (a).** Let $e$ be a local frame over $U$ with $\omega|_U = \sum_j \omega_j \otimes e_j$, $a|_U = \sum_{i,l} a_{il} \otimes E_{il}$, $b|_U = \sum_{l', j} b_{l'j} \otimes E_{l'j}$ (Lemma 2 (b), (d)). Pointwise on $U$,
> $$\alpha \wedge \omega = \sum_j (\alpha \wedge \omega_j) \otimes e_j \qquad \text{(linearity of } (\alpha \wedge \cdot) \otimes \operatorname{id} \text{ and its value on decomposables)},$$
> $$a \wedge \omega = \sum_{i, l, j} (a_{il} \wedge \omega_j) \otimes E_{il}(e_j) = \sum_{i, l, j} (a_{il} \wedge \omega_j) \otimes \delta_{lj}\, e_i = \sum_i \Big(\sum_j a_{ij} \wedge \omega_j\Big) \otimes e_i \qquad \text{(bilinearity and the value on decomposables; } E_{il}(e_j) = \delta_{lj} e_i \text{; bilinearity of } \otimes\text{)},$$
> $$a \wedge b = \sum_{i, l, l', j} (a_{il} \wedge b_{l'j}) \otimes E_{il} \circ E_{l'j} = \sum_{i, l, j} (a_{il} \wedge b_{lj}) \otimes E_{ij} \qquad \text{(bilinearity; } E_{il} \circ E_{l'j} = \delta_{ll'} E_{ij} \text{, which follows from } E_{il}(E_{l'j}(e_r)) = \delta_{jr}\, E_{il}(e_{l'}) = \delta_{jr}\delta_{ll'}\, e_i\text{)}.$$
> By uniqueness of coefficients (Lemma 2), these are the stated local formulas. **Smoothness:** each entry $\alpha \wedge \omega_j$, $\sum_j a_{ij} \wedge \omega_j$, $\sum_l a_{il} \wedge b_{lj}$ is a smooth scalar form on $U$ (the wedge product of smooth forms is smooth and $\Omega^\bullet(U)$ is closed under sums, [[Def - The Wedge Product on a Manifold]]), so by Lemma 2 (b) the products are smooth sections over $U$; since $M$ is covered by frame domains, they are smooth sections over $M$. This proves (a).
>
> **Step 2: the right wedge, part (b).** For $\beta \in \Omega^p(M)$, $s \in \Gamma(E)$, $\alpha \in \Omega^r(M)$,
> $$(\beta \otimes s) \wedge \alpha = (-1)^{pr}\, \alpha \wedge (\beta \otimes s) = (-1)^{pr} (\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes s = (\beta \wedge \alpha) \otimes s \qquad \text{(definition of the right wedge; value on decomposables; graded anticommutativity } \alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{pr}\beta \wedge \alpha \text{ of the wedge product properties, part (c))}.$$
> The graded anticommutativity invoked is part (c) of [[Thm - Wedge Product Properties]].
>
> **Step 3: the evaluation formulas, part (c).** Both sides of the claimed identity for $a \wedge \omega$ are, at each point $m$, bilinear in $(a_m, \omega_m)$: the left side by Step 0 and the linearity of $\Phi$ (Lemma 1), the right side because $a(\dots)$ and $\omega(\dots)$ are linear in $a$ and $\omega$ respectively and $a(\dots)(\omega(\dots))$ is bilinear in the pair. Hence it suffices to verify the identity for $a = \alpha \otimes \phi$ and $\omega = \beta \otimes s$ with $\alpha \in \Omega^q(M)$, $\phi \in \Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$, $\beta \in \Omega^p(M)$, $s \in \Gamma(E)$, since decomposables span each fibre. For these,
> $$(a \wedge \omega)(X_1, \dots, X_{p+q}) = (\alpha \wedge \beta)(X_1, \dots, X_{p+q})\, \phi(s) \qquad \text{(value on decomposables; evaluation of a decomposable, Lemma 1)}$$
> $$= \frac{(p+q)!}{p!\,q!}\, \operatorname{Alt}(\alpha \otimes \beta)(X_1, \dots, X_{p+q})\, \phi(s) = \frac{1}{p!\,q!} \sum_{\sigma \in S_{p+q}} \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, \alpha(X_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(q)})\, \beta(X_{\sigma(q+1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(p+q)})\, \phi(s) \qquad \text{(determinant convention for } \wedge \text{; the formula for } \operatorname{Alt} \text{ with } r = p + q\text{)},$$
> The determinant convention for the wedge is that of [[Def - The Wedge Product on a Manifold]], and the alternating-sum formula for $\operatorname{Alt}$ is that of [[Def - Alternating Tensor and Lambda k V Dual]].
> and the right-hand side of the claimed identity is
> $$\frac{1}{p!\,q!} \sum_\sigma \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, a(X_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(q)})\Big(\omega(X_{\sigma(q+1)}, \dots)\Big) = \frac{1}{p!\,q!} \sum_\sigma \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, \alpha(X_{\sigma(1)}, \dots, X_{\sigma(q)})\, \phi\Big(\beta(X_{\sigma(q+1)}, \dots)\, s\Big) \qquad \text{(evaluation of decomposables: } a(\dots) = \alpha(\dots)\phi \text{ and } \omega(\dots) = \beta(\dots) s\text{)},$$
> which equals the previous line by linearity of $\phi$. For $p = q = 1$ the sum over $S_2 = \{\operatorname{id}, (1\,2)\}$ with $\frac{1}{1!\,1!} = 1$ gives $a(X_1)(\omega(X_2)) - a(X_2)(\omega(X_1))$. The identity for $a \wedge b$ is proved by the same computation with $\phi \circ \psi$ in place of $\phi(s)$ and the evaluation $b(\dots) = \beta(\dots)\psi$ for $b = \beta \otimes \psi$, the only change being that $\phi(\beta(\dots) s)$ is replaced by $\phi \circ (\beta(\dots)\psi) = \beta(\dots)\, \phi \circ \psi$ (composition is linear in each factor); every step survives this substitution. Finally $(a \wedge a)(X, Y) = a(X)a(Y) - a(Y)a(X) = [a(X), a(Y)]$ by the $p = q = 1$ case with $b = a$. This proves (c).
>
> **Step 4: associativity and compatibility, part (d).** Each identity in (d) is, pointwise, an equality between two maps that are multilinear in the forms involved (Step 0), so it suffices to check it on decomposables. Write $a = \gamma \otimes \phi$, $b = \beta \otimes \psi$, $c = \beta' \otimes \chi$, $\omega = \delta \otimes s$ with scalar forms $\gamma, \beta, \beta', \delta$ of degrees $q, p, p', p$ respectively and $\phi, \psi, \chi \in \Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$, $s \in \Gamma(E)$; here we reuse the letter $\beta$ for the scalar part of $b$ and $\delta$ for that of $\omega$. Then
> $$(\gamma \wedge \alpha) \wedge (\delta \otimes s) = \big((\gamma \wedge \alpha) \wedge \delta\big) \otimes s = \big(\gamma \wedge (\alpha \wedge \delta)\big) \otimes s = \gamma \wedge \big(\alpha \wedge (\delta \otimes s)\big) \qquad \text{(values on decomposables; associativity of the scalar wedge, part (b) of the wedge product properties; values on decomposables)},$$
> The associativity of the scalar wedge invoked is part (b) of [[Thm - Wedge Product Properties]].
> $$(a \wedge b) \wedge \omega = \big((\gamma \wedge \beta) \otimes \phi\circ\psi\big) \wedge (\delta \otimes s) = \big((\gamma \wedge \beta) \wedge \delta\big) \otimes \phi(\psi(s)) = \big(\gamma \wedge (\beta \wedge \delta)\big) \otimes \phi(\psi(s)) = a \wedge (b \wedge \omega) \qquad \text{(values on decomposables; associativity of the scalar wedge; } b \wedge \omega = (\beta \wedge \delta) \otimes \psi(s)\text{)},$$
> $$(a \wedge b) \wedge c = \big((\gamma \wedge \beta) \wedge \beta'\big) \otimes (\phi \circ \psi) \circ \chi = \big(\gamma \wedge (\beta \wedge \beta')\big) \otimes \phi \circ (\psi \circ \chi) = a \wedge (b \wedge c) \qquad \text{(values on decomposables; associativity of } \wedge \text{ and of composition)},$$
> $$a \wedge (\alpha \wedge \omega) = (\gamma \wedge \alpha \wedge \delta) \otimes \phi(s) = (-1)^{qr} (\alpha \wedge \gamma \wedge \delta) \otimes \phi(s) = (-1)^{qr}\, \alpha \wedge (a \wedge \omega) \qquad \text{(values on decomposables and associativity; graded anticommutativity } \gamma \wedge \alpha = (-1)^{qr} \alpha \wedge \gamma \text{; values on decomposables)},$$
> and the identity $a \wedge (\alpha \wedge b) = (-1)^{qr} \alpha \wedge (a \wedge b)$ follows from the last computation with $\delta \otimes s$ replaced by $\beta \otimes \psi$ and $\phi(s)$ by $\phi \circ \psi$, every step surviving the substitution. When $q$ is even, $(-1)^{qr} = 1$ for every $r$, which is the statement that an even-degree $\operatorname{End}E$-valued form commutes with all scalar forms. This proves (d).
>
> **Step 5: $C^\infty(M)$-bilinearity, part (e).** Each product is defined pointwise by a bilinear map of fibres, and multiplying either argument by $f \in C^\infty(M)$ multiplies its value at $m$ by the scalar $f(m)$, which the fibrewise bilinear map pulls out. This proves (e).
>
> **Conclusion.** The three products are well defined, smooth, $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear, computed in a frame by matrix multiplication with wedge of entries, evaluated on vector fields by the alternating-sum formula, associative, and compatible with one another with the graded sign for scalar forms. $\blacksquare$

**The scalar case.** When $E = \underline{\mathbb{R}}$, the fibrewise isomorphisms $\Lambda^p T_m^*M \otimes \mathbb{R} \to \Lambda^p T_m^*M$, $\beta \otimes t \mapsto t\beta$, and $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$, $\phi \mapsto \phi(1)$, identify $\Omega^p(M; \underline{\mathbb{R}}) = \Omega^p(M; \operatorname{End}\underline{\mathbb{R}}) = \Omega^p(M)$, and under these identifications all three products become the ordinary wedge product of scalar forms; in particular the graded commutation rule $\alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{pq}\beta \wedge \alpha$ of [[Thm - Wedge Product Properties]] is recovered (Corollary 5). For $E$ of rank $k \ge 2$ the pairing and the wedge-composition are **not** graded-commutative (see the examples).

> [!note]- Corollary 5 (the scalar case recovers the ordinary wedge product and its graded commutation rules), with proof
> **Statement.** Let $E = \underline{\mathbb{R}} = M \times \mathbb{R}$. The maps $\iota \colon \Omega^p(M; \underline{\mathbb{R}}) \to \Omega^p(M)$, $\iota(\beta \otimes t) = t\beta$ (extended linearly on each fibre), and $\kappa \colon \Omega^p(M; \operatorname{End}\underline{\mathbb{R}}) \to \Omega^p(M)$, $\kappa(\beta \otimes \phi) = \phi(1)\,\beta$, are $C^\infty(M)$-linear isomorphisms, and for $\alpha \in \Omega^q(M)$, $a, b \in \Omega^\bullet(M; \operatorname{End}\underline{\mathbb{R}})$, $\omega \in \Omega^\bullet(M; \underline{\mathbb{R}})$: $\iota(\alpha \wedge \omega) = \alpha \wedge \iota(\omega)$, $\iota(a \wedge \omega) = \kappa(a) \wedge \iota(\omega)$, $\kappa(a \wedge b) = \kappa(a) \wedge \kappa(b)$. Consequently $a \wedge b = (-1)^{\deg a \deg b}\, b \wedge a$ and $a \wedge \omega = (-1)^{\deg a \deg \omega}\, \omega \wedge a$ (with the right wedge defined through $\iota$) for the trivial line bundle.
>
> **Proof.** **Fibrewise isomorphisms.** For a real vector space $V$, the linear map $V \otimes \mathbb{R} \to V$, $v \otimes t \mapsto tv$, is an isomorphism with inverse $v \mapsto v \otimes 1$ (both composites are the identity on decomposables, which span, and $v \otimes t = tv \otimes 1$ by bilinearity of $\otimes$); the map $\operatorname{End}(\mathbb{R}) \to \mathbb{R}$, $\phi \mapsto \phi(1)$, is an isomorphism since a linear map $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is multiplication by $\phi(1)$. Applied fibrewise with $V = \Lambda^p T_m^*M$, these give bijections $\iota$ and $\kappa$ on sections, which are smooth in both directions because in the global frame $e_1 = 1$ of $\underline{\mathbb{R}}$ (respectively $E_{11} = \operatorname{id}$ of $\operatorname{End}\underline{\mathbb{R}}$) the coefficient column of $\omega$ is the single form $\iota(\omega)$ (Lemma 2 (b)), and $C^\infty(M)$-linearity is pointwise. **Compatibility with the products.** On decomposables, $\iota(\alpha \wedge (\beta \otimes t)) = \iota((\alpha \wedge \beta) \otimes t) = t\,\alpha \wedge \beta = \alpha \wedge \iota(\beta \otimes t)$ (values on decomposables; definition of $\iota$; bilinearity of $\wedge$); $\iota((\gamma \otimes \phi) \wedge (\beta \otimes t)) = \iota((\gamma \wedge \beta) \otimes \phi(t)) = \phi(1)\, t\, \gamma \wedge \beta = \kappa(\gamma \otimes \phi) \wedge \iota(\beta \otimes t)$ (values on decomposables; $\phi(t) = t\phi(1)$; definitions of $\kappa$ and $\iota$); $\kappa((\gamma \otimes \phi) \wedge (\beta \otimes \psi)) = \kappa((\gamma \wedge \beta) \otimes \phi \circ \psi) = \phi(\psi(1))\, \gamma \wedge \beta = \phi(1)\psi(1)\, \gamma \wedge \beta = \kappa(\gamma \otimes \phi) \wedge \kappa(\beta \otimes \psi)$ (values on decomposables; definition of $\kappa$; $\phi(\psi(1)) = \psi(1)\phi(1)$ by linearity of $\phi$). Since both sides of each identity are bilinear and decomposables span the fibres, the identities hold in general. **Graded commutation.** Transporting through the isomorphisms, $\kappa(a \wedge b) = \kappa(a) \wedge \kappa(b) = (-1)^{\deg a \deg b} \kappa(b) \wedge \kappa(a) = (-1)^{\deg a \deg b} \kappa(b \wedge a)$ by graded anticommutativity of scalar forms ([[Thm - Wedge Product Properties]] (c)), and $\kappa$ is injective; the statement for $a \wedge \omega$ is identical with $\iota$ in place of the second $\kappa$. **Conclusion.** For the trivial line bundle the three products are the ordinary wedge product, and the graded commutation rules hold. $\blacksquare$

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The structural content of the page is that $\Omega^\bullet(M; E) := \bigoplus_{p \ge 0} \Omega^p(M; E)$ is a **graded module over the graded-commutative algebra $\Omega^\bullet(M)$**, that $\Omega^\bullet(M; \operatorname{End}E) := \bigoplus_p \Omega^p(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ is a **graded associative algebra over $\Omega^\bullet(M)$** with unit the identity section $\operatorname{id}_E \in \Omega^0(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, and that $\Omega^\bullet(M; E)$ is a **graded left module over $\Omega^\bullet(M; \operatorname{End}E)$** compatibly with the $\Omega^\bullet(M)$-structure. Each of these statements is a repackaging of Proposition 4: the module axiom $(\gamma \wedge \alpha) \wedge \omega = \gamma \wedge (\alpha \wedge \omega)$ and the unit axiom $1 \wedge \omega = \omega$ (the case $\alpha = 1 \in \Omega^0(M)$ of the decomposable formula) make $\Omega^\bullet(M; E)$ an $\Omega^\bullet(M)$-module; associativity $(a \wedge b) \wedge c = a \wedge (b \wedge c)$ and $\operatorname{id}_E \wedge b = b = b \wedge \operatorname{id}_E$ (on decomposables, $(1 \otimes \operatorname{id}) \wedge (\beta \otimes \psi) = \beta \otimes \psi$) make $\Omega^\bullet(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ an associative unital graded algebra; the identity $(a \wedge b) \wedge \omega = a \wedge (b \wedge \omega)$ is the left-module axiom; and the sign rule $a \wedge (\alpha \wedge \omega) = (-1)^{\deg a \deg \alpha}\alpha \wedge (a \wedge \omega)$ says that the scalar forms sit in the graded centre of the whole structure. In the language of graded algebra, $\Omega^\bullet(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ is the algebra of "matrices with entries in $\Omega^\bullet(M)$" glued along the conjugation cocycle $a' = g^{-1} a g$ of Lemma 2 (d), and $\Omega^\bullet(M; E)$ is the corresponding module of "columns", glued along $\omega' = g^{-1}\omega$.

There is also a functorial reading, which the series exploits in the next section. The assignment $E \mapsto \Omega^p(M; E)$ is a functor from vector bundles over $M$ and [[Def - Bundle Homomorphism|bundle homomorphisms]] to $C^\infty(M)$-modules: a bundle homomorphism $\phi \colon E \to F$ induces $\phi_* \colon \Omega^p(M; E) \to \Omega^p(M; F)$, $\phi_*(\beta \otimes s) = \beta \otimes \phi(s)$ (pointwise the map $\operatorname{id} \otimes \phi_m$), and $(\psi \circ \phi)_* = \psi_* \circ \phi_*$ because both sides are $\operatorname{id} \otimes (\psi_m \circ \phi_m)$ on each fibre. The pairing $a \wedge \omega$ is, for $a \in \Omega^0(M; \operatorname{End}E) = \Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$, exactly $a_*\omega$; for higher-degree $a$ it is the graded extension of this functoriality. The evaluation of Corollary 3 identifies $\Omega^p(M; E)$ with a subspace of the alternating $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear maps $\mathfrak{X}(M)^p \to \Gamma(E)$; that this subspace is everything — that every such multilinear map comes from a unique bundle-valued form — is the [[Thm - Tensoriality Lemma for C-Infinity-Linear Maps]], proved in this chapter, and is the tool by which the curvature is recognised as an element of $\Omega^2(M; \operatorname{End}E)$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

In gauge theory and in physics the objects of this page are the fields. A section of a vector bundle $E$ associated to a principal bundle is a *matter field*; a connection, once one is fixed, differs from any other by an element of $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, which in a local frame is the *gauge potential* $A$, a matrix of $1$-forms; and the curvature is the *field strength* $F \in \Omega^2(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, whose local expression $dA + A \wedge A$ ([[Thm - Local Formula for the Curvature of a Connection]]) uses precisely the wedge-composition defined here — the term $A \wedge A$, which vanishes for scalar $1$-forms by graded anticommutativity, does not vanish for matrix-valued ones because the pairing is not graded-commutative, and this non-vanishing is the entire difference between electromagnetism and non-abelian gauge theory. When the structure group is a Lie group $G$ with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ acting on the fibres, the $\operatorname{End}E$-valued forms with values in the image of $\mathfrak{g}$ are the Lie-algebra-valued forms of chapter IV, and the identity $(a \wedge a)(X, Y) = [a(X), a(Y)]$ of Proposition 4 is the reason the structure equation for the curvature there carries the factor $\tfrac12$ in $\tfrac12[A \wedge A]$: the bracket of Lie-algebra-valued $1$-forms is normalised as $[\alpha \wedge \beta](X, Y) = [\alpha(X), \beta(Y)] - [\alpha(Y), \beta(X)]$, so that $[A \wedge A](X, Y) = 2[A(X), A(Y)] = 2 (A \wedge A)(X, Y)$ for matrix groups, as recorded in the series conventions.

In algebraic topology and complex geometry the same spaces appear as the cochains of *twisted de Rham complexes*: once a connection $\nabla$ is fixed, $d^\nabla \colon \Omega^p(M; E) \to \Omega^{p+1}(M; E)$ is defined ([[Def - Exterior Covariant Derivative on a Vector Bundle]]), and when $\nabla$ is flat the sequence is a complex whose cohomology is the cohomology of $M$ with coefficients in the local system defined by $E$; on a complex manifold the Dolbeault complex $\Omega^{0, q}(M; E)$ of a holomorphic bundle is the same construction with $\Lambda^{0,q}$ in place of $\Lambda^p$. In Riemannian geometry, $TM$-valued forms are the natural home of the torsion, $T \in \Omega^2(M; TM)$, and of the second fundamental form, and $\operatorname{End}(TM)$-valued $2$-forms are the home of the Riemann curvature; Cartan's structural equations of [[Def - Connection 1-Forms (Cartan)]] and [[Def - Curvature 2-Forms (Cartan)]] are the local frame descriptions of Lemma 2 written out for $E = TM$.

**True name:** operationally, an $E$-valued $p$-form is *a column of $k$ ordinary $p$-forms that transforms by $g^{-1}$ under a change of frame $e' = eg$*, and an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued $p$-form is *a $k \times k$ matrix of ordinary $p$-forms that transforms by conjugation $g^{-1}(\cdot)g$*; the three products are *matrix multiplication with wedge of entries*. Everything else on the page — the tensor product bundle, the fibrewise identification with alternating maps, the universal-property constructions — is the bookkeeping that guarantees these columns and matrices are the local faces of global, frame-independent objects. The official definition is preferred over the operational one only because the transformation laws are consequences rather than axioms, and so need not be checked afresh for each new product.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the differential of a function and wedges of differentials (Haydys Example 2, p. 6).** Let $f \in C^\infty(M)$. The [[Def - The Differential of a Function as a 1-Form|differential]] $df$ is a smooth section of $T^*M = \Lambda^1 T^*M$, hence $df \in \Omega^1(M) = \Omega^1(M; \underline{\mathbb{R}})$ under the identification $\iota$ of Corollary 5; in a chart, $df = \sum_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}\, dx^i$ with smooth coefficients, which is the smoothness clause verified directly. More generally, for $f_1, \dots, f_p \in C^\infty(M)$ the form $\omega := df_1 \wedge \dots \wedge df_p$ is an element of $\Omega^p(M)$: each $df_i$ is a smooth $1$-form, and the wedge product of smooth forms is a smooth form ([[Def - The Wedge Product on a Manifold]]), so the section clause and the alternation clause hold; its evaluation on vector fields is, by the determinant identity of [[Thm - Wedge Product Properties]] (e), $\omega(X_1, \dots, X_p) = \det\big(df_i(X_j)\big) = \det\big(X_j f_i\big)$, which is alternating in the $X_j$ because a determinant changes sign when two of its columns are transposed. Tensoring with a section produces the first genuinely bundle-valued instance: for $s \in \Gamma(E)$, $df \otimes s \in \Omega^1(M; E)$ is decomposable, and $(df \otimes s)(X) = (Xf)\, s$ by the decomposable evaluation rule.

**Is an instance — a concrete $E$-valued $1$-form and its transformation law.** Let $M = \mathbb{R}^2$ with coordinates $(x, y)$ and $E = \underline{\mathbb{R}}^2 = \mathbb{R}^2 \times \mathbb{R}^2$ with the global frame $e = (e_1, e_2)$ of constant sections. Set
$$\omega := dx \otimes e_1 + x\,dy \otimes e_2 \in \Omega^1(\mathbb{R}^2; \underline{\mathbb{R}}^2), \qquad \omega_e = \begin{pmatrix} dx \\ x\,dy \end{pmatrix}.$$
This is a smooth section because its coefficient forms $dx$ and $x\,dy$ are smooth (Lemma 2 (b)). Its evaluations are $\omega(\partial_x) = dx(\partial_x)\, e_1 + x\,dy(\partial_x)\, e_2 = e_1$ and $\omega(\partial_y) = x\, e_2$ (Corollary 3; $dx(\partial_x) = 1$, $dy(\partial_x) = 0$, $dy(\partial_y) = 1$). Now change frame by the constant matrix $g = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, so $e' = eg$ means $e'_1 = e_2$ and $e'_2 = e_1$. Lemma 2 (c) predicts $\omega_{e'} = g^{-1}\omega_e = g\,\omega_e = (x\,dy,\ dx)^{\mathsf{T}}$ since $g^{-1} = g$. Directly: $\omega = x\,dy \otimes e_2 + dx \otimes e_1 = x\,dy \otimes e'_1 + dx \otimes e'_2$, so the coefficient column in $e'$ is indeed $(x\,dy, dx)^{\mathsf{T}}$. With $g = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$ instead, $e'_1 = 2e_1$ and $e'_2 = e_2$, and $\omega = \tfrac12 dx \otimes e'_1 + x\,dy \otimes e'_2$, so $\omega_{e'} = (\tfrac12 dx, x\,dy)^{\mathsf{T}} = g^{-1}\omega_e$, as predicted.

**Is an instance — $\operatorname{End}E$-valued $1$-forms, their pairing and wedge-composition, and an odd form with non-zero square.** On the same bundle let $E_{ij}$ be the matrix units of $e$ and put $a := dx \otimes E_{12}$, $b := dy \otimes E_{21}$, both in $\Omega^1(\mathbb{R}^2; \operatorname{End}\underline{\mathbb{R}}^2)$, with matrices $a = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & dx \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $b = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ dy & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. By the matrix formula of Proposition 4 (a),
$$a \wedge b = \begin{pmatrix} dx \wedge dy & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = dx \wedge dy \otimes E_{11}, \qquad b \wedge a = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & dy \wedge dx \end{pmatrix} = -\,dx \wedge dy \otimes E_{22} \qquad \text{(matrix multiplication with wedge of entries; } dy \wedge dx = -dx \wedge dy\text{)}.$$
Thus $a \wedge b \ne b \wedge a$ and $a \wedge b \ne -\,b \wedge a$: the wedge-composition is neither commutative nor graded-anticommutative, even though both factors are $1$-forms. Pairing with $\omega$ from the previous example: $a \wedge \omega$ has coefficient column $a \wedge \omega_e = (dx \wedge x\,dy,\ 0)^{\mathsf{T}} = (x\, dx \wedge dy, 0)^{\mathsf{T}}$, so $a \wedge \omega = x\, dx \wedge dy \otimes e_1$; evaluating, $(a \wedge \omega)(\partial_x, \partial_y) = x\, e_1$, and the evaluation formula of Proposition 4 (c) gives the same: $a(\partial_x)(\omega(\partial_y)) - a(\partial_y)(\omega(\partial_x)) = E_{12}(x e_2) - 0 = x\, e_1$. Finally let $c := a + b = dx \otimes E_{12} + dy \otimes E_{21}$. Then
$$c \wedge c = a \wedge a + a \wedge b + b \wedge a + b \wedge b = 0 + dx \wedge dy \otimes E_{11} - dx \wedge dy \otimes E_{22} + 0 = dx \wedge dy \otimes (E_{11} - E_{22}) \ne 0 \qquad \text{(bilinearity; } a \wedge a = dx \wedge dx \otimes E_{12}E_{12} = 0 \text{ and likewise } b \wedge b = 0 \text{; the two computations above)},$$
an $\operatorname{End}E$-valued $1$-form whose square is non-zero, in contrast with the scalar rule that odd forms square to zero. This is exactly the phenomenon behind the term $A \wedge A$ in the local curvature formula: $(c \wedge c)(\partial_x, \partial_y) = [c(\partial_x), c(\partial_y)] = [E_{12}, E_{21}] = E_{11} - E_{22}$, in agreement with Proposition 4 (c).

**Is NOT an instance — there is no trace of an $E$-valued form, although $\operatorname{End}E$-valued forms have one.** First the positive half. The [[Def - Trace|trace]] $\operatorname{tr} \colon \operatorname{End}(E_m) \to \mathbb{R}$ is linear and independent of the basis in which it is computed; hence $\operatorname{id} \otimes \operatorname{tr} \colon \Lambda^p T_m^*M \otimes \operatorname{End}(E_m) \to \Lambda^p T_m^*M$ is a well-defined linear map (tensor product of linear maps), and applying it pointwise defines $\operatorname{tr} \colon \Omega^p(M; \operatorname{End}E) \to \Omega^p(M)$. In a frame, $\operatorname{tr}\big(\sum_{i,j} a_{ij} \otimes E_{ij}\big) = \sum_{i,j} \operatorname{tr}(E_{ij})\, a_{ij} = \sum_i a_{ii}$ (since $\operatorname{tr} E_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$), a smooth form, so $\operatorname{tr} a$ is smooth; and the formula is frame-independent, as it must be: with $a' = g^{-1} a g$ (Lemma 2 (d)),
$$\sum_i a'_{ii} = \sum_i \sum_{j, l} (g^{-1})_{ij}\, a_{jl}\, g_{li} = \sum_{j, l} a_{jl} \sum_i g_{li}\,(g^{-1})_{ij} = \sum_{j, l} a_{jl}\, \delta_{lj} = \sum_j a_{jj} \qquad \text{(entries of } g^{\pm 1} \text{ are functions and commute with forms; } g g^{-1} = 1\text{)}.$$
Now the negative half. A "trace" of $E$-valued forms would be a rule assigning to $\omega \in \Omega^p(M; E)$ a scalar form by a fixed linear formula in the coefficient column, the same formula in every frame, that is, a linear functional $\lambda \colon E_m \to \mathbb{R}$ on each fibre defined without reference to a basis. We claim that the only such $\lambda$ is zero. Precisely: **if $W$ is a real vector space and $\lambda \colon W \to \mathbb{R}$ is linear with $\lambda \circ g = \lambda$ for every $g \in GL(W)$, then $\lambda = 0$.** Proof: let $w \in W$ be non-zero; extend $w$ to a basis of $W$ and let $g \in GL(W)$ be the linear map that sends $w$ to $2w$ and fixes the other basis vectors (invertible, since it is diagonal with non-zero diagonal in that basis). Then $\lambda(w) = \lambda(gw) = \lambda(2w) = 2\lambda(w)$ (invariance; definition of $g$; linearity), so $\lambda(w) = 0$; as $w$ was arbitrary, $\lambda = 0$. The same argument with a bilinear $\mu \colon W \times W \to W$ and $g = 2\operatorname{id}_W$ gives $2\mu(v, w) = g\mu(v, w) = \mu(gv, gw) = 4\mu(v, w)$, so $\mu = 0$: **there is no frame-independent product of two $E$-valued forms**, which is the impossibility invoked in the Axiom Motivation. The concrete symptom: the candidate "sum of the coefficients", $\omega \mapsto \sum_j \omega_j$, gives $dx + x\,dy$ for the $\omega$ above in the frame $e$ and $\tfrac12 dx + x\,dy$ in the frame $e' = e \operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$; a rule whose output depends on the frame defines nothing on $M$. The trace of $\operatorname{End}E$-valued forms, by contrast, is frame-independent because $\operatorname{tr}(g^{-1} \Phi g) = \operatorname{tr}\Phi$ — the conjugation action, unlike the action on $E_m$ itself, does admit an invariant linear functional.

**Corollary — the degree-zero cases.** $\Omega^0(M; \operatorname{End}E) = \Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$, and for $a, b \in \Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$ the wedge-composition $a \wedge b$ is the pointwise composition $a \circ b$, because on decomposables $(1 \otimes \phi) \wedge (1 \otimes \psi) = (1 \wedge 1) \otimes \phi \circ \psi = 1 \otimes \phi \circ \psi$ and $1 \wedge 1 = 1$ in $\Omega^0(M) = C^\infty(M)$. Likewise for $a \in \Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$ and $s \in \Gamma(E)$, $a \wedge s = a(s)$ is the pointwise application, by the same computation with $\phi(v)$ in place of $\phi \circ \psi$. So the algebra $\Omega^\bullet(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ contains the (non-commutative, for $k \ge 2$) algebra $\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$ of endomorphism fields in degree zero, and the products of this page extend composition and evaluation to higher degrees.

**Corollary — the top and above-top degrees.** For $n = \dim M$, $\Lambda^n T_m^*M$ is one-dimensional, spanned in a chart by $dx^1 \wedge \dots \wedge dx^n$ ([[Def - Differential k-Form on a Manifold]]), so by Lemma 2 (a) the bundle $\Lambda^n T^*M \otimes E$ has rank $k$ and, in a chart with a frame, $\Omega^n(U; E) = \{\sum_j f_j\, dx^1 \wedge \dots \wedge dx^n \otimes e_j : f_j \in C^\infty(U)\}$: an $E$-valued top form is a section of $E$ times the coordinate volume form, and if $M$ is oriented and carries a volume form $\mathrm{vol}$, $\Omega^n(M; E) = \{\mathrm{vol} \otimes s : s \in \Gamma(E)\} \cong \Gamma(E)$. For $p > n$, $\Lambda^p T_m^*M = 0$ ([[Def - Alternating Tensor and Lambda k V Dual]]: an alternating $p$-tensor on an $n$-dimensional space with $p > n$ vanishes, as any $p$ vectors are linearly dependent), so $\Omega^p(M; E) = 0$; consequently the pairing and the wedge-composition of forms whose degrees sum to more than $n$ vanish identically.

**Calibration check.** Three verifications the reader can carry out from this page alone. First, check that $\Omega^0(M; \operatorname{End}E) = \Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$ with the wedge-composition equal to pointwise composition, by taking $p = q = 0$ in the matrix formula $(a \wedge b)_{ij} = \sum_l a_{il} \wedge b_{lj}$ and noting that the wedge of two functions is their product. Second, take $M = \mathbb{R}^n$, $E = \underline{\mathbb{R}}^k$, and write down $\Omega^n(\mathbb{R}^n; \underline{\mathbb{R}}^k)$ explicitly as the columns $(f_1, \dots, f_k)^{\mathsf{T}} dx^1 \wedge \dots \wedge dx^n$; confirm that it is a free $C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n)$-module of rank $k$, and that pairing with any $a \in \Omega^q(\mathbb{R}^n; \operatorname{End}\underline{\mathbb{R}}^k)$ with $q \ge 1$ gives zero. Third, verify with the matrices $a = dx \otimes E_{12}$ and $b = dy \otimes E_{21}$ above that $a \wedge b - b \wedge a = dx \wedge dy \otimes (E_{11} + E_{22}) = dx \wedge dy \otimes \operatorname{id}$ and $a \wedge b + b \wedge a = dx \wedge dy \otimes (E_{11} - E_{22})$, so that neither the commutator nor the anticommutator vanishes: the pairing is not commutative in either sense, and only the sign rule against *scalar* forms, $a \wedge (\alpha \wedge \omega) = (-1)^{qr}\alpha \wedge (a \wedge \omega)$, survives. A reader who can also explain why the transformation law of a column is $g^{-1}$ while that of a matrix is conjugation — the column is the coefficient vector of a section-like object, the matrix represents an endomorphism, and $\phi(e') = \phi(e) g = e \Phi g = e' g^{-1} \Phi g$ — has understood the definition.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Connections as $\Omega^1(M; E)$-valued derivations *(from this chapter)*
> A [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle|connection]] is an $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\nabla \colon \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ with $\nabla(fs) = df \otimes s + f\nabla s$; the target space, the decomposable $df \otimes s$, and the directional derivative $\nabla_v s = \iota_v \nabla s$ all live on this page. The difference of two connections is $C^\infty(M)$-linear and therefore an element of $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End}E)$ acting through the pairing — this is [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]].

> [!tip] The exterior covariant derivative and the curvature *(from this chapter)*
> The [[Def - Exterior Covariant Derivative on a Vector Bundle|exterior covariant derivative]] $d^\nabla \colon \Omega^p(M; E) \to \Omega^{p+1}(M; E)$ is defined on decomposables by $d^\nabla(\omega \otimes s) = d\omega \otimes s + (-1)^p \omega \wedge \nabla s$, using the right wedge of this page, and satisfies $d^\nabla(\alpha \wedge \omega) = d\alpha \wedge \omega + (-1)^q \alpha \wedge d^\nabla\omega$. Its square is the pairing with the [[Def - Curvature of a Vector-Bundle Connection|curvature]] $F_\nabla \in \Omega^2(M; \operatorname{End}E)$, $d^\nabla d^\nabla \omega = F_\nabla \wedge \omega$ ([[Thm - Existence of the Curvature Form]]); the step "$\eta \wedge F_\nabla s = F_\nabla \wedge (\eta \otimes s)$ because $F_\nabla$ has even degree" in that proof is the sign rule of Proposition 4 (d) with $q = 2$. Locally $F = dA + A \wedge A$ ([[Thm - Local Formula for the Curvature of a Connection]]) with the wedge-composition of matrices of $1$-forms.

> [!tip] Tensoriality *(from this chapter)*
> The [[Thm - Tensoriality Lemma for C-Infinity-Linear Maps]] shows that every $C^\infty(M)$-linear map $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^p(M; F)$ is the pairing with a unique element of $\Omega^p(M; \operatorname{Hom}(E, F))$, so that the evaluation of Corollary 3 is a bijection between bundle-valued forms and alternating $C^\infty(M)$-multilinear maps. This is how the curvature, the torsion, and the difference of two connections are recognised as forms.

> [!tip] Lie-algebra-valued forms *(from Gauge Theory IV)*
> On a principal bundle the connection and curvature forms take values in a Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ rather than in $\operatorname{End}E$; [[Def - Lie-Algebra-Valued Differential Forms and Their Bracket]] replaces the wedge-composition by the bracket $[\alpha \wedge \beta]$, and the identity $(a \wedge a)(X, Y) = [a(X), a(Y)]$ proved here is the bridge: for a matrix Lie algebra, $\tfrac12 [A \wedge A] = A \wedge A$.
