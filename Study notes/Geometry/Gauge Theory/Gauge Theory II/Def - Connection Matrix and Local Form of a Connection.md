# Notation

This page depends on the following standing conventions of the series. Manifolds are smooth, Hausdorff, and second countable, and "smooth" means $C^\infty$. For a vector bundle $E \to M$ we write $\Gamma(E)$ for its smooth sections and $\Gamma(U; E)$ for the smooth sections over an open subset $U \subseteq M$; we write $\Omega^p(M; E) = \Gamma(\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E)$ for the $E$-valued $p$-forms (Haydys writes $\Omega^p(E)$ for the same space). A connection on a vector bundle is a covariant derivative $\nabla : \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ satisfying the Leibniz rule $\nabla(fs) = df \otimes s + f \nabla s$. In a local frame $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$, written as a row of sections, $\nabla e = e \cdot A$ with $A \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ the connection matrix; locally $\nabla = d + A$; a frame change $e' = eg$ gives $A' = g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg$ (Haydys's equation (14)).

The symbols used on this page are the following.

- $M$ is a smooth manifold of dimension $n$, and $\pi : E \to M$ is a smooth real [[Def - Vector Bundle|vector bundle]] of rank $k$, so that each fibre $E_m = \pi^{-1}(m)$ is a real vector space of dimension $k$.
- $U \subseteq M$ is an open subset over which $E$ admits a [[Def - Local Frame|local frame]]; $E|_U = \pi^{-1}(U) \to U$ is the restricted bundle.
- $\nabla : \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ is a [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle|connection]] (equivalently, a covariant derivative) on $E$; the set of all connections on $E$ is $\mathcal{A}(E)$.
- $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$ is a local frame of $E$ over $U$: each $e_j \in \Gamma(U; E)$, and for each $m \in U$ the vectors $e_1(m), \dots, e_k(m)$ form a basis of $E_m$. We regard $e$ as a **row** of sections.
- $\sigma = (\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_k)^{\mathsf{T}} : U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ is a smooth **column** of functions, and $e \sigma := \sum_{j=1}^k \sigma_j e_j \in \Gamma(U; E)$ is the section with components $\sigma$ in the frame $e$. Every section of $E|_U$ has exactly one such expression (this is [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]], restated below).
- $d\sigma := (d\sigma_1, \dots, d\sigma_k)^{\mathsf{T}}$ is the column of differentials, a column of elements of $\Omega^1(U)$.
- $\mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R})$ is the vector space of all real $k \times k$ matrices (the Lie algebra of $GL_k(\mathbb{R})$, though only its vector-space structure is used on this page), and $\Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ is the space of $k \times k$ matrices $A = (A_{ij})_{i,j=1}^k$ whose entries $A_{ij} \in \Omega^1(U)$ are smooth $1$-forms on $U$; equivalently, $\Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R})) = \Omega^1(U) \otimes \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R})$.
- For a row of sections $e$ and a matrix of $1$-forms $A$, the product $e \cdot A$ is the row of $E$-valued $1$-forms whose $j$-th entry is $(e \cdot A)_j := \sum_{i=1}^k e_i \otimes A_{ij} \in \Omega^1(U; E)$; that is, the row index $i$ of $A$ is contracted against the frame. For a matrix of $1$-forms $A$ and a column of functions $\sigma$, $A\sigma$ is the column of $1$-forms with entries $(A\sigma)_i := \sum_{j=1}^k A_{ij} \sigma_j \in \Omega^1(U)$, and for a column of $1$-forms $\tau = (\tau_1, \dots, \tau_k)^{\mathsf{T}}$ we set $e \tau := \sum_{j=1}^k e_j \otimes \tau_j \in \Omega^1(U; E)$. All matrix products on this page are ordinary matrix products in which entries are multiplied by the wedge product of forms with functions (which, for a $0$-form and a $1$-form, is ordinary multiplication); no two $1$-forms are ever multiplied on this page, so no sign questions arise.
- $g : U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ is a smooth map into the invertible $k \times k$ matrices, and $dg = (dg_{ij})$ is its entrywise differential, an element of $\Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$. The rotated frame is $e' := e g$, meaning $e'_j = \sum_{i=1}^k e_i \, g_{ij}$; it is again a local frame over $U$ because $g(m)$ is invertible for each $m$.
- $A(\nabla, e)$ denotes the connection matrix of $\nabla$ with respect to $e$, the object defined on this page.
- On a chart $(U; x^1, \dots, x^n)$ of $M$ we use the coordinate frame $(\partial_1, \dots, \partial_n)$ of $TM$, $\partial_i = \partial/\partial x^i$, and the Einstein summation convention over repeated coordinate indices. $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ are the [[Def - Christoffel Symbols|Christoffel symbols]] of an affine connection on $TM$, $\nabla_{\partial_i} \partial_j = \Gamma^k_{ij} \partial_k$.

> [!warning] Convention: row-vector notation (Haydys) versus index notation (Riemannian Geometry I)
> Haydys writes the frame as a row $e$ and the defining relation as $\nabla e = e \cdot A$, so that the $j$-th column of $A$ carries the components of $\nabla e_j$: $\nabla e_j = \sum_i e_i \otimes A_{ij}$. The page [[Def - Connection 1-Forms (Cartan)]] in Riemannian Geometry I writes the same relation in index form, $\nabla e_b = e_a \otimes \omega^a{}_b$, and calls $\omega = (\omega^a{}_b)$ the connection matrix. The two matrices are identical: $A_{ab} = \omega^a{}_b$, with the upper index of $\omega$ the row index of $A$. Consequently the transformation law $\omega' = g^{-1} \omega g + g^{-1} dg$ of [[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Connection 1-Forms]] is, symbol for symbol, Haydys's equation (14), $A' = g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg$; no conversion is needed.

> [!warning] Convention: which way the frame-change matrix goes
> Haydys uses two opposite conventions for the change-of-frame matrix on consecutive pages. On p. 6 (his equation (6)) he writes $e = e' \cdot g$ and derives $\sigma' = g \sigma$ for the columns of components; on p. 8 he writes $e' = e g$ and derives (14). We use $e' = e g$ throughout, so that with our $g$ the component columns satisfy $\sigma' = g^{-1} \sigma$ (proved in the Definition section below); Haydys's $g$ of p. 6 is the inverse of ours. Also, p. 6 writes $g : U \cap U' \to GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ where $GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ is meant ($k$ is the rank of $E$, $n$ the dimension of $M$); this is item 1 of the source's typo list and we use the corrected form.

# Axiom Motivation

A [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle|connection]] $\nabla$ on a vector bundle $E \to M$ is, by definition, a global operator: it takes a section defined on all of $M$ and returns an $E$-valued $1$-form on all of $M$, subject only to $\mathbb{R}$-linearity and the Leibniz rule $\nabla(fs) = df \otimes s + f \nabla s$. The definition answers the question "what should a derivative of sections be?" but it gives no way to write one down, and no way to compute with one. Haydys's own remark (p. 8) sets the task precisely: the existence theorem for connections — [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]], which shows that $\mathcal{A}(E)$ is a non-empty affine space modelled on $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ — tells us that connections exist, "but the reader may wish to have a more direct way to put his hands on a connection". The present page supplies that way. The guiding question is: **what finite list of ordinary differential forms determines a connection completely on an open set, and how does that list change when the bookkeeping changes?**

The mechanism that makes the question answerable is the one already used for sections. Over an open set $U$ on which $E$ has a local frame $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$, every section $s$ of $E|_U$ is $s = e\sigma = \sum_j \sigma_j e_j$ for a unique column $\sigma : U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ of smooth functions — this is [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]], and it is Haydys's observation that "locally any section of a vector bundle can be thought of as a map $\sigma : U \to \mathbb{R}^k$" (his p. 6, our source item R2.1.3). Functions $U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ have a derivative, namely the differential $d\sigma$, and $\sigma \mapsto d\sigma$ satisfies the Leibniz rule $d(f\sigma) = df \, \sigma + f \, d\sigma$. So on $E|_U$ there is one connection we can write down at once: the one that differentiates components, $e\sigma \mapsto e \, d\sigma$. We shall call it the **frame connection** $d^e$ of $e$; it is the connection for which the frame sections are parallel, $d^e e_j = 0$. Any other connection $\nabla$ on $E|_U$ must differ from $d^e$, and the Leibniz rule forces the difference to be tensorial: for a smooth function $f$,
$$(\nabla - d^e)(fs) = df \otimes s + f \nabla s - df \otimes s - f \, d^e s = f \, (\nabla - d^e)(s) \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule for } \nabla \text{ and for } d^e \text{; the } df \otimes s \text{ terms cancel)},$$
so $\nabla - d^e$ is a $C^\infty(U)$-linear map $\Gamma(U; E) \to \Omega^1(U; E)$. Such a map is determined by its values on the $k$ frame sections, and each value $\nabla e_j - d^e e_j = \nabla e_j$ is an $E$-valued $1$-form on $U$, hence a combination $\sum_i e_i \otimes A_{ij}$ of the frame with coefficients that are ordinary $1$-forms. The finite list we were looking for is therefore the $k \times k$ matrix $A = (A_{ij})$ of $1$-forms — the **connection matrix** — and the connection is recovered as $\nabla(e\sigma) = e(d\sigma + A\sigma)$, which one abbreviates to $\nabla = d + A$. A reader who has followed this paragraph could have invented the definition: it is the unique way to record a connection relative to a frame once one knows that the frame connection exists and that differences of connections are tensorial.

We now examine each clause of the definition and ask what fails without it. **The entries of $A$ are $1$-forms, not functions.** The directional derivative $\nabla_v s = \iota_v \nabla s$ must depend linearly on the direction $v \in T_mM$, because $\nabla s$ is a $1$-form with values in $E$; hence the correction $A\sigma$ to $d\sigma$ must itself be a $1$-form in each entry. Were one to allow only functions $a_{ij} \in C^\infty(U)$, the "connection" $e\sigma \mapsto e(d\sigma + a\sigma)$ would not land in $\Omega^1(U; E)$ at all, since $a\sigma$ is a column of functions, and the sum $d\sigma + a\sigma$ is not defined. Were one to allow $2$-forms, the output would lie in $\Omega^1 \oplus \Omega^2$; the codomain clause $\nabla : \Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ of the definition of a connection forbids both. **The matrix is a full $k \times k$ matrix with no constraint on its entries.** This is what makes the correspondence between connections on $E|_U$ and matrices of $1$-forms a bijection, the second claim of the Definition section. If one restricted $A$ to, say, diagonal matrices, one would lose genuinely occurring connections: the Levi-Civita connection of the round sphere in the coordinate frame $(\partial_\theta, \partial_\varphi)$ has the off-diagonal entries $A_{\theta\varphi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta \, d\varphi$ and $A_{\varphi\theta} = \cot\theta \, d\varphi$ (the Christoffel symbols $\Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi} = \cot\theta$, from [[Ex - Christoffel Symbols of the Round Metric on the Sphere]]), and a diagonal matrix cannot represent it. Restrictions on $A$ are meaningful only in the presence of extra structure — a metric on $E$ and an orthonormal frame force $A$ to be skew-symmetric — and are then theorems about that structure, not part of the definition of the connection matrix. **The matrix is defined relative to a frame, and the definition says so.** One might hope for a canonical matrix attached to $\nabla$ alone. There is none, and the transformation law shows exactly why: under $e' = eg$ the matrix changes by $A' = g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg$, and the inhomogeneous term $g^{-1} dg$ means that $A$ is not the local expression of any section of $T^*M \otimes \operatorname{End} E$. If $A$ were such a tensor, then $A = 0$ in one frame would force $A' = 0$ in every frame; but the frame connection $d^e$, whose matrix in the frame $e$ is $0$, has matrix $A' = g^{-1} dg \neq 0$ in any frame $e' = eg$ with $g$ non-constant (verified in the Examples section). The dependence on $e$ is therefore not a blemish of the definition but a fact about connections, and it is what will later be understood as the gauge dependence of a gauge potential.

What goes wrong without the construction? Without a local form there is no way to compute the curvature: the local formula $F = dA + A \wedge A$ of [[Thm - Local Formula for the Curvature of a Connection]] is a formula in the connection matrix, and it is how every explicit curvature on later pages is computed. Without the transformation law there is no way to say when a family of matrices $A_\alpha$, one per trivialising open set, defines a global connection; with it, the answer is that the family must satisfy $A_\beta = g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1} A_\alpha g_{\alpha\beta} + g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1} dg_{\alpha\beta}$ on overlaps, which is the form in which connections are handled on principal bundles in chapter IV. And without the bijection between connections on $E|_U$ and matrices of $1$-forms, the existence part of [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]] would have no local input to glue: the local connections $\nabla^U$ that the partition-of-unity argument sews together are exactly the frame connections $d^e$ of this page.

Finally, the smallest case shows the whole shape of the definition. Let $k = 1$, so that $E|_U$ is a line bundle with a nowhere-vanishing section $e$, and every section is $fe$ for a unique smooth function $f$. Then $A$ is a single $1$-form $a \in \Omega^1(U)$, defined by $\nabla e = e \otimes a$, and the local form reads $\nabla(fe) = e \otimes (df + a f)$. If we replace $e$ by $e' = e g$ with $g : U \to \mathbb{R}^\times$ a nowhere-vanishing function, then $a' = a + g^{-1} dg = a + d \log |g|$, since $g^{-1} A g = a$ for a scalar $a$. Every feature of the general case is visible: the correction to $d$ is a $1$-form, it is tensorial in the sense that $\nabla - d^e$ is $C^\infty$-linear, and it shifts by an exact form under a change of the trivialising section.

# The Definition

Throughout this section $E \to M$ is a real vector bundle of rank $k$, $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ is a connection on $E$, and $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$ is a local frame of $E$ over the open set $U \subseteq M$.

**Step 0: a connection acts on local sections.** The connection $\nabla$ is defined on global sections, whereas the frame sections $e_j$ are defined only on $U$. Before $\nabla e_j$ can be written, we must show that $\nabla$ restricts to an operator $\Gamma(U; E) \to \Omega^1(U; E)$ that still satisfies the Leibniz rule. This is the content of the following lemma, which is used on this page without further comment once proved.

> [!note]- Lemma A (a connection is local and restricts to open sets)
> **Statement.** Let $\nabla$ be a connection on $E \to M$ and $U \subseteq M$ open.
> (i) *Locality.* If $s \in \Gamma(E)$ vanishes on an open set $V \subseteq M$, then $\nabla s$ vanishes on $V$.
> (ii) *Restriction.* There is a unique $\mathbb{R}$-linear operator $\nabla^U : \Gamma(U; E) \to \Omega^1(U; E)$ such that $\nabla^U(s|_U) = (\nabla s)|_U$ for every global section $s \in \Gamma(E)$, and $\nabla^U$ satisfies the Leibniz rule $\nabla^U(fs) = df \otimes s + f \nabla^U s$ for all $f \in C^\infty(U)$ and $s \in \Gamma(U; E)$. We write $\nabla$ for $\nabla^U$.
>
> **Proof.** We are given a connection $\nabla$, that is, an $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\Gamma(E) \to \Omega^1(M; E)$ with $\nabla(fs) = df \otimes s + f \nabla s$ for all $f \in C^\infty(M)$, $s \in \Gamma(E)$. We use the [[Thm - Existence of Smooth Bump Functions|existence of smooth bump functions]]: for every point $m$ of a manifold and every open neighbourhood $V$ of $m$ there is a smooth function $\chi : M \to [0, 1]$ with $\chi \equiv 1$ on an open neighbourhood of $m$ and $\operatorname{supp} \chi \subset V$.
>
> **Part (i): locality.** Assume $s|_V = 0$ and fix $m \in V$; we need to show $(\nabla s)_m = 0$. Choose a bump function $\chi$ with $\chi \equiv 1$ on a neighbourhood of $m$ and $\operatorname{supp} \chi \subset V$. Then $\chi s = 0$ as a global section, since $\chi = 0$ outside $V$ and $s = 0$ inside $V$. Hence
> $$0 = \nabla(\chi s) = d\chi \otimes s + \chi \nabla s \qquad \text{(since } \chi s = 0 \text{ and } \nabla 0 = 0 \text{ by } \mathbb{R}\text{-linearity; then the Leibniz rule)}.$$
> Evaluating at $m$: $(d\chi)_m = 0$ because $\chi$ is constant near $m$, and $\chi(m) = 1$, so $0 = (\nabla s)_m$. Since $m \in V$ was arbitrary, $\nabla s$ vanishes on $V$.
>
> **Part (ii), construction.** Let $s \in \Gamma(U; E)$ and $m \in U$. Choose a bump function $\chi$ with $\chi \equiv 1$ near $m$ and $\operatorname{supp} \chi \subset U$, and let $\widetilde{\chi s} \in \Gamma(E)$ be the section equal to $\chi s$ on $U$ and to $0$ on $M \setminus U$; it is smooth because it is smooth on $U$ and vanishes on the open set $M \setminus \operatorname{supp} \chi$, and these two open sets cover $M$. Define
> $$(\nabla^U s)_m := \big(\nabla \widetilde{\chi s}\big)_m.$$
>
> **Part (ii), independence of the bump function.** Let $\chi_1, \chi_2$ be two admissible bump functions. Both $\widetilde{\chi_1 s}$ and $\widetilde{\chi_2 s}$ agree with $s$ on the open set $W := \{ \chi_1 = 1 \}^\circ \cap \{ \chi_2 = 1 \}^\circ \ni m$ (the interiors of the sets where each equals $1$), so their difference vanishes on $W$, and by Part (i) $\nabla(\widetilde{\chi_1 s} - \widetilde{\chi_2 s})$ vanishes on $W$; by $\mathbb{R}$-linearity of $\nabla$, $(\nabla \widetilde{\chi_1 s})_m = (\nabla \widetilde{\chi_2 s})_m$. Hence $(\nabla^U s)_m$ is well defined.
>
> **Part (ii), smoothness.** For a fixed $\chi$ the same $\chi$ works for every point of the open set $W_\chi := \{ \chi = 1 \}^\circ$, so on $W_\chi$ we have $\nabla^U s = (\nabla \widetilde{\chi s})|_{W_\chi}$, a smooth $E$-valued $1$-form. Since such sets $W_\chi$ cover $U$, $\nabla^U s \in \Omega^1(U; E)$.
>
> **Part (ii), compatibility with global sections.** If $s = \tilde{s}|_U$ for a global section $\tilde{s}$, then $\widetilde{\chi s} - \tilde{s}$ vanishes on $W_\chi$, so by Part (i) $(\nabla \widetilde{\chi s})_m = (\nabla \tilde{s})_m$ for $m \in W_\chi$; that is, $\nabla^U(\tilde{s}|_U) = (\nabla \tilde{s})|_U$.
>
> **Part (ii), $\mathbb{R}$-linearity.** For $s_1, s_2 \in \Gamma(U; E)$, $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$, and $m \in U$, one bump function $\chi$ serves for both, and $\widetilde{\chi(s_1 + \lambda s_2)} = \widetilde{\chi s_1} + \lambda \widetilde{\chi s_2}$; applying $\nabla$, which is $\mathbb{R}$-linear, and evaluating at $m$ gives $\nabla^U(s_1 + \lambda s_2) = \nabla^U s_1 + \lambda \nabla^U s_2$.
>
> **Part (ii), Leibniz rule.** Let $f \in C^\infty(U)$, $s \in \Gamma(U; E)$, $m \in U$. Choose $\chi$ as above and put $\chi_2 := \chi^2$, which is again admissible ($\chi_2 \equiv 1$ near $m$, $\operatorname{supp} \chi_2 \subset U$). Then $\widetilde{\chi_2 (fs)} = \widetilde{\chi f} \cdot \widetilde{\chi s}$, where $\widetilde{\chi f} \in C^\infty(M)$ is $\chi f$ extended by zero. Hence
> $$(\nabla^U (fs))_m = \big(\nabla(\widetilde{\chi f} \, \widetilde{\chi s})\big)_m = \big(d\widetilde{\chi f}\big)_m \otimes \widetilde{\chi s}(m) + \widetilde{\chi f}(m) \, \big(\nabla \widetilde{\chi s}\big)_m \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule for } \nabla \text{ on global data)}$$
> $$= df_m \otimes s(m) + f(m) \, (\nabla^U s)_m \qquad \text{(since } \chi \equiv 1 \text{ near } m\text{, so } \widetilde{\chi f} = f \text{ and } \widetilde{\chi s} = s \text{ near } m \text{, and } d \text{ is local)}.$$
> This is the Leibniz rule for $\nabla^U$ at $m$.
>
> **Part (ii), uniqueness.** Any operator $\nabla'$ with $\nabla'(\tilde{s}|_U) = (\nabla \tilde{s})|_U$ that is $\mathbb{R}$-linear and satisfies the Leibniz rule on $U$ is local on $U$ by the argument of Part (i) run inside $U$; hence for $s \in \Gamma(U; E)$ and $\chi$ as above, $\nabla' s$ agrees near $m$ with $\nabla'(\widetilde{\chi s}|_U) = (\nabla \widetilde{\chi s})|_U$, which is $\nabla^U s$ near $m$. So $\nabla' = \nabla^U$.
>
> Therefore a connection acts on local sections over any open set, by an operator that is again a connection on $E|_U$. $\blacksquare$

**Step 0, continued: $E$-valued $1$-forms have unique frame coefficients.** We also need the fact that a local frame of $E$ spans $\Omega^1(U; E)$ over $\Omega^1(U)$, with unique coefficients. This is the local description of bundle-valued forms proved on [[Def - Bundle-Valued Differential Forms]] — every $\omega \in \Omega^p(U; E)$ can be written in exactly one way as $\omega = \sum_j e_j \otimes \omega_j$ with $\omega_j \in \Omega^p(U)$ — and for completeness we record the argument for $p = 1$ here.

> [!note]- Lemma B (frame coefficients of an $E$-valued $1$-form)
> **Statement.** Let $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$ be a local frame of $E$ over $U$. For every $\omega \in \Omega^1(U; E)$ there are unique $1$-forms $\omega_1, \dots, \omega_k \in \Omega^1(U)$ with $\omega = \sum_{j=1}^k e_j \otimes \omega_j$.
>
> **Proof.** We must show existence and uniqueness of the $\omega_j$.
>
> **Uniqueness.** Fix $m \in U$ and $v \in T_mM$. Evaluating the identity $\omega = \sum_j e_j \otimes \omega_j$ at $(m, v)$ gives $\omega_m(v) = \sum_j \omega_j|_m(v) \, e_j(m)$ in $E_m$. Since $e_1(m), \dots, e_k(m)$ is a basis of $E_m$ (definition of a local frame), the numbers $\omega_j|_m(v)$ are the coordinates of the vector $\omega_m(v)$ in that basis and are therefore uniquely determined by $\omega$. As $m$ and $v$ were arbitrary, the $\omega_j$ are unique.
>
> **Existence.** Define $\omega_j|_m(v)$ to be the $j$-th coordinate of $\omega_m(v) \in E_m$ in the basis $e(m)$. For fixed $m$ this is linear in $v$, because $v \mapsto \omega_m(v)$ is linear and coordinates in a fixed basis are linear. It remains to show that $\omega_j$ is a smooth $1$-form on $U$. Let $(V; x^1, \dots, x^n)$ be a chart with $V \subseteq U$. The sections $dx^a \otimes e_j$ ($a = 1, \dots, n$; $j = 1, \dots, k$) form a local frame of $T^*M \otimes E$ over $V$: at each $m \in V$ the elements $dx^a|_m \otimes e_j(m)$ form a basis of $T^*_mM \otimes E_m$, since $(dx^a|_m)$ and $(e_j(m))$ are bases of the factors and the tensor products of two bases form a basis of the tensor product; they are smooth sections since they are tensor products of smooth sections (the local frame of a tensor product bundle induced by frames of the factors, [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles]]). By [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]] — every smooth section of a bundle over $V$ is a combination of a local frame with smooth coefficient functions — there are smooth functions $f^a_j \in C^\infty(V)$ with $\omega|_V = \sum_{a, j} f^a_j \, dx^a \otimes e_j$. Comparing with the definition of $\omega_j$ at each point, $\omega_j|_V = \sum_a f^a_j \, dx^a$, which is a smooth $1$-form on $V$. Since charts $V \subseteq U$ cover $U$, each $\omega_j$ is smooth on $U$.
>
> Therefore every $E$-valued $1$-form on $U$ has unique smooth frame coefficients. $\blacksquare$

## The connection matrix

**Definition (connection matrix).** Let $\nabla$ be a connection on $E$ and $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$ a local frame over $U$. By Lemma A, $\nabla e_j \in \Omega^1(U; E)$ for each $j$, and by Lemma B there are unique $1$-forms $A_{1j}, \dots, A_{kj} \in \Omega^1(U)$ with
$$\nabla e_j = \sum_{i=1}^k e_i \otimes A_{ij} \qquad (j = 1, \dots, k).$$
The $k \times k$ matrix of $1$-forms
$$A = A(\nabla, e) := (A_{ij})_{i, j = 1}^k \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$$
is the **connection matrix** of $\nabla$ with respect to $e$ (Haydys's equation (13)). In row-vector notation the defining relation reads
$$\nabla e = e \cdot A. \tag{13}$$

The existence and uniqueness of $A$ given $\nabla$ and $e$ is exactly Lemma B applied to the $k$ forms $\nabla e_1, \dots, \nabla e_k$; there is nothing further to prove. The matrix $A$ is a matrix of $1$-forms and not of numbers: evaluated on a tangent vector $v \in T_mM$ it gives the numerical matrix $A(v) = (A_{ij}(v)) \in \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R})$, which records the directional derivatives $\nabla_v e_j = \sum_i A_{ij}(v) e_i(m)$ of the frame sections in the direction $v$.

## The local form of a connection

**Proposition (local form, $\nabla = d + A$).** Let $A = A(\nabla, e)$. For every section $s \in \Gamma(U; E)$, written $s = e \sigma$ with its unique component column $\sigma : U \to \mathbb{R}^k$,
$$\nabla s = \nabla(e \sigma) = e \, (d\sigma + A\sigma), \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \nabla\Big( \sum_j \sigma_j e_j \Big) = \sum_i e_i \otimes \Big( d\sigma_i + \sum_j A_{ij} \sigma_j \Big).$$
Consequently the component column of $\nabla s$ in the frame $e$ is $d\sigma + A\sigma$, which is expressed by saying that **locally $\nabla = d + A$**; and $\nabla|_U$ is uniquely determined by its connection matrix $A$.

> [!note]- Proof of the local form
> We are given $\nabla$, $e$, and $A = A(\nabla, e)$, and we must show $\nabla(e\sigma) = e(d\sigma + A\sigma)$ for every smooth column $\sigma$, and then that $A$ determines $\nabla|_U$.
>
> **Expand by linearity and the Leibniz rule.** Since $e\sigma = \sum_j \sigma_j e_j$ with $\sigma_j \in C^\infty(U)$,
> $$\nabla(e\sigma) = \sum_{j=1}^k \nabla(\sigma_j e_j) = \sum_{j=1}^k \big( d\sigma_j \otimes e_j + \sigma_j \nabla e_j \big) \qquad \text{(} \mathbb{R}\text{-linearity of } \nabla \text{, then the Leibniz rule of Lemma A(ii) for each term)}.$$
>
> **Substitute the defining relation (13).** By definition of the connection matrix, $\nabla e_j = \sum_i e_i \otimes A_{ij}$, so
> $$\sum_j \sigma_j \nabla e_j = \sum_j \sigma_j \sum_i e_i \otimes A_{ij} = \sum_i e_i \otimes \Big( \sum_j A_{ij} \sigma_j \Big) = e \, (A\sigma) \qquad \text{(exchange the two finite sums; } \sigma_j \text{ is a function and commutes with the } 1\text{-form } A_{ij}\text{)}.$$
> Also $\sum_j d\sigma_j \otimes e_j = \sum_j e_j \otimes d\sigma_j = e \, d\sigma$ (the tensor product $\Omega^1(U) \otimes_{C^\infty} \Gamma(U; E)$ is written with the section first on this page; the two orders denote the same element of $\Omega^1(U; E)$).
>
> **Combine.** Adding the two displayed lines, $\nabla(e\sigma) = e \, d\sigma + e \, (A\sigma) = e \, (d\sigma + A\sigma)$, as claimed.
>
> **Uniqueness of $\nabla|_U$ given $A$.** If $\nabla, \hat{\nabla}$ are two connections on $E$ with $A(\nabla, e) = A(\hat{\nabla}, e) = A$, then for every $s \in \Gamma(U; E)$, writing $s = e\sigma$ (possible in exactly one way by [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]]), the local form gives $\nabla s = e(d\sigma + A\sigma) = \hat{\nabla} s$. Hence $\nabla$ and $\hat{\nabla}$ agree on $\Gamma(U; E)$.
>
> Therefore $\nabla$ acts on components by $\sigma \mapsto d\sigma + A\sigma$, and the connection matrix determines the connection over $U$. $\blacksquare$

**Unpacking in the smallest case.** For $k = 1$ the frame is a single nowhere-vanishing section $e$, the connection matrix is one $1$-form $a$ with $\nabla e = e \otimes a$, and the local form is $\nabla(f e) = e \otimes (df + a f)$ for every function $f$. For $k = 2$ and a chart with coordinates $(x, y)$ on $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$, the matrix $A$ has four entries, each of the form $A_{ij} = P_{ij} \, dx + Q_{ij} \, dy$ with smooth functions $P_{ij}, Q_{ij}$; the partial covariant derivative $\nabla_{\partial_x} s = \iota_{\partial_x} \nabla s$ of $s = \sigma_1 e_1 + \sigma_2 e_2$ has components $\partial_x \sigma_i + \sum_j P_{ij} \sigma_j$, so that "$\nabla_x = \partial_x + P$" — the form in which physicists write a covariant derivative, $\nabla_\mu = \partial_\mu + A_\mu$, with $A_\mu$ the numerical matrix $A(\partial_\mu)$.

## Every matrix of $1$-forms is a connection matrix

**Proposition (converse).** Let $e$ be a local frame of $E$ over $U$ and let $A \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ be an arbitrary $k \times k$ matrix of $1$-forms on $U$. Define $\nabla^A : \Gamma(U; E) \to \Omega^1(U; E)$ by
$$\nabla^A(e\sigma) := e \, (d\sigma + A\sigma) \qquad (\sigma : U \to \mathbb{R}^k \text{ smooth}).$$
Then $\nabla^A$ is a connection on $E|_U$, and its connection matrix with respect to $e$ is $A(\nabla^A, e) = A$. In particular, for fixed $e$ the map $\nabla \mapsto A(\nabla, e)$ is a bijection from the connections on $E|_U$ onto $\Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$. (That such a local connection extends to a connection on all of $E$ is the content of the existence part of [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]]; Haydys's "any $A \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ appears as a connection matrix of some connection (cf. Theorem 11)" refers to that gluing.)

> [!note]- Proof of the converse
> We are given $e$ and $A$ and must show: $\nabla^A$ is well defined, $\mathbb{R}$-linear, satisfies the Leibniz rule, and has connection matrix $A$; then that $\nabla \mapsto A(\nabla, e)$ is bijective.
>
> **Step 0: well-definedness.** By [[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]] — every smooth section $s$ of $E|_U$ is $s = e\sigma$ for exactly one smooth column $\sigma$, and conversely every smooth $\sigma$ gives a smooth section — the assignment $s \mapsto \sigma$ is a well-defined bijection $\Gamma(U; E) \to C^\infty(U; \mathbb{R}^k)$, so $\nabla^A s := e(d\sigma + A\sigma)$ depends only on $s$. The output is in $\Omega^1(U; E)$: each $d\sigma_i$ is a smooth $1$-form, each $(A\sigma)_i = \sum_j A_{ij} \sigma_j$ is a smooth $1$-form (a sum of products of smooth $1$-forms with smooth functions), and $e_i \otimes (\text{smooth } 1\text{-form})$ is a smooth $E$-valued $1$-form.
>
> **$\mathbb{R}$-linearity.** If $s = e\sigma$ and $s' = e\sigma'$ and $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$, then $s + \lambda s' = e(\sigma + \lambda \sigma')$ (uniqueness of components), and
> $$\nabla^A(s + \lambda s') = e\big( d(\sigma + \lambda\sigma') + A(\sigma + \lambda\sigma') \big) = e(d\sigma + A\sigma) + \lambda \, e(d\sigma' + A\sigma') = \nabla^A s + \lambda \nabla^A s' \qquad \text{(} d \text{ is } \mathbb{R}\text{-linear; matrix multiplication is linear in the column)}.$$
>
> **Leibniz rule.** Let $f \in C^\infty(U)$ and $s = e\sigma$. Then $fs = e(f\sigma)$, so
> $$\nabla^A(fs) = e\big( d(f\sigma) + A(f\sigma) \big) = e\big( df \, \sigma + f \, d\sigma + f \, A\sigma \big) \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule for } d \text{ on each component, } d(f\sigma_i) = \sigma_i \, df + f \, d\sigma_i \text{; } A(f\sigma) = f A\sigma \text{ since } f \text{ is a scalar function)}$$
> $$= \sum_i e_i \otimes \sigma_i \, df + f \, e(d\sigma + A\sigma) = df \otimes (e\sigma) + f \, \nabla^A s = df \otimes s + f \, \nabla^A s \qquad \text{(bilinearity of } \otimes \text{ over } C^\infty(U)\text{; definition of } \nabla^A s).$$
>
> **The connection matrix of $\nabla^A$ is $A$.** The frame section $e_j$ has component column $\varepsilon_j$, the $j$-th standard basis vector of $\mathbb{R}^k$ regarded as a constant map $U \to \mathbb{R}^k$. Hence
> $$\nabla^A e_j = e\big( d\varepsilon_j + A\varepsilon_j \big) = e \, (A\varepsilon_j) = \sum_i e_i \otimes A_{ij} \qquad \text{(} d\varepsilon_j = 0 \text{ since } \varepsilon_j \text{ is constant; } A\varepsilon_j \text{ is the } j\text{-th column of } A).$$
> Comparing with the definition of the connection matrix, $A(\nabla^A, e) = A$.
>
> **Bijectivity.** Surjectivity of $\nabla \mapsto A(\nabla, e)$ onto $\Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ is the previous paragraph. Injectivity is the uniqueness statement of the local-form proposition: two connections on $E|_U$ with the same matrix in the frame $e$ agree on every section.
>
> Therefore $\nabla^A = d + A$ is a connection on $E|_U$ with connection matrix $A$, and connections on $E|_U$ correspond bijectively to matrices of $1$-forms once a frame is fixed. $\blacksquare$

**The frame connection.** The case $A = 0$ gives the connection $d^e := \nabla^0$, $d^e(e\sigma) = e \, d\sigma$, whose connection matrix in the frame $e$ is $0$; it is the connection for which the frame sections are parallel, $d^e e_j = 0$. In terms of the [[Def - Local Trivialization|local trivialisation]] $\psi_U : E|_U \to U \times \mathbb{R}^k$ induced by $e$ (namely $\psi_U^{-1}(m, x) = e(m) x$; that every frame induces a trivialisation in this way is Haydys's Exercise 5, [[Ex - Local Frames Correspond to Local Trivialisations]]), $d^e s = \psi_U^{-1} \, d(\psi_U s)$, which is the local connection $\nabla^U$ of Haydys's proof of Theorem 11. Every connection on $E|_U$ is $d^e + A$ for a unique $A$: this is the affine structure of [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]] seen locally, with $d^e$ as origin.

## Change of frame: the transformation law

Let $e' = eg$ be a second local frame over $U$, with $g : U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ smooth. We first record how components change (source item R2.1.3), then how the connection matrix changes (equation (14)).

**Lemma C (components transform by $g^{-1}$).** If $s = e\sigma = e'\sigma'$, then $\sigma' = g^{-1} \sigma$.

*Proof.* We must show $\sigma = g\sigma'$. By definition of $e'$, $e' \sigma' = \sum_j \sigma'_j e'_j = \sum_j \sigma'_j \sum_i g_{ij} e_i = \sum_i \Big( \sum_j g_{ij} \sigma'_j \Big) e_i = e \, (g\sigma')$ (exchange the finite sums). Hence $e\sigma = e(g\sigma')$, and by uniqueness of components in the frame $e$ ([[Thm - Local Frames Span Sections]]), $\sigma = g\sigma'$. Since $g(m)$ is invertible at each $m$, $\sigma' = g^{-1}\sigma$; and $g^{-1} : U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ is smooth because matrix inversion is a smooth map on $GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ (Cramer's rule expresses the entries of $g^{-1}$ as polynomials in the entries of $g$ divided by $\det g \neq 0$). $\blacksquare$

**Theorem (transformation law for the connection matrix; Haydys (14)).** Let $\nabla$ be a connection on $E$, let $e$ and $e' = eg$ be local frames over $U$ with $g : U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ smooth, and let $A = A(\nabla, e)$, $A' = A(\nabla, e')$. Then
$$A' = g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg. \tag{14}$$

This is precisely [[Thm - Gauge Transformation Law for Connection 1-Forms]] from Riemannian Geometry I, whose statement we restate in that page's notation: *if $\nabla$ is an affine connection on a vector bundle $E \to M$ and $e' = e g$ are two local frames on $U$ related by a smooth $g : U \to GL(n, \mathbb{R})$, then the matrices of connection $1$-forms satisfy $\omega' = g^{-1} \omega g + g^{-1} dg$.* By the convention callout in the Notation section, $\omega = A$ entry for entry, so the two statements coincide, and the complete proof on that page (its Steps 1–5) proves (14). Because the computation is short and is the heart of the present page, we also reproduce it here in the row-vector notation, following Haydys's remark R2.1.5.

> [!note]- Proof of the transformation law (14)
> We are given $\nabla$, $e$, $g$, $e' = eg$, and the matrices $A$, $A'$ defined by $\nabla e = e \cdot A$ and $\nabla e' = e' \cdot A'$. We need to show $A' = g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg$.
>
> **Compute $\nabla e'_j$ with the Leibniz rule.** Since $e'_j = \sum_i g_{ij} e_i$ with $g_{ij} \in C^\infty(U)$,
> $$\nabla e'_j = \sum_i \nabla(g_{ij} e_i) = \sum_i \big( dg_{ij} \otimes e_i + g_{ij} \nabla e_i \big) \qquad \text{(} \mathbb{R}\text{-linearity, then the Leibniz rule of Lemma A(ii))}$$
> $$= \sum_i e_i \otimes dg_{ij} + \sum_i g_{ij} \sum_l e_l \otimes A_{li} = \sum_l e_l \otimes \Big( dg_{lj} + \sum_i A_{li} g_{ij} \Big) \qquad \text{(definition (13) of } A \text{; relabel } i \to l \text{ in the first sum and exchange the finite sums in the second)}.$$
> In row-vector notation this is $\nabla e' = e \cdot (Ag + dg)$, which is Haydys's line $\nabla(eg) = (\nabla e) g + e \otimes dg = e(Ag + dg)$.
>
> **Re-express in the new frame.** Since $e' = eg$ and $g$ is pointwise invertible, $e = e' g^{-1}$, that is, $e_l = \sum_p e'_p (g^{-1})_{pl}$. Substituting,
> $$\nabla e'_j = \sum_p e'_p \otimes \Big( \sum_l (g^{-1})_{pl} \big( dg_{lj} + \sum_i A_{li} g_{ij} \big) \Big) = e' \cdot \big( g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg \big)_{\bullet j} \qquad \text{(substitution of } e = e' g^{-1} \text{ and regrouping of finite sums; the } (g^{-1})_{pl} \text{ are functions and pass through } \otimes).$$
> That is, $\nabla e' = e' \cdot (g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg)$.
>
> **Compare with the defining relation for $A'$.** By definition, $\nabla e' = e' \cdot A'$. By Lemma B applied to the frame $e'$, the frame coefficients of the $E$-valued $1$-form $\nabla e'_j$ with respect to $e'$ are unique. Comparing the two expressions column by column, $A'_{\bullet j} = (g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg)_{\bullet j}$ for each $j$, hence
> $$A' = g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg.$$
>
> Therefore the connection matrix transforms by conjugation plus the inhomogeneous term $g^{-1} dg$. $\blacksquare$

**Reading the law.** The first term $g^{-1} A g$ is how a section of $T^*M \otimes \operatorname{End} E$ transforms: if $a \in \Omega^1(U; \operatorname{End} E)$ has matrix $a_e$ in the frame $e$ (meaning $a \, e_j = \sum_i e_i \otimes (a_e)_{ij}$), then its matrix in the frame $e' = eg$ is $g^{-1} a_e g$ (the same computation as above with no Leibniz term, since $a$ is $C^\infty$-linear). The second term $g^{-1} dg$ is a matrix of $1$-forms that depends only on the frame change and not on $\nabla$; it is the connection matrix of $d^e$ with respect to $e'$ (see the Examples section), and in chapter IV it will be recognised as the pull-back by $g$ of the Maurer–Cartan form of $GL_k(\mathbb{R})$. The presence of this term is the precise sense in which a connection is not a tensor.

**Unpacking in the smallest case.** For $k = 1$, $g$ is a nowhere-vanishing function, $g^{-1} A g = A$ because $1 \times 1$ matrices commute, and (14) reads $A' = A + g^{-1} dg = A + d\log|g|$. For $k = 2$ and a constant rotation $g = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha \\ \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{pmatrix}$, $dg = 0$ and $A' = g^{-1} A g$ is the ordinary conjugation of the matrix of $1$-forms; the inhomogeneous term appears only when the frame rotates from point to point.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The connection matrix is best understood as a coordinate on the space of connections, and the transformation law as the change-of-coordinates rule. A local frame $e$ over $U$ is the same thing as a [[Def - Local Trivialization|local trivialisation]] $\psi_U : E|_U \to U \times \mathbb{R}^k$, $\psi_U(e(m) x) = (m, x)$ ([[Ex - Local Frames Correspond to Local Trivialisations]]). Under $\psi_U$, sections of $E|_U$ become maps $\sigma : U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ and connections on $E|_U$ become connections on the product bundle $U \times \mathbb{R}^k$. The product bundle carries one distinguished connection, the componentwise differential $d$, and by the converse proposition every connection on it is $d + A$ for a unique $A \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$. The set of connections on $U \times \mathbb{R}^k$ is therefore an affine space with the vector space $\Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ acting simply transitively by $(A, \nabla) \mapsto \nabla + A$, and $d$ is a distinguished origin. The connection matrix $A(\nabla, e)$ is the position vector of $\psi_U$-transported $\nabla$ relative to the origin $d$. This is the local, trivialised version of [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]]: globally there is no distinguished origin, because there is no distinguished frame, and $\mathcal{A}(E)$ is an affine space modelled on $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ with no canonical zero.

A second frame $e' = eg$ is a second trivialisation, and the two are related by the transition function $g : U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ ([[Def - Transition Function of a Vector Bundle]]): $\psi'_U \circ \psi_U^{-1}(m, x) = (m, g(m)^{-1} x)$ by Lemma C. The transformation law (14) says that the two origins $d^e$ and $d^{e'}$ differ by $g^{-1} dg$, and that the affine coordinates of a fixed connection in the two charts are related by the affine map $A \mapsto g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg$ — a linear part (conjugation, the action of the transition function on $\operatorname{End}$-valued forms) plus a translation that measures the disagreement of the two origins. A global connection on $E$ is thus the same thing as a family of matrices $A_\alpha \in \Omega^1(U_\alpha; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$, one for each member of a trivialising cover $\{U_\alpha\}$ with transition functions $g_{\alpha\beta}$, satisfying $A_\beta = g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1} A_\alpha g_{\alpha\beta} + g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1} dg_{\alpha\beta}$ on every overlap $U_\alpha \cap U_\beta$: given the family, the local connections $d + A_\alpha$ agree on overlaps by the uniqueness half of the local-form proposition and so define a connection on $E$; given a connection, its connection matrices satisfy the relation by (14). This description is the one that generalises from vector bundles to principal bundles, where the $A_\alpha$ become the **local connection forms** $s_\alpha^* \omega$ of a connection $1$-form $\omega$ ([[Def - Local Connection Form and Gauge Potential]]) and the relation becomes $A_\beta = \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}} A_\alpha + g_{\alpha\beta}^* \theta$ with $\theta$ the Maurer–Cartan form ([[Thm - Transformation of Local Connection and Curvature Forms]]); for matrix groups $g^* \theta = g^{-1} dg$ and one recovers (14) verbatim.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**Riemannian geometry.** For $E = TM$ with the [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita connection]] of a metric and $e$ the coordinate frame $(\partial_1, \dots, \partial_n)$ of a chart, the connection matrix is $A_{kj} = \Gamma^k_{ij} \, dx^i$, the [[Def - Christoffel Symbols|Christoffel symbols]] packaged as $1$-forms; this is verified in the Examples section. The classical transformation law of the Christoffel symbols under a change of coordinates — with its notorious second-derivative term $\frac{\partial x'^c}{\partial x^k} \frac{\partial^2 x^k}{\partial x'^a \partial x'^b}$ — is (14) with $g$ the Jacobian matrix of the coordinate change: the second derivatives of the coordinate change are exactly the entries of $g^{-1} dg$. Cartan's method of moving frames uses an orthonormal rather than a coordinate frame, and then the connection matrix is skew-symmetric, $A \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{o}(n))$ (the orthonormal-frame paragraph of [[Def - Connection 1-Forms (Cartan)]]).

**Gauge theory and physics.** In a local trivialisation a covariant derivative is $\nabla_\mu = \partial_\mu + A_\mu$, where $A_\mu := A(\partial_\mu)$ is the matrix-valued function obtained by evaluating the connection matrix on the coordinate vector field $\partial_\mu$; the matrix of $1$-forms $A = A_\mu \, dx^\mu$ is the **gauge potential**, and (14) is the gauge transformation law of the potential. For a complex line bundle with a unitary connection, $A = i \, a$ for a real $1$-form $a$ and $g = e^{i\chi}$, so (14) becomes $a' = a + d\chi$, the gauge freedom of the electromagnetic vector potential. The gauge group of the bundle acts on connections by exactly the formula (14) — [[Thm - Gauge Action on Connection Matrices]] — so the change-of-frame law of this page and the gauge action of §2.4 are the same computation read in two ways.

**Ordinary differential equations.** The equation $\nabla s = 0$ for a section along a curve $\gamma$ reads, in a frame, $\dot\sigma + A(\dot\gamma) \sigma = 0$, a linear system of ordinary differential equations with coefficient matrix $-A(\dot\gamma(t))$; its solutions are the parallel sections, and the fundamental matrix of the system is the parallel transport of chapter V. The inhomogeneous term of (14) is the familiar fact that the coefficient matrix of a linear system changes by $g^{-1} \dot g$ under a time-dependent change of variables $\sigma = g \sigma'$.

**True name.** The definition of the connection matrix is already operational — it is literally the recipe "apply $\nabla$ to the frame and read off coefficients" — but its most useful characterisation is the one the Axiom Motivation derived: **$A(\nabla, e)$ is the matrix of the $C^\infty$-linear difference $\nabla - d^e$ between $\nabla$ and the frame connection of $e$**, the connection that declares $e$ parallel. Every property of $A$ follows from this reading: $A$ is a matrix of $1$-forms because differences of connections are $\operatorname{End} E$-valued $1$-forms; $A$ depends on $e$ because $d^e$ does; and (14) is the statement that $d^e$ and $d^{e'}$ differ by $g^{-1} dg$ while the tensorial part $\nabla - d^e$ merely conjugates.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Example 1 (instance — coordinate frame of the tangent bundle).** Let $\nabla$ be an affine connection on $TM$ ([[Def - Affine Connection on a Vector Bundle]], which is the same notion as a connection on the vector bundle $TM$), let $(U; x^1, \dots, x^n)$ be a chart, and let $e = (\partial_1, \dots, \partial_n)$ be the coordinate frame. The Christoffel symbols are defined by $\nabla_{\partial_i} \partial_j = \Gamma^k_{ij} \partial_k$ ([[Def - Christoffel Symbols]]). We verify that the connection matrix is
$$A_{kj} = \Gamma^k_{ij} \, dx^i \qquad (\text{sum over } i).$$
*Verification.* The connection matrix is defined by $\nabla \partial_j = \sum_k \partial_k \otimes A_{kj}$ with $A_{kj} \in \Omega^1(U)$. Evaluating both sides on $\partial_i$: the left side gives $\iota_{\partial_i} \nabla \partial_j = \nabla_{\partial_i} \partial_j = \Gamma^k_{ij} \partial_k$ (definition of the directional derivative $\nabla_v s = \iota_v \nabla s$ on [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle]], and definition of the Christoffel symbols), and the right side gives $\sum_k A_{kj}(\partial_i) \, \partial_k$. Since $(\partial_k(m))$ is a basis of $T_mM$, $A_{kj}(\partial_i) = \Gamma^k_{ij}$ for all $i, j, k$. A $1$-form on a chart domain is determined by its values on the coordinate fields, and $\Gamma^k_{ij} \, dx^i$ takes the value $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ on $\partial_i$; hence $A_{kj} = \Gamma^k_{ij} \, dx^i$, as claimed. Every clause of the definition is met: each entry is a smooth $1$-form (the $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ are smooth functions), and the defining relation (13) holds. The local form reads $(\nabla Y)^k = dY^k + \Gamma^k_{ij} \, dx^i \, Y^j$ for $Y = Y^j \partial_j$, which on evaluation at $\partial_i$ is the classical $(\nabla_{\partial_i} Y)^k = \partial_i Y^k + \Gamma^k_{ij} Y^j$ recorded on [[Def - Christoffel Symbols]].

*A concrete instance.* Take $M = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ with the Euclidean metric in polar coordinates, $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2 \, d\theta^2$, and $\nabla$ the Levi-Civita connection. The non-zero Christoffel symbols are $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r$ and $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} = 1/r$ ([[Ex - The Levi-Civita Connection of Polar Coordinates]]; from the Christoffel formula, $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac12 g^{rr}(-\partial_r g_{\theta\theta}) = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 \cdot (-2r) = -r$ and $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \tfrac12 g^{\theta\theta} \partial_r g_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac12 r^{-2} \cdot 2r = 1/r$, all other symbols vanishing because $g_{rr} = 1$ and $g_{\theta\theta} = r^2$ depend only on $r$ and the metric is diagonal). In the frame $e = (\partial_r, \partial_\theta)$ the formula $A_{kj} = \Gamma^k_{ij} dx^i$ gives $A_{rr} = \Gamma^r_{rr} dr + \Gamma^r_{\theta r} d\theta = 0$, $A_{r\theta} = \Gamma^r_{r\theta} dr + \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} d\theta = -r \, d\theta$, $A_{\theta r} = \Gamma^\theta_{rr} dr + \Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} d\theta = \tfrac1r d\theta$, and $A_{\theta\theta} = \Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} dr + \Gamma^\theta_{\theta\theta} d\theta = \tfrac1r dr$:
$$A(\nabla, (\partial_r, \partial_\theta)) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -r \, d\theta \\ \tfrac1r \, d\theta & \tfrac1r \, dr \end{pmatrix}.$$
This matrix is re-derived from the transformation law in Example 2, which serves as an independent check.

**Example 2 (instance — the product connection in a rotated frame; $A' = g^{-1} dg$).** Let $E = U \times \mathbb{R}^k$ be the product bundle with its standard frame $e = (e_1, \dots, e_k)$, $e_j(m) = (m, \varepsilon_j)$, and let $\nabla = d$ be the product connection, $d(e\sigma) = e \, d\sigma$, whose connection matrix in the frame $e$ is $0$ (each $e_j$ has constant components $\varepsilon_j$, so $d e_j = e \, d\varepsilon_j = 0$). Let $e' = eg$ with $g : U \to GL_k(\mathbb{R})$ smooth. We verify by direct computation that $A' := A(d, e') = g^{-1} dg$, and then that (14) gives the same answer.

*Direct verification.* By the definition of $e'$ and of $d$,
$$d \, e'_j = d\Big( \sum_i g_{ij} e_i \Big) = \sum_i \big( dg_{ij} \otimes e_i + g_{ij} \, d e_i \big) = \sum_i e_i \otimes dg_{ij} \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule for the connection } d \text{; } d e_i = 0),$$
that is, $d \, e' = e \cdot dg$. Substituting $e = e' g^{-1}$ (Lemma C's regrouping, as in the proof of (14)), $d \, e' = e' \cdot (g^{-1} dg)$, and by uniqueness of frame coefficients (Lemma B) the connection matrix of $d$ with respect to $e'$ is $A' = g^{-1} dg$. Each entry $(g^{-1} dg)_{ij} = \sum_l (g^{-1})_{il} \, dg_{lj}$ is a smooth $1$-form, so $A' \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ as the definition requires.

*Via (14).* With $A = A(d, e) = 0$, the transformation law gives $A' = g^{-1} \cdot 0 \cdot g + g^{-1} dg = g^{-1} dg$, in agreement with the direct computation.

*The case $k = 1$, $g = e^{f}$.* Here $g^{-1} dg = e^{-f} \, d(e^f) = e^{-f} e^f \, df = df$ (chain rule), so the product connection on a trivial line bundle has connection matrix $df$ in the frame $e' = e^f e$: rescaling the trivialising section by $e^f$ changes the potential by the exact form $df$.

*The polar-coordinate check.* On $U = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}$ (with $\theta$ taken on any simply connected subdomain, say $\theta \in (-\pi, \pi)$ on $U$ minus the negative real axis, so that $\theta$ is a genuine function), the Cartesian frame $(\partial_x, \partial_y)$ of $TU$ is parallel for the Levi-Civita connection of the Euclidean metric, so the Levi-Civita connection is the product connection $d$ of the trivialisation $TU \cong U \times \mathbb{R}^2$ by $(\partial_x, \partial_y)$. The polar frame is $(\partial_r, \partial_\theta) = (\partial_x, \partial_y) \, g$ with
$$g = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{(chain rule: } \partial_r = \cos\theta \, \partial_x + \sin\theta \, \partial_y, \ \partial_\theta = -r\sin\theta \, \partial_x + r\cos\theta \, \partial_y),$$
$\det g = r \neq 0$, and
$$g^{-1} = \frac1r \begin{pmatrix} r\cos\theta & r\sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad dg = \begin{pmatrix} -\sin\theta \, d\theta & -\sin\theta \, dr - r\cos\theta \, d\theta \\ \cos\theta \, d\theta & \cos\theta \, dr - r\sin\theta \, d\theta \end{pmatrix} \qquad \text{(adjugate formula; entrywise differentiation)}.$$
Multiplying out (each entry of $g^{-1} dg$ is a sum of products of a function with a $1$-form):
$$(g^{-1} dg)_{11} = \cos\theta(-\sin\theta \, d\theta) + \sin\theta(\cos\theta \, d\theta) = 0,$$
$$(g^{-1} dg)_{12} = \cos\theta(-\sin\theta \, dr - r\cos\theta \, d\theta) + \sin\theta(\cos\theta \, dr - r\sin\theta \, d\theta) = -r(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) \, d\theta = -r \, d\theta,$$
$$(g^{-1} dg)_{21} = \tfrac1r\big( -\sin\theta(-\sin\theta \, d\theta) + \cos\theta(\cos\theta \, d\theta) \big) = \tfrac1r \, d\theta,$$
$$(g^{-1} dg)_{22} = \tfrac1r\big( -\sin\theta(-\sin\theta \, dr - r\cos\theta \, d\theta) + \cos\theta(\cos\theta \, dr - r\sin\theta \, d\theta) \big) = \tfrac1r\big( (\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta) \, dr + (r\sin\theta\cos\theta - r\sin\theta\cos\theta) \, d\theta \big) = \tfrac1r \, dr.$$
Hence $g^{-1} dg = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -r \, d\theta \\ \frac1r d\theta & \frac1r dr \end{pmatrix}$, which is exactly the matrix found from the Christoffel symbols in Example 1. The two computations — one from the definition of the Christoffel symbols, one from the transformation law applied to the flat frame — agree, as (14) says they must.

**Non-example 1 (a matrix of functions is not a connection matrix).** Let $a = (a_{ij})$ be a $k \times k$ matrix of smooth functions on $U$ and consider the would-be rule $e\sigma \mapsto e(d\sigma + a\sigma)$. The column $d\sigma$ has entries in $\Omega^1(U)$ while $a\sigma$ has entries in $C^\infty(U) = \Omega^0(U)$; their sum is not an element of $\Omega^1(U)^k$, so the rule does not define a map $\Gamma(U; E) \to \Omega^1(U; E)$ and fails the codomain clause of the definition of a connection. The clause "entries of $A$ are $1$-forms" cannot be relaxed to "entries are functions". (Of course, $a$ *does* define a connection matrix once it is paired with a $1$-form, for instance $a \, dx^1$; the point is that the pairing with a direction is essential.)

**Non-example 2 (the connection matrix is not a tensor).** Suppose, for contradiction, that for every connection $\nabla$ there were a section $\alpha_\nabla \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ whose matrix in every frame $e$ equals $A(\nabla, e)$. The matrix of a section of $T^*M \otimes \operatorname{End} E$ transforms by conjugation, $\alpha_{e'} = g^{-1} \alpha_e g$, so if it vanishes in one frame over $U$ it vanishes in every frame over $U$. Take $\nabla = d$ on $U \times \mathbb{R}$ (so $k = 1$), $e$ the standard frame, and $e' = e^{f} e$ with $f$ non-constant. Then $A(d, e) = 0$ but $A(d, e') = df \neq 0$ by Example 2 — a contradiction with the tensorial transformation. Hence no such $\alpha_\nabla$ exists: the connection matrix is not the local expression of a tensor, and the inhomogeneous term in (14) is unavoidable.

**Corollary 1 (the matrix of a shifted connection).** Let $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End} E)$, and let $\nabla + a$ be the connection $(\nabla + a)s := \nabla s + a \cdot s$ (that this is a connection is part (c) of [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space]]: *for $\nabla \in \mathcal{A}(E)$ and $a \in \Omega^1(M; \operatorname{End} E)$, $s \mapsto \nabla s + a \cdot s$ is again a connection*). If $a_e \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{gl}_k(\mathbb{R}))$ is the matrix of $a$ in the frame $e$, defined by $a \cdot e_j = \sum_i e_i \otimes (a_e)_{ij}$, then
$$A(\nabla + a, e) = A(\nabla, e) + a_e.$$
*Proof.* By definition of $\nabla + a$ and of the two matrices, $(\nabla + a) e_j = \nabla e_j + a \cdot e_j = \sum_i e_i \otimes A_{ij} + \sum_i e_i \otimes (a_e)_{ij} = \sum_i e_i \otimes (A_{ij} + (a_e)_{ij})$ (definition of the connection matrix; definition of $a_e$; bilinearity of $\otimes$). By uniqueness of frame coefficients (Lemma B), $A(\nabla + a, e) = A + a_e$. $\blacksquare$
In particular the connection matrix of the difference of two connections is the difference of their connection matrices, and since differences of connections are tensorial, the difference $A(\nabla, e) - A(\hat{\nabla}, e)$ transforms by conjugation alone: subtracting two instances of (14) for the same $g$, the $g^{-1} dg$ terms cancel.

**Corollary 2 (parallel sections satisfy a linear system).** A section $s = e\sigma$ of $E|_U$ satisfies $\nabla s = 0$ if and only if its component column satisfies $d\sigma + A\sigma = 0$, that is, $d\sigma_i = -\sum_j A_{ij} \sigma_j$ for $i = 1, \dots, k$. *Proof.* By the local form, $\nabla s = e(d\sigma + A\sigma)$, and by Lemma B an $E$-valued $1$-form vanishes if and only if all its frame coefficients vanish. $\blacksquare$ This is a first-order linear system of partial differential equations for $\sigma$; restricted to a curve it becomes the ordinary differential equation of parallel transport, and its integrability condition is the vanishing of $dA + A \wedge A$, the curvature of [[Thm - Local Formula for the Curvature of a Connection]].

**Corollary 3 (the standard frame of a product bundle).** For the product bundle $U \times \mathbb{R}^k$ with standard frame $e$ and any connection $\nabla$, the connection $\nabla$ equals $d + A$ with $A = A(\nabla, e)$ in the literal sense that $\nabla \sigma = d\sigma + A\sigma$ for maps $\sigma : U \to \mathbb{R}^k$ regarded as sections. This is the local-form proposition with the identification $e\sigma \leftrightarrow \sigma$; it is the form in which every connection on a trivial bundle is written on later pages, for instance $\nabla = d + A$ with $A \in i\,\Omega^1(M)$ on a trivial Hermitian line bundle.

**Calibration check.** First, let $E = U \times \mathbb{R}^k$ with its standard frame $e$, let $\alpha \in \Omega^1(U)$ be a $1$-form, and let $\nabla := d + \alpha \otimes \operatorname{id}$, that is, $\nabla \sigma = d\sigma + \alpha \, \sigma$. Then $\nabla e_j = e(d\varepsilon_j + \alpha \, \varepsilon_j) = e_j \otimes \alpha$ (since $d\varepsilon_j = 0$), so $A(\nabla, e) = \alpha \, I_k$, the scalar matrix with $\alpha$ on the diagonal; in a rotated frame $e' = eg$, (14) gives $A' = g^{-1}(\alpha I_k) g + g^{-1} dg = \alpha I_k + g^{-1} dg$, because a scalar matrix commutes with $g$. Second, when $g$ is constant, $dg = 0$ and (14) reduces to $A' = g^{-1} A g$; if moreover $A = a \, I_k$ is scalar (as in the first check), then $A' = a \, g^{-1} I_k g = a \, I_k = A$: a scalar connection matrix is unchanged by a constant change of frame, which is what one expects since $\nabla = d + a \otimes \operatorname{id}$ does not see the choice of basis of $\mathbb{R}^k$ at all. Third, the connection matrix of the frame connection $d^e$ in its own frame is $0$, and by (14) its matrix in the frame $e' = eg$ is $g^{-1} dg$; conversely, $A(\nabla, e') = 0$ for some frame $e'$ if and only if $\nabla = d^{e'}$ on $U$, so a connection has a vanishing connection matrix in some frame over $U$ exactly when it admits a parallel frame over $U$ — a condition that, by the integrability remark in Corollary 2, forces the curvature to vanish on $U$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Local formula for the curvature *(from Gauge Theory II, §2.3)*
> The connection matrix is the input to the formula $F = dA + A \wedge A$ for the curvature matrix in a frame, proved on [[Thm - Local Formula for the Curvature of a Connection]]; the transformation law (14) is what makes $F$ transform homogeneously, $F' = g^{-1} F g$, so that the curvature, unlike the connection matrix, is a tensor.

> [!tip] The gauge action *(from Gauge Theory II, §2.4)*
> A gauge transformation $g \in \mathcal{G}(E)$ acts on connections by $\nabla^g s = g^{-1} \nabla(gs)$, and in a frame this is $A \mapsto g^{-1} A g + g^{-1} dg$ — the same formula as (14), now read as an action on connections with the frame held fixed rather than as a change of frame with the connection held fixed ([[Thm - Gauge Action on Connection Matrices]]).

> [!tip] Local connection forms on principal bundles *(from Gauge Theory IV)*
> On a principal $G$-bundle a connection is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form $\omega$ on the total space, and its pull-backs $A_s = s^* \omega$ by local sections are the analogues of the connection matrix; the law $A_{s'} = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} A_s + g^* \theta$ under $s' = s \cdot g$ ([[Def - Local Connection Form and Gauge Potential]], [[Thm - Transformation of Local Connection and Curvature Forms]]) specialises to (14) when $G = GL_k(\mathbb{R})$.

> [!tip] Parallel transport *(from Gauge Theory V)*
> Corollary 2 turns the equation $\nabla s = 0$ along a curve into the linear system $\dot\sigma + A(\dot\gamma) \sigma = 0$; its solution operator is parallel transport, and its dependence on the path is holonomy ([[Def - Parallel Transport in a Principal Bundle]], [[Def - Holonomy Group of a Connection]]).
