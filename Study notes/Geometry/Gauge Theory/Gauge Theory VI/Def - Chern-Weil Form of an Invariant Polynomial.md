# Notation

Throughout, $G$ is a Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_eG$, and $\pi : P \to M$ is a principal $G$-bundle over a smooth manifold $M$; the group acts on the **right**, $R_g(p) = p \cdot g$. We fix a [[Def - Connection on a Principal Bundle|connection]] on $P$, that is a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued one-form $\omega \in \Omega^1(P; \mathfrak{g})$ with $\omega(\xi_P) = \xi$ and $R_g^* \omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} \omega$, and write $\Omega \in \Omega^2(P; \mathfrak{g})$ for its [[Def - Curvature of a Principal Connection|curvature]], the $\mathfrak{g}$-valued two-form
$$\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac12 [\omega \wedge \omega],$$
which is **horizontal** ($\Omega(V, \cdot) = 0$ whenever $V$ is tangent to a fibre) and **$\operatorname{Ad}$-equivariant** ($R_g^* \Omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} \Omega$). The bracket $[\alpha \wedge \beta]$ of [[Def - Lie-Algebra-Valued Differential Forms and Their Bracket|Lie-algebra-valued forms]] is the one fixed for the series, $[\alpha \wedge \beta](X, Y) = [\alpha(X), \beta(Y)] - [\alpha(Y), \beta(X)]$ on one-forms; $\operatorname{Ad}_g$ is the adjoint representation of $G$ on $\mathfrak{g}$ (for a matrix group $\operatorname{Ad}_g X = gXg^{-1}$).

We denote by $\mathbb{K}$ the coefficient field, either $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. A homogeneous [[Def - Ad-Invariant Polynomial|$\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant polynomial]] of degree $d$ is a map $p : \mathfrak{g} \to \mathbb{K}$ which is a polynomial of degree $d$ in linear coordinates on $\mathfrak{g}$, is homogeneous ($p(\lambda \xi) = \lambda^d p(\xi)$), and is invariant ($p(\operatorname{Ad}_g \xi) = p(\xi)$ for all $g \in G$, $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$). Its **polarisation** is the unique symmetric $\mathbb{K}$-multilinear form
$$\lambda = \tilde p : \underbrace{\mathfrak{g} \times \cdots \times \mathfrak{g}}_{d} \longrightarrow \mathbb{K}, \qquad \tilde p(\xi, \dots, \xi) = p(\xi),$$
which is again $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant, $\tilde p(\operatorname{Ad}_g \xi_1, \dots, \operatorname{Ad}_g \xi_d) = \tilde p(\xi_1, \dots, \xi_d)$; its existence, uniqueness, and invariance are established on [[Def - Ad-Invariant Polynomial]]. We write $I(G)$ for the graded ring of $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant polynomials on $\mathfrak{g}$. Fix once and for all a basis $Y_1, \dots, Y_N$ of $\mathfrak{g}$ (so $N = \dim \mathfrak{g}$); a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued two-form $\Phi \in \Omega^2(P; \mathfrak{g})$ is then written in components $\Phi = \sum_{j=1}^N \Phi^j\, Y_j$ with scalar two-forms $\Phi^j \in \Omega^2(P; \mathbb{K})$.

The exterior algebra $\Omega^\bullet(P) = \bigoplus_q \Omega^q(P)$ carries the wedge product $\wedge$; we use the determinant (Kobayashi–Nomizu) normalisation of $\wedge$ fixed on **[[Thm - Wedge Product Properties]]**, in which $dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n$ evaluated on the standard basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ is $1$. We write $\Omega^{\mathrm{even}}(P) = \bigoplus_{q \text{ even}} \Omega^q(P)$ for the even part, and $\Omega^p(M; E) = \Gamma(\Lambda^p T^*M \otimes E)$ for $E$-valued forms. The [[Def - Adjoint Bundles ad P and Ad P|adjoint bundle]] is $\operatorname{ad} P = P \times_{\operatorname{Ad}} \mathfrak{g}$, the vector bundle associated to $P$ through the adjoint representation, whose fibre over $\pi(p)$ is $\{[p, \xi] : \xi \in \mathfrak{g}\}$ with $[p \cdot g, \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} \xi] = [p, \xi]$.

This is a compound page: it defines four interlocking notions — the Chern–Weil form $p(\Omega)$ on the total space $P$, its descent $p(F_\omega)$ to the base $M$, the vector-bundle form $p(F_\nabla)$, and the characteristic class $c_p(P) = [p(F_\omega)]$ — because they are one construction seen at four levels, and none of them is intelligible in isolation from the others.

> [!warning] Convention: coefficient field
> Haydys works "for definiteness" over $\mathbb{C}$; Bär allows $\mathbb{K} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$. The series takes $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ by default: the polynomials that produce the standard characteristic classes are normalised to be real-valued (for instance $\tfrac{i}{2\pi} \operatorname{tr}$ rather than $\operatorname{tr}$ on $\mathfrak{u}(r)$; see [[Def - Ad-Invariant Polynomial]]), so their Chern–Weil classes live in $H^{2d}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M; \mathbb{R})$. A complex-valued $p$ gives a class in $H^{2d}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M; \mathbb{C}) = H^{2d}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M; \mathbb{R}) \otimes_{\mathbb{R}} \mathbb{C}$; nothing in the construction changes.

> [!warning] Convention: the antisymmetrisation constant
> Bär defines the base form $\lambda \circ \bar\Omega$ pointwise by (his p. 59)
> $$(\lambda \circ \bar\Omega)(X_1, \dots, X_{2d}) = \frac{1}{d!} \sum_{\sigma \in S_{2d}} \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, \lambda\big(\bar\Omega(X_{\sigma(1)}, X_{\sigma(2)}), \dots, \bar\Omega(X_{\sigma(2d-1)}, X_{\sigma(2d)})\big),$$
> and asserts it equals the basis formula $\sum_{j_1, \dots, j_d} \Omega^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \Omega^{j_d}\, \lambda(Y_{j_1}, \dots, Y_{j_d})$. In the series' determinant normalisation of $\wedge$ these two are **not** equal with the constant $\tfrac{1}{d!}$: testing on $\Omega^{j_1} = dx^1 \wedge dx^2$, $\Omega^{j_2} = dx^3 \wedge dx^4$ at $(e_1, e_2, e_3, e_4)$, the basis formula gives $1$ while the $\tfrac{1}{d!}$-sum gives $\tfrac{1}{2!} \cdot 4 = 2$. The constant that reproduces the basis formula is $\tfrac{1}{2^d}$, and we use it below; the discrepancy is exactly the over-counting of the $2^d$ sign-preserving swaps inside the $d$ argument pairs of an antisymmetric bilinear input. This is a normalisation of the *pointwise* formula only: the operative definition on both sides is the basis formula, and the two agree once the constant is corrected.

# Axiom Motivation

We have, on the total space $P$, a curvature two-form $\Omega$ taking values in the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, and we would like to manufacture from it a genuine, scalar-valued closed differential form on the *base* $M$ whose cohomology class measures how twisted the bundle is. Two obstructions stand in the way, and the definition is precisely the object that clears both at once.

The first obstruction is that $\Omega$ lives upstairs, on the total space $P$, and has values in $\mathfrak{g}$, whereas we want a form downstairs, on $M$, with values in $\mathbb{K}$. We cannot simply push $\Omega$ forward: $\pi : P \to M$ is not injective, and $\Omega$ is not $G$-invariant — under the right action it transforms by $R_g^* \Omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} \Omega$, so its value genuinely depends on which point $p$ of the fibre we sit at. A naive component, say $\Omega^1$ in some basis, is a scalar two-form on $P$ but it is not invariant, so it does not descend to $M$; and it is not even canonical, since it depends on the basis. What *is* canonical about $\Omega$ is only its $\operatorname{Ad}$-orbit at each point. So we need a way of extracting from $\Omega$ a quantity that (a) has scalar values, (b) is unchanged when $\Omega$ is replaced by $\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} \Omega$, and (c) is horizontal so that it can be read off the base. Requirement (b) is exactly $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariance, and requirement (a) with (b) is precisely what an $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant polynomial supplies: $p$ eats the (matrix of) two-forms and returns a scalar form, and $p(\operatorname{Ad}_g \xi) = p(\xi)$ makes the output insensitive to the fibre coordinate. The definition of an invariant polynomial was reverse-engineered on [[Def - Ad-Invariant Polynomial]] to be exactly the class of functions of the curvature that survive this descent.

The second obstruction is that $\Omega$ is a *form*, not a number, so "evaluate the polynomial $p$ on it" is not literally meaningful until we say what it means to raise a two-form to a power or multiply two of them. Here the wedge product is forced on us — it is the only associative product on forms — but a polynomial $p(\xi) = \sum a_{i_1 \cdots i_d} \xi_{i_1} \cdots \xi_{i_d}$ is a *commutative* expression, and the wedge product is only graded-commutative. The construction can only make sense if the pieces being multiplied commute. They do, because $\Omega$ has even degree $2$ and even-degree forms commute under $\wedge$ (Lemma 1 below). This is not a technicality to be waved past: it is the reason the degree of the polynomial must be applied to a form of *even* degree, and it is why odd-degree analogues (which would need anticommuting inputs) do not produce this construction. Drop the evenness — try to feed a one-form into a symmetric polynomial — and $p$ is not even well defined, because $\alpha \wedge \alpha = 0$ for a one-form $\alpha$ collapses every square term.

What must the construction capture, and what must it exclude? It must capture the full $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant content of the curvature and nothing else. If we used a polynomial that was *not* $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant — say $q(\xi) = \xi_{11}$, the top-left matrix entry — then $q(\Omega)$ would be a perfectly good two-form on $P$ but it would not be horizontal-and-invariant, would not descend, and would depend on the trivialisation; it would measure an artefact of the frame, not the geometry. Invariance is the exact condition separating geometry from bookkeeping. Conversely the construction must not throw away information that invariant polynomials can see: two different invariant polynomials of the same degree, such as $\xi \mapsto (\operatorname{tr} \xi)^2$ and $\xi \mapsto \operatorname{tr}(\xi^2)$, produce genuinely different Chern–Weil forms (verified below), and both are needed — their combination is what builds the second Chern class. So the map $p \mapsto p(F_\omega)$ must be injective enough to keep the whole ring $I(G)$ in play, which it is: it is a ring homomorphism $I(G) \to \Omega^{\mathrm{even}}(M)$.

Finally, why demand a *cohomology class* rather than resting content with the form $p(F_\omega)$ itself? Because the form depends on the connection $\omega$, which is a choice, whereas the topology of $P$ does not. The content of the [[Thm - Chern-Weil Theorem|Chern–Weil theorem]] — proved on its own page and invoked here only to license the last clause of the definition — is that $p(F_\omega)$ is closed and that its class $[p(F_\omega)]$ is the same for every connection. Only after quotienting by exactness does the arbitrary choice of $\omega$ disappear and leave an invariant of the bundle. The definition therefore ends not with a form but with a class $c_p(P) \in H^{2d}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$, and every downstream use — Chern classes, Pontryagin classes, the Euler class, the topological bound in Yang–Mills theory — is a special case obtained by choosing $p$.

# The Definition

Let $p \in I(G)$ be an $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant homogeneous polynomial of degree $d$, with polarisation $\tilde p$, and let $\Omega \in \Omega^2(P; \mathfrak{g})$ be the curvature of a connection $\omega$ on $P$.

**The Chern–Weil form on the total space.** Writing $\Omega = \sum_{j=1}^N \Omega^j\, Y_j$ in the fixed basis, the **Chern–Weil form of $p$** is
$$p(\Omega) \;:=\; \sum_{j_1, \dots, j_d = 1}^N \tilde p(Y_{j_1}, \dots, Y_{j_d})\; \Omega^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \Omega^{j_d} \;\in\; \Omega^{2d}(P; \mathbb{K}).$$
This is the "basis formula". Equivalently, for a matrix group $G \subseteq GL_r$, one thinks of $\Omega$ as an $r \times r$ matrix whose entries are ordinary two-forms and substitutes it into the polynomial $p$, all products taken with $\wedge$: for instance $p = \operatorname{tr}$ gives $\sum_i \Omega_{ii}$ and $p = \det$ gives the wedge-determinant of the matrix. The two descriptions coincide, and both make sense, only because of the following lemma.

> [!note]- Lemma 1 (even-degree forms commute; the basis formula is well posed)
> **Statement.** For $\alpha \in \Omega^{2a}(P)$ and $\beta \in \Omega^{2b}(P)$ one has $\alpha \wedge \beta = \beta \wedge \alpha$; hence $\Omega^{\mathrm{even}}(P)$ is a commutative subalgebra of $\Omega^\bullet(P)$, and the wedge-product of two-forms in the basis formula may be reordered freely. Consequently the basis formula is independent of the ordering of the factors and, using symmetry of $\tilde p$, may be read as substitution into a commutative polynomial.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We must show $\alpha \wedge \beta = \beta \wedge \alpha$ for $\alpha, \beta$ of even degrees $2a, 2b$. **Invoke graded commutativity of the wedge product.** By **[[Thm - Wedge Product Properties]]**, for any $\alpha \in \Omega^{r}(P)$ and $\beta \in \Omega^{s}(P)$,
> > $$\alpha \wedge \beta = (-1)^{rs}\, \beta \wedge \alpha \qquad \text{(graded commutativity of } \wedge\text{)}.$$
> > **Substitute the even degrees.** With $r = 2a$ and $s = 2b$ the sign is $(-1)^{(2a)(2b)} = (-1)^{4ab} = +1$ (since $4ab$ is even), so
> > $$\alpha \wedge \beta = \beta \wedge \alpha \qquad \text{(the sign } (-1)^{4ab} = 1\text{)}.$$
> > **Conclude the subalgebra is commutative.** The even part $\Omega^{\mathrm{even}}(P)$ is closed under $\wedge$ (a product of two even-degree forms has even degree), contains the constants, and by the previous line any two of its elements commute; hence it is a commutative associative unital $\mathbb{K}$-algebra. In the basis formula every factor $\Omega^{j_a}$ has degree $2$, hence is even, so the factors may be permuted without sign. Reordering the factors $\Omega^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \Omega^{j_d}$ by a permutation $\rho$ and simultaneously relabelling the summation indices $j_a \mapsto j_{\rho(a)}$ leaves the sum unchanged, because $\tilde p$ is symmetric ($\tilde p(Y_{j_{\rho(1)}}, \dots, Y_{j_{\rho(d)}}) = \tilde p(Y_{j_1}, \dots, Y_{j_d})$, established on [[Def - Ad-Invariant Polynomial]]) and the wedge factors commute. Therefore the basis formula is a well-defined element of $\Omega^{2d}(P; \mathbb{K})$ that does not depend on any ordering. $\blacksquare$

The basis formula names a basis, but the object does not depend on it. This is the content of the pointwise formula, which also connects the definition to Bär's antisymmetrised form.

> [!note]- Lemma 2 (pointwise formula; basis-independence; agreement of the two definitions)
> **Statement.** For all tangent vectors $X_1, \dots, X_{2d}$ at a point of $P$,
> $$p(\Omega)(X_1, \dots, X_{2d}) = \frac{1}{2^d} \sum_{\sigma \in S_{2d}} \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\; \tilde p\big(\Omega(X_{\sigma(1)}, X_{\sigma(2)}), \dots, \Omega(X_{\sigma(2d-1)}, X_{\sigma(2d)})\big).$$
> The right-hand side refers to no basis, so $p(\Omega)$ is independent of the chosen basis $Y_1, \dots, Y_N$; and it is Bär's form $\lambda \circ \bar\Omega$ with the corrected constant $\tfrac{1}{2^d}$ (Convention callout above), so the "basis formula" and the "antisymmetrised formula" define the same form.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We compute the value of the basis-formula on tangent vectors and read off the antisymmetrised expression. **Expand the wedge of two-forms.** In the determinant normalisation of $\wedge$ (**[[Thm - Wedge Product Properties]]**), the wedge of $d$ two-forms $\Omega^{j_1}, \dots, \Omega^{j_d}$ evaluates on $2d$ vectors as
> > $$(\Omega^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \Omega^{j_d})(X_1, \dots, X_{2d}) = \frac{1}{2^d} \sum_{\sigma \in S_{2d}} \operatorname{sign}(\sigma) \prod_{a=1}^{d} \Omega^{j_a}(X_{\sigma(2a-1)}, X_{\sigma(2a)}) \qquad \text{(iterated } \tfrac{1}{p!\,q!}\text{ wedge formula, each } p = q = 2\text{)}.$$
> > The constant is $\prod_{a=1}^d \tfrac{1}{2!} = \tfrac{1}{2^d}$. **Substitute into the basis formula and exchange the finite sums.** Using the definition of $p(\Omega)$,
> > $$p(\Omega)(X_1, \dots, X_{2d}) = \sum_{j_1, \dots, j_d} \tilde p(Y_{j_1}, \dots, Y_{j_d})\, \frac{1}{2^d} \sum_{\sigma} \operatorname{sign}(\sigma) \prod_{a} \Omega^{j_a}(X_{\sigma(2a-1)}, X_{\sigma(2a)}) \qquad \text{(previous line)}$$
> > $$= \frac{1}{2^d} \sum_{\sigma} \operatorname{sign}(\sigma) \sum_{j_1, \dots, j_d} \tilde p(Y_{j_1}, \dots, Y_{j_d}) \prod_{a} \Omega^{j_a}(X_{\sigma(2a-1)}, X_{\sigma(2a)}) \qquad \text{(finite sums, all reorderings legitimate).}$$
> > **Collapse the component sums with multilinearity.** For each fixed $\sigma$ and each argument slot $a$, the numbers $\Omega^{j_a}(X_{\sigma(2a-1)}, X_{\sigma(2a)})$ are the components of the single Lie-algebra element $\Omega(X_{\sigma(2a-1)}, X_{\sigma(2a)}) = \sum_{j_a} \Omega^{j_a}(X_{\sigma(2a-1)}, X_{\sigma(2a)})\, Y_{j_a}$. Since $\tilde p$ is $d$-linear, summing $Y_{j_a}$ against its component in slot $a$ replaces $Y_{j_a}$ by that Lie-algebra element:
> > $$\sum_{j_1, \dots, j_d} \tilde p(Y_{j_1}, \dots, Y_{j_d}) \prod_{a} \Omega^{j_a}(\cdots) = \tilde p\big(\Omega(X_{\sigma(1)}, X_{\sigma(2)}), \dots, \Omega(X_{\sigma(2d-1)}, X_{\sigma(2d)})\big) \qquad \text{(} d\text{-linearity of } \tilde p\text{).}$$
> > **Combine.** Substituting this into the previous display gives
> > $$p(\Omega)(X_1, \dots, X_{2d}) = \frac{1}{2^d} \sum_{\sigma \in S_{2d}} \operatorname{sign}(\sigma)\, \tilde p\big(\Omega(X_{\sigma(1)}, X_{\sigma(2)}), \dots, \Omega(X_{\sigma(2d-1)}, X_{\sigma(2d)})\big),$$
> > which is the asserted pointwise formula. **Read off the consequences.** The right-hand side is built only from $\tilde p$ and from the values of $\Omega$ on pairs of tangent vectors; it names no basis, so the value of $p(\Omega)$ — and hence $p(\Omega)$ itself — is independent of the choice of $Y_1, \dots, Y_N$. It is exactly Bär's $\lambda \circ \bar\Omega$ (with $\lambda = \tilde p$) once the printed constant $\tfrac{1}{d!}$ is corrected to $\tfrac{1}{2^d}$ as in the Convention callout, so the two definitions coincide. Therefore the basis formula and the antisymmetrised formula produce one and the same form. $\blacksquare$

**The Chern–Weil form on the base.** The form $p(\Omega)$ lives on $P$, but it descends. We record why, since this descent *is* the definition of $p(F_\omega)$.

> [!note]- Lemma 3 (the Chern–Weil form is basic and invariant, hence descends to $M$)
> **Statement.** $p(\Omega)$ is horizontal and $G$-invariant, that is basic and invariant; consequently there is a unique form $p(F_\omega) \in \Omega^{2d}(M; \mathbb{K})$ with $\pi^* p(F_\omega) = p(\Omega)$. We call $p(F_\omega)$ the **Chern–Weil form of $p$ on the base**.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > We must verify horizontality and invariance and then invoke the descent theorem. **Step 0 (components of a horizontal form are horizontal).** By [[Def - Curvature of a Principal Connection|definition of the principal curvature]], $\Omega$ is horizontal: $\Omega(V, \cdot) = 0$ for every vertical vector $V$ (a vector tangent to a fibre). Fixing the basis $Y_1, \dots, Y_N$, the components satisfy $\Omega^j(V, \cdot)\, Y_j$ summing to $\Omega(V, \cdot) = 0$, and since the $Y_j$ are linearly independent each $\Omega^j(V, \cdot) = 0$; thus every scalar two-form $\Omega^j$ is horizontal.
> > **Step 1 ($p(\Omega)$ is horizontal).** A wedge product of horizontal forms is horizontal: if $V$ is vertical then contracting $\Omega^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \Omega^{j_d}$ with $V$ produces a sum of terms each containing a factor $\iota_V \Omega^{j_a} = \Omega^{j_a}(V, \cdot) = 0$ (interior product is an antiderivation). Hence each summand of the basis formula, and so $p(\Omega)$, vanishes on any vertical vector; $p(\Omega)$ is horizontal.
> > **Step 2 ($p(\Omega)$ is $G$-invariant).** Using the pullback $R_g^*$ (an algebra homomorphism of $\Omega^\bullet(P)$) and the $\operatorname{Ad}$-equivariance $R_g^* \Omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} \Omega$ of the curvature,
> > $$R_g^*\, p(\Omega) = \sum_{j_1, \dots, j_d} \tilde p(Y_{j_1}, \dots, Y_{j_d})\, (R_g^* \Omega^{j_1}) \wedge \cdots \wedge (R_g^* \Omega^{j_d}) \qquad \text{(} R_g^* \text{ is multiplicative for } \wedge\text{)}.$$
> > Now $R_g^* \Omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} \Omega = \sum_j \Omega^j\, (\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} Y_j)$, that is the scalar forms are unchanged and only the Lie-algebra vectors are transported by $\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}$. Feeding this into the previous line and using $d$-linearity of $\tilde p$,
> > $$R_g^*\, p(\Omega) = \sum_{j_1, \dots, j_d} \tilde p(\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} Y_{j_1}, \dots, \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} Y_{j_d})\; \Omega^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \Omega^{j_d} \qquad \text{(regrouping; } d\text{-linearity of } \tilde p\text{)}$$
> > $$= \sum_{j_1, \dots, j_d} \tilde p(Y_{j_1}, \dots, Y_{j_d})\; \Omega^{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge \Omega^{j_d} = p(\Omega) \qquad \text{(} \operatorname{Ad}\text{-invariance of } \tilde p\text{).}$$
> > So $R_g^* p(\Omega) = p(\Omega)$ for every $g \in G$; $p(\Omega)$ is $G$-invariant.
> > **Step 3 (descent).** A form on $P$ that is horizontal and invariant is *basic*. We now invoke the descent theorem in its trivial-representation case. Restated: for a principal $G$-bundle $\pi : P \to M$, a representation $\rho$ of $G$ on a space $V$, and each $q \ge 0$, the [[Thm - Bundle-Valued Forms on the Base are Basic Equivariant Forms on the Total Space|bundle-valued/basic-equivariant correspondence]] is a bijection between $\Omega^q(M; P \times_\rho V)$ and the basic $\rho$-equivariant $V$-valued $q$-forms on $P$; for the **trivial** representation $\rho = 1$ on $V = \mathbb{K}$ it specialises to the statement that $\pi^* : \Omega^q(M; \mathbb{K}) \to \{\text{basic invariant scalar } q\text{-forms on } P\}$ is a bijection. Since $p(\Omega)$ is a basic invariant $\mathbb{K}$-valued $2d$-form on $P$, it lies in the image, so there is a **unique** $p(F_\omega) \in \Omega^{2d}(M; \mathbb{K})$ with $\pi^* p(F_\omega) = p(\Omega)$. Uniqueness holds because $\pi^*$ is injective on forms (as $\pi$ is a surjective submersion). $\blacksquare$

**Bär's route through the adjoint bundle.** There is an equivalent, coordinate-free description of $p(F_\omega)$ that never mentions the total space, and it is worth naming because later pages use it. The base curvature of the connection is the $\operatorname{ad} P$-valued two-form
$$\bar\Omega = F_\omega \in \Omega^2(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$$
corresponding to $\Omega$ under the same descent theorem (now with $\rho = \operatorname{Ad}$ on $V = \mathfrak{g}$): concretely $F_\omega(X, Y) = [p, \Omega(\hat X, \hat Y)]$ for horizontal lifts $\hat X, \hat Y$ of $X, Y$ at a point $p$ above the base point, a value independent of $p$ by equivariance. The invariant symmetric form $\tilde p = \lambda$ descends fibrewise to $\operatorname{ad} P$: the map $([p, X_1], \dots, [p, X_d]) \mapsto \lambda(X_1, \dots, X_d)$ is well defined because a change of representative $[p, X_i] = [p \cdot g, \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} X_i]$ leaves the value fixed, by $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariance of $\lambda$; this is [[Thm - Bundle-Valued Forms on the Base are Basic Equivariant Forms on the Total Space|the fibrewise descent of $\lambda$]] (Bär's Theorem, restated: for an invariant symmetric $d$-linear $\lambda$ on $\mathfrak{g}$, the assignment above is a well-defined symmetric $d$-linear map on each fibre of $\operatorname{ad} P$). Then $p(F_\omega) = \lambda \circ \bar\Omega$, where $\lambda \circ \bar\Omega$ evaluates the fibrewise $\lambda$ on $d$ copies of $\bar\Omega$, antisymmetrised over the $2d$ tangent inputs exactly as in Lemma 2. That $\lambda \circ \bar\Omega$ agrees with the $p(F_\omega)$ of Lemma 3 is immediate from Lemma 2, since both are $\pi^*$-related to the same total-space form.

**The vector-bundle Chern–Weil form.** Let $E \to M$ be a vector bundle of rank $r$ with a [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle|connection]] $\nabla$ whose structure group is $G \subseteq GL_r(\mathbb{K})$, and let $p \in I(G)$. The [[Def - Frame Bundle of a Vector Bundle|(reduced) frame bundle]] $\operatorname{Fr}(E)$ is a principal $G$-bundle, and $\nabla$ corresponds to a principal connection $\omega$ on $\operatorname{Fr}(E)$ with the same curvature (the correspondence of chapter IV). We define
$$p(F_\nabla) := p(F_\omega) \in \Omega^{2d}(M; \mathbb{K}).$$
Explicitly and without mentioning the frame bundle: over a trivialising open set $U$ with a local frame $e = (e_1, \dots, e_r)$, the [[Def - Curvature of a Vector-Bundle Connection|curvature]] is the matrix $F = dA + A \wedge A$ of two-forms, and $p(F_\nabla)|_U = p(F)$, the polynomial $p$ evaluated on the matrix of two-forms with all products taken as $\wedge$. This is frame-independent: under a change of frame $e' = e g$ the curvature transforms by conjugation $F' = g^{-1} F g$, and $p(g^{-1} F g) = p(F)$ because $p$ is $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant (for a matrix group $\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} F = g^{-1} F g$), so the locally defined forms agree on overlaps and glue to a global $p(F_\nabla)$. The two descriptions — through $\operatorname{Fr}(E)$ and through local frames — coincide by Lemma 2 applied on each trivialising set.

**The characteristic class.** The [[Thm - Chern-Weil Theorem|Chern–Weil theorem]] — stated and proved on its own page — supplies the two facts that turn the form into a topological invariant. Restated:

> [!note] Restatement of the Chern–Weil theorem (proof on [[Thm - Chern-Weil Theorem]])
> For a principal $G$-bundle $P \to M$, a connection $\omega$, and $p \in I(G)$ of degree $d$: **(i)** the form $p(F_\omega)$ is closed, $d\, p(F_\omega) = 0$; **(ii)** for two connections $\omega_0, \omega_1$ the difference $p(F_{\omega_1}) - p(F_{\omega_0})$ is exact. Hence the de Rham class $[p(F_\omega)]$ is independent of the connection, and $p \mapsto [p(F_\omega)]$ is a ring homomorphism $I(G) \to H^{\mathrm{even}}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$.

With this in hand we may finally define the class. For $p \in I(G)$ of degree $d$, the **characteristic class of $P$ associated with $p$** is
$$c_p(P) := [\,p(F_\omega)\,] \in H^{2d}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M; \mathbb{K}),$$
where $\omega$ is any connection on $P$; by clause (ii) of the theorem the class does not depend on $\omega$, and by clause (i) it is a well-defined de Rham class. Here
$$H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M; \mathbb{K}) = \frac{\ker\big(d : \Omega^k(M; \mathbb{K}) \to \Omega^{k+1}(M; \mathbb{K})\big)}{\operatorname{im}\big(d : \Omega^{k-1}(M; \mathbb{K}) \to \Omega^k(M; \mathbb{K})\big)}$$
is the [[Def - de Rham Cohomology|de Rham cohomology]] (the quotient is well defined because $d \circ d = 0$), whose dimensions $b_k(M) = \dim_{\mathbb{R}} H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M; \mathbb{R})$ are the [[Def - Betti Numbers|Betti numbers]]. For a vector bundle $E$ with a $G$-connection we write $c_p(E) := c_p(\operatorname{Fr}(E)) = [p(F_\nabla)]$.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The whole construction is one map of algebras. The descent theorem, in its trivial-representation form, identifies the differential forms on the base with the basic invariant forms on the total space,
$$\Omega^\bullet(M; \mathbb{K}) \;\xrightarrow[\ \cong\ ]{\ \pi^*\ }\; \Omega^\bullet_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; \mathbb{K})^G,$$
an isomorphism of differential graded algebras (it commutes with $d$ and with $\wedge$). Under this identification the Chern–Weil form is the image of the curvature under the unique algebra homomorphism
$$\operatorname{Sym}^\bullet(\mathfrak{g}^*)^G = I(G) \longrightarrow \Omega^{\mathrm{even}}(M; \mathbb{K}), \qquad p \longmapsto p(F_\omega),$$
obtained by substituting the even-degree, $\operatorname{ad}$-valued object $F_\omega$ for the variable — legitimate precisely because $\Omega^{\mathrm{even}}$ is commutative (Lemma 1), so a commutative polynomial algebra maps into it. Passing to cohomology, the [[Thm - Chern-Weil Theorem|Chern–Weil theorem]] promotes this to the **Chern–Weil homomorphism**
$$w_P : I(G) \longrightarrow H^{\mathrm{even}}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M; \mathbb{K}), \qquad w_P(p) = c_p(P),$$
a ring homomorphism that no longer depends on the connection. This homomorphism is *natural*: for a smooth map $f : N \to M$ one has $w_{f^*P} = f^* \circ w_P$ (proved on the naturality page). In categorical language, $c_p$ is a natural transformation from the functor $P \mapsto (\text{isomorphism classes of principal } G\text{-bundles})$ to the functor $M \mapsto H^{2d}_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$; the object defined on this page, $p(F_\omega)$, is a chosen cochain representative of that natural transformation, and the passage to $c_p(P)$ is the passage from the representative to the class it represents.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** Operationally, $p(F_\omega)$ is "the curvature, made into a scalar by an invariant polynomial, so that it can be read off the base." The invariant polynomial is a dial that selects which combination of curvature components to watch; the wedge product is the only way to multiply the two-forms; and $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariance is the exact price of admission for descending to $M$. Everything else in the theory is a choice of $p$: the coefficients of $\det(\lambda \mathbf{1} + \tfrac{i}{2\pi} \xi)$ give the Chern classes, $\operatorname{tr}(\xi^2)$ gives (up to a constant) the second Chern and first Pontryagin classes, the Pfaffian gives the Euler class.

This is the differential-geometric face of a construction that appears across mathematics as "take an invariant of a matrix and apply it to a curvature." In Riemannian geometry the Gauss curvature is $\operatorname{Pf}$ applied to the curvature of a surface, and the [[Thm - Gauss-Bonnet Theorem for Surfaces|Gauss–Bonnet theorem]] identifies the resulting class with the Euler characteristic; the [[Thm - Theorema Egregium of Gauss|Theorema Egregium]] is the invariance of that curvature scalar under isometries, a shadow of the $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariance demanded here. In topology, characteristic classes were first built combinatorially (obstruction theory, classifying spaces) and only later realised as Chern–Weil forms; the theorem that the two agree — that the differential-geometric $c_p$ computes the topological invariant — is what makes curvature integrals compute integers. In the physics of gauge fields the same form $\operatorname{tr}(F \wedge F)$ is the instanton density whose integral is the topological charge, and its role in the Yang–Mills energy bound (chapter VII) is a direct application. The compression is that all of these are one map $I(G) \to H^\bullet(M)$; naming the field only names which polynomial $p$ and which group $G$ are in play.

A second compression concerns *why a class and not a form*. The form $p(F_\omega)$ carries metric-and-connection information; the class $c_p(P)$ carries only bundle-topological information. The map "form $\mapsto$ class" is the projection that discards the analysis and keeps the topology, and the Chern–Weil theorem is the assertion that this projection is well defined on the curvature. This is the same move as passing from a closed form to its cohomology class in Hodge theory, or from a cocycle to its cohomology class in any cohomology theory: the representative depends on choices, the class does not.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the trace on a $U(1)$-bundle gives the curvature itself.** Let $G = U(1)$, so $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{u}(1) = i\mathbb{R}$, a one-dimensional abelian Lie algebra; take $p = \operatorname{tr}$, which on $1 \times 1$ matrices is the identity map $i t \mapsto i t$, homogeneous of degree $d = 1$ and trivially $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant (the group is abelian, so $\operatorname{Ad}_g = \operatorname{id}$). The curvature is a single imaginary-valued two-form $F_\omega \in \Omega^2(M; i\mathbb{R})$. With the basis $Y_1 = i$ of $\mathfrak{u}(1)$, write $F_\omega = F^1\, Y_1 = F^1 \cdot i$ with $F^1 \in \Omega^2(M; \mathbb{R})$; the polarisation of a degree-one polynomial is the polynomial itself, $\tilde p(Y_1) = \operatorname{tr}(i) = i$, and the basis formula gives
$$p(F_\omega) = \tilde p(Y_1)\, F^1 = i\, F^1 = F_\omega.$$
So the Chern–Weil form of $\operatorname{tr}$ is the curvature two-form itself, verifying the definition clause by clause: it is a degree-$2 \cdot 1 = 2$ form, it is $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant trivially, and it descends because on an abelian group every horizontal form already invariant. (Multiplying by the normalising constant $\tfrac{i}{2\pi}$ turns $\operatorname{tr}$ into the polynomial whose class is the first Chern class $c_1$; the bare trace gives $F_\omega$.)

**Is an instance — the determinant on an $\mathfrak{su}(2)$-bundle.** Let $G = SU(2)$, so $\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{su}(2)$ consists of trace-free skew-Hermitian $2 \times 2$ matrices; take $p = \det$, homogeneous of degree $d = 2$ (the determinant of a $2 \times 2$ matrix is quadratic in its entries) and $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant because $\det(g \xi g^{-1}) = \det \xi$. For $\xi \in \mathfrak{su}(2)$ the Cayley–Hamilton relation on $2 \times 2$ matrices reads $\xi^2 - (\operatorname{tr} \xi)\, \xi + (\det \xi)\, \mathbf{1} = 0$; taking traces and using $\operatorname{tr} \xi = 0$ gives $\operatorname{tr}(\xi^2) = -2 \det \xi$, that is $\det \xi = -\tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}(\xi^2)$. Substituting the curvature matrix $F = F_\omega$ (a matrix of two-forms) and using that even forms commute (Lemma 1), the same algebraic identity holds at the level of forms,
$$\det(F) = -\tfrac12 \operatorname{tr}(F \wedge F) \in \Omega^4(M; \mathbb{R}),$$
a four-form ($2d = 4$). This verifies the definition: $p = \det$ is a genuine invariant polynomial, its Chern–Weil form is a $4$-form, and it computes (up to the normalisation $\tfrac{1}{4\pi^2}$) the second Chern class $c_2$ of the bundle, since $c_2(F) = \tfrac{1}{8\pi^2} \operatorname{tr}(F \wedge F) = -\tfrac{1}{4\pi^2} \det(F)$ for $\mathfrak{su}(2)$-valued $F$ (the normalisation is fixed on [[Def - Chern Classes]]).

**Is NOT an instance of the same form — two invariant polynomials of one degree give different forms.** Both $p(\xi) = (\operatorname{tr} \xi)^2$ and $q(\xi) = \operatorname{tr}(\xi^2)$ are $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant homogeneous polynomials of degree $2$, yet their Chern–Weil forms differ, so the map $p \mapsto p(F_\omega)$ genuinely depends on $p$ and cannot be collapsed to "the degree-$2$ form of the bundle." We exhibit the difference on a rank-two matrix of two-forms. Let $\Phi = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha & 0 \\ 0 & \beta \end{pmatrix}$ with two-forms $\alpha = i\, dx^1 \wedge dx^2$ and $\beta = i\, dx^3 \wedge dx^4$ on $\mathbb{R}^4$ (skew-Hermitian entries, so $\Phi$ is of the type of a $\mathfrak{u}(2)$-curvature). Then, using $\alpha \wedge \alpha = 0$ and $\beta \wedge \beta = 0$ (a decomposable two-form wedged with itself vanishes) and $\alpha \wedge \beta = i^2\, dx^1 \wedge dx^2 \wedge dx^3 \wedge dx^4 = -\,\mathrm{vol}$,
$$(\operatorname{tr} \Phi)^2 = (\alpha + \beta) \wedge (\alpha + \beta) = \alpha\wedge\alpha + 2\,\alpha \wedge \beta + \beta\wedge\beta = 2\,\alpha \wedge \beta = -2\,\mathrm{vol},$$
$$\operatorname{tr}(\Phi \wedge \Phi) = \alpha \wedge \alpha + \beta \wedge \beta = 0,$$
where $2\,\alpha \wedge \beta$ appears because even forms commute (Lemma 1). The two forms $-2\,\mathrm{vol}$ and $0$ are visibly different, so $p(\Phi) \ne q(\Phi)$. Their difference is $(\operatorname{tr} \Phi)^2 - \operatorname{tr}(\Phi \wedge \Phi) = -2\,\mathrm{vol} = 2 \det(\Phi)$, consistent with the identity $(\operatorname{tr})^2 - \operatorname{tr}(\cdot^2) = 2 \det$ for $2 \times 2$ matrices; it is the presence of *both* independent degree-two invariants that is later assembled into $c_1^2$ and $c_2$ (see [[Thm - Axioms and Properties of Chern Classes]]). This confirms the Axiom Motivation's claim that the construction must not throw away information distinguishing invariant polynomials of equal degree.

**Corollary — the Chern–Weil form of a flat connection vanishes in positive degree.** If $\omega$ is a [[Def - Flat Connection|flat connection]], meaning $F_\omega = 0$, and $\deg p = d \ge 1$, then $p(F_\omega) = 0$. Indeed every summand of the basis formula contains at least one factor $\Omega^{j_a}$, that is at least one factor of the curvature, so it vanishes when the curvature vanishes; equivalently, $\tilde p(0, \dots, 0) = 0$ for a $d$-linear form with $d \ge 1$. Consequently the class $c_p(P) = 0$ for every $p$ of positive degree whenever $P$ admits a flat connection, which is the source of Chern–Weil obstructions to flatness developed on [[Thm - Trivial Bundles Have Vanishing Characteristic Classes]].

**Corollary — the Chern–Weil form is gauge invariant.** A [[Def - Gauge Transformation|gauge transformation]] $g$ acts on a connection so that its curvature transforms by $F \mapsto \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} F$ (for a matrix group, $F \mapsto g^{-1} F g$); this is the content of [[Thm - Gauge Transformations Act on Connections and Curvature|the theorem on how gauge transformations act on connections and curvature]], restated: if $g$ is a gauge transformation of $P$ and $\omega$ a connection with curvature $F_\omega$, the gauge-transformed connection has curvature $\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} F_\omega$ (pointwise the adjoint action of $g$ on the $\operatorname{ad} P$-valued curvature). Then
$$p(\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} F_\omega) = p(F_\omega),$$
because $p$ (equivalently $\tilde p$) is $\operatorname{Ad}$-invariant, exactly the computation of Lemma 3, Step 2, carried out fibrewise with $g$ in place of the structure-group element. Hence $p(F_\omega)$ is unchanged under gauge transformations, and a fortiori the class $c_p(P)$ is gauge invariant — as it must be, being an invariant of the bundle alone.

**Calibration check.** First, recompute the $U(1)$ example with the normalised polynomial $\tfrac{i}{2\pi}\operatorname{tr}$: its Chern–Weil form is $\tfrac{i}{2\pi} F_\omega$, a *real* closed two-form (since $F_\omega$ is imaginary-valued), whose class is $c_1$; this matches the series normalisation $c(E) = \det(1 + \tfrac{i}{2\pi} F)$. Second, confirm that a constant polynomial $p \equiv c$ of degree $0$ gives the constant function $c$ as its Chern–Weil form and the class $c \cdot 1 \in H^0_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$, so that $c_p$ for $p = 1$ is the unit of the cohomology ring — consistent with $p \mapsto c_p$ being a ring homomorphism. Third, check the flat-connection corollary against the product connection on a trivial bundle $M \times G$, whose curvature vanishes identically; every positive-degree Chern–Weil form is then zero, as computed directly on [[Thm - Trivial Bundles Have Vanishing Characteristic Classes]].

> Taking $p = \operatorname{Pf}$, the [[Def - Pfaffian|Pfaffian]] on $\mathfrak{so}(2m)$, gives the [[Def - Euler Class of an Oriented Vector Bundle|Euler class]]; taking the even coefficients of $\det(1 - \tfrac{1}{2\pi} F)$ on a metric connection gives the [[Def - Pontryagin Classes|Pontryagin classes]]. > [!tip] The Chern–Simons form *(from §6.4)*
> When $p(F_\omega)$ is exact — as it is on a trivial bundle — its primitive is a **Chern–Simons form**, a form on $M$ whose exterior derivative recovers the Chern–Weil form; the transgression that produces it is the mechanism behind the [[Thm - Chern-Weil Theorem|Chern–Weil theorem's]] connection-independence clause, and it defines the Chern–Simons functional.
