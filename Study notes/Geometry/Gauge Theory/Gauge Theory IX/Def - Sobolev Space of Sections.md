# Notation

Throughout, $M$ is a smooth, Hausdorff, second-countable manifold of dimension $n$; when a Riemannian structure is needed we fix a [[Def - Riemannian Metric|Riemannian metric]] $g$ on $M$ with associated [[Def - Riemannian Volume Form|volume form]] $\operatorname{vol} = \operatorname{vol}_g$, which in a positively oriented chart $(x^1, \dots, x^n)$ is $\operatorname{vol} = \sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}\, dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n$. We write $E \to M$ for a smooth real or complex vector bundle of rank $r$, $\Gamma(E)$ for its space of smooth [[Def - Section of a Vector Bundle|sections]], and $\Gamma_c(E)$ for the smooth sections of compact support (so $\Gamma_c(E) = \Gamma(E)$ when $M$ is compact). On $\mathbb{R}^n$ the symbol $C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n)$ denotes the smooth functions of compact support. A **fibre metric** on $E$ is a smoothly varying inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ on the fibres, with associated pointwise norm $|v|$ for $v \in E_m$.

A [[Def - Connection on a Vector Bundle|connection]] on $E$ is written $\nabla^E$ and a connection on the cotangent bundle $T^*M$ (in practice the Levi-Civita connection determined by $g$) is written $\nabla^M$. These induce, by the standard tensor and dual constructions recorded on [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles]], a connection on every bundle $(T^*M)^{\otimes i} \otimes E$; we denote the $i$-th **iterated covariant derivative** by
$$\nabla^i u := \underbrace{\nabla \circ \cdots \circ \nabla}_{i}\, u \in \Gamma\big((T^*M)^{\otimes i} \otimes E\big), \qquad \nabla^0 u := u,$$
where at stage $j$ the operator $\nabla$ is the induced connection on $(T^*M)^{\otimes(j-1)} \otimes E$ acting on $\nabla^{j-1}u$. The pointwise norm $|\nabla^i u|$ is taken with respect to the fibre metric on $(T^*M)^{\otimes i} \otimes E$ built from $g$ and the fibre metric on $E$. On $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the flat metric and trivial connection, $\nabla^i u$ is the tensor $(\partial_{j_1} \cdots \partial_{j_i} u)_{j_1, \dots, j_i}$ of all $i$-th partial derivatives and $|\nabla^i u|^2 = \sum_{j_1, \dots, j_i} |\partial_{j_1} \cdots \partial_{j_i} u|^2$.

We write $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{L^p}$, $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{W^{k,p}}$, $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{C^r}$ for the norms defined below, and $H_k := W^{k,2}$ for the Hilbertable members of the scale. On the circle $S^1 = \mathbb{R} / 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ we use the Fourier coefficient $\hat{u}(m) := \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} u(\theta)\, e^{-im\theta}\, d\theta$ for $m \in \mathbb{Z}$.

This is a compound page: it defines five interlocking notions — the Lebesgue-type completion $L^p$, the Sobolev norm and space $W^{k,p}$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$, its bundle version $W^{k,p}(M; E)$ built from a metric and connections, the integer-order Hilbert scale $H_k = W^{k,2}$, and the uniform norms $C^r$ — because in gauge theory these never appear singly: an elliptic estimate compares a $W^{k,2}$ norm to a $W^{k+\ell,2}$ norm, a Sobolev embedding compares $W^{k,2}$ to $C^r$, and a compactness argument compares $W^{k,2}$ to $L^2$, so all five must be pinned down together and on the same footing.

> [!warning] Convention: this series works at $p = 2$
> Every theorem in this chapter is proved for the Hilbert case $p = 2$, and the integer-order scale $H_k = W^{k,2}$ is the object all later chapters use (the configuration spaces of gauge theory are modelled on $W^{k,2}$ spaces, never on general $W^{k,p}$). Haydys states the embedding, compactness, and multiplication theorems for all $W^{k,p}$ with $p > 1$; those general-$p$ statements are recorded verbatim, where they occur, inside Convention callouts as *unproved context*, with a textbook reference. We record here only the definitional part: for $p \geq 1$ the map $u \mapsto \lVert u \rVert_{L^p}$ is a genuine norm on $C_c^\infty$ (the triangle inequality is Minkowski's inequality, which holds for every $p \geq 1$), so the completion below makes sense for every $p \geq 1$; Haydys restricts to $p > 1$ because that is the range in which the duality and embedding theory is cleanest, but the definitions do not require it.

# Axiom Motivation

The definition is best discovered by watching a natural strategy fail for want of it. Consider the oldest boundary-value problem in the theory of elliptic equations, the **Dirichlet problem**: given a bounded domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ with smooth boundary and a prescribed boundary value $\varphi$ on $\partial\Omega$, find a function $u$, twice continuously differentiable inside and continuous up to the boundary, that is harmonic and matches $\varphi$ on the boundary,
$$\Delta u = 0 \ \text{ in } \Omega, \qquad u|_{\partial\Omega} = \varphi, \qquad u \in C^2(\Omega) \cap C^0(\overline{\Omega}),$$
where we use throughout the sign convention $\Delta = -\sum_{i=1}^n \partial_i^2$ (the geometer's non-negative Laplacian). Dirichlet's idea, made precise by Riemann, was to obtain $u$ by minimising the **energy**
$$E(v) := \tfrac{1}{2} \int_\Omega |\nabla v(x)|^2\, dx$$
over all admissible $v \in C^2(\Omega) \cap C^0(\overline{\Omega})$ with $v|_{\partial\Omega} = \varphi$. The reason this ought to work is a one-line computation. If $u$ is a minimiser and $w$ is any smooth variation vanishing on the boundary, then $t \mapsto E(u + tw)$ has a minimum at $t = 0$, so its derivative there vanishes; expanding,
$$E(u + tw) = \tfrac{1}{2}\int_\Omega |\nabla u|^2\, dx + t \int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla w\, dx + \tfrac{t^2}{2}\int_\Omega |\nabla w|^2\, dx \qquad \text{(bilinearity of the integrand in } \nabla(u + tw)\text{)},$$
so differentiating and setting $t = 0$,
$$0 = \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0} E(u + tw) = \int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla w\, dx = -\int_\Omega \Big(\sum_{i=1}^n \partial_i^2 u\Big) w\, dx = \int_\Omega (\Delta u)\, w\, dx \qquad \text{(integration by parts, } w|_{\partial\Omega} = 0 \text{ kills the boundary term; } \Delta = -\textstyle\sum \partial_i^2 \text{)}.$$
Because this holds for **every** such $w$, the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations forces $\Delta u = 0$: a minimiser is automatically harmonic. So a solution of the Dirichlet problem exists provided the energy attains its minimum. The natural way to produce that minimiser is the **direct method**: choose a sequence $u_k$ of admissible functions with $E(u_k)$ decreasing to the infimum, and extract a limit.

Here the plan breaks, and it breaks precisely because the space $C^2(\Omega) \cap C^0(\overline{\Omega})$, equipped with the energy, is not complete. A minimising sequence is controlled only in the quantity $\int |\nabla u_k|^2$; nothing prevents the $u_k$ from developing ever-sharper kinks whose limit has a corner. Weierstrass's celebrated critique of Dirichlet's principle exhibited exactly such energy-bounded sequences whose limit is not twice differentiable — the sequence is "Cauchy in energy" but its limit falls out of $C^2$. The infimum is real and finite, but the minimiser the direct method wants to hand us is not a classical function. **The space is too small: it does not contain the limits of its own bounded-energy sequences.**

The remedy is the one that rescued the real numbers from the same defect. Just as $\mathbb{R}$ is manufactured from $\mathbb{Q}$ by adjoining the limits of Cauchy sequences of rationals, we manufacture the arena for the direct method by adjoining to the smooth functions the limits of Cauchy sequences in the energy norm. That completion is the Sobolev space. In it the minimising sequence converges (a bounded sequence in a Hilbert space has a weakly convergent subsequence, and the energy is weakly lower semicontinuous), so the minimiser exists as a Sobolev function; elliptic regularity — proved later in this chapter — then upgrades it back to a smooth harmonic function. The definition below is nothing more than the systematic completion that makes this possible, together with the bookkeeping needed to measure not just the values but the first $k$ derivatives.

We now examine each ingredient of the definition by asking what fails without it.

**The completion.** If we kept only smooth functions and merely equipped them with the norm, we would have a normed space that is not complete, and the entire direct method would be unavailable: a Cauchy sequence of smooth functions in the $W^{1,2}$ norm need not converge to a smooth function. The explicit witness lives already on the circle: the piecewise-smooth ramp with a single corner is a $W^{1,2}(S^1)$-limit of smooth functions but is not itself $C^1$, so the smooth functions are a proper dense subspace of a strictly larger complete space, and only the larger space is closed under the limits the analysis produces. Dropping the completion returns us to the incomplete space that made Dirichlet's principle fail.

**The derivatives in the norm.** If we measured only the values, that is if we used the bare $L^p$ norm, a Cauchy sequence would control the function but not its gradient, and the energy $\int |\nabla u|^2$ would be invisible to the topology. The completion would then contain objects with no controlled derivative at all — for instance an indicator function, which is an honest element of $L^2(S^1)$ but has no gradient in $L^2$ (verified in the Examples below). A variational problem whose functional depends on the gradient cannot be posed in a space that does not see the gradient. Including $\sum_{i=0}^k \lVert \nabla^i u \rVert_{L^p}^p$ in the norm is exactly what makes the first $k$ derivatives survive the completion.

**The connection.** On $\mathbb{R}^n$ the higher derivative $\nabla^i u$ is unambiguous — it is the array of $i$-th partials. On a manifold or in a vector bundle it is not: the naive second derivative of a section, taken by differentiating components twice in a chart, is not independent of the chart, because comparing the value of a section at one point with its value at a nearby point requires a rule for transporting fibres, and that rule is a connection. Without a connection $\nabla^2 u$ is not even a well-defined tensor, and the norm $\lVert \nabla^2 u \rVert_{L^p}$ has no meaning. The connection is not optional decoration; it is what turns "the second derivative of a section" into a coordinate-independent object that can be measured.

**The independence of the choices.** One might worry that having chosen a metric $g$, a fibre metric, and connections $\nabla^E, \nabla^M$ to write the norm, the resulting space depends on all of this arbitrary data. It does, but only up to equivalence of norms, and only when $M$ is compact. The norm genuinely changes when the data change; the topology, and hence the completion as a topological vector space, does not. This is the content of the theorem on the next page, [[Thm - Sobolev Norms are Independent of the Metric and Connections up to Equivalence]], and it is what licenses the notation $W^{k,p}(M; E)$ with no decoration recording the choices. Were this to fail, "the Sobolev space" would not be a well-defined object and every later theorem would have to carry the auxiliary data as hypotheses.

A reader who has followed these four failure analyses could reconstruct the definition unaided: complete the smooth sections (to get limits), in a norm that includes the first $k$ derivatives (to control the gradient), where the derivatives are taken covariantly (to be coordinate-free), on a compact base (so that the choices do not matter).

# The Definition

We build the scale in four stages: the plain completion $L^p$; the flat Sobolev space $W^{k,p}(\mathbb{R}^n)$; the bundle version $W^{k,p}(M; E)$; and the uniform norms $C^r$.

**Stage 1: the spaces $L^p$.** For $p \geq 1$ and $u \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n)$ set
$$\lVert u \rVert_{L^p} := \Big( \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} |u(x)|^p\, dx \Big)^{1/p}.$$
The integral is a Riemann integral of a compactly supported continuous integrand, so no measure theory is needed to give the norm meaning on $C_c^\infty$. The space $L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is the [[Def - Cauchy Sequence and Complete Metric Space|completion]] of $\big(C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n), \lVert \cdot \rVert_{L^p}\big)$: its points are equivalence classes of $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{L^p}$-Cauchy sequences of smooth compactly supported functions, two sequences being identified when the norm of their difference tends to zero, and the norm extends by continuity. For $p = 2$ the norm comes from the [[Def - Inner Product Space|inner product]]
$$\langle u, v \rangle_{L^2} := \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} u\, \overline{v}\, dx$$
(the complex conjugate is dropped in the real case), which extends to the completion by continuity and makes $L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$ a Hilbert space — a complete inner product space.

**Stage 2: the flat Sobolev spaces.** For a non-negative integer $k$ and $p \geq 1$ define the **Sobolev norm** of $u \in C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n)$ by
$$\lVert u \rVert_{W^{k,p}} := \Big( \sum_{i=0}^{k} \lVert \nabla^i u \rVert_{L^p}^p \Big)^{1/p}, \qquad \lVert \nabla^i u \rVert_{L^p} = \Big( \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} |\nabla^i u(x)|^p\, dx \Big)^{1/p},$$
where $\nabla^i u$ is the tensor of $i$-th partial derivatives and $|\nabla^i u|$ its Euclidean norm as in the Notation. The **Sobolev space** $W^{k,p}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is the completion of $\big(C_c^\infty(\mathbb{R}^n), \lVert \cdot \rVert_{W^{k,p}}\big)$. Because $|\nabla^i u|^2 = \sum_{j_1, \dots, j_i} |\partial_{j_1} \cdots \partial_{j_i} u|^2$ is comparable, with constants depending only on $i$ and $n$, to $\sum_{|\alpha| = i} |\partial^\alpha u|^2$ (the two differ only by the multinomial multiplicities of the mixed partials), the norm above is equivalent to the familiar multi-index form $\big(\sum_{|\alpha| \leq k} \lVert \partial^\alpha u \rVert_{L^p}^p\big)^{1/p}$; we use whichever is convenient and record when a computation depends on the choice.

**Stage 3: the bundle Sobolev spaces.** Fix a Riemannian metric $g$ on $M$, a fibre metric on $E$, and connections $\nabla^E$ on $E$ and $\nabla^M$ on $T^*M$; these induce connections and fibre metrics on each $(T^*M)^{\otimes i} \otimes E$, so that the iterated covariant derivative $\nabla^i u \in \Gamma\big((T^*M)^{\otimes i} \otimes E\big)$ and its pointwise norm $|\nabla^i u|$ are defined for $u \in \Gamma(E)$ (see the Notation and [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles]]). For a non-negative integer $k$ and $p \geq 1$ set
$$\lVert u \rVert_{W^{k,p}} := \Big( \sum_{i=0}^{k} \int_{M} |\nabla^i u|^p\, \operatorname{vol} \Big)^{1/p}, \qquad u \in \Gamma_c(E),$$
and define $W^{k,p}(M; E)$ to be the completion of $\big(\Gamma_c(E), \lVert \cdot \rVert_{W^{k,p}}\big)$ (with $\Gamma_c(E) = \Gamma(E)$ when $M$ is compact). By construction $W^{0,p}(M; E) = L^p(M; E)$ — the case $k = 0$ carries no derivatives — and since each additional summand only enlarges the norm, the identity map on $\Gamma_c(E)$ is norm-decreasing from $W^{k,p}$ to $W^{m,p}$ for $k \geq m$ and extends to a continuous inclusion of completions,
$$L^p(M; E) = W^{0,p}(M; E) \supseteq W^{1,p}(M; E) \supseteq W^{2,p}(M; E) \supseteq \cdots.$$

**The integer-order Hilbert scale.** When $p = 2$ we write
$$H_k := H_k(M; E) := W^{k,2}(M; E),$$
which is a Hilbert space: the norm comes from the inner product
$$\langle u, v \rangle_{H_k} := \sum_{i=0}^{k} \int_M \langle \nabla^i u, \nabla^i v \rangle\, \operatorname{vol},$$
where $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ on the right is the fibre metric on $(T^*M)^{\otimes i} \otimes E$; this is bilinear (sesquilinear in the complex case), positive definite on $\Gamma_c(E)$, and extends by continuity to the completion. Negative-order members $H_k$ with $k < 0$ are defined by Fourier series and duality on the sibling page [[Def - Fourier Series on the Torus and Sobolev Spaces of Integer Order]] and are not needed for the completion definition given here.

**Stage 4: the uniform norms.** For a non-negative integer $r$ define, on $\Gamma(E)$ (or on the sections whose first $r$ derivatives are bounded, when $M$ is non-compact),
$$\lVert u \rVert_{C^r} := \sum_{i=0}^{r} \sup_{m \in M} |\nabla^i u(m)|,$$
and let $C^r(M; E)$ be the space of $r$-times continuously differentiable sections with $\lVert u \rVert_{C^r} < \infty$, a Banach space under this norm. The Sobolev and uniform scales are compared by the [[Thm - Sobolev Embedding Theorem|Sobolev embedding theorem]] later in the chapter; the point of listing both here is that ellipticity is stated in the Sobolev scale but regularity conclusions are wanted in the uniform scale, so both must be available.

> [!note] Remark (notation, after Haydys's Remark 135)
> The literature is inconsistent about Sobolev notation: the symbol $L^s_r$ means $W^{r,s}$ in some texts and $W^{s,r}$ in others, and $H^s$, $H_s$, $W^{s,2}$, $L^2_s$ all denote the same object in different books. We follow Haydys in writing $W^{k,p}$ for the general space, with the first superscript the order $k$ (number of derivatives) and the second the integrability exponent $p$, because that ordering is the one most consistently used. For the Hilbert case $p = 2$ we abbreviate $H_k := W^{k,2}$ (equivalently $L^2_k$ in the notation of some sources). There is one meaning of each symbol on this page and throughout the series.

The dependence of the norm on the auxiliary data $(g, \text{fibre metric}, \nabla^E, \nabla^M)$ is real: change the metric and the numbers $\lVert u \rVert_{W^{k,p}}$ change. What does not change, when $M$ is compact, is the topology, and hence the space $W^{k,p}(M; E)$ as a topological vector space. Precisely, the next page establishes the following, which we restate at every point where it is invoked.

> [!note] Invoked here: independence of the choices (proved on the next page)
> **Theorem** ([[Thm - Sobolev Norms are Independent of the Metric and Connections up to Equivalence]]). Let $M$ be compact and $E \to M$ a vector bundle. For any two choices $(g, \nabla^E, \nabla^M)$ and $(g', \nabla'^E, \nabla'^M)$ of Riemannian metric and connections, and any two fibre metrics on $E$, the resulting norms $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{W^{k,2}}$ and $\lVert \cdot \rVert'_{W^{k,2}}$ on $\Gamma(E)$ are equivalent for every $k \geq 0$: there are constants $c, C > 0$ with $c\,\lVert u \rVert'_{W^{k,2}} \leq \lVert u \rVert_{W^{k,2}} \leq C\,\lVert u \rVert'_{W^{k,2}}$ for all $u \in \Gamma(E)$. Consequently $W^{k,2}(M; E)$ is well defined as a topological vector space (indeed as a Hilbertable space) independently of the choices.

This is why we may speak of "the" Sobolev space $H_k(M; E)$ on a compact manifold without naming the data; the mechanism, spelled out on that page, is that on a compact manifold every smooth object is bounded and the difference of two connections is a smooth zeroth-order object, so the two norms differ by bounded factors.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

Each Sobolev space is a *completion*, and completions are characterised by a universal property, so the cleanest structural description of $W^{k,p}(M; E)$ is as the solution to a universal problem rather than as a set of equivalence classes.

Let $(V, \lVert \cdot \rVert)$ be a normed space — for us $V = \Gamma_c(E)$ with a Sobolev norm. A **completion** of $V$ is a Banach space $\overline{V}$ together with a linear isometry $\iota : V \to \overline{V}$ with dense image, satisfying the universal property: for every Banach space $B$ and every bounded linear map $f : V \to B$, there is a **unique** bounded linear map $\overline{f} : \overline{V} \to B$ with $\overline{f} \circ \iota = f$ and $\lVert \overline{f} \rVert = \lVert f \rVert$. In categorical language $\iota : V \to \overline{V}$ is the unit of the left adjoint to the forgetful functor from Banach spaces (with bounded linear maps of norm at most one, or with all bounded maps for the non-isometric version) to normed spaces; concretely, $\overline{V}$ is the universal recipient of $V$ among complete spaces, and $W^{k,p}(M; E) := \overline{\Gamma_c(E)}$ is this universal object for the Sobolev norm.

The universal property determines $\overline{V}$ up to unique isometric isomorphism, so it is a legitimate definition once existence is known. Existence is the concrete construction used above, and we verify it in full for the record.

> [!note]- Existence and uniqueness of the completion (full proof)
> **Construction.** Let $\mathcal{C}$ be the set of $\lVert \cdot \rVert$-Cauchy sequences in $V$. Declare $(x_j) \sim (y_j)$ when $\lVert x_j - y_j \rVert \to 0$. This is an equivalence relation: **reflexivity** holds since $\lVert x_j - x_j \rVert = 0 \to 0$; **symmetry** since $\lVert x_j - y_j \rVert = \lVert y_j - x_j \rVert$; **transitivity** since if $\lVert x_j - y_j \rVert \to 0$ and $\lVert y_j - z_j \rVert \to 0$ then $\lVert x_j - z_j \rVert \leq \lVert x_j - y_j \rVert + \lVert y_j - z_j \rVert \to 0$ (by the triangle inequality). Let $\overline{V} := \mathcal{C}/\!\sim$, with vector operations $[(x_j)] + [(y_j)] := [(x_j + y_j)]$ and $\lambda[(x_j)] := [(\lambda x_j)]$, which are well defined because the sum and scalar multiple of Cauchy sequences are Cauchy and the class of the result is unchanged when a representative is replaced by an equivalent one (again by the triangle inequality and $|\lambda|$-homogeneity of the norm).
>
> **The norm on $\overline{V}$.** For $(x_j) \in \mathcal{C}$ the real sequence $\lVert x_j \rVert$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{R}$, since $\big|\, \lVert x_j \rVert - \lVert x_l \rVert \,\big| \leq \lVert x_j - x_l \rVert$ (reverse triangle inequality), so it converges; set $\lVert [(x_j)] \rVert_{\overline{V}} := \lim_j \lVert x_j \rVert$. This is independent of the representative: if $(x_j) \sim (y_j)$ then $\big|\, \lVert x_j \rVert - \lVert y_j \rVert \,\big| \leq \lVert x_j - y_j \rVert \to 0$. It is a norm: homogeneity and the triangle inequality pass to the limit, and if $\lim_j \lVert x_j \rVert = 0$ then $(x_j) \sim (0) $, so the class is zero.
>
> **The embedding.** Define $\iota(x) := [(x, x, x, \dots)]$, the class of the constant sequence. It is linear and $\lVert \iota(x) \rVert_{\overline{V}} = \lim_j \lVert x \rVert = \lVert x \rVert$, so $\iota$ is an isometry. Its image is dense: given $[(x_j)] \in \overline{V}$ and $\varepsilon > 0$, choose $N$ with $\lVert x_j - x_N \rVert < \varepsilon$ for all $j \geq N$ (Cauchy); then $\lVert [(x_j)] - \iota(x_N) \rVert_{\overline{V}} = \lim_j \lVert x_j - x_N \rVert \leq \varepsilon$.
>
> **Completeness.** Let $(\xi^{(m)})_m$ be a Cauchy sequence in $\overline{V}$. By density pick $y_m \in V$ with $\lVert \xi^{(m)} - \iota(y_m) \rVert_{\overline{V}} < 1/m$. Then $\lVert \iota(y_m) - \iota(y_l) \rVert_{\overline{V}} \leq 1/m + 1/l + \lVert \xi^{(m)} - \xi^{(l)} \rVert_{\overline{V}}$ (triangle inequality), so $(y_m)$ is Cauchy in $V$ (using that $\iota$ is an isometry), hence defines $\eta := [(y_m)] \in \overline{V}$. Now $\lVert \xi^{(m)} - \eta \rVert_{\overline{V}} \leq \lVert \xi^{(m)} - \iota(y_m) \rVert_{\overline{V}} + \lVert \iota(y_m) - \eta \rVert_{\overline{V}} < 1/m + \lim_l \lVert y_m - y_l \rVert \to 0$, so $\xi^{(m)} \to \eta$. Thus $\overline{V}$ is complete.
>
> **Universal property.** Let $B$ be a Banach space and $f : V \to B$ bounded linear with norm $\lVert f \rVert$. For $[(x_j)] \in \overline{V}$ the sequence $f(x_j)$ is Cauchy in $B$, since $\lVert f(x_j) - f(x_l) \rVert_B \leq \lVert f \rVert\, \lVert x_j - x_l \rVert$; define $\overline{f}([(x_j)]) := \lim_j f(x_j)$, which exists by completeness of $B$ and is independent of the representative (if $(x_j) \sim (y_j)$ then $\lVert f(x_j) - f(y_j) \rVert_B \leq \lVert f \rVert\, \lVert x_j - y_j \rVert \to 0$). It is linear, extends $f$ (on constant sequences $\overline{f}(\iota(x)) = f(x)$), and satisfies $\lVert \overline{f}([(x_j)]) \rVert_B = \lim_j \lVert f(x_j) \rVert_B \leq \lVert f \rVert \lim_j \lVert x_j \rVert = \lVert f \rVert\, \lVert [(x_j)] \rVert_{\overline{V}}$, so $\lVert \overline{f} \rVert \leq \lVert f \rVert$; the reverse inequality holds because $\overline{f}$ restricts to $f$ on the isometric copy of $V$. **Uniqueness:** any bounded extension agrees with $\overline{f}$ on the dense subspace $\iota(V)$ and hence everywhere by continuity.
>
> **Uniqueness of the completion.** If $(\overline{V}_1, \iota_1)$ and $(\overline{V}_2, \iota_2)$ both satisfy the universal property, apply it to $\iota_2 : V \to \overline{V}_2$ to extend it to $\Phi : \overline{V}_1 \to \overline{V}_2$, and symmetrically to get $\Psi : \overline{V}_2 \to \overline{V}_1$; then $\Psi \circ \Phi$ and the identity both extend $\iota_1$, so by the uniqueness clause $\Psi \circ \Phi = \mathrm{id}$, and likewise $\Phi \circ \Psi = \mathrm{id}$, so $\Phi$ is an isometric isomorphism intertwining the embeddings. Therefore the completion is unique up to unique isometric isomorphism. $\blacksquare$

For $p = 2$ the norm comes from an inner product, and the completion of an inner product space is a Hilbert space (the inner product extends by the polarisation identity and continuity, exactly as the norm does above), so $H_k(M; E)$ is the universal Hilbert-space recipient of $\Gamma_c(E)$ in the $\lVert \cdot \rVert_{H_k}$ norm. This structural reading is what makes the definition portable: on the torus it will be realised as a weighted sequence space, and the universal property guarantees that the two constructions produce the same space.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**The completion pattern.** Every space on this page is an instance of one move: take the objects you can compute with (smooth compactly supported functions or sections), impose a norm that measures exactly the quantities the problem controls, and pass to the completion. This is the same manufacture that produces $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$ (complete the rationals in the absolute value), $L^2$ from continuous functions (complete in the mean-square norm), and the reals-valued distributions from test functions (complete in a family of seminorms). The Sobolev norm is distinguished only by *what* it measures — the function together with its first $k$ derivatives — which is dictated by the differential order of the equations one intends to solve.

**True name.** The official definition is a completion, but the operational content of "$u \in W^{k,p}$" is: *$u$ and its first $k$ derivatives all lie in $L^p$*, where the derivatives are understood in the sense inherited from the completion (the weak, or distributional, derivatives — the limits of the classical derivatives of the approximating smooth functions). Concretely, to certify $u \in W^{k,p}(M; E)$ one exhibits a sequence $u_j \in \Gamma_c(E)$ that is Cauchy in the $W^{k,p}$ norm and converges to $u$; equivalently, one verifies that $u$ and its first $k$ derivatives are $p$-integrable. This is the picture to keep in mind: a Sobolev function is a smooth function up to a controlled amount of roughness, the amount being measured by how many derivatives remain integrable.

**Fourier realisation on the torus.** The most useful compression of the whole scale is that on the flat torus $T^n = \mathbb{R}^n / 2\pi\mathbb{Z}^n$, and in particular on the circle $S^1$, the Sobolev space $H_k$ is nothing but a weighted space of Fourier coefficients: $u \in H_k$ if and only if $\sum_{\xi} (1 + |\xi|^2)^k |\hat{u}(\xi)|^2 < \infty$. The abstract completion becomes a concrete sequence space, differentiation becomes multiplication by the frequency, and smoothness becomes rapid decay of coefficients. This realisation, proved on [[Def - Fourier Series on the Torus and Sobolev Spaces of Integer Order]] and [[Thm - Sobolev Norms on the Torus via Fourier Coefficients]], is the computational engine of the entire chapter, and it is the reason the manifold case is proved by cutting $M$ into chart-pieces of a torus.

> [!warning] Convention: identification with the Lebesgue space, and why we do not use it
> Classical measure theory identifies the completion $L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)$ defined above with the Lebesgue space of ($p$-th power) integrable functions modulo almost-everywhere equality. Two theorems make the identification: the completeness of the Lebesgue space (the **Riesz–Fischer theorem**, e.g. Folland, *Real Analysis*, 2nd ed., Theorem 6.6) and the density of $C_c^\infty$ in it (e.g. Folland, *Real Analysis*, 2nd ed., Proposition 8.17). Under this identification the abstract element $[(u_j)]$ corresponds to the almost-everywhere limit of a suitable subsequence of the $u_j$. **This identification is not used anywhere in this series.** We define and manipulate $L^p$ and $W^{k,p}$ purely as completions and, on the torus, as weighted sequence spaces; no Lebesgue integration theory is invoked in any proof. The vault's [[Def - Lp Spaces]] develops the Lebesgue viewpoint and is provided as context only. We record the identification so that a reader who knows the measure-theoretic definition sees that the two agree, not because any argument below depends on it.

**Distributions of negative order.** For $k < 0$ the spaces $H_k$ (defined by duality on the Fourier page) are spaces of distributions, and the reader who knows distribution theory will recognise them as such; but the series deliberately avoids distribution theory, replacing it everywhere by the duality pairing on weighted sequence spaces, so that every statement about negative-order spaces reduces to an elementary inequality about sequences.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — every smooth function on a compact manifold lies in every $H_k$.** If $M$ is compact and $u \in \Gamma(E)$, then each $\nabla^i u$ is a continuous section of a bundle over a compact space, hence bounded, so $\int_M |\nabla^i u|^2\, \operatorname{vol} \leq \big(\sup_M |\nabla^i u|\big)^2 \operatorname{vol}(M) < \infty$ for every $i$; summing over $i \leq k$ gives $\lVert u \rVert_{H_k} < \infty$ for every $k \geq 0$. Thus $\Gamma(E) \subseteq \bigcap_{k \geq 0} H_k(M; E)$, and the smooth sections are the "infinitely regular" members of the scale — the Sobolev embedding theorem later shows this containment is an equality.

**Calibration instance — the norm of $e^{in\theta}$ on the circle.** Take $M = S^1 = \mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$ with the flat metric $g = d\theta^2$ (so $\operatorname{vol} = d\theta$ and the connection is $\nabla = d/d\theta$), $E$ the trivial complex line bundle, and $u_n(\theta) = e^{in\theta}$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. We verify $\lVert u_n \rVert_{W^{k,2}}$ clause by clause. The $i$-th derivative is
$$\nabla^i u_n = \frac{d^i}{d\theta^i} e^{in\theta} = (in)^i e^{in\theta} \qquad \text{(differentiating the exponential } i \text{ times)},$$
so its pointwise norm is $|\nabla^i u_n| = |in|^i \cdot |e^{in\theta}| = |n|^i$ (constant in $\theta$, since $|e^{in\theta}| = 1$). Hence
$$\lVert \nabla^i u_n \rVert_{L^2}^2 = \int_0^{2\pi} |n|^{2i}\, d\theta = 2\pi\, n^{2i} \qquad \text{(integrating a constant over } [0, 2\pi]\text{)},$$
and summing the definition of the Sobolev norm,
$$\lVert u_n \rVert_{W^{k,2}}^2 = \sum_{i=0}^{k} \lVert \nabla^i u_n \rVert_{L^2}^2 = 2\pi \sum_{i=0}^{k} n^{2i} \qquad \text{(by the Stage-2/Stage-3 definition with } p = 2\text{)}.$$
For $|n| \geq 1$ this is a finite geometric-type sum dominated by its top term: $2\pi\, n^{2k} \leq \lVert u_n \rVert_{W^{k,2}}^2 \leq 2\pi (k+1)\, n^{2k}$, so $\lVert u_n \rVert_{W^{k,2}} \asymp \sqrt{2\pi}\, |n|^k$ as $|n| \to \infty$. This matches the Fourier weight exactly: in the normalisation of [[Def - Fourier Series on the Torus and Sobolev Spaces of Integer Order]] the coefficient sequence of $u_n$ is $\hat{u}_n(m) = \delta_{mn}$, so the weighted norm is $\lVert u_n \rVert_k^2 = \sum_m (1 + m^2)^k \delta_{mn} = (1 + n^2)^k \asymp n^{2k}$, agreeing with the derivative computation up to the bounded constants that Part (i) of [[Thm - Sobolev Norms on the Torus via Fourier Coefficients]] guarantees. The exponentials are thus the eigen-directions on which the whole Sobolev scale is transparently diagonal.

**Is an instance — the $W^{1,2}(S^1)$ criterion.** A convergent trigonometric series lies in $W^{1,2}(S^1)$ exactly when its coefficients decay fast enough to make the weighted sum finite. We invoke, and restate, the criterion proved on the sibling page.

> [!note] Invoked here: the Fourier criterion for $W^{1,2}(S^1)$ (proved on the sibling page)
> **Theorem** ([[Thm - Sobolev Norms on the Torus via Fourier Coefficients]], case $n = 1$, $k = 1$). For $u$ on $S^1$ with Fourier coefficients $\hat{u}(m)$, one has $u \in W^{1,2}(S^1)$ if and only if $\sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} (1 + m^2) |\hat{u}(m)|^2 < \infty$, and then $\lVert u \rVert_{W^{1,2}}^2$ is equivalent to $2\pi \sum_m (1 + m^2)|\hat{u}(m)|^2$.

For example $u(\theta) = \sum_{m \neq 0} m^{-2} e^{im\theta}$ has $\sum_m (1 + m^2)|\hat{u}(m)|^2 = \sum_{m \neq 0} (1 + m^2) m^{-4} \asymp \sum_{m \neq 0} m^{-2} < \infty$, so $u \in W^{1,2}(S^1)$; whereas the same series with coefficients $m^{-1}$ fails the criterion, as the next non-example shows in its sharpest form.

**Is NOT an instance — the indicator of an arc is $L^2$ but not $W^{1,2}$ on $S^1$.** Let $u = \mathbf{1}_{[a,b]}$ be the indicator of a proper closed arc $[a, b] \subsetneq S^1$ with $0 < b - a < 2\pi$. We verify both halves.

*It lies in $L^2(S^1)$.* Approximate $u$ by smooth functions: for each $j$ let $u_j \in C^\infty(S^1)$ equal $1$ on $[a + \tfrac1j, b - \tfrac1j]$, equal $0$ outside $[a, b]$, and take values in $[0, 1]$ on the two transition arcs of width $1/j$ (such $u_j$ exist by smoothing a piecewise-linear ramp with a standard mollifier). Then $u$ and $u_j$ differ only on the two transition arcs, each of length $1/j$, where $|u - u_j| \leq 1$, so $\lVert u - u_j \rVert_{L^2}^2 = \int_{S^1} |u - u_j|^2\, d\theta \leq 2 \cdot \tfrac1j \to 0$. Hence $(u_j)$ is $L^2$-Cauchy and represents an element of $L^2(S^1)$, which we call $u$; and $\lVert u \rVert_{L^2}^2 = \lim_j \lVert u_j \rVert_{L^2}^2 = b - a < \infty$.

*It does not lie in $W^{1,2}(S^1)$.* Compute the Fourier coefficients directly. For $m \neq 0$,
$$\hat{u}(m) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_a^b e^{-im\theta}\, d\theta = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot \frac{e^{-ima} - e^{-imb}}{im} \qquad \text{(elementary integral of } e^{-im\theta}\text{)},$$
so
$$|\hat{u}(m)| = \frac{|e^{-ima} - e^{-imb}|}{2\pi |m|} = \frac{2\,\big|\sin\big(\tfrac{m(b - a)}{2}\big)\big|}{2\pi|m|} = \frac{\big|\sin\big(\tfrac{m(b-a)}{2}\big)\big|}{\pi |m|} \qquad \text{(} |e^{i\alpha} - e^{i\beta}| = 2|\sin(\tfrac{\alpha - \beta}{2})| \text{)}.$$
Now test the criterion of the invoked theorem:
$$\sum_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} (1 + m^2)|\hat{u}(m)|^2 \geq \sum_{m \neq 0} m^2 \cdot \frac{\sin^2\big(\tfrac{m(b-a)}{2}\big)}{\pi^2 m^2} = \frac{1}{\pi^2} \sum_{m \neq 0} \sin^2\Big(\frac{m(b-a)}{2}\Big) \qquad \text{(dropping the } +1 \text{ and cancelling } m^2\text{)}.$$
The summand $\sin^2(m(b-a)/2)$ is bounded below by a fixed positive constant on infinitely many $m$. Indeed, write $c := (b - a)/2 \in (0, \pi)$. If $c/\pi$ is irrational, the sequence $mc \bmod \pi$ is equidistributed, so $\sin^2(mc) \geq \tfrac12$ for a positive density of indices and in particular infinitely often. If $c/\pi$ is rational, the sequence $\sin^2(mc)$ is periodic in $m$; it is not identically zero, for otherwise every coefficient $\hat{u}(m)$ with $m \neq 0$ would vanish and $u$ would be constant, contradicting that $[a,b]$ is a proper arc — so on each period it attains a positive value $\delta > 0$, hence $\sin^2(mc) \geq \delta$ on a full arithmetic progression of $m$. In either case the summand does not tend to $0$, and a series of non-negative terms whose terms do not tend to zero diverges; therefore $\sum_m (1 + m^2)|\hat{u}(m)|^2 = \infty$, and by the criterion $u \notin W^{1,2}(S^1)$.

This is the sharpest illustration of why derivatives must enter the norm: the indicator is a perfectly good $L^2$ element, but its jump makes its coefficients decay only like $1/|m|$, and one extra factor of $m$ from the derivative destroys square-summability. A function may be in every $L^p$ and in no $W^{1,2}$; the Sobolev norm sees a discontinuity that the Lebesgue norm cannot.

**Calibration check.** Three verifications a reader should be able to carry out from the page alone. First, confirm from the Stage-3 definition that $W^{0,2}(M; E) = L^2(M; E)$: the norm with $k = 0$ has the single summand $\int_M |u|^2 \operatorname{vol}$, which is the $L^2$ norm, and the two completions coincide. Second, confirm the first inclusion in the chain is a contraction: for $u \in \Gamma_c(E)$, $\lVert u \rVert_{L^2}^2 = \int_M |u|^2 \operatorname{vol} \leq \int_M |u|^2 \operatorname{vol} + \int_M |\nabla u|^2 \operatorname{vol} = \lVert u \rVert_{W^{1,2}}^2$, so the identity extends to a norm-$\leq 1$ inclusion $W^{1,2}(M; E) \hookrightarrow L^2(M; E)$. Third, redo the $e^{in\theta}$ computation with $k = 2$ to get $\lVert e^{in\theta} \rVert_{W^{2,2}}^2 = 2\pi(1 + n^2 + n^4)$, and check that the ratio to the Fourier weight $(1 + n^2)^2 = 1 + 2n^2 + n^4$ stays between fixed positive constants for all $n$, as the equivalence of norms requires.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Sobolev embedding and Rellich compactness *(from this chapter)*
> The whole point of building the scale is that the members are comparable: enough derivatives in $L^2$ force continuity ([[Thm - Sobolev Embedding Theorem|the Sobolev embedding theorem]], $H_k \hookrightarrow C^r$ for $k - n/2 > r$), and a bounded set in a higher space is precompact in a lower one ([[Thm - Rellich Compactness Theorem|Rellich compactness]], $H_k \hookrightarrow H_m$ compact for $k > m$). These two, both proved later in the chapter through the Fourier realisation on the torus, are the analytic backbone of every existence and regularity theorem in the series.

> [!tip] The gauge-theoretic configuration space *(from Gauge Theory XI)*
> The spaces of connections and sections on which gauge theory does analysis are **Sobolev completions**: one works with $W^{k,2}$ connections and gauge transformations, not smooth ones, precisely so that the direct method and the implicit function theorem have a complete space to run in, and the smoothness of solutions is recovered at the end by elliptic regularity — the modern realisation of Dirichlet's principle that motivated this page.

> [!tip] The Sobolev algebra and the gauge group *(from §9.2)*
> When $2k > n$ the multiplication theorem makes $H_k(M)$ a Banach algebra, so that pointwise products of Sobolev sections stay Sobolev; this is what lets the group of $W^{k,2}$ gauge transformations be a group at all and, via composition with the exponential, a Banach Lie group modelling the configuration space.
