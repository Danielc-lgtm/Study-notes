# Notation

Throughout this page $n \geq 1$ and $r \geq 1$ are fixed integers. The **flat $n$-torus** is the quotient group
$$T^n := \mathbb{R}^n / (2\pi\mathbb{Z}^n),$$
that is, $\mathbb{R}^n$ with two points identified when they differ by an element of the lattice $2\pi\mathbb{Z}^n = \{(2\pi m_1, \dots, 2\pi m_n) : m_j \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. A point of $T^n$ is written $x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, understood modulo $2\pi$ in each coordinate; a fundamental domain is the cube $[0, 2\pi)^n$, and integration over $T^n$ means $\int_{T^n} f \, dx := \int_{[0,2\pi)^n} f(x) \, dx_1 \cdots dx_n$, so that $\operatorname{vol}(T^n) = (2\pi)^n$. The **flat metric** on $T^n$ is the Riemannian metric descended from the Euclidean metric $\sum_j dx_j \otimes dx_j$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$; it is well defined because the lattice acts by translations, which are Euclidean isometries. A function or map on $T^n$ is the same thing as a $2\pi\mathbb{Z}^n$-periodic function or map on $\mathbb{R}^n$, and it is smooth on $T^n$ exactly when it is smooth and periodic on $\mathbb{R}^n$; we write $C^\infty(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ for the smooth $\mathbb{C}^r$-valued functions.

We use the standard Hermitian inner product on the fibre $\mathbb{C}^r$,
$$\langle v, w \rangle_{\mathbb{C}^r} := \sum_{a=1}^r v_a \overline{w_a}, \qquad |v| := \langle v, v\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^r}^{1/2},$$
conjugate-linear in the second slot; when $r = 1$ this is $v\overline{w}$. The **dual lattice** is $\mathbb{Z}^n$: its elements $\xi = (\xi_1, \dots, \xi_n) \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ index the **characters** $x \mapsto e^{i\langle\xi, x\rangle}$, where $\langle\xi, x\rangle := \sum_{j=1}^n \xi_j x_j$ is the pairing between a frequency $\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ and a point $x \in T^n$ (this expression is well defined modulo $2\pi\mathbb{Z}^n$ precisely because $\xi$ is integer-valued). We write $|\xi|^2 = \sum_j \xi_j^2$ for the squared Euclidean length of a frequency, and throughout we abbreviate the **Bessel weight**
$$w_k(\xi) := (1 + |\xi|^2)^k \qquad (\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n, \ k \in \mathbb{Z}),$$
a strictly positive real number for every $\xi$ and every integer $k$, with $w_k(\xi)\, w_m(\xi) = w_{k+m}(\xi)$ and $w_k(\xi)^{-1} = w_{-k}(\xi)$.

For a multi-index $\alpha = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n) \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^n$ we write $|\alpha| = \sum_j \alpha_j$, $\partial^\alpha = \partial_{x_1}^{\alpha_1} \cdots \partial_{x_n}^{\alpha_n}$, and $\xi^\alpha = \xi_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots \xi_n^{\alpha_n}$. The symbol $\ell^2$ denotes the Hilbert space of square-summable sequences; $\delta_{\xi\eta}$ is the Kronecker delta, equal to $1$ if $\xi = \eta$ and $0$ otherwise. We write $H_k$ rather than the equivalent notations $L^2_k$ or $W^{k,2}$; the identity $H_k = W^{k,2}$ with equivalent norms is the content of the sibling page **[[Thm - Sobolev Norms on the Torus via Fourier Coefficients]]** and is not assumed here.

This is a **compound page**: it introduces five interlocking objects — the torus $T^n$, the Fourier coefficients $\hat u(\xi)$ of a smooth function, the norm $\|\cdot\|_k$ and the Sobolev space $H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ for every integer $k$ (positive, zero, and negative), its vector-valued form, and the bilinear $L^2$ pairing $H_k \times H_{-k} \to \mathbb{C}$ — because they are inseparable: the whole point of the construction is that Fourier coefficients turn the Sobolev norm into an explicit weighted sum, so that the entire scale of spaces, in both directions of $k$, is a single family of weighted sequence spaces built from one orthonormal system.

> [!warning] Convention: Sobolev spaces are $L^2$-based in this series
> Following the design decision recorded in the chapter manifest, every Sobolev space in this series is modelled on $L^2$. We write $H_k := W^{k,2}$ and prove every estimate for the exponent $p = 2$. Haydys's general statements for $W^{k,p}$ with $p > 1$ are recorded verbatim, without proof, in the Convention callouts of the theorem pages that need them; on the torus the choice $p = 2$ is not a loss of generality but a genuine simplification, because $L^2$ is a Hilbert space and the characters $e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle}$ form an orthogonal basis, so all norms diagonalise. The reference for the $L^2$ torus construction adopted here is Lawson–Michelsohn, *Spin Geometry*, Chapter III §5, with Wells, *Differential Analysis on Complex Manifolds*, Chapter IV §1–4 as the second reference.

> [!warning] Convention: placement of the $(2\pi)^{-n}$ in the Fourier transform
> We normalise the Fourier coefficient with the full factor $(2\pi)^{-n}$ in front of the integral, so that the coefficient of a character is $\pm 1$ and the constant function $1$ has $\hat u(0) = 1$. With this convention the Plancherel identity carries a factor $(2\pi)^n$: $\int_{T^n} |u|^2 \, dx = (2\pi)^n \sum_\xi |\hat u(\xi)|^2$ (proved on **[[Thm - Fourier Series of Smooth Functions on the Torus]]**). Some texts instead split the factor as $(2\pi)^{-n/2}$ on both the transform and its inverse to make the transform unitary, and others put no factor on the coefficient and $(2\pi)^{-n}$ on the series. The conversion is a global multiplicative constant on the coefficients and changes none of the spaces $H_k$, none of the norm-equivalences, and none of the qualitative statements; only the numerical constants in the pairing and Plancherel identities move, and every page that states such a constant states it under the present convention.

# Axiom Motivation

We are building the Sobolev scale on a compact manifold, and the general definition — take smooth sections, measure them and their first $k$ covariant derivatives in $L^2$, and complete — is already in hand on **[[Def - Sobolev Space of Sections]]**. That definition is correct but opaque: from it one cannot read off whether a given section lies in $H_k$, one cannot compare different values of $k$ without an inequality argument, and one cannot even make sense of a *negative* number of derivatives, which we will need the moment we ask where the formal adjoint of a differential operator sends things. The purpose of the present construction is to replace the opaque completion by a transparent, computable model on the one manifold where a completely explicit orthogonal basis is available: the flat torus. Everything on a general compact manifold will later be reduced to the torus through charts and a partition of unity on **[[Thm - Sobolev Spaces of Sections on a Compact Manifold via Charts]]**, so getting the torus exactly right is the whole foundation.

Why the torus, and why Fourier series? Because the flat torus is the quotient $\mathbb{R}^n / 2\pi\mathbb{Z}^n$ of a group by a lattice, its geometry is translation-invariant, and the operators we care about — the partial derivatives $\partial_j$ and hence the Laplacian $\Delta = -\sum_j \partial_j^2$ — commute with all translations. A general principle of harmonic analysis is that a commuting family of operators is simultaneously diagonalised by the characters of the symmetry group, and the characters of $T^n$ are exactly the functions $e^{i\langle\xi, x\rangle}$ for $\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n$. On such a character, differentiation acts by a scalar: $\partial_j e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle} = i\xi_j e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle}$, so $\Delta e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle} = |\xi|^2 e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle}$. This is the single fact that makes the torus tractable. If we expand a smooth function $u = \sum_\xi \hat u(\xi) e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle}$ in characters, then applying any partial derivative multiplies the $\xi$-th coefficient by a power of $i\xi$, and measuring $u$ together with $k$ of its derivatives in $L^2$ becomes, after Plancherel, a *weighted sum of the squared coefficients*, the weight being a polynomial of degree $k$ in $|\xi|$. The correct weight is $(1 + |\xi|^2)^k$, and once we adopt it the Sobolev norm is no longer an infimum over completing sequences but a closed-form expression in the Fourier coefficients.

This already fixes the shape of the definition, and it is worth seeing what each ingredient buys and what its omission would cost.

**Why the weight is $(1 + |\xi|^2)^k$ and not $|\xi|^{2k}$.** The naive weight suggested by the computation $\Delta e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle} = |\xi|^2 e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle}$ is $|\xi|^{2k}$: the eigenvalue of $\Delta^k$. But this weight vanishes at the zero frequency $\xi = 0$. For $k > 0$ that would make the norm blind to the constant part of $u$ — the seminorm $\big(\sum_\xi |\xi|^{2k} |\hat u(\xi)|^2\big)^{1/2}$ assigns norm zero to every constant function, so it is not a norm at all and its completion collapses the constants. For $k < 0$ it is worse: the factor $|\xi|^{2k} = |0|^{2k}$ at $\xi = 0$ is $+\infty$, so the constant function $u \equiv 1$, with $\hat u(0) = 1$, would have infinite norm and fail to lie in the space. Replacing $|\xi|^{2k}$ by $(1 + |\xi|^2)^k$ repairs both defects at once: the added $1$ is a strictly positive floor, equal to the eigenvalue of the shifted operator $1 + \Delta$, which is positive-definite and invertible for every $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. The weight $(1 + |\xi|^2)^k$ is comparable to $|\xi|^{2k}$ for large $|\xi|$, so it captures the same high-frequency decay — the same "number of derivatives" — while behaving well at the origin. This is why the Bessel weight, not the pure Laplacian weight, is the right object; the two norms are equivalent on any single space $H_k$ but only the Bessel weight nests the whole scale consistently and admits negative $k$.

**Why the whole integer scale, including $k < 0$.** If we restricted to $k \geq 0$ we would have the spaces of functions with finitely many $L^2$ derivatives, and no more. But a differential operator $L$ of order $\ell$, extended to Sobolev spaces, lowers the order by $\ell$ (differentiation costs derivatives), and its formal adjoint $L^*$ satisfies a pairing identity $\langle Lu, \phi\rangle = \langle u, L^*\phi\rangle$ that only closes up if there is a space *below* $L^2$ for $Lu$ to live in when $u$ is only barely regular. Concretely, the elliptic estimate and the Fredholm theory of the following sections require testing an element of $H_k$ against smooth functions and reading off its regularity from how large the pairing can be; the space of admissible test outputs is exactly $H_{-k}$. Allowing $k$ to be any integer, positive or negative, and pairing $H_k$ against $H_{-k}$, is what turns the collection of Sobolev spaces into a self-dual scale on which differential operators and their adjoints all act. Drop the negative $k$ and the adjoint of $\partial_j$ has nowhere to send an $L^2$ function; keep them, and the calculus is complete.

**Why completion is unavoidable.** One might hope to work directly with the smooth functions and never complete. But the theorems of this chapter — elliptic regularity, Rellich compactness, the Fredholm alternative — are existence statements: they produce a solution as a limit of an approximating sequence, and the limit is generally not smooth. The classical failure is Dirichlet's principle, discussed on **[[Def - Sobolev Space of Sections]]**: a minimising sequence for an energy functional need not converge in any space of classically differentiable functions, and the whole method breaks unless we work in a complete space where the limit is guaranteed to exist. Completion is the price of compactness and of the existence theorems it powers; the merit of the Fourier construction is that on the torus the completion is not an abstract fog but the concrete, fully described weighted sequence space $\ell^2_k$, in which we can point at limits and compute norms.

**A word on the name "distribution."** For $k < 0$ the elements of $H_k(T^n)$ are no longer functions in the ordinary sense — a sequence of Fourier coefficients that grows polynomially, tamed only by the negative-power weight, need not be the coefficient sequence of any pointwise-defined function. It is customary, following Schwartz, to call such an object a **distribution of order $-k$**: it is defined not by its values but by how it pairs against smooth test functions, exactly as the $L^2$ pairing $H_k \times H_{-k} \to \mathbb{C}$ below prescribes. We use the word only as a name; no theory of distributions is invoked or needed anywhere in this series. Everything we do with $H_k$ for negative $k$ is carried out with the explicit weighted-sequence model and the pairing, which are elementary.

Could a reader invent this definition from the four observations above? Yes: the demand for an explicit basis forces the torus and the characters; the demand that differentiation be diagonal forces the weight to be a power of the Laplacian eigenvalue; the demand that constants behave and that negative orders exist forces the shift to $(1 + |\xi|^2)$; and the demand for existence theorems forces completion. What remains is to write it down precisely and prove that the completion really is the weighted sequence space.

# The Definition

We build the definition in five steps: the Fourier coefficients, the norm, the space as a completion, the concrete sequence-space model, and the pairing.

**Step 1: Fourier coefficients.** For a smooth function $u \in C^\infty(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ and a frequency $\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n$, the **$\xi$-th Fourier coefficient** of $u$ is the vector
$$\hat u(\xi) := (2\pi)^{-n} \int_{T^n} u(x)\, e^{-i\langle\xi, x\rangle} \, dx \ \in \ \mathbb{C}^r.$$
The integrand is a smooth $\mathbb{C}^r$-valued function on the compact set $T^n$, so the integral (taken componentwise) exists as an ordinary Riemann integral; $\hat u(\xi)$ is a genuine element of $\mathbb{C}^r$, defined for every $\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n$.

**Step 2: the Sobolev norm.** For an integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $u \in C^\infty(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ we define
$$\|u\|_k^2 := \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n} (1 + |\xi|^2)^k\, |\hat u(\xi)|^2 = \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n} w_k(\xi)\, |\hat u(\xi)|^2 \ \in \ [0, \infty].$$
For $k \leq 0$ the weights $w_k(\xi) \leq 1$ are bounded, and $\|u\|_k^2 \leq \|u\|_0^2 < \infty$ automatically (the last finiteness is Plancherel, Step 5 below); for $k > 0$ the weights grow, but the coefficients of a smooth function decay faster than any power of $|\xi|$ (this rapid decrease is part (i) of **[[Thm - Fourier Series of Smooth Functions on the Torus]]**), so the sum still converges and $\|u\|_k < \infty$ for every smooth $u$ and every integer $k$. Thus $\|\cdot\|_k$ is a well-defined finite nonnegative quantity on $C^\infty(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$.

That $\|\cdot\|_k$ is a **norm** — homogeneous, subadditive, and separating points — is nearly immediate. Homogeneity $\|\lambda u\|_k = |\lambda|\, \|u\|_k$ follows from $\widehat{\lambda u}(\xi) = \lambda \hat u(\xi)$; the triangle inequality is the triangle inequality of the weighted $\ell^2$ norm (Step 4). The one point needing a source is that $\|u\|_k = 0$ forces $u = 0$: since every weight $w_k(\xi)$ is strictly positive, $\|u\|_k = 0$ means $\hat u(\xi) = 0$ for all $\xi$, and a *continuous* function on $T^n$ all of whose Fourier coefficients vanish is identically zero. This uniqueness statement is proved by the Fejér-kernel argument on **[[Thm - Fourier Series of Smooth Functions on the Torus]]** (the uniqueness lemma). We record here that there is no circularity: that proof uses only the coefficients and an approximate identity, never the norm $\|\cdot\|_k$, so it may legitimately be invoked to see that $\|\cdot\|_k$ separates points.

**Step 3: the Sobolev space as a completion.** For each integer $k$ the space
$$H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r) := \text{the completion of } \big(C^\infty(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r),\ \|\cdot\|_k\big)$$
is the completion of the normed space of smooth functions in the norm $\|\cdot\|_k$, in the sense of **[[Def - Cauchy Sequence and Complete Metric Space]]**: its elements are equivalence classes of $\|\cdot\|_k$-Cauchy sequences of smooth functions, two sequences identified when their difference is a $\|\cdot\|_k$-null sequence, with the extended norm. By construction $H_k$ is a Banach space, $C^\infty(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ sits inside it as a dense subspace (in the $\|\cdot\|_k$ topology), and the inclusion is isometric. Because the norm comes from the inner product introduced in Step 5, $H_k$ is in fact a Hilbert space in the sense of **[[Def - Inner Product Space]]**. When $r = 1$ we write $H_k(T^n) := H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C})$.

**Step 4: the weighted-sequence model.** The completion of Step 3 is abstract; the following identification makes it concrete and is the form in which every later computation uses $H_k$. Let
$$\ell^2_k := \Big\{ c = (c_\xi)_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n},\ c_\xi \in \mathbb{C}^r \ :\ \|c\|_{\ell^2_k}^2 := \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n} (1 + |\xi|^2)^k\, |c_\xi|^2 < \infty \Big\}$$
be the **weighted $\ell^2$ space** with the Hermitian inner product
$$\langle c, d\rangle_{\ell^2_k} := \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n} (1 + |\xi|^2)^k\, \langle c_\xi, d_\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^r}.$$

> **Proposition (sequence-space model).** For every integer $k$, the Fourier map $\mathcal{F}: u \mapsto (\hat u(\xi))_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n}$ extends to an isometric isomorphism of Hilbert spaces
> $$\mathcal{F}: H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r) \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } \ell^2_k.$$
> In particular $H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ is canonically identified with the set of coefficient families $(c_\xi)$ satisfying $\sum_\xi (1 + |\xi|^2)^k |c_\xi|^2 < \infty$, the norm being that sum.

> [!note]- Proof of the sequence-space model
> We must show four things: that $\ell^2_k$ is complete; that $\mathcal{F}$ is a norm-preserving linear map of $(C^\infty, \|\cdot\|_k)$ into $\ell^2_k$; that its image is dense in $\ell^2_k$; and that a norm-preserving linear map into a complete space with dense image realises the completion. We take these as labelled steps.
>
> **Step 0 (preconditions): the target inner product is well defined and $\|\cdot\|_{\ell^2_k}$ is a norm.** For $c, d \in \ell^2_k$ the series defining $\langle c, d\rangle_{\ell^2_k}$ converges absolutely: by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on each term, $(1+|\xi|^2)^k |\langle c_\xi, d_\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^r}| \leq (1+|\xi|^2)^k |c_\xi|\,|d_\xi| = \big[(1+|\xi|^2)^{k/2}|c_\xi|\big]\big[(1+|\xi|^2)^{k/2}|d_\xi|\big]$, and summing over $\xi$ and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for ordinary square-summable sequences (**[[Thm - Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality]]**, in the sequence space $\ell^2$) gives $\sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^k |\langle c_\xi, d_\xi\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^r}| \leq \|c\|_{\ell^2_k} \|d\|_{\ell^2_k} < \infty$. Hence $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\ell^2_k}$ is a well-defined Hermitian inner product, sesquilinear and positive-definite (the weights are strictly positive, so $\|c\|_{\ell^2_k} = 0$ forces every $c_\xi = 0$), and $\|\cdot\|_{\ell^2_k}$ is the associated norm.
>
> **Step 1: $\ell^2_k$ is complete.** The map $\Theta: \ell^2_k \to \ell^2(\mathbb{Z}^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ defined by $(\Theta c)_\xi := (1 + |\xi|^2)^{k/2} c_\xi$ is a linear isometry onto the unweighted square-summable sequences, with inverse $(\Theta^{-1} b)_\xi = (1+|\xi|^2)^{-k/2} b_\xi$ (each weight is invertible), because $\|\Theta c\|_{\ell^2}^2 = \sum_\xi |(1+|\xi|^2)^{k/2} c_\xi|^2 = \sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^k |c_\xi|^2 = \|c\|_{\ell^2_k}^2$ (by the definition of the two norms). It therefore suffices to prove that $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z}^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ is complete, since a space isometric to a complete space is complete. Let $(b^{(j)})_{j \geq 1}$ be Cauchy in $\ell^2$. For each fixed $\xi$, $|b^{(j)}_\xi - b^{(l)}_\xi| \leq \|b^{(j)} - b^{(l)}\|_{\ell^2}$ (a single term is dominated by the whole sum), so $(b^{(j)}_\xi)_j$ is Cauchy in the complete space $\mathbb{C}^r$ and converges to some $b_\xi \in \mathbb{C}^r$. We show $b := (b_\xi) \in \ell^2$ and $b^{(j)} \to b$. Fix $\varepsilon > 0$ and choose $J$ with $\|b^{(j)} - b^{(l)}\|_{\ell^2} \leq \varepsilon$ for all $j, l \geq J$. For any finite set $S \subset \mathbb{Z}^n$ and $j, l \geq J$, $\sum_{\xi \in S} |b^{(j)}_\xi - b^{(l)}_\xi|^2 \leq \varepsilon^2$ (a partial sum is at most the full sum); letting $l \to \infty$ (finite sum, so the limit passes inside) gives $\sum_{\xi \in S} |b^{(j)}_\xi - b_\xi|^2 \leq \varepsilon^2$, and taking the supremum over all finite $S$ gives $\|b^{(j)} - b\|_{\ell^2}^2 \leq \varepsilon^2$ for $j \geq J$. In particular $b^{(J)} - b \in \ell^2$, hence $b = b^{(J)} - (b^{(J)} - b) \in \ell^2$, and $\|b^{(j)} - b\|_{\ell^2} \leq \varepsilon$ for $j \geq J$, so $b^{(j)} \to b$. Thus $\ell^2$, and therefore $\ell^2_k$, is complete.
>
> **Step 2: $\mathcal{F}$ is linear and norm-preserving, and its image contains the trigonometric polynomials.** Linearity of $u \mapsto (\hat u(\xi))$ is linearity of the integral. Norm preservation is definitional: $\|\mathcal{F}u\|_{\ell^2_k}^2 = \sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^k |\hat u(\xi)|^2 = \|u\|_k^2$ (the two are the same sum). For the image, we compute the coefficients of a character. **Character orthogonality:** for $\eta, \xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n$,
> $$\widehat{e^{i\langle\eta,\cdot\rangle}}(\xi) = (2\pi)^{-n}\int_{T^n} e^{i\langle\eta,x\rangle} e^{-i\langle\xi,x\rangle}\,dx = (2\pi)^{-n}\prod_{j=1}^n \int_0^{2\pi} e^{i(\eta_j - \xi_j) x_j}\,dx_j \qquad \text{(the exponential factorises over coordinates)}.$$
> Each one-dimensional integral is $\int_0^{2\pi} e^{imt}\,dt = 2\pi$ if $m = 0$, and $\int_0^{2\pi} e^{imt}\,dt = \big[\tfrac{1}{im} e^{imt}\big]_0^{2\pi} = 0$ if $m \in \mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}$ (since $e^{2\pi i m} = 1$, by periodicity of the exponential). Hence the product is $(2\pi)^n$ exactly when $\eta_j = \xi_j$ for every $j$, and $0$ otherwise, so $\widehat{e^{i\langle\eta,\cdot\rangle}}(\xi) = \delta_{\eta\xi}$. Consequently, for any finitely supported family $(c_\xi)_{\xi \in S}$ (with $S \subset \mathbb{Z}^n$ finite and $c_\xi \in \mathbb{C}^r$), the trigonometric polynomial $p(x) := \sum_{\xi \in S} c_\xi\, e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle}$ is smooth and satisfies $\hat p(\eta) = c_\eta$ for $\eta \in S$ and $\hat p(\eta) = 0$ otherwise (by linearity and the character computation). Thus $\mathcal{F}$ maps the smooth functions *onto* every finitely supported sequence.
>
> **Step 3: finitely supported sequences are dense in $\ell^2_k$.** Let $c \in \ell^2_k$ and $\varepsilon > 0$. For $R > 0$ let $c^R$ be the truncation with $c^R_\xi = c_\xi$ for $|\xi| \leq R$ and $c^R_\xi = 0$ otherwise; each $c^R$ is finitely supported. Then $\|c - c^R\|_{\ell^2_k}^2 = \sum_{|\xi| > R} (1+|\xi|^2)^k |c_\xi|^2$, which is the tail of the convergent series $\sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^k |c_\xi|^2 = \|c\|_{\ell^2_k}^2 < \infty$, hence tends to $0$ as $R \to \infty$ (the tail of a convergent series of nonnegative terms). Choose $R$ so that this is below $\varepsilon^2$; then $\|c - c^R\|_{\ell^2_k} < \varepsilon$. So the finitely supported sequences are dense in $\ell^2_k$.
>
> **Step 4: a norm-preserving map into a complete space with dense image realises the completion.** By Step 2, $\mathcal{F}: (C^\infty(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r), \|\cdot\|_k) \to \ell^2_k$ is a linear isometry (norm-preserving, hence injective because $\|\cdot\|_k$ separates points by Step 2 of the main text); by Steps 2–3 its image contains the finitely supported sequences, which are dense in $\ell^2_k$; and by Step 1, $\ell^2_k$ is complete. It remains only to invoke the following general fact, which we prove for completeness.
>
> > [!note]- Lemma (concrete completions): an isometry into a Banach space with dense image is a completion
> > **Statement.** Let $(V, \|\cdot\|_V)$ be a normed space and $\iota: V \to W$ a linear isometry into a Banach space $(W, \|\cdot\|_W)$, meaning $\|\iota v\|_W = \|v\|_V$ for all $v \in V$, with $\iota(V)$ dense in $W$. Then $(W, \iota)$ is a completion of $V$: the abstract completion $\widehat V$ of **[[Def - Cauchy Sequence and Complete Metric Space]]** is isometrically isomorphic to $W$ by the unique continuous linear extension of $\iota$.
> >
> > **Full proof.** The abstract completion $\widehat V$ is a Banach space containing (an isometric copy of) $V$ as a dense subspace, its elements being equivalence classes of $\|\cdot\|_V$-Cauchy sequences in $V$ modulo null sequences, with the extended norm; this is the construction on **[[Def - Cauchy Sequence and Complete Metric Space]]**. We extend $\iota$ to $\widehat V$. Given $\hat v \in \widehat V$, represent it by a Cauchy sequence $(v_m)$ in $V$; then $(\iota v_m)$ is Cauchy in $W$ because $\|\iota v_m - \iota v_l\|_W = \|v_m - v_l\|_V$ (isometry) and $(v_m)$ is Cauchy, so $\iota v_m$ converges in the complete space $W$ to a limit we call $\widehat\iota(\hat v)$. This limit is independent of the representative: if $(v_m')$ is another representative then $\|v_m - v_m'\|_V \to 0$, hence $\|\iota v_m - \iota v_m'\|_W \to 0$ (isometry), so the two limits coincide. The map $\widehat\iota: \widehat V \to W$ so defined is linear (limits of linear combinations) and isometric: $\|\widehat\iota(\hat v)\|_W = \lim_m \|\iota v_m\|_W = \lim_m \|v_m\|_V = \|\hat v\|_{\widehat V}$ (norm is continuous; the last equality is the definition of the extended norm). An isometry is injective (if $\|\widehat\iota(\hat v)\|_W = 0$ then $\|\hat v\|_{\widehat V} = 0$, so $\hat v = 0$). For surjectivity: the image $\widehat\iota(\widehat V)$ is complete, being the isometric image of the complete space $\widehat V$, hence closed in $W$; it contains $\iota(V)$, which is dense in $W$; a closed set containing a dense set is all of $W$. Therefore $\widehat\iota$ is a bijective linear isometry, i.e. an isometric isomorphism $\widehat V \cong W$. $\blacksquare$
>
> **Conclusion.** Applying the lemma with $V = (C^\infty(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r), \|\cdot\|_k)$, $W = \ell^2_k$, and $\iota = \mathcal{F}$, whose hypotheses were verified in Steps 1–3, the completion $H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ of $V$ is isometrically isomorphic to $\ell^2_k$ by the continuous extension of $\mathcal{F}$. This is the asserted isomorphism, and it identifies $H_k$ with the family of coefficient sequences of finite $\ell^2_k$ norm. $\blacksquare$

From now on we treat $H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ and $\ell^2_k$ as the same object, writing $\hat u(\xi)$ for the $\xi$-th coordinate of an element $u \in H_k$ (defined for every $u$, smooth or not) and $\|u\|_k^2 = \sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^k |\hat u(\xi)|^2$ for its norm.

**Step 5: the $L^2$ inner product and the $H_k$–$H_{-k}$ pairing.** On $H_0(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r) = \ell^2_0 = \ell^2(\mathbb{Z}^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ the norm comes from the inner product
$$\langle u, v\rangle := (2\pi)^n \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n} \langle\hat u(\xi), \hat v(\xi)\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^r} = (2\pi)^n \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n} \sum_{a=1}^r \hat u(\xi)_a\, \overline{\hat v(\xi)_a},$$
called the **$L^2$ inner product** because on smooth functions it equals $\int_{T^n} \langle u(x), v(x)\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^r}\, dx$ by the Plancherel identity, part (iii) of **[[Thm - Fourier Series of Smooth Functions on the Torus]]**. This is the source of the identity $H_0(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r) = L^2(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ with $\|u\|_{L^2}^2 = (2\pi)^n \|u\|_0^2$, proved on that page; we do not prove it here but record the normalisation.

More generally, for any integer $k$ we define the **$L^2$ pairing** between $H_k$ and $H_{-k}$ by the same formula without the conjugate, extended bilinearly:
$$\langle u, v\rangle := (2\pi)^n \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n} \langle\hat u(\xi), \overline{\hat v(\xi)}\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^r}^{\text{bil}}, \qquad \text{where } \langle a, b\rangle^{\text{bil}} := \sum_{a'} a_{a'} b_{a'} \text{ is the bilinear pairing on } \mathbb{C}^r,$$
so that on real-valued data it reduces to the previous inner product. Its fundamental property is the following estimate, which is exactly what makes $H_{-k}$ the dual of $H_k$.

> **Proposition (pairing bound).** For $u \in H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ and $v \in H_{-k}(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ the pairing series converges absolutely and
> $$\big|\langle u, v\rangle\big| \ \leq\ (2\pi)^n\, \|u\|_k\, \|v\|_{-k}.$$

> [!note]- Proof of the pairing bound
> **Goal.** We show the series $\sum_\xi \langle\hat u(\xi), \overline{\hat v(\xi)}\rangle^{\text{bil}}$ converges absolutely with the stated bound; absolute convergence then makes the definition of $\langle u, v\rangle$ unambiguous.
>
> **Estimate term by term and factor the weight.** For each $\xi$, the elementary Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on $\mathbb{C}^r$ gives $\big|\langle\hat u(\xi), \overline{\hat v(\xi)}\rangle^{\text{bil}}\big| \leq |\hat u(\xi)|\,|\hat v(\xi)|$ (the bilinear pairing is bounded by the product of Euclidean norms, since $|\sum_{a'} a_{a'} b_{a'}| \leq |a|\,|b|$). Now insert the weight and its inverse, using $w_k(\xi)^{1/2} w_{-k}(\xi)^{1/2} = 1$ (valid since $w_k(\xi) > 0$ and $w_k w_{-k} = w_0 = 1$):
> $$|\hat u(\xi)|\,|\hat v(\xi)| = \Big[(1+|\xi|^2)^{k/2}\,|\hat u(\xi)|\Big]\cdot\Big[(1+|\xi|^2)^{-k/2}\,|\hat v(\xi)|\Big] \qquad \text{(multiply and divide by } (1+|\xi|^2)^{k/2}\text{)}.$$
>
> **Sum by Cauchy–Schwarz in $\ell^2$.** Summing over $\xi$ and applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for square-summable sequences (**[[Thm - Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality]]**) to the two bracketed families,
> $$\sum_\xi |\hat u(\xi)|\,|\hat v(\xi)| \ \leq\ \Big(\sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^k |\hat u(\xi)|^2\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^{-k} |\hat v(\xi)|^2\Big)^{1/2} = \|u\|_k\,\|v\|_{-k} \qquad \text{(definition of } \|\cdot\|_k, \|\cdot\|_{-k}\text{)}.$$
> Both factors are finite because $u \in H_k$ and $v \in H_{-k}$, so the sum $\sum_\xi |\hat u(\xi)|\,|\hat v(\xi)|$ converges, giving absolute convergence of the pairing series. Multiplying by $(2\pi)^n$ and combining with the term-by-term estimate yields $|\langle u, v\rangle| \leq (2\pi)^n \sum_\xi |\hat u(\xi)|\,|\hat v(\xi)| \leq (2\pi)^n \|u\|_k \|v\|_{-k}$.
>
> **Conclusion.** The pairing $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle: H_k \times H_{-k} \to \mathbb{C}$ is well defined and bounded, with operator bound $(2\pi)^n$. Therefore, for each fixed $u \in H_k$, the map $v \mapsto \langle u, v\rangle$ is a bounded linear functional on $H_{-k}$; that this correspondence is an isomorphism $H_{-k} \cong (H_k)^*$ is proved on **[[Thm - Sobolev Norms on the Torus via Fourier Coefficients]]**. $\blacksquare$

**Step 6: the real-valued subspace.** For $\mathbb{C}^r$-valued data with a real structure — in the applications $r = 1$ and we want real functions, or $E$ is a real bundle complexified — the **real Sobolev space** $H_k(T^n; \mathbb{R}^r)$ is the closure in $H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ of the real-valued smooth functions $C^\infty(T^n; \mathbb{R}^r)$. In coefficient terms it is
$$H_k(T^n; \mathbb{R}^r) = \big\{ u \in H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r) : \hat u(-\xi) = \overline{\hat u(\xi)} \text{ for all } \xi \big\},$$
the reality condition being exactly the statement that $u(x) = \overline{u(x)}$: a smooth function is real-valued if and only if its Fourier coefficients satisfy $\hat u(-\xi) = \overline{\hat u(\xi)}$, because conjugating $\hat u(\xi) = (2\pi)^{-n}\int u\, e^{-i\langle\xi,x\rangle} dx$ gives $\overline{\hat u(\xi)} = (2\pi)^{-n}\int \overline u\, e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle} dx = \widehat{\overline u}(-\xi)$, so $\overline u = u$ if and only if $\widehat{\overline u}(-\xi) = \hat u(-\xi)$, i.e. $\overline{\hat u(\xi)} = \hat u(-\xi)$; this condition is closed under the $\|\cdot\|_k$-limit, so it characterises the closure. On $H_k(T^n; \mathbb{R}^r)$ the pairing takes real values.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The construction has a clean structural reading that explains why it is canonical rather than a matter of taste, and it is worth stating because the same universal property reappears every time a Sobolev space is built on a manifold by charts.

**$H_k$ as a completion, characterised by its universal property.** A completion of a normed space $V$ is not merely *a* complete space containing $V$ densely; it is characterised up to unique isometric isomorphism by the universal property that every bounded linear map from $V$ into a Banach space $W$ factors uniquely through it. Concretely, $H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ is the unique (up to unique isometry) Banach space equipped with an isometric dense inclusion of $(C^\infty, \|\cdot\|_k)$ such that any $\|\cdot\|_k$-bounded linear map out of $C^\infty$ extends uniquely and continuously to it. The Lemma on concrete completions proved above is precisely the statement that $(\ell^2_k, \mathcal{F})$ satisfies this universal property; that is what licenses us to *identify* the abstract completion with the concrete sequence space rather than merely comparing them. Every later reduction — from the torus to a compact manifold on **[[Thm - Sobolev Spaces of Sections on a Compact Manifold via Charts]]** — is an instance of the same principle: one exhibits a concrete space with an isometric dense image and reads off that it is the completion.

**The scale as a self-dual filtered family.** The integers index a decreasing chain of continuous inclusions
$$\cdots \hookrightarrow H_{k+1} \hookrightarrow H_k \hookrightarrow H_{k-1} \hookrightarrow \cdots, \qquad \|u\|_{k-1} \leq \|u\|_k,$$
where the norm inequality is immediate from $w_{k-1}(\xi) = (1+|\xi|^2)^{-1} w_k(\xi) \leq w_k(\xi)$ termwise (proved in full on **[[Thm - Sobolev Norms on the Torus via Fourier Coefficients]]**). The $L^2$ pairing turns this filtered family into a self-dual system: the pairing bound above exhibits $H_{-k}$ as embedded in the continuous dual $(H_k)^*$, and the surjectivity of that embedding (the perfect-pairing statement) makes $H_{-k} = (H_k)^*$ with $H_0 = L^2$ self-dual in the middle. Structurally, then, the Sobolev scale on the torus is the family $\{\ell^2_k\}_{k \in \mathbb{Z}}$ of weightings of one fixed Hilbert space $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z}^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ by the powers of the single positive multiplier $\xi \mapsto (1 + |\xi|^2)$, with the multiplier's inverse implementing the duality. This is the cleanest description of what "a Sobolev space" is on the torus: a re-weighting of $\ell^2$ by a power of the shifted Laplacian's spectrum.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** Stripped of the geometric packaging, $H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ is the domain of the operator $(1 + \Delta)^{k/2}$ acting on $L^2(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$, and $\|u\|_k = (2\pi)^{-n/2}\|(1+\Delta)^{k/2} u\|_{L^2}$ up to the fixed normalising constant. The operator $(1+\Delta)^{k/2}$ is defined by its action on characters, $(1+\Delta)^{k/2} e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle} = (1+|\xi|^2)^{k/2} e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle}$ (functional calculus of the self-adjoint operator $\Delta$, whose spectrum on the torus is $\{|\xi|^2 : \xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n\}$), and it is exactly the multiplier $\Theta$ of the completeness proof. So the operational content of "$u$ has $k$ derivatives in $L^2$" is "$(1+\Delta)^{k/2} u$ is in $L^2$", and the whole scale is one operator raised to varying powers. This is the point of view from which pseudodifferential operators generalise the construction, and it is why the Sobolev scale interpolates so cleanly.

**Connection to harmonic analysis and the Peter–Weyl theorem.** The characters $\{e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle} : \xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n\}$ are the irreducible unitary representations of the abelian compact Lie group $T^n$, and their completeness in $L^2(T^n)$ is the Peter–Weyl theorem specialised to a torus. The Sobolev construction is thus the analytic refinement of Peter–Weyl: not only do the characters span $L^2$, but weighting the expansion by powers of the representation-theoretic Laplacian eigenvalue produces the entire scale of smoothness spaces. On a general compact Lie group the same weighting by the Casimir eigenvalue produces Sobolev spaces adapted to the group; the torus is the abelian, and hence fully explicit, model.

**Connection to signal processing and to the theory of distributions.** For $k > 0$, membership in $H_k$ is a decay condition on the Fourier coefficients — smoothness is high-frequency decay, exactly as in the engineering statement that a smooth signal has a rapidly decaying spectrum. For $k < 0$, the elements are the periodic distributions of finite order: a coefficient sequence of at most polynomial growth of degree $< -k - n/2$ (so that the weighted sum converges) that acts on test functions by the pairing. The Dirac comb $\sum_\xi e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle}$, the coefficient sequence identically $1$, is the periodisation of the Dirac delta and is the canonical negative-order element; the Example below computes exactly which $H_k$ it lands in. All of this is available here without any distribution theory because the pairing and the sequence model make the "distribution" nothing more than a weighted-$\ell^2$ sequence.

**Compression.** The definition compresses to one sentence: *$H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$ is the Hilbert space $\ell^2$ of Fourier coefficient sequences re-weighted by $(1+|\xi|^2)^k$, and the $L^2$ pairing makes $H_{-k}$ its dual.* Everything else — the completion, the norm, the negative orders, the duality — is unpacked from that.

# Examples / Corollaries

Every claim below is verified from the definition and the sequence-space model; the only external inputs are the elementary Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the lattice-sum threshold **[[Thm - Convergence of the Lattice Sum]]**, restated where used.

**Is an instance — a single character.** Fix $\xi_0 \in \mathbb{Z}^n$ and let $u(x) = e^{i\langle\xi_0, x\rangle}$. By the character-orthogonality computation of Step 2, $\hat u(\xi) = \delta_{\xi_0 \xi}$, a sequence supported at the single frequency $\xi_0$ with value $1$ there. Hence for every integer $k$,
$$\|u\|_k^2 = \sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^k \,|\delta_{\xi_0\xi}|^2 = (1+|\xi_0|^2)^k, \qquad \text{so} \qquad \|u\|_k = (1+|\xi_0|^2)^{k/2}.$$
This is finite for every $k$, so $u \in H_k(T^n)$ for all $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ (as every character is smooth, this is expected). The computation is worth keeping: it shows that a single character sits at "height" $(1+|\xi_0|^2)^{k/2}$ in the $H_k$ norm, growing in $k$ like a power of its frequency, which is the quantitative form of "higher frequencies cost more derivatives."

**Is an instance / non-instance depending on $k$ — the Dirac comb.** Let $u = \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n} e^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle}$, understood as the element of the sequence model whose coefficients are $\hat u(\xi) = 1$ for every $\xi$ (this is not the Fourier series of any function, but it is a perfectly good coefficient sequence, and the question is which $H_k$ it lies in). Its norm is
$$\|u\|_k^2 = \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n} (1+|\xi|^2)^k \cdot 1 = \sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n} (1+|\xi|^2)^k.$$
Write this as $\sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^{-t}$ with $t = -k$. By the lattice-sum threshold — *for $t \in \mathbb{R}$, $\sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n}(1+|\xi|^2)^{-t} < \infty$ if and only if $2t > n$* (**[[Thm - Convergence of the Lattice Sum]]**) — the sum converges if and only if $2(-k) > n$, that is,
$$\text{the Dirac comb lies in } H_k(T^n) \iff 2k < -n.$$
So in dimension $n = 1$ it lies in $H_k$ exactly for $k \leq -1$ (since $2k < -1$ and $k$ integer means $k \leq -1$); in dimension $n = 4$ exactly for $k \leq -3$. It lies in no space of nonnegative order, confirming that it is a genuinely negative-order object — a distribution, the periodised delta, of order $-k \geq \lceil (n+1)/2\rceil$. This is the sharp example separating the levels of the scale.

**Is NOT an instance — a unit-modulus coefficient sequence in any nonnegative space.** Let $(c_\xi)_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^n}$ be any family with $|c_\xi| = 1$ for all $\xi$ — the Dirac comb is the special case $c_\xi \equiv 1$. For an integer $k$ with $k \geq -n/2$,
$$\|(c_\xi)\|_k^2 = \sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^k |c_\xi|^2 = \sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^k = \sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^{-(-k)},$$
and here $-k \leq n/2$, so $2(-k) \leq n$, so by the lattice-sum threshold (the "only if" direction, restated above) the series diverges. Hence $(c_\xi) \notin H_k(T^n)$ for every integer $k \geq -n/2$: a coefficient sequence that does not decay at all fails to define an element of any Sobolev space of order at least $-n/2$. The failure is sharp in the sense that decay is exactly what the weight cannot supply on its own — the space only tolerates non-decaying coefficients once the weight $(1+|\xi|^2)^k$ decays fast enough, which needs $k < -n/2$.

**Corollary — the inclusion $H_k \subset H_m$ for $k \geq m$, quantitatively.** For integers $k \geq m$ and any $u \in H_k(T^n; \mathbb{C}^r)$, since $(1+|\xi|^2)^m \leq (1+|\xi|^2)^k$ termwise (the base is $\geq 1$ and the exponent is smaller),
$$\|u\|_m^2 = \sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^m |\hat u(\xi)|^2 \leq \sum_\xi (1+|\xi|^2)^k |\hat u(\xi)|^2 = \|u\|_k^2 \qquad \text{(termwise weight comparison)},$$
so $u \in H_m$ and $\|u\|_m \leq \|u\|_k$. Thus the identity on smooth functions extends to a norm-nonincreasing inclusion $H_k \hookrightarrow H_m$. (The much deeper fact that this inclusion is *compact* for $k > m$ is the Rellich theorem of the next section, and is not claimed here.)

**Corollary — the norm controls each individual coefficient.** For any $u \in H_k$ and any single frequency $\eta$, keeping only the $\eta$-term in the defining sum gives $(1+|\eta|^2)^k |\hat u(\eta)|^2 \leq \|u\|_k^2$, hence
$$|\hat u(\eta)| \leq (1+|\eta|^2)^{-k/2}\, \|u\|_k.$$
In particular the coefficient map $u \mapsto \hat u(\eta)$ is a bounded linear functional on $H_k$ for every $\eta$ (of norm $\leq (1+|\eta|^2)^{-k/2}$), so convergence in $H_k$ implies coefficientwise convergence. This is the elementary reason the sequence model is legitimate: two elements of $H_k$ with the same coefficients are equal.

**Calibration check.** First, in dimension $n = 1$, verify that $\|e^{i m\theta}\|_k = (1 + m^2)^{k/2}$ for $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ from the single-character computation, and that consequently $\|\cos\theta\|_0^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 + 1)^0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(1+1)^0 = 1$ once $\cos\theta = \tfrac12(e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta})$ is expanded (its two coefficients are $\tfrac12$ at $\pm 1$, giving $\|\cos\theta\|_0^2 = |\tfrac12|^2 + |\tfrac12|^2 = \tfrac12$; here $\|u\|_0^2 = \tfrac12$ corresponds to $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2 = \pi = (2\pi)\cdot\tfrac12$, matching $\|u\|_{L^2}^2 = 2\pi\|u\|_0^2$). Second, confirm from the threshold that in $n = 2$ the Dirac comb lies in $H_k$ if and only if $k \leq -2$ (since $2k < -2$ means $k < -1$, so $k \leq -2$), and that it therefore fails to lie in $H_{-1}(T^2)$: indeed $\sum_{\xi \in \mathbb{Z}^2}(1+|\xi|^2)^{-1}$ diverges, being comparable to $\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}(1+|x|^2)^{-1} dx = \infty$. Third, check that the pairing bound is consistent with self-duality at $k = 0$: for $u = v$ real-valued it reads $\langle u, u\rangle = (2\pi)^n\|u\|_0^2 = \|u\|_{L^2}^2 \leq (2\pi)^n \|u\|_0 \|u\|_0$, an equality, so the constant $(2\pi)^n$ is sharp.
