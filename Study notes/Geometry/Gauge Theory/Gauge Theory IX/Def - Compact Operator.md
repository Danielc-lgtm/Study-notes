# Notation

Throughout, $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ are [[Def - Cauchy Sequence and Complete Metric Space|Banach spaces]] over the scalar field $\mathbb{F}$, which is either $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$; a Banach space is a normed vector space that is complete as a [[Def - Metric Space|metric space]] under the distance $d(u,v) = \lVert u - v \rVert$ induced by its norm. We write $\lVert \cdot \rVert_X$ for the norm of $X$, dropping the subscript when the space is clear. The **closed unit ball** of $X$ is
$$B_X := \{x \in X : \lVert x \rVert_X \le 1\},$$
a fixed set for the whole page. A map $T : X \to Y$ is a **bounded linear operator** if it is linear and $\lVert T \rVert := \sup_{x \in B_X} \lVert Tx \rVert_Y < \infty$; the number $\lVert T \rVert$ is its **operator norm**, and $\mathcal{B}(X,Y)$ denotes the vector space of all bounded linear operators $X \to Y$, itself a Banach space under the operator norm. For a linear operator, boundedness is equivalent to continuity, and $\lVert Tx \rVert \le \lVert T \rVert \, \lVert x \rVert$ for all $x$.

For a subset $S \subseteq Y$ we write $\overline{S}$ for its closure. We call $S$ **relatively compact** (or precompact) if $\overline{S}$ is a [[Def - Compact Space|compact space]] in the subspace topology, and **[[Def - Totally Bounded Metric Space|totally bounded]]** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a finite set $\{y_1, \dots, y_N\} \subseteq Y$ — a *finite $\varepsilon$-net* — with $S \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^N \{y : \lVert y - y_i \rVert < \varepsilon\}$. The identity operator on $X$ is $\mathrm{Id}_X$. In the examples we use the Sobolev spaces $H_k(T^n)$ on the flat torus $T^n = \mathbb{R}^n / 2\pi\mathbb{Z}^n$ from [[Def - Sobolev Space of Sections]] and the sequence space $\ell^2 = \{(x_j)_{j \ge 1} : \sum_j |x_j|^2 < \infty\}$ with $\lVert x \rVert^2 = \sum_j |x_j|^2$ and standard basis $(e_j)_{j\ge 1}$, $e_j$ having a $1$ in slot $j$ and zeros elsewhere.

> [!warning] Convention: which balls, and the standing setting
> Some authors define compactness of $T$ through the *open* unit ball, or through an arbitrary bounded set; because $T$ is linear these all coincide, as we prove in the Definition. Haydys states the compactness of the Sobolev embedding (Theorem 136(ii), item A-T5.1.1(ii)) and works with bounded linear maps between Banach spaces in the definition of a Fredholm operator (Definition 150, item A-D5.2.6); the present page isolates the notion of a compact operator that both of those use. Our Banach spaces are always complete by definition, which is what makes the "totally bounded" and "relatively compact" formulations interchangeable.

# Axiom Motivation

The difficulty that this definition exists to repair is the failure of the Bolzano–Weierstrass property in infinite dimensions. In a finite-dimensional normed space every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence, and this single fact powers almost all of finite-dimensional analysis: minima of continuous functions are attained, closed bounded sets are compact, linear operators between finite-dimensional spaces have kernels and cokernels whose dimensions add up correctly. In an infinite-dimensional Banach space this property is simply gone. The standard basis $(e_j)_{j \ge 1}$ of $\ell^2$ is bounded — every $e_j$ has norm $1$ — yet $\lVert e_j - e_k \rVert = \sqrt{2}$ for $j \ne k$, so no subsequence is even Cauchy. The closed unit ball, the most basic bounded set there is, is not compact. We would like to identify the operators that *restore* this lost compactness: the operators $K$ for which, no matter how badly the domain fails Bolzano–Weierstrass, the images $Kx_j$ of a bounded sequence do have a convergent subsequence. That is exactly what a compact operator is, and it is the class of operators for which the infinite-dimensional theory recovers the finite-dimensional bookkeeping — most importantly the Fredholm property, finite-dimensional kernels and cokernels, that A-D5.2.6 defines and that elliptic operators possess.

The desideratum, then, is a property of a bounded operator $K : X \to Y$ that (i) is automatic for every operator whose range is finite-dimensional, since those land inside a space where Bolzano–Weierstrass holds; (ii) is stable under the operations we will actually perform — sums, composition on either side with bounded operators, and operator-norm limits — so that the class is a usable ideal rather than an ad hoc list; and (iii) is strictly stronger than mere boundedness, so that the identity on an infinite-dimensional space is excluded, for otherwise the class would contain everything and prove nothing. The definition "$K(B_X)$ is relatively compact" meets all three, and each of its clauses earns its place.

Consider first the clause **relatively compact**, meaning we ask $\overline{K(B_X)}$ to be compact rather than $K(B_X)$ itself. If we demanded that $K(B_X)$ be compact — closed as well as covering-compact — we would exclude most of the operators we want. The image of a ball under a bounded operator is essentially never closed: take the diagonal operator $K$ on $\ell^2$ with $K e_j = j^{-1} e_j$. The vectors $Ke_j = j^{-1}e_j$ all lie in $K(B_{\ell^2})$ and converge to $0$, and $0 = K \cdot 0 \in K(B_{\ell^2})$, so this particular limit is retained; but a generic bounded operator sends the ball to a set whose closure adds genuinely new boundary points, and insisting on a closed image would make the class fragile and small. Taking the closure first is the minimal repair: it asks only that the image be *within bounded reach* of a compact set, which is precisely what the subsequence-extraction property needs. Drop the closure, and the operator with $\lambda_j = j^{-1}$ would fail a test it morally passes.

Consider next the clause **the unit ball** $B_X$, rather than an arbitrary bounded set. This is a genuine economy that linearity makes free, and it is worth seeing why, because it also tells us the clause cannot be weakened to a smaller set. If $S \subseteq X$ is any bounded set, say $S \subseteq \{x : \lVert x \rVert \le M\}$, then $S \subseteq M \cdot B_X$, so $K(S) \subseteq M \cdot K(B_X)$; scaling by the fixed constant $M$ is a homeomorphism of $Y$, so it carries the relatively compact set $\overline{K(B_X)}$ to the relatively compact set $\overline{M \cdot K(B_X)} = M \cdot \overline{K(B_X)}$, and $K(S)$, sitting inside a relatively compact set, is itself relatively compact. Thus "the image of *every* bounded set is relatively compact" is equivalent to "the image of the *unit* ball is relatively compact"; the unit ball is the cheapest single test set that certifies all the others. We could not, on the other hand, test only a *subspace* or a *finite-dimensional slice* of the ball: a rank-one perturbation of a non-compact operator would slip through such a weakened test while still failing to restore compactness.

Consider finally the clause **bounded** (equivalently continuous) in the standing hypothesis on $K$. For a linear map this is very nearly implied by the compactness clause itself: if $\overline{K(B_X)}$ is compact then it is bounded (a compact subset of a metric space is bounded, being covered by finitely many balls of radius $1$), so $\sup_{x \in B_X}\lVert Kx \rVert < \infty$, which is exactly $\lVert K \rVert < \infty$. We nonetheless keep boundedness in the hypotheses because the natural objects the theory produces — differential operators viewed between the wrong pair of Sobolev spaces, for instance — are not automatically defined on all of $X$, and stating boundedness up front fixes the domain and the register. The lesson of the per-clause analysis is that compactness is the Goldilocks strengthening of boundedness: weaker (mere boundedness) and the identity leaks in, wrecking Fredholm theory; a naive strengthening (closed image, or a smaller test set) and the class becomes too small or too brittle to be an operator ideal. A reader who has internalised "restore Bolzano–Weierstrass, and take the closure so the class is robust, and test on the unit ball because linearity lets you" could write the definition unaided.

# The Definition

Let $X$ and $Y$ be Banach spaces. A bounded linear operator $K : X \to Y$ is a **compact operator** if the image of the closed unit ball is relatively compact:
$$\overline{K(B_X)} \ \text{is a compact subset of } Y.$$
We write $\mathcal{K}(X,Y) \subseteq \mathcal{B}(X,Y)$ for the set of compact operators, and $\mathcal{K}(X) = \mathcal{K}(X,X)$.

The following three statements are equivalent for a bounded linear operator $K : X \to Y$ between Banach spaces, and any one of them may be taken as the definition:

1. **(Relative compactness.)** $\overline{K(B_X)}$ is compact in $Y$.
2. **(Total boundedness.)** $K(B_X)$ is a totally bounded subset of $Y$.
3. **(Sequential criterion.)** Every bounded sequence $(x_j)_{j\ge 1}$ in $X$ has a subsequence $(x_{j_i})_i$ for which $(Kx_{j_i})_i$ converges in $Y$.

The sequential criterion (3) is the form used in practice — it is how compactness is checked and how it is applied — while (1) is the cleanest to state and (2) is the one that makes the finite-rank approximation arguments run. We prove the equivalences now; each uses the metric-space compactness theorem below, restated at its point of use.

> [!note]- Proof that (1), (2), and (3) are equivalent
> We invoke the following result, whose complete proof is on its own page.
> > **Theorem (compactness in metric spaces, three equivalents).** For a [[Def - Metric Space|metric space]] $(M,d)$ the following are equivalent: $M$ is [[Def - Compact Space|compact]]; $M$ is sequentially compact (every sequence has a convergent subsequence); $M$ is complete and [[Def - Totally Bounded Metric Space|totally bounded]]. See [[Thm - Compactness in Metric Spaces (Three Equivalents)]].
>
> We prove (1) $\Leftrightarrow$ (2) and then (1) $\Leftrightarrow$ (3). Write $S := K(B_X) \subseteq Y$ throughout.
>
> **Direction (1) $\Rightarrow$ (2).** Assume $\overline{S}$ is compact. A compact metric space is totally bounded (by the three-equivalents theorem, compact implies complete and totally bounded), so $\overline{S}$ is totally bounded. Since $S \subseteq \overline{S}$, any finite $\varepsilon$-net of $\overline{S}$ is in particular a finite $\varepsilon$-net of $S$ (every point of $S$ is a point of $\overline{S}$, hence within $\varepsilon$ of a net point). Therefore $S$ is totally bounded, which is (2).
>
> **Direction (2) $\Rightarrow$ (1).** Assume $S$ is totally bounded. We first show its closure $\overline{S}$ is totally bounded. Fix $\varepsilon > 0$ and choose (by total boundedness of $S$) a finite set $\{y_1, \dots, y_N\} \subseteq Y$ with $S \subseteq \bigcup_i \{y : \lVert y - y_i\rVert < \varepsilon/2\}$. Let $z \in \overline{S}$. Then there is $s \in S$ with $\lVert z - s \rVert < \varepsilon/2$ (definition of closure), and there is an index $i$ with $\lVert s - y_i \rVert < \varepsilon/2$ (the net covers $S$); by the triangle inequality $\lVert z - y_i \rVert \le \lVert z - s\rVert + \lVert s - y_i \rVert < \varepsilon$. Hence $\{y_1,\dots,y_N\}$ is a finite $\varepsilon$-net for $\overline{S}$, so $\overline{S}$ is totally bounded. Next, $\overline{S}$ is a closed subset of the complete space $Y$, hence complete (a closed subset of a complete metric space is complete: a Cauchy sequence in $\overline{S}$ is Cauchy in $Y$, so it converges in $Y$, and its limit lies in $\overline{S}$ because $\overline{S}$ is closed). Being complete and totally bounded, $\overline{S}$ is compact by the three-equivalents theorem. This is (1).
>
> **Direction (1) $\Rightarrow$ (3).** Assume $\overline{S}$ is compact, and let $(x_j)$ be a bounded sequence in $X$, say $\lVert x_j \rVert \le M$ for all $j$. If $M = 0$ then every $x_j = 0$ and $(Kx_j)$ is the constant sequence $0$, which converges; so assume $M > 0$. Then $x_j / M \in B_X$, so $K(x_j/M) \in S \subseteq \overline{S}$ for every $j$. By the three-equivalents theorem the compact space $\overline{S}$ is sequentially compact, so the sequence $\big(K(x_j/M)\big)_j$ in $\overline{S}$ has a subsequence $\big(K(x_{j_i}/M)\big)_i$ converging to some $w \in \overline{S}$. Multiplying by the fixed scalar $M$ (scalar multiplication is continuous), $Kx_{j_i} = M\,K(x_{j_i}/M) \to Mw$ in $Y$. Thus $(Kx_{j_i})_i$ converges, which is (3).
>
> **Direction (3) $\Rightarrow$ (1).** Assume the sequential criterion, and let $(z_j)_j$ be any sequence in $\overline{S}$; we produce a convergent subsequence, which shows $\overline{S}$ is sequentially compact and hence (three-equivalents theorem) compact. For each $j$, since $z_j \in \overline{S} = \overline{K(B_X)}$, choose $x_j \in B_X$ with $\lVert z_j - Kx_j \rVert < 1/j$ (a point of the closure is a limit of points of the set). The sequence $(x_j)$ is bounded, $\lVert x_j \rVert \le 1$, so by (3) there is a subsequence $(x_{j_i})_i$ with $Kx_{j_i} \to w$ in $Y$ for some $w$. Then
> $$\lVert z_{j_i} - w \rVert \le \lVert z_{j_i} - Kx_{j_i} \rVert + \lVert Kx_{j_i} - w \rVert < \tfrac{1}{j_i} + \lVert Kx_{j_i} - w\rVert \qquad \text{(triangle inequality, then } \lVert z_{j_i} - Kx_{j_i}\rVert < 1/j_i \text{)},$$
> and both terms on the right tend to $0$ as $i \to \infty$ (the first because $j_i \to \infty$, the second by the choice of $w$). Hence $z_{j_i} \to w$; and $w \in \overline{S}$ because $w$ is the limit of the points $Kx_{j_i} \in S$. So $(z_j)$ has a subsequence converging inside $\overline{S}$, establishing sequential compactness, and (1) follows.
>
> The four directions together give (1) $\Leftrightarrow$ (2) and (1) $\Leftrightarrow$ (3), so all three characterisations coincide. $\blacksquare$

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

Compact operators are the smallest robust class of operators that behaves, spectrally and Fredholm-theoretically, like a matrix. This is the source of their ubiquity across analysis. In the **spectral theory of Hilbert spaces**, a compact self-adjoint operator $K$ on a separable Hilbert space has a genuine eigenbasis: there is an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors with real eigenvalues $\lambda_n$, and the only possible accumulation point of the eigenvalues is $0$ — the spectral theorem for compact self-adjoint operators, which is the exact infinite-dimensional analogue of the diagonalisation of a symmetric matrix and which fails outright for non-compact self-adjoint operators such as multiplication operators with continuous spectrum. In the **theory of integral equations**, from which the notion historically arose, an integral operator $(Ku)(x) = \int k(x,y) u(y)\,dy$ with a square-integrable or continuous kernel $k$ is compact, and this is precisely what makes the Fredholm alternative — "$u - Ku = f$ is solvable for all $f$, or else the homogeneous equation $u - Ku = 0$ has a nontrivial finite-dimensional space of solutions" — available for such equations. Within this series, compactness is the analytic engine of elliptic theory: the [[Thm - Rellich Compactness Theorem|Rellich theorem]] makes the Sobolev inclusion compact, and it is that compactness, fed through the elliptic estimate, that forces elliptic operators on closed manifolds to have finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel in [[Thm - Elliptic Operators on Closed Manifolds are Fredholm]], and ultimately makes the Seiberg–Witten and Yang–Mills moduli spaces finite-dimensional.

**True name.** The official definition is a topological statement about the image of a ball, but the operational characterisation — the property one actually reaches for — is different and worth stating separately. On a Hilbert space (more generally a reflexive Banach space), a bounded operator $K$ is compact **if and only if it maps weakly convergent sequences to norm-convergent sequences**: whenever $x_j \rightharpoonup x$ weakly, $Kx_j \to Kx$ in norm. This is the true content of compactness — it *upgrades* the weak convergence that bounded sequences always have (by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, every bounded sequence in a Hilbert space has a weakly convergent subsequence) into the strong convergence that analysis needs. A second operational characterisation, valid on any Hilbert space, is algebraic: $K$ is compact if and only if it is the operator-norm limit of finite-rank operators (the approximation-property description, part (iv) of [[Thm - Basic Properties of Compact Operators]]). This says that $\mathcal{K}(X)$ is exactly the operator-norm closure of the finite-rank operators, so that compact operators are, structurally, the closed two-sided ideal of $\mathcal{B}(X)$ generated by the rank-one operators. When one needs to *prove* an operator compact, the finite-rank-approximation description is usually the tool; when one needs to *use* compactness, the weak-to-strong description is usually the tool.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — every finite-rank operator.** A bounded operator $K : X \to Y$ has **finite rank** if $\dim K(X) < \infty$. Every such $K$ is compact. To see it, use the sequential criterion (3): let $(x_j)$ be bounded, $\lVert x_j \rVert \le M$. Then $Kx_j \in K(X)$, a finite-dimensional normed subspace of $Y$, and $\lVert Kx_j \rVert \le \lVert K\rVert M$, so $(Kx_j)$ is a bounded sequence in the finite-dimensional space $K(X)$. The lemma below (proved in full) says that a bounded sequence in a finite-dimensional normed space has a convergent subsequence; applying it produces a subsequence of $(Kx_j)$ converging in $K(X) \subseteq Y$. Hence $K$ satisfies (3) and is compact.

> [!note]- Lemma: a bounded sequence in a finite-dimensional normed space has a convergent subsequence
> **Statement.** Let $(Z, \lVert\cdot\rVert)$ be a normed space over $\mathbb{F} \in \{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}\}$ with $\dim Z = d < \infty$. Then every bounded sequence in $Z$ has a subsequence convergent in $Z$.
>
> **Full proof.** Fix a basis $b_1, \dots, b_d$ of $Z$ and define the linear bijection
> $$T : \mathbb{F}^d \to Z, \qquad T(a) = \sum_{i=1}^d a_i b_i, \qquad a = (a_1,\dots,a_d),$$
> and equip $\mathbb{F}^d$ with the Euclidean norm $\lVert a\rVert_2 = \big(\sum_i |a_i|^2\big)^{1/2}$. We identify $\mathbb{C}^d$ isometrically with $\mathbb{R}^{2d}$ (real and imaginary parts) when $\mathbb{F} = \mathbb{C}$, so that the Heine–Borel theorem below applies verbatim.
>
> *Step 1: $T$ is bounded, hence continuous.* For $a \in \mathbb{F}^d$,
> $$\lVert Ta \rVert = \Big\lVert \sum_i a_i b_i \Big\rVert \le \sum_i |a_i|\,\lVert b_i\rVert \le \Big(\sum_i \lVert b_i\rVert\Big)\max_i |a_i| \le C_1 \lVert a\rVert_2 \qquad \text{(triangle inequality; } \max_i|a_i| \le \lVert a\rVert_2 \text{)},$$
> with $C_1 := \sum_i \lVert b_i \rVert$. In particular $\big|\,\lVert Ta\rVert - \lVert Ta'\rVert\,\big| \le \lVert T(a - a')\rVert \le C_1 \lVert a - a'\rVert_2$, so $a \mapsto \lVert Ta\rVert$ is continuous on $\mathbb{F}^d$.
>
> *Step 2: $T$ is bounded below.* We claim there is $c > 0$ with $\lVert Ta \rVert \ge c \lVert a\rVert_2$ for all $a$. Suppose not. Then for each $n$ there is $a^{(n)} \ne 0$ with $\lVert T a^{(n)}\rVert < \tfrac1n \lVert a^{(n)}\rVert_2$; normalising by setting $\alpha^{(n)} := a^{(n)}/\lVert a^{(n)}\rVert_2$ (so $\lVert \alpha^{(n)}\rVert_2 = 1$) and using homogeneity of $T$, $\lVert T\alpha^{(n)}\rVert < \tfrac1n \to 0$. The Euclidean unit sphere $S = \{\alpha : \lVert \alpha\rVert_2 = 1\}$ is closed and bounded in $\mathbb{F}^d \cong \mathbb{R}^{(\dim_\mathbb{R})}$, hence compact by [[Thm - Heine–Borel Theorem|the Heine–Borel theorem]], hence sequentially compact by [[Thm - Compactness in Metric Spaces (Three Equivalents)|the three-equivalents theorem]]. So a subsequence $\alpha^{(n_k)} \to \alpha^\ast$ with $\lVert \alpha^\ast\rVert_2 = 1$ (the sphere is closed, so the limit stays on it). By continuity of $T$ (Step 1), $T\alpha^{(n_k)} \to T\alpha^\ast$; but $\lVert T\alpha^{(n_k)}\rVert \to 0$, so $\lVert T\alpha^\ast\rVert = 0$, i.e. $T\alpha^\ast = 0$. Since $T$ is injective and $\alpha^\ast \ne 0$, this is a contradiction. Hence the bound $c > 0$ exists.
>
> *Step 3: conclusion.* Let $(z_n)$ be bounded in $Z$, $\lVert z_n\rVert \le M$. Write $z_n = T a^{(n)}$ with $a^{(n)} = T^{-1} z_n \in \mathbb{F}^d$. By Step 2, $c\lVert a^{(n)}\rVert_2 \le \lVert Ta^{(n)}\rVert = \lVert z_n\rVert \le M$, so $\lVert a^{(n)}\rVert_2 \le M/c$; the sequence $(a^{(n)})$ is bounded in $\mathbb{F}^d$. It therefore lies in a closed ball, which is compact by [[Thm - Heine–Borel Theorem|Heine–Borel]] and so sequentially compact; extract a subsequence $a^{(n_k)} \to a^\ast$ in $\mathbb{F}^d$. By continuity of $T$ (Step 1), $z_{n_k} = Ta^{(n_k)} \to Ta^\ast \in Z$. Thus $(z_n)$ has a convergent subsequence. $\blacksquare$

**Is an instance — a diagonal operator on $\ell^2$ with $\lambda_j \to 0$.** Let $(\lambda_j)_{j\ge 1}$ be a bounded scalar sequence and define $K : \ell^2 \to \ell^2$ by $K\big((x_j)_j\big) = (\lambda_j x_j)_j$, equivalently $Ke_j = \lambda_j e_j$ on the standard basis. First, $K$ is bounded: with $\Lambda := \sup_j |\lambda_j| < \infty$,
$$\lVert Kx \rVert^2 = \sum_{j\ge 1} |\lambda_j x_j|^2 \le \Lambda^2 \sum_{j\ge1}|x_j|^2 = \Lambda^2 \lVert x\rVert^2 \qquad \text{(each } |\lambda_j| \le \Lambda \text{)},$$
so $\lVert K\rVert \le \Lambda$. **Claim: $K$ is compact if $\lambda_j \to 0$.** For each $N \ge 1$ define the truncation $K_N \big((x_j)_j\big) = (\lambda_1 x_1, \dots, \lambda_N x_N, 0, 0, \dots)$, a bounded operator with range inside $\operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_N)$, hence of rank at most $N$, hence compact by the finite-rank example above. We estimate the operator-norm distance from $K$ to $K_N$: for $x \in B_{\ell^2}$,
$$\lVert (K - K_N)x\rVert^2 = \sum_{j > N} |\lambda_j x_j|^2 \le \Big(\sup_{j > N}|\lambda_j|^2\Big)\sum_{j > N}|x_j|^2 \le \Big(\sup_{j>N}|\lambda_j|\Big)^2 \lVert x\rVert^2 \qquad \text{(pull out the sup; } \textstyle\sum_{j>N}|x_j|^2 \le \lVert x\rVert^2 \le 1 \text{)},$$
so $\lVert K - K_N\rVert \le \sup_{j > N}|\lambda_j|$. Because $\lambda_j \to 0$, the tail supremum $\sup_{j > N}|\lambda_j| \to 0$ as $N \to \infty$; hence $K_N \to K$ in operator norm. Now invoke the closure property, restated at the point of use:
> **Theorem (compact operators are norm-closed).** The set $\mathcal{K}(X,Y)$ of compact operators is a closed subspace of $\mathcal{B}(X,Y)$ in the operator norm: an operator-norm limit of compact operators is compact. (Part (i) of [[Thm - Basic Properties of Compact Operators]].)

Since each $K_N$ is compact and $K_N \to K$ in operator norm, $K$ is compact by this theorem. This example is sharp in both directions, as the calibration check records: the condition $\lambda_j \to 0$ is not merely sufficient but necessary.

**Is an instance — the Sobolev inclusion $H_k(T^n) \hookrightarrow H_m(T^n)$ for $k > m$.** For integers $k > m$ the inclusion map $\iota : H_k(T^n) \to H_m(T^n)$, which sends a function to itself measured in the weaker norm, is a compact operator: every sequence bounded in the $H_k$ norm has a subsequence converging in the $H_m$ norm. This is a substantial theorem — the torus form of the Rellich compactness theorem — and its complete proof, via the finite-rank Fourier truncations $T_R u = \sum_{|\xi| \le R} \hat u(\xi) e^{i\langle \xi, x\rangle}$ and the tail estimate $\lVert u - T_R u\rVert_m^2 \le (1 + R^2)^{m-k}\lVert u\rVert_k^2$, is on [[Thm - Rellich Compactness Theorem]]. We do not reproduce it here; we record only that it is the single most important instance of a compact operator in this chapter, because it is what makes elliptic operators Fredholm.

**Is NOT an instance — the identity on an infinite-dimensional Banach space.** Let $X$ be infinite-dimensional and consider $\mathrm{Id}_X : X \to X$. This is bounded ($\lVert \mathrm{Id}_X\rVert = 1$) but **not compact**. Indeed, $\mathrm{Id}_X(B_X) = B_X$ is already closed (a closed ball is closed), so $\overline{\mathrm{Id}_X(B_X)} = B_X$, and compactness of $\mathrm{Id}_X$ would say precisely that $B_X$ is compact. But this is forbidden by Riesz's lemma, restated at its point of use:
> **Theorem (Riesz's lemma, corollary form).** A normed space whose closed unit ball is compact is finite-dimensional. (Part (i) of [[Thm - Riesz-Schauder Theory for Compact Perturbations of the Identity]].)

Since $X$ is infinite-dimensional, $B_X$ is not compact, so $\mathrm{Id}_X$ is not a compact operator. Concretely on $X = \ell^2$: the bounded sequence $(e_j)$ has $\mathrm{Id}(e_j) = e_j$, and $\lVert e_j - e_k\rVert = \sqrt2$ for $j \ne k$, so no subsequence of $(e_j)$ is Cauchy and the sequential criterion (3) fails outright. This is the non-example that gives the whole notion its point: compactness is exactly the property that the identity lacks in infinite dimensions, and its absence for $\mathrm{Id}_X$ is why $\mathrm{Id}_X + K$ (a compact perturbation of the identity) is the natural class in which the Fredholm alternative lives.

**Calibration check.** First, a diagonal operator $K$ on $\ell^2$ with $Ke_j = \lambda_j e_j$ is compact **if and only if** $\lambda_j \to 0$. We proved sufficiency above; for necessity, suppose $\lambda_j \not\to 0$. Then some $\delta > 0$ and some subsequence have $|\lambda_{j_i}| \ge \delta$. The unit vectors $(e_{j_i})_i$ are bounded, yet $\lVert Ke_{j_i} - Ke_{j_l}\rVert^2 = |\lambda_{j_i}|^2 + |\lambda_{j_l}|^2 \ge 2\delta^2$ for $i \ne l$ (the vectors $e_{j_i}, e_{j_l}$ are orthonormal), so $(Ke_{j_i})_i$ has no Cauchy subsequence and (3) fails; $K$ is not compact. Second, verify that a *nonzero* scalar multiple of $\mathrm{Id}_X$ on infinite-dimensional $X$ is never compact — if $\alpha \ne 0$ then $\alpha \, \mathrm{Id}_X$ compact would make $\overline{(\alpha\,\mathrm{Id}_X)(B_X)} = \{y : \lVert y\rVert \le |\alpha|\}$ compact, again contradicting Riesz's lemma. Third, check the trivial edge case: the zero operator is compact, since $\overline{0(B_X)} = \{0\}$ is a single point, which is compact. If you can also explain why "$K(B_X)$ relatively compact" needed the *closure* — because the image of the ball is generically not closed, so demanding $K(B_X)$ itself compact would exclude honest compact operators — you have understood the definition.

> The compactness of the Sobolev inclusion ([[Thm - Rellich Compactness Theorem]]) combines with the elliptic estimate to make the kernel of an elliptic operator finite-dimensional and its range closed, so that elliptic operators on closed manifolds are Fredholm ([[Thm - Elliptic Operators on Closed Manifolds are Fredholm]]). Compactness is the analytic input; ellipticity supplies the estimate.

> [!tip] The Fredholm Alternative *(from this chapter)*
> For $K$ compact, $\mathrm{Id} + K$ is Fredholm of index zero: its kernel is finite-dimensional, its range is closed of finite codimension, and $\dim\ker(\mathrm{Id}+K) = \dim\operatorname{coker}(\mathrm{Id}+K)$. This Riesz–Schauder theory, on [[Thm - Riesz-Schauder Theory for Compact Perturbations of the Identity]], is the exact sense in which a compact perturbation of the identity behaves like a finite matrix perturbation of the identity.
