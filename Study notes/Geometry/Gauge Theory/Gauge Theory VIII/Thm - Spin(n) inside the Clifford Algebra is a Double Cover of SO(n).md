# Notation

Throughout, $n\ge 2$ is an integer and $\mathbb{R}^n$ carries the standard Euclidean inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ with $|u|^2 = \langle u,u\rangle$. We work inside the **Clifford algebra** $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ of the negative-definite form, defined on the sibling page **[[Def - Clifford Algebra and Clifford Multiplication]]**: it is the unital associative $\mathbb{R}$-algebra generated by $\mathbb{R}^n$ subject to the relation $u\cdot u = -|u|^2\cdot 1$ for every $u\in\mathbb{R}^n$, equivalently (polarising) $u\cdot w + w\cdot u = -2\langle u,w\rangle\cdot 1$. On the standard basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ this reads $e_i\cdot e_i = -1$ and $e_i\cdot e_j = -e_j\cdot e_i$ for $i\ne j$. The monomials $e_I := e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_r}$ over increasing multi-indices $I = \{i_1<\cdots<i_r\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ (with $e_\varnothing := 1$) form a basis, so $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$. A unit vector $v\in S^{n-1} = \{v\in\mathbb{R}^n : |v| = 1\}$ satisfies $v\cdot v = -1$, hence is invertible with $v^{-1} = -v$.

> [!warning] Convention: the Clifford sign
> This series (following Haydys) uses $u\cdot u = -|u|^2$, so that $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is the vault's $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n, -|\cdot|^2)$, the Clifford algebra of the *negative* of the Euclidean form. The vault's **[[Def - Clifford Algebra]]** and **[[Def - Pin and Spin Groups]]** are written for a general quadratic space $(V,Q)$ with $v\cdot v = Q(v)$; to translate, put $Q = -|\cdot|^2$. Every displayed formula below carries its own sign, so no translation is needed while reading. The opposite convention $u\cdot u = +|u|^2$ (common in the index-theory literature) gives an isomorphic double-cover theory; only the intermediate scalar $v\cdot v = \pm 1$ changes.

The **even subalgebra** is $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$, spanned by the $e_I$ with $|I|$ even; it is the $+1$-eigenspace of the grading automorphism $\alpha$, the unique algebra automorphism of $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ with $\alpha(u) = -u$ for $u\in\mathbb{R}^n$ (so $\alpha = +\mathrm{id}$ on $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ and $\alpha = -\mathrm{id}$ on the odd part $\mathrm{Cl}^1$). We write $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$ for the group of invertible elements and $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times = \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times\cap\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for the even invertibles.

The remaining symbols are fixed on **[[Def - Spin Group and the Double Cover of SO(n)]]** and recalled here so the page stands alone:

- $\mathrm{Spin}(n) := \{v_1\cdots v_{2k} : k\ge 0,\ v_i\in S^{n-1}\}\subset\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$, the set of products of an even number of unit vectors (the empty product, $k = 0$, is $1$). It is a subgroup: a product of two such elements is again an even product; and $(v_1\cdots v_{2k})^{-1} = v_{2k}^{-1}\cdots v_1^{-1} = (-v_{2k})\cdots(-v_1) = v_{2k}\cdots v_1$ is an even product of unit vectors.
- $\xi : \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times\to\mathrm{Aut}(\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n))$, $\xi(a)x = a\cdot x\cdot a^{-1}$, the **conjugation (adjoint) action**; and the **twisted adjoint action** $\tilde\xi(a)x = \alpha(a)\cdot x\cdot a^{-1}$. Both are group homomorphisms. On $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ (even elements) they agree, $\xi = \tilde\xi$, because $\alpha(a) = a$ there.
- For a unit vector $v$, $R_v : \mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$, $R_v(u) = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v$, is the **reflection across the hyperplane** $v^\perp = \{u : \langle u,v\rangle = 0\}$; it is orthogonal with $\det R_v = -1$.
- $\mathfrak{so}(n) = \{A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{R}) : A^{\mathsf T} = -A\}$, the Lie algebra of $SO(n)$, with $\langle Au,w\rangle = -\langle u,Aw\rangle$; $\dim\mathfrak{so}(n) = \binom{n}{2}$.
- $\mathfrak{spin}(n) := \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{e_i\cdot e_j : i<j\}\subseteq\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$, with the commutator bracket $[x,y] = x\cdot y - y\cdot x$.
- $E_{ab}\in\mathfrak{gl}(n,\mathbb{R})$ is the matrix with a $1$ in row $a$, column $b$, and $0$ elsewhere; $I_n$ is the $n\times n$ identity.

# Statement

> **Theorem (the Clifford model of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a nontrivial double cover of $SO(n)$).** Let $n\ge 2$ and let $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\subset\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$, $\xi$, and $\mathfrak{spin}(n)$ be as above. Then:
>
> **(i) Lie group.** $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a compact, connected, embedded Lie subgroup of $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$, with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{spin}(n) = \operatorname{span}\{e_i\cdot e_j : i<j\}$ of dimension $\binom{n}{2}$.
>
> **(ii) Covering homomorphism.** The map $\xi : \mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)$, $\xi(a)u = a\cdot u\cdot a^{-1}$, is a well-defined surjective homomorphism of Lie groups, with
> $$\ker\xi = \{+1, -1\},$$
> and $\{\pm 1\}$ lies in the centre of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$.
>
> **(iii) Local diffeomorphism and the Lie algebra isomorphism.** $\xi$ is a two-sheeted covering map and a local diffeomorphism. Consequently its differential
> $$d\xi = d_1\xi : \mathfrak{spin}(n)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{so}(n)$$
> is an isomorphism of Lie algebras, with inverse
> $$\lambda : \mathfrak{so}(n)\longrightarrow\mathfrak{spin}(n), \qquad \lambda(A) = \tfrac14\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle Ae_i,e_j\rangle\, e_i\cdot e_j.$$
>
> **(iv) Nontriviality.** The curve $\gamma(t) = \cos t + \sin t\,(e_1\cdot e_2)$, $t\in[0,\pi]$, lies in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ and joins $\gamma(0) = 1$ to $\gamma(\pi) = -1$, the two points of the fibre $\xi^{-1}(I_n)$. Hence $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is connected while the fibre has two elements, so $\xi$ is a **nontrivial** double cover: there is no isomorphism of covering groups $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\cong SO(n)\times\{\pm 1\}$.

This page proves the assertions that the definition page states without proof (Haydys's remark R4.1.1 and the "it can be shown" of I4.1.2), namely that the concrete $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\subset\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$ really is a compact connected Lie group double covering $SO(n)$; it also proves the centrality of $\{\pm 1\}$ (Haydys's R4.3.3 / I4.3.4, "one can show", "clear for $n = 3, 4$"). It replaces the statement-only page `Thm - Spin(n) is the Double Cover of SO(n)` in the earlier vault, which is never cited.

# Motivation

The definition page **[[Def - Spin Group and the Double Cover of SO(n)]]** opens from the *topological* characterisation of the spin group: because $SO(n)$ is connected but has fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n\ge 3$, covering-space theory produces a connected double cover, and one *declares* this cover to be $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$. That characterisation is clean but inert. It names a group by a universal property and tells us nothing we can compute with — not what the elements are, not how they multiply, not how they act on $\mathbb{R}^n$, and above all not how to differentiate the covering map, which is exactly what the construction of the Dirac operator will require.

This theorem earns the name from the other direction. We take the *concrete* object — the set of even products of unit vectors inside the Clifford algebra, an object we can multiply, invert, differentiate, and act with — and prove that it has every property the topological definition demanded: it is a compact connected Lie group, and conjugation gives a two-to-one covering homomorphism onto $SO(n)$. The strategic payoff is that the proof uses **no fundamental-group input whatsoever**. Connectedness is proved by exhibiting an explicit path; the kernel is computed by a linear-algebra argument inside the Clifford algebra; the covering property is the inverse function theorem applied to a differential we compute by hand. The one topological fact the definition page quoted, that **$\pi_1(SO(n))\cong\mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n\ge 3$** (so that $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is in fact the *universal* cover and is simply connected), is established only in chapter XII and is *never invoked here*; this page proves the strictly weaker, and entirely sufficient, statement that $\xi$ is a connected double cover.

The reason this matters for gauge theory is that a spinor field is a section of a bundle associated to a principal $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$-bundle through a representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ — a representation that does *not* factor through $SO(n)$. To build such a bundle over an oriented Riemannian manifold, one lifts the structure group of the orthonormal frame bundle from $SO(n)$ to $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ along $\xi$. Everything downstream — the spin connection, the spinor bundle, the Dirac operator, the Weitzenböck formula, the entire Seiberg–Witten apparatus of chapter XI — rests on $\xi$ being a covering with a differential we can invert explicitly. Part (iii), the formula $\lambda(A) = \tfrac14\sum\langle Ae_i,e_j\rangle e_i\cdot e_j$, is the piece those constructions actually call: it converts a skew-symmetric connection or curvature matrix into a Clifford element that can act on spinors.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening).** The literal hypothesis is mild — an integer $n\ge 2$ — so the useful question is: when does a problem secretly present you with "the double cover of a rotation group", so that this theorem applies?

The first disguised source is **a representation that changes sign under a $2\pi$ rotation.** Whenever a physical or geometric quantity transforms under rotations but returns to *minus* itself after a full $2\pi$ turn and only to itself after $4\pi$ — a Pauli spinor, a fermionic wavefunction, the "belt" of the belt trick — the object is not a representation of $SO(n)$ at all; it is a genuine (linear) representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ that is only *projective* for $SO(n)$. The bridge is part (iv): the noncontractible loop in $SO(n)$ lifts to the *path* $\gamma$ from $1$ to $-1$, and the sign the spinor picks up is precisely the failure of $\gamma$ to close. Recognising a sign-under-$2\pi$ ambiguity as "this lives on $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$" is the entire move. *Example problem:* explain why the state of a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle acquires a factor $-1$ under a $2\pi$ rotation, and identify the group actually acting.

The second disguised source is **a set of anticommuting square roots of $-1$.** Any collection of operators $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_n$ on some vector space with $\gamma_i\gamma_j + \gamma_j\gamma_i = -2\delta_{ij}$ — the Dirac gamma matrices, or fermionic creation/annihilation combinations — generates a copy of $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ by the universal property (**[[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property]]**: a linear $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to A$ with $f(u)^2 = -|u|^2$ extends uniquely to an algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)\to A$). Inside that copy sits $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ as the even unit-vector products, and this theorem hands you its action on the "vector" indices for free. The bridge is that the even products of the $\gamma_i$ that square correctly are exactly the image of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, acting by $\xi$. *Example problem:* given the $4\times 4$ Dirac matrices, exhibit the $\mathrm{Spin}(3,1)$ (or, after Wick rotation, $\mathrm{Spin}(4)$) generators as commutators $\tfrac14[\gamma_i,\gamma_j]$ and identify their action on the four-vector index.

The third disguised source is **a compact connected Lie group $G$ with a two-to-one homomorphism onto $SO(n)$ whose differential is an isomorphism.** Any such $G$ is forced to be *the* connected double cover, hence isomorphic to $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ over $SO(n)$; the bridge is that a surjective homomorphism with two-element kernel and bijective differential is a two-sheeted covering (Lemma 6 below), and connected double covers of a connected group are unique. This is exactly how the low-dimensional coincidences are recognised: $Sp(1)\to SO(3)$ and $Sp(1)\times Sp(1)\to SO(4)$ are covers with the right kernel and differential, so they *are* $\mathrm{Spin}(3)$ and $\mathrm{Spin}(4)$. *Example problem:* show $Sp(1) = \{q\in\mathbb{H} : |q| = 1\}$ acting on $\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}\cong\mathbb{R}^3$ by $q\cdot h = qh\bar q$ is a double cover of $SO(3)$, and conclude $\mathrm{Spin}(3)\cong Sp(1)$ (**[[Thm - Spin Groups in Dimensions Three and Four via Quaternions]]**).

**Targets (Output Amplification).** The bare conclusion — $\xi$ is a nontrivial double cover with $d\xi$ an isomorphism — becomes much more when combined with further ingredients.

Combine part (iii) with **an oriented Riemannian manifold and a lift of its frame bundle.** Given the principal $SO(n)$-bundle of oriented orthonormal frames, a lift of its structure group along $\xi$ is a spin structure, and the isomorphism $\lambda : \mathfrak{so}(n)\to\mathfrak{spin}(n)$ transports the Levi-Civita connection form (an $\mathfrak{so}(n)$-valued $1$-form) to a $\mathfrak{spin}(n)$-valued $1$-form, the *spin connection*. The further result is the spinor bundle with its Dirac operator (**[[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator]]**); without the explicit $\lambda$ there would be no way to write the connection acting on spinors.

Combine part (i)–(iii) with **the topological fact $\pi_1(SO(n))\cong\mathbb{Z}/2$** (chapter XII). Since $\xi$ is a connected two-sheeted cover of $SO(n)$ and $SO(n)$ has fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}/2$, the total space $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is simply connected for $n\ge 3$ — it is the *universal* cover, and the long exact homotopy sequence of the covering pins $\pi_1(\mathrm{Spin}(n)) = 0$. The further result is that $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ realises the abstract object of the definition page's Haydys characterisation, closing the circle between the concrete and topological definitions. (This page does not use $\pi_1(SO(n))$; the implication runs the other way.)

Combine the differential formula with **the representation theory of $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.** The irreducible complex Clifford module restricts along $\xi$ to the spinor representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, and $\lambda$ tells you how $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ acts on it. In dimension four this produces the splitting into self-dual and anti-self-dual pieces and the chiral spinor representations $\slashed S^\pm$ (**[[Thm - Spinor Representations of Spin(4) and the Self-Dual Forms]]**), the linear-algebra backbone of Seiberg–Witten theory. The payoff is that abstract "half-integer spin" representations become concrete quaternionic modules one can compute with.

# Why Is It True

Strip away the machinery and the theorem is one geometric fact dressed in algebra. The fact is the classical theorem of **Cartan and Dieudonné**: every rotation of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a composite of an *even* number of reflections in hyperplanes (and every reflection has determinant $-1$, so an even number is needed to get determinant $+1$). The Clifford algebra is engineered so that reflection has an algebraic name: twisted conjugation by a unit vector $v$ *is* the reflection $R_v$ across $v^\perp$. Therefore conjugation by a product of unit vectors is a composite of reflections, and conjugation by an *even* product is a composite of an *even* number of reflections — a rotation. Cartan–Dieudonné says every rotation arises this way, so $\xi$ is onto $SO(n)$.

That explains surjectivity. The two-to-one-ness is the second half. Which even products conjugate $\mathbb{R}^n$ trivially, that is, commute with every vector? An element commuting with everything in a generated algebra is central, and a short basis computation shows the only *even* central elements are the real scalars. Among real scalars, only those that are actually even products of *unit* vectors survive, and the reversal-norm $a\mapsto a\cdot a^{\mathsf t}$ (which equals $1$ on every unit-vector product, because unit vectors square to $-1$ and pair off) forces such a scalar $\lambda$ to satisfy $\lambda^2 = 1$. So the kernel is exactly $\{\pm 1\}$, two elements.

> **The mechanism in one sentence: conjugation by a unit vector is (minus) a reflection, so even products of unit vectors act by rotations; Cartan–Dieudonné makes this action surjective, and the only even products acting trivially are the two scalars $\pm 1$.**

The final ingredient, that the two-to-one map is a genuine *covering* rather than merely two-to-one, is smoothness. Once we know $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a compact Lie group (it is a closed, indeed compact, subgroup of the units of a finite-dimensional algebra, by Cartan–Dieudonné's bound on the number of factors), the differential $d\xi$ can be computed on the basis $e_i\cdot e_j$ and comes out to twice the standard rotation generator $e_i\mapsto e_j$, $e_j\mapsto -e_i$ — a linear isomorphism onto $\mathfrak{so}(n)$. A homomorphism of Lie groups whose differential is an isomorphism is a local diffeomorphism; with a discrete two-element kernel and connected source it is a two-sheeted cover. Nontriviality is visible to the naked eye: the explicit curve $\cos t + \sin t\,e_1e_2$ runs from $1$ to $-1$ without leaving $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, so the two sheets are joined — the cover is connected, unlike the trivial cover $SO(n)\times\{\pm 1\}$.

# What Makes This Hard

The subtle steps are three, and each is a place where a careless argument silently fails. First, the reflection identity requires the *twisted* adjoint $\tilde\xi(v)u = \alpha(v)uv^{-1}$, not the plain conjugation $\xi(v)u = vuv^{-1}$; the two differ by a sign on a single vector ($\xi(v) = -\tilde\xi(v)$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$), and only on the *even* group $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ do they coincide — so one must use $\tilde\xi$ to see reflections but is entitled to write $\xi$ on the final group. Second, computing the kernel needs both halves: that a trivially-conjugating even element is a real *scalar* (a basis-and-parity computation, not obvious, and false for the whole algebra when $n$ is odd, where the volume element is also central) and that the only such scalars in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ are $\pm 1$ (the norm argument, which is where the unit-norm hypothesis finally pays off). Third, "two-to-one implies covering" is genuinely a theorem, not a definition: it needs the differential to be an isomorphism (so $\xi$ is a local diffeomorphism, via the inverse function theorem) and it needs the source to be a manifold at all (the closed subgroup theorem), neither of which is free. The most common error is to assert the covering property from surjectivity and a two-element kernel alone, forgetting that a two-to-one group homomorphism can fail to be a local homeomorphism.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** Realise each reflection of $\mathbb{R}^n$ as twisted conjugation by a unit vector, so that $\xi$ maps even unit-vector products to even products of reflections, i.e. into $SO(n)$; get surjectivity from Cartan–Dieudonné; get the kernel by proving an even element conjugating trivially is a scalar and then that unit-vector scalars are $\pm 1$; bound the number of factors (again Cartan–Dieudonné) to see $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a continuous image of a compact product of spheres, hence compact, hence a Lie group by the closed subgroup theorem; compute $d\xi$ on $e_i\cdot e_j$ to get an isomorphism onto $\mathfrak{so}(n)$; upgrade "isomorphism of differentials + discrete kernel + connected" to "two-sheeted covering" by the inverse function theorem and homogeneity; and exhibit the path from $1$ to $-1$ for connectedness and nontriviality.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Reflection formula and landing in $SO(n)$.** Show $\tilde\xi(v)u = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v = R_v(u)$ for unit $v$, and deduce $\xi(a) = R_{v_1}\cdots R_{v_{2k}}\in SO(n)$ for $a = v_1\cdots v_{2k}$.
   - *Hint:* Use $\alpha(v) = -v$, $v^{-1} = -v$, and $v\cdot u = -u\cdot v - 2\langle u,v\rangle$.
   - *Why needed:* It is what makes $\xi$ a map to $SO(n)$ at all.

2. **Cartan–Dieudonné.** Every $T\in O(n)$ is a product of at most $n$ hyperplane reflections; hence $SO(n)$ is exactly the even products.
   - *Hint:* Induct on $n$. If $T$ fixes a unit vector $e$, drop to $e^\perp$; otherwise reflect $Te$ back to $e$ using $R_{Te-e}$.
   - *Why needed:* Surjectivity of $\xi$ and the bound on the number of factors (for compactness).

3. **Kernel is scalar.** An even $a$ with $\xi(a) = \mathrm{id}$ commutes with all vectors, hence is a real scalar.
   - *Hint:* Expand $a = \sum_I a_I e_I$ over even $I$; $[a,e_j] = 2\sum_{I\ni j}a_I e_I e_j$; linear independence forces $a_I = 0$ for $I\ne\varnothing$.
   - *Why needed:* First half of $\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$.

4. **Scalars in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ are $\pm 1$; centrality.** The reversal $a^{\mathsf t}$ gives $a\cdot a^{\mathsf t} = 1$ on $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$; a scalar $\lambda$ then has $\lambda^2 = 1$. Scalars are central in the whole algebra.
   - *Hint:* $a^{\mathsf t}$ reverses factors and fixes vectors; unit vectors square to $-1$ and cancel from the outside in.
   - *Why needed:* Second half of $\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$, plus the centrality of $\{\pm 1\}$.

5. **Compactness and Lie structure.** $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is the image of $(S^{n-1})^{2n}$ under multiplication, hence compact; a compact subgroup of a Lie group is closed, so the closed subgroup theorem makes it a Lie group.
   - *Hint:* Every element is $\pm v_1\cdots v_{2k}$ with $2k\le n$ (Subgoals 2, 4); pad with pairs $ww = -1$ and flip one sign to reach exactly $2n$ factors.
   - *Why needed:* Part (i), and the manifold structure needed for the differential.

6. **Differential, local diffeomorphism, covering.** $d\xi(e_i e_j)$ is twice the $(i,j)$ rotation generator; $d\xi$ is injective (discrete kernel) onto $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, so an isomorphism with inverse $\lambda$; the inverse function theorem plus homogeneity make $\xi$ a local diffeomorphism, and the two-element fibre makes it a two-sheeted cover.
   - *Hint:* Differentiate $\gamma_{ij}(t) = \cos t + \sin t\,e_ie_j$ to get $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_0\xi(\gamma_{ij}) = [e_ie_j,\,\cdot\,]$.
   - *Why needed:* Part (iii), and part (i)'s identification of $\mathfrak{spin}(n)$.

7. **Connectedness and nontriviality.** The path $\gamma(t) = \cos t + \sin t\,e_1e_2$ joins $1$ to $-1$; pairing off unit vectors connects any element to $\pm 1$; so $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is connected and the cover is nontrivial.
   - *Hint:* $S^{n-1}$ is path-connected for $n\ge 2$; move one factor of a pair $uw$ along $S^{n-1}$ to the other, reaching $uu = -1$.
   - *Why needed:* Connectedness in part (i), and all of part (iv).

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: twisted conjugation by a unit vector is a reflection; even products act by rotations
> **Statement:** For a unit vector $v\in S^{n-1}$ and $u\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $\tilde\xi(v)u = -v\cdot u\cdot v^{-1} = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v = R_v(u)$, an orthogonal map of determinant $-1$. Consequently, for $a = v_1\cdots v_{2k}\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, $\xi(a) = \tilde\xi(a) = R_{v_1}\circ\cdots\circ R_{v_{2k}}$ preserves $\mathbb{R}^n$ and lies in $SO(n)$; so $\xi : \mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)$ is a well-defined homomorphism.
>
> **Hint:** $\alpha(v) = -v$ and $v^{-1} = -v$; then the Clifford relation $v\cdot u + u\cdot v = -2\langle u,v\rangle$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the reason $\xi$ takes values in $SO(n)$, and the bridge between the algebra and the geometry. (The same computation is on the definition page's Proposition; it is reproved here so this page is self-contained.)
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **What is to be shown.** The reflection identity, its orthogonality and determinant, and that even products give elements of $SO(n)$.
> >
> > **Step 1 — reduce twisted conjugation to a Clifford product.** Since $v$ is odd, $\alpha(v) = -v$; since $|v| = 1$, $v\cdot v = -|v|^2 = -1$, so $v^{-1} = -v$. Therefore
> > $$\tilde\xi(v)u = \alpha(v)\cdot u\cdot v^{-1} = (-v)\cdot u\cdot(-v) = v\cdot u\cdot v \qquad \text{(} \alpha(v) = -v,\ v^{-1} = -v\text{)}.$$
> > (Equivalently $\tilde\xi(v)u = -v\cdot u\cdot v^{-1}$, replacing one $-v$ by $v^{-1}$.)
> >
> > **Step 2 — apply the Clifford relation.** Polarising $u\cdot u = -|u|^2$ gives $v\cdot u + u\cdot v = -2\langle u,v\rangle\cdot 1$, hence $v\cdot u = -u\cdot v - 2\langle u,v\rangle$. Substituting into Step 1,
> > $$v\cdot u\cdot v = (-u\cdot v - 2\langle u,v\rangle)\cdot v = -u\cdot(v\cdot v) - 2\langle u,v\rangle\, v = -u\cdot(-1) - 2\langle u,v\rangle v = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v \qquad \text{(} v\cdot v = -1\text{)}.$$
> > So $\tilde\xi(v)u = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v$; in particular it lies in $\mathbb{R}^n$, so $\tilde\xi(v)$ preserves $\mathbb{R}^n$.
> >
> > **Step 3 — identify the reflection, orthogonality, determinant.** The map $R_v(u) = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v$ fixes each $u\in v^\perp$ (there $\langle u,v\rangle = 0$) and sends $v\mapsto v - 2\langle v,v\rangle v = v - 2v = -v$ (since $|v| = 1$); a linear map fixing the hyperplane $v^\perp$ and negating $v$ is the reflection across $v^\perp$. It is orthogonal:
> > $$\langle R_v u, R_v w\rangle = \langle u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v,\ w - 2\langle w,v\rangle v\rangle = \langle u,w\rangle - 2\langle u,v\rangle\langle w,v\rangle - 2\langle w,v\rangle\langle u,v\rangle + 4\langle u,v\rangle\langle w,v\rangle|v|^2 = \langle u,w\rangle \qquad \text{(} |v|^2 = 1\text{, terms cancel)}.$$
> > In the orthonormal basis $(v,\ \text{basis of } v^\perp)$ its matrix is $\operatorname{diag}(-1,1,\dots,1)$, so $\det R_v = -1$; hence $\tilde\xi(v) = R_v\in O(n)\setminus SO(n)$.
> >
> > **Step 4 — even products.** For $a = v_1\cdots v_{2k}\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)\subset\mathrm{Cl}^0$, we have $\alpha(a) = a$ (grading; $|I|$ even), so $\tilde\xi(a) = \alpha(a)\cdot(\,\cdot\,)\cdot a^{-1} = a\cdot(\,\cdot\,)\cdot a^{-1} = \xi(a)$. Because $\tilde\xi$ is a homomorphism ($\tilde\xi(ab) = \tilde\xi(a)\tilde\xi(b)$, as $\alpha$ is an algebra homomorphism),
> > $$\xi(a) = \tilde\xi(v_1)\circ\cdots\circ\tilde\xi(v_{2k}) = R_{v_1}\circ\cdots\circ R_{v_{2k}} \qquad \text{(Step 3, homomorphism property)}.$$
> > Each $R_{v_i}$ preserves $\mathbb{R}^n$ and lies in $O(n)$, so $\xi(a)$ preserves $\mathbb{R}^n$ and lies in $O(n)$; and $\det\xi(a) = \prod_{i=1}^{2k}\det R_{v_i} = (-1)^{2k} = +1$, so $\xi(a)\in SO(n)$.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** For unit vectors twisted conjugation is reflection, and conjugation by an even product is a product of an even number of reflections, hence a rotation; so $\xi(\mathrm{Spin}(n))\subseteq SO(n)$ and $\xi$ is a homomorphism $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: the Cartan–Dieudonné theorem
> **Statement:** For every $n\ge 1$, every $T\in O(n)$ is a product of at most $n$ reflections in hyperplanes; the identity is the empty product. Consequently $SO(n) = \{R_{v_1}\cdots R_{v_{2k}} : 2k\le n,\ v_i\in S^{n-1}\}$, the products of an *even* number ($\le n$) of reflections.
>
> **Hint:** Induct on $n$. Fix a unit vector $e$. If $Te = e$, restrict to $e^\perp$; otherwise the reflection $R_{w}$ with $w = Te - e$ sends $Te$ back to $e$, and $R_w T$ fixes $e$.
>
> **Why needed:** Surjectivity of $\xi$ (write any rotation as an even product of reflections, then lift), and the bound "$\le n$ factors" used for compactness in Lemma 5.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **What is to be shown.** By induction on $n$: every $T\in O(n)$ is a product of at most $n$ hyperplane reflections. Reflections have determinant $-1$, so a product of $m$ of them has determinant $(-1)^m$; hence $T\in SO(n)$ forces $m$ even, and conversely an even product of reflections is orthogonal with determinant $+1$, i.e. in $SO(n)$ — this gives the "Consequently" once the main claim is proved.
> >
> > **Base case $n = 1$.** $O(1) = \{+1, -1\}$. The map $+1$ is the empty product (zero reflections). The map $-1$ is $u\mapsto -u$, which for the unit vector $v = 1\in\mathbb{R}^1$ is $R_v(u) = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v = u - 2u = -u$; so $-1$ is one reflection. At most $1 = n$ reflections in either case.
> >
> > **Inductive step.** Assume the claim for $n - 1$; let $T\in O(n)$ and fix the unit vector $e = e_n$.
> >
> > *Case (a): $Te = e$.* For $u\in e^\perp$, $\langle Tu, e\rangle = \langle Tu, Te\rangle = \langle u, e\rangle = 0$ (as $T$ is orthogonal and $Te = e$), so $Tu\in e^\perp$; thus $T$ restricts to $T' := T|_{e^\perp}\in O(e^\perp)\cong O(n-1)$. By the inductive hypothesis $T' = R'_{w_1}\cdots R'_{w_m}$ with $m\le n-1$ and $w_i\in e^\perp$ unit vectors, each $R'_{w_i}$ a reflection of $e^\perp$. Each extends to the reflection $R_{w_i}$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (which fixes $e$, since $e\in w_i^\perp$ as $w_i\in e^\perp$) and agrees with $R'_{w_i}$ on $e^\perp$. Then $R_{w_1}\cdots R_{w_m}$ fixes $e$ and equals $T$ on $e^\perp$, hence equals $T$ on all of $\mathbb{R}^n = \mathbb{R}e\oplus e^\perp$. So $T$ is a product of $m\le n-1\le n$ reflections.
> >
> > *Case (b): $Te\ne e$.* Set $w := Te - e\ne 0$ and let $R_w$ be the reflection across $w^\perp$, $R_w(u) = u - 2\dfrac{\langle u,w\rangle}{\langle w,w\rangle}w$ (the unit-normalised form of $R$). We claim $R_w(Te) = e$. Using $|Te| = |e| = 1$,
> > $$\langle w,w\rangle = \langle Te - e, Te - e\rangle = |Te|^2 - 2\langle Te,e\rangle + |e|^2 = 2 - 2\langle Te,e\rangle \qquad \text{(bilinearity)},$$
> > $$\langle Te, w\rangle = \langle Te,\ Te - e\rangle = |Te|^2 - \langle Te,e\rangle = 1 - \langle Te,e\rangle \qquad \text{(bilinearity)}.$$
> > Since $Te\ne e$ and both are unit vectors, $\langle Te,e\rangle < 1$, so $\langle w,w\rangle = 2(1 - \langle Te,e\rangle)\ne 0$ and
> > $$2\,\frac{\langle Te,w\rangle}{\langle w,w\rangle} = 2\cdot\frac{1 - \langle Te,e\rangle}{2(1 - \langle Te,e\rangle)} = 1, \qquad\text{hence}\qquad R_w(Te) = Te - 1\cdot w = Te - (Te - e) = e.$$
> > Thus $R_w T$ fixes $e$: $(R_w T)e = R_w(Te) = e$. By Case (a), $R_w T$ is a product of at most $n-1$ reflections, so $T = R_w(R_w T)$ (using $R_w^2 = \mathrm{id}$) is a product of at most $1 + (n-1) = n$ reflections.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** In both cases $T$ is a product of at most $n$ reflections, completing the induction; and the parity remark gives $SO(n) = \{$even products of $\le n$ reflections$\}$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: an even element commuting with every vector is a real scalar
> **Statement:** If $a\in\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$ satisfies $a\cdot u = u\cdot a$ for all $u\in\mathbb{R}^n$, then $a\in\mathbb{R}\cdot 1$. In particular, if $a\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ and $\xi(a) = \mathrm{id}$, then $a$ is a real scalar.
>
> **Hint:** Expand $a$ in the basis $\{e_I : |I|\text{ even}\}$ and compute the commutator with each $e_j$; an even $e_I$ anticommutes with $e_j$ exactly when $j\in I$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the first half of the kernel computation $\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **What is to be shown.** That the only even elements commuting with all of $\mathbb{R}^n$ are the scalars. (The final clause follows because $\xi(a) = \mathrm{id}$ means $a\cdot u\cdot a^{-1} = u$, i.e. $a\cdot u = u\cdot a$ for all $u$.)
> >
> > **Step 1 — commutation signs of basis monomials with a generator.** For a basis monomial $e_I$ ($I$ an increasing multi-index) and a generator $e_j$, moving $e_j$ across $e_I$ gives a sign:
> > $$e_j\cdot e_I = (-1)^{|I|}\, e_I\cdot e_j\ \ (j\notin I), \qquad e_j\cdot e_I = (-1)^{|I|+1}\, e_I\cdot e_j\ \ (j\in I).$$
> > Indeed, if $j\notin I$ then $e_j$ anticommutes with each of the $|I|$ distinct factors of $e_I$, giving $(-1)^{|I|}$. If $j\in I$, write $j = i_p$; bringing the leading $e_j$ rightward to its partner costs $(-1)^{p-1}$ from the $p-1$ preceding factors and then $e_j\cdot e_j = -1$, while $e_I\cdot e_j$ costs $(-1)^{|I|-p}$ from the $|I|-p$ following factors and then $e_j\cdot e_j = -1$; the ratio is $(-1)^{(p-1)-(|I|-p)} = (-1)^{2p-1-|I|} = (-1)^{|I|+1}$.
> >
> > **Step 2 — the commutator of an even element.** Write $a = \sum_{I}a_I e_I$ over increasing $I$ with $|I|$ even (as $a\in\mathrm{Cl}^0$). For $|I|$ even, Step 1 says $e_j$ *commutes* with $e_I$ when $j\notin I$ (sign $(-1)^{|I|} = +1$) and *anticommutes* when $j\in I$ (sign $(-1)^{|I|+1} = -1$). Hence
> > $$a\cdot e_j - e_j\cdot a = \sum_I a_I\,(e_I\cdot e_j - e_j\cdot e_I) = \sum_{I : j\in I} a_I\,(e_I\cdot e_j - (-e_I\cdot e_j)) = 2\sum_{I : j\in I} a_I\, e_I\cdot e_j \qquad \text{(Step 1, } |I|\text{ even)}.$$
> >
> > **Step 3 — force the coefficients to vanish.** Suppose $a$ commutes with every $e_j$, so the left side is $0$ for each $j$. For fixed $j$, the elements $\{e_I\cdot e_j : j\in I\}$ are, up to a sign, the distinct basis monomials $e_{I\setminus\{j\}}$ (removing $j$ from $I$ gives distinct index sets for distinct $I$), hence linearly independent. Therefore $2\sum_{I\ni j}a_I e_I\cdot e_j = 0$ forces $a_I = 0$ for every $I$ containing $j$. Ranging over all $j\in\{1,\dots,n\}$, we get $a_I = 0$ for every nonempty $I$. Thus $a = a_\varnothing\cdot 1\in\mathbb{R}\cdot 1$.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** An even element commuting with all vectors is a real scalar. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 4: the spinor norm; the only scalars in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ are $\pm 1$; centrality
> **Statement:** There is a unique linear anti-automorphism (the *reversal*) $(\,\cdot\,)^{\mathsf t} : \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)\to\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ with $u^{\mathsf t} = u$ for $u\in\mathbb{R}^n$ and $(x\cdot y)^{\mathsf t} = y^{\mathsf t}\cdot x^{\mathsf t}$; it fixes scalars. For every $a\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ the *norm* $N(a) := a\cdot a^{\mathsf t} = 1$. Consequently the only real scalars in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ are $+1$ and $-1$, so $\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$, and $\{\pm 1\}$ lies in the centre of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$.
>
> **Hint:** Build $(\,\cdot\,)^{\mathsf t}$ from the universal property applied to the opposite algebra. For a unit-vector product, $a\cdot a^{\mathsf t}$ cancels from the outside in because each $v_i\cdot v_i = -1$ and there are evenly many.
>
> **Why needed:** It completes $\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$ (with Lemma 3) and gives the centrality claim (Haydys R4.3.3 / I4.3.4) for free.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Step 0 — construct the reversal.** Let $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^{\mathrm{op}}$ be the opposite algebra (same vector space, product $x\ast y := y\cdot x$). The inclusion $f : \mathbb{R}^n\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^{\mathrm{op}}$ satisfies $f(u)\ast f(u) = u\cdot u = -|u|^2\cdot 1$, so by the **[[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property]]** — for a quadratic form $Q$ and a linear $f:V\to A$ into a unital algebra with $f(v)\cdot f(v) = Q(v)1_A$, there is a unique algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(V,Q)\to A$ extending $f$, here with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$ — $f$ extends uniquely to an algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)\to\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^{\mathrm{op}}$. Read back in $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, this is a linear map $(\,\cdot\,)^{\mathsf t}$ with $(x\cdot y)^{\mathsf t} = y^{\mathsf t}\cdot x^{\mathsf t}$ and $u^{\mathsf t} = u$ for $u\in\mathbb{R}^n$; on a monomial $(v_1\cdots v_r)^{\mathsf t} = v_r\cdots v_1$, and $1^{\mathsf t} = 1$, so scalars are fixed.
> >
> > **Step 1 — the norm is $1$ on $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$.** Let $a = v_1\cdots v_{2k}$ with $v_i\in S^{n-1}$. Then $a^{\mathsf t} = v_{2k}\cdots v_1$, and
> > $$a\cdot a^{\mathsf t} = v_1\cdots v_{2k-1}\,(v_{2k}\cdot v_{2k})\,v_{2k-1}\cdots v_1 = v_1\cdots v_{2k-1}(-1)v_{2k-1}\cdots v_1 = -\,v_1\cdots v_{2k-1}\cdot v_{2k-1}\cdots v_1 \qquad \text{(} v_{2k}\cdot v_{2k} = -1\text{)}.$$
> > Cancelling the innermost pair repeatedly, each $v_i\cdot v_i = -1$ contributing a factor $-1$ across all $2k$ cancellations,
> > $$a\cdot a^{\mathsf t} = (-1)^{2k}\cdot 1 = 1 \qquad \text{(} 2k \text{ pairs, each } v_i\cdot v_i = -1\text{)}.$$
> >
> > **Step 2 — scalars in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ are $\pm 1$.** If $a = \lambda\cdot 1\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ with $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$, then $a^{\mathsf t} = \lambda\cdot 1$ (Step 0 fixes scalars), so $N(a) = a\cdot a^{\mathsf t} = \lambda^2$. By Step 1, $\lambda^2 = 1$, hence $\lambda = \pm 1$. Both values occur in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$: $1$ is the empty product, and for any unit vector $v$, $v\cdot v = -1$ is a product of two unit vectors, so $-1\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$.
> >
> > **Step 3 — the kernel.** If $a\in\ker\xi$, i.e. $a\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ and $\xi(a) = \mathrm{id}$, then by Lemma 3 $a$ is a real scalar, and by Step 2 $a\in\{\pm 1\}$. Conversely $\xi(\pm 1)u = (\pm 1)u(\pm 1)^{-1} = u$, so $\pm 1\in\ker\xi$. Hence $\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$.
> >
> > **Step 4 — centrality.** The elements $\pm 1$ are real scalars, and real scalars commute with every element of the algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$; in particular they commute with every element of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$. So $\{\pm 1\}\subseteq Z(\mathrm{Spin}(n))$.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** The kernel of $\xi$ on $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is exactly the central subgroup $\{\pm 1\}$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 5: $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is compact, connected, and a Lie group
> **Statement:** For $n\ge 2$, $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a compact, connected subgroup of $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$; being compact it is closed, so by the closed subgroup theorem it is an embedded Lie subgroup, hence a compact connected Lie group.
>
> **Hint:** Compactness: $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is the image of $(S^{n-1})^{2n}$ under multiplication. Connectedness: pair off unit vectors and slide one factor of each pair to the other along $S^{n-1}$.
>
> **Why needed:** Part (i), and it supplies the manifold structure that Lemma 6 differentiates.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Step 1 — every element uses at most $2n$ factors (compactness).** Let $a\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ and put $T := \xi(a)\in SO(n)$ (Lemma 1). By Cartan–Dieudonné (Lemma 2), $T = R_{v_1}\cdots R_{v_{2k}}$ with unit vectors $v_i$ and $2k\le n$. Set $b := v_1\cdots v_{2k}\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$; then $\xi(b) = R_{v_1}\cdots R_{v_{2k}} = T = \xi(a)$ (Lemma 1), so $\xi(ab^{-1}) = \mathrm{id}$ and $ab^{-1}\in\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$ (Lemma 4). Thus $a = \pm b = \pm v_1\cdots v_{2k}$ with $2k\le n$.
> >
> > Now pad to exactly $2n$ unit-vector factors. Because $2n - 2k\ge 2n - n = n\ge 2$ is even and positive, insert $n - k$ pairs $(w,w)$ with $w = e_1$; each pair equals $e_1\cdot e_1 = -1$, contributing $(-1)^{n-k}$ overall. To match the sign $\pm$ in $a = \pm v_1\cdots v_{2k}$, use that replacing $v_1$ by the unit vector $-v_1$ negates the product; choose the sign of the first factor so that
> > $$a = \underbrace{e_1\cdot e_1\cdots e_1\cdot e_1}_{2(n-k)\ \text{factors}}\cdot(\pm v_1)\cdot v_2\cdots v_{2k},$$
> > a product of exactly $2n$ unit vectors. Hence $\mathrm{Spin}(n) = \Phi\big((S^{n-1})^{2n}\big)$, where $\Phi : (S^{n-1})^{2n}\to\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$, $\Phi(v_1,\dots,v_{2n}) = v_1\cdots v_{2n}$, is continuous (Clifford multiplication is a fixed bilinear map iterated). The domain is a finite product of the compact spheres $S^{n-1}$ ($n\ge 2$), hence compact; so its continuous image $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is compact. As a compact subset of the Hausdorff space $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$, $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is closed there.
> >
> > **Step 2 — connectedness.** First, a single pair of unit vectors is connected to $-1$. Given unit vectors $u,w$, choose a continuous path $s\mapsto w_s$ in $S^{n-1}$ with $w_0 = w$ and $w_1 = u$ (possible since $S^{n-1}$ is path-connected for $n\ge 2$). Then $s\mapsto u\cdot w_s$ is a continuous path in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ from $u\cdot w$ to $u\cdot u = -1$. Now take any $a = v_1\cdots v_{2k}\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ and group the factors into $k$ pairs $(v_1 v_2),(v_3 v_4),\dots$; multiplying the $k$ paths just built (multiplication is continuous) connects $a$ to $(-1)^k\in\{+1,-1\}$. Finally $+1$ and $-1$ are connected by the path $\gamma(t) = \cos t + \sin t\,(e_1\cdot e_2)$, $t\in[0,\pi]$, which lies in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ (it equals $u_t\cdot e_1$ with the unit vectors $u_t = -\cos t\,e_1 - \sin t\,e_2$ and $e_1$; see Lemma 6, Step 4, for the identity $u_t\cdot e_1 = \cos t + \sin t\,e_1 e_2$) and satisfies $\gamma(0) = 1$, $\gamma(\pi) = -1$. Hence every element is connected to $1$: $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is path-connected, so connected.
> >
> > **Step 3 — Lie structure.** The units $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$ form a Lie group: they are an open subset of the finite-dimensional vector space $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$ (the determinant of left multiplication is a nonzero continuous function on them), with smooth multiplication and inversion. By Steps 1–2, $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a closed subgroup of this Lie group. By the **[[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem|closed subgroup theorem]]** — a subgroup of a Lie group that is closed as a subset is an embedded smooth submanifold, hence itself a Lie group in the subspace topology — $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is an embedded Lie subgroup. Being additionally compact and connected (Steps 1–2), it is a compact connected Lie group.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a compact connected embedded Lie subgroup of $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 6: the differential of $\xi$ is a Lie algebra isomorphism, and $\xi$ is a two-sheeted covering
> **Statement:** The one-parameter subgroups $\gamma_{ij}(t) = \cos t + \sin t\,(e_i\cdot e_j)$ ($i<j$) show $e_i\cdot e_j\in\mathfrak{spin}(n) = T_1\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, and $d_1\xi(e_i\cdot e_j)$ is the skew-symmetric endomorphism $u\mapsto [e_i\cdot e_j, u]$ sending $e_i\mapsto 2e_j$, $e_j\mapsto -2e_i$, $e_k\mapsto 0$ ($k\ne i,j$). Hence $\mathfrak{spin}(n) = \operatorname{span}\{e_i\cdot e_j : i<j\}$ has dimension $\binom{n}{2}$, and $d_1\xi : \mathfrak{spin}(n)\to\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is a Lie algebra isomorphism with inverse $\lambda(A) = \tfrac14\sum_{i,j}\langle Ae_i,e_j\rangle e_i\cdot e_j$. Being a homomorphism of Lie groups whose differential is an isomorphism, with discrete two-element kernel and connected source, $\xi$ is a two-sheeted covering map and a local diffeomorphism.
>
> **Hint:** Differentiate $\xi(\gamma(t))u = \gamma(t)u\gamma(t)^{-1}$ at $t = 0$ to get the commutator; the injectivity of $d_1\xi$ comes from the discreteness of $\ker\xi$ via the exponential.
>
> **Why needed:** Part (iii), the identification of $\mathfrak{spin}(n)$ in part (i), and the covering property.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Step 1 — $e_i\cdot e_j$ are tangent vectors.** For $i\ne j$ the curve $\gamma_{ij}(t) = \cos t + \sin t\,(e_i\cdot e_j)$ lies in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ (Step 4 below writes it as a product of two unit vectors), with $\gamma_{ij}(0) = 1$ and $\gamma_{ij}'(0) = e_i\cdot e_j$. Hence $e_i\cdot e_j\in T_1\mathrm{Spin}(n) = \mathfrak{spin}(n)$ for all $i<j$, so $\operatorname{span}\{e_i\cdot e_j : i<j\}\subseteq\mathfrak{spin}(n)$.
> >
> > **Step 2 — compute the differential.** By the **[[Thm - Lie Group Homomorphism Induces Lie Algebra Homomorphism|Lie functor]]** (a smooth homomorphism $f$ of Lie groups has a differential $d_ef$ that is a Lie algebra homomorphism, with $f\circ\exp = \exp\circ\,d_ef$), $d_1\xi$ is a Lie algebra homomorphism $\mathfrak{spin}(n)\to\mathfrak{so}(n)$. Differentiating $\xi(\gamma_{ij}(t))u = \gamma_{ij}(t)\cdot u\cdot\gamma_{ij}(t)^{-1}$ at $t = 0$, with $\gamma_{ij}(0) = 1$ and $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_0\gamma_{ij}^{-1} = -\gamma_{ij}'(0) = -e_i e_j$,
> > $$d_1\xi(e_i e_j)(u) = \gamma_{ij}'(0)\cdot u\cdot 1 + 1\cdot u\cdot\big(-\gamma_{ij}'(0)\big) = (e_i e_j)\cdot u - u\cdot(e_i e_j) = [e_i e_j, u] \qquad \text{(product rule)}.$$
> > Evaluate the commutator on basis vectors (writing $e_ie_j$ for $e_i\cdot e_j$; recall $e_ie_i = -1$, $e_ie_j = -e_je_i$):
> > $$[e_i e_j, e_i] = e_i e_j e_i - e_i e_i e_j = (-e_i e_i e_j) - (-e_j) = e_j + e_j = 2e_j \qquad \text{(} e_j e_i = -e_i e_j,\ e_i e_i = -1\text{)},$$
> > $$[e_i e_j, e_j] = e_i e_j e_j - e_j e_i e_j = -e_i - (-e_i e_j e_j) = -e_i - e_i = -2e_i \qquad \text{(} e_j e_j = -1,\ e_j e_i = -e_i e_j\text{)},$$
> > $$[e_i e_j, e_k] = 0 \quad (k\ne i,j) \qquad \text{(} e_k \text{ anticommutes with both } e_i \text{ and } e_j\text{, so commutes with } e_i e_j\text{)}.$$
> > So $d_1\xi(e_i e_j)$ sends $e_i\mapsto 2e_j$, $e_j\mapsto -2e_i$, and fixes $\operatorname{span}\{e_k : k\ne i,j\}$ at $0$. Its matrix is $2(E_{ji} - E_{ij})$, which is skew-symmetric, so $d_1\xi(e_i e_j)\in\mathfrak{so}(n)$; and the images $\{2(E_{ji} - E_{ij}) : i<j\}$ are $2$ times the standard basis of $\mathfrak{so}(n)$, hence linearly independent and spanning.
> >
> > **Step 3 — injectivity, dimension, and the inverse $\lambda$.** The kernel $\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$ (Lemma 4) is discrete. If $X\in\ker d_1\xi$, then $\xi(\exp(tX)) = \exp(t\,d_1\xi(X)) = \exp(0) = I_n$ for all $t$ (Lie functor), so the continuous curve $t\mapsto\exp(tX)$ lies in the discrete set $\{\pm 1\}$ and starts at $\exp(0) = 1$; hence $\exp(tX) \equiv 1$ and $X = \tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_0\exp(tX) = 0$. So $d_1\xi$ is injective on $\mathfrak{spin}(n)$. Injectivity into the $\binom{n}{2}$-dimensional space $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ gives $\dim\mathfrak{spin}(n)\le\binom{n}{2}$; combined with the reverse inequality from Step 1 (the $\binom{n}{2}$ independent vectors $e_i e_j$, $i<j$), we get
> > $$\mathfrak{spin}(n) = \operatorname{span}\{e_i\cdot e_j : i<j\}, \qquad \dim\mathfrak{spin}(n) = \tbinom{n}{2} = \dim\mathfrak{so}(n),$$
> > and the injective linear map $d_1\xi$ between equidimensional spaces is a linear isomorphism; being also a Lie algebra homomorphism, it is a Lie algebra isomorphism. To identify the inverse, let $A\in\mathfrak{so}(n)$ with entries $a_{ij} := \langle Ae_i,e_j\rangle = -a_{ji}$, $a_{ii} = 0$. Using $a_{ji} = -a_{ij}$ and $e_j e_i = -e_i e_j$,
> > $$\lambda(A) = \tfrac14\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}e_i e_j = \tfrac14\Big(\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}e_i e_j + \sum_{i>j}a_{ij}e_i e_j\Big) = \tfrac14\Big(\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}e_i e_j + \sum_{i<j}(-a_{ij})(-e_i e_j)\Big) = \tfrac12\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}\,e_i e_j.$$
> > Applying $d_1\xi$ (Step 2) and evaluating on $e_p$,
> > $$d_1\xi(\lambda(A))e_p = \tfrac12\sum_{i<j}a_{ij}\,d_1\xi(e_i e_j)e_p = \tfrac12\Big(\sum_{j>p}a_{pj}(2e_j) + \sum_{i<p}a_{ip}(-2e_i)\Big) = \sum_{j>p}a_{pj}e_j + \sum_{i<p}a_{pi}e_i = \sum_{q}a_{pq}e_q = Ae_p,$$
> > using $-a_{ip} = a_{pi}$ in the middle step and $a_{pp} = 0$ at the end. Hence $d_1\xi\circ\lambda = \mathrm{id}_{\mathfrak{so}(n)}$, so $\lambda = (d_1\xi)^{-1}$.
> >
> > **Step 4 — the reference curve as a product of unit vectors.** For unit vectors $u_t := -\cos t\,e_1 - \sin t\,e_2$ (indeed $|u_t|^2 = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$) and $e_1$,
> > $$u_t\cdot e_1 = (-\cos t\,e_1 - \sin t\,e_2)\cdot e_1 = -\cos t\,(e_1 e_1) - \sin t\,(e_2 e_1) = \cos t + \sin t\,(e_1 e_2) \qquad \text{(} e_1 e_1 = -1,\ e_2 e_1 = -e_1 e_2\text{)},$$
> > so $\gamma(t) = \cos t + \sin t\,(e_1 e_2)\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ for all $t$, as used in Step 1 and in Lemma 5.
> >
> > **Step 5 — local diffeomorphism and covering.** Since $d_1\xi$ is a linear isomorphism, the **[[Thm - The Inverse Function Theorem|inverse function theorem]]** (a smooth map whose differential at a point is invertible restricts to a diffeomorphism of a neighbourhood onto its image) gives an open neighbourhood $V_0$ of $1$ in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ mapped diffeomorphically by $\xi$ onto an open neighbourhood of $I_n$ in $SO(n)$. By homogeneity — $\xi\circ L_a = L_{\xi(a)}\circ\xi$ for each $a$, where $L$ denotes left translation, a diffeomorphism — $\xi$ is a local diffeomorphism at every point. In particular $\xi$ is open, and its image is an open subgroup of the connected group $SO(n)$, hence all of $SO(n)$: $\xi$ is surjective (also seen directly from Lemma 2). The fibres are the cosets of $\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$: $\xi^{-1}(\xi(a)) = a\{\pm 1\} = \{a, -a\}$, two distinct points since $a\ne -a$ (as $2a\ne 0$).
> >
> > To see that $\xi$ is evenly covering, shrink $V_0$ to an open neighbourhood $V$ of $1$ with $V\cap(-V) = \varnothing$: the map $(x,y)\mapsto x^{-1}y$ is continuous and sends $(1,1)\mapsto 1\ne -1$, so there is a neighbourhood $V$ of $1$ with $x^{-1}y\ne -1$ for all $x,y\in V$, i.e. $-x\notin V$, i.e. $V\cap(-V) = \varnothing$; keep $V\subseteq V_0$ so $\xi|_V$ is a diffeomorphism onto the open set $U := \xi(V)\ni I_n$. Then $\xi^{-1}(U) = V\sqcup(-V)$: if $\xi(b)\in U$, pick the unique $v\in V$ with $\xi(v) = \xi(b)$; then $bv^{-1}\in\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$, so $b = \pm v\in V\cup(-V)$, and the union is disjoint. Both $\xi|_V$ and $\xi|_{-V}$ (which equals $u\mapsto\xi(-u) = \xi(u)$) are diffeomorphisms onto $U$, so $U$ is evenly covered by two sheets. For a general point $\xi(a)\in SO(n)$, the translated neighbourhood $\xi(a)U$ has $\xi^{-1}(\xi(a)U) = aV\sqcup(-a)V$, again two sheets each mapped diffeomorphically (translations are diffeomorphisms intertwined by $\xi$). By the definition of a **[[Def - Covering Space|covering space]]** (a map for which every point of the base has an evenly covered neighbourhood), $\xi$ is a two-sheeted covering map.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** $d\xi = d_1\xi : \mathfrak{spin}(n)\to\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is a Lie algebra isomorphism with inverse $\lambda$, and $\xi$ is a two-sheeted covering map and a local diffeomorphism. $\blacksquare$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Fix $n\ge 2$. We assemble the six lemmas.
>
> **Step 0 — $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a subgroup and $\xi$ is a homomorphism into $SO(n)$.** That $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is closed under multiplication and inversion is recalled in the Notation section (a product of two even unit-vector words is an even word; $(v_1\cdots v_{2k})^{-1} = v_{2k}\cdots v_1$), and $1$ is the empty word, so $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a subgroup of $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$. By **Lemma 1**, for $a = v_1\cdots v_{2k}\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ the conjugation $\xi(a) = R_{v_1}\circ\cdots\circ R_{v_{2k}}$ preserves $\mathbb{R}^n$ and lies in $SO(n)$; and $\xi(ab) = \xi(a)\xi(b)$ because conjugation is a homomorphism. So $\xi : \mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)$ is a well-defined group homomorphism.
>
> **Part (i) — Lie group.** By **Lemma 5**, $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a compact connected embedded Lie subgroup of $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$. By **Lemma 6** (Steps 1, 3), its Lie algebra is $T_1\mathrm{Spin}(n) = \mathfrak{spin}(n) = \operatorname{span}\{e_i\cdot e_j : i<j\}$, of dimension $\binom{n}{2}$.
>
> **Part (ii) — covering homomorphism.** $\xi$ is a homomorphism into $SO(n)$ (Step 0); it is smooth because conjugation $a\mapsto(u\mapsto a\cdot u\cdot a^{-1})$ is smooth on the Lie group $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$ (multiplication is polynomial, inversion is smooth on a Lie group) and restricts smoothly to the embedded submanifold $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, with values in the embedded submanifold $SO(n)\subset GL(\mathbb{R}^n)$; so $\xi$ is a Lie group homomorphism. It is surjective by **Lemma 2** (every $T\in SO(n)$ is $R_{v_1}\cdots R_{v_{2k}} = \xi(v_1\cdots v_{2k})$) — equivalently by the open-image argument in **Lemma 6**, Step 5. Its kernel is $\{\pm 1\}$ by **Lemma 4** (Step 3), and $\{\pm 1\}$ is central by **Lemma 4** (Step 4).
>
> **Part (iii) — covering and Lie algebra isomorphism.** By **Lemma 6** (Step 5), $\xi$ is a two-sheeted covering map and a local diffeomorphism; by **Lemma 6** (Steps 2–3), its differential $d\xi = d_1\xi : \mathfrak{spin}(n)\to\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is a Lie algebra isomorphism with inverse
> $$\lambda(A) = \tfrac14\sum_{i,j=1}^n\langle Ae_i,e_j\rangle\,e_i\cdot e_j.$$
>
> **Part (iv) — nontriviality.** By **Lemma 6** (Step 4), $\gamma(t) = \cos t + \sin t\,(e_1\cdot e_2)$ lies in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ for all $t$; evaluating, $\gamma(0) = \cos 0 + \sin 0\,(e_1 e_2) = 1$ and $\gamma(\pi) = \cos\pi + \sin\pi\,(e_1 e_2) = -1$. Both endpoints lie in $\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\} = \xi^{-1}(I_n)$ (Part (ii)), so $\gamma|_{[0,\pi]}$ is a path in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ joining the two distinct points of the fibre over $I_n$. Since $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is connected (Part (i)) while $\xi^{-1}(I_n)$ has two elements, the covering is nontrivial: a trivial double cover of the connected group $SO(n)$ would be $SO(n)\times\{\pm 1\}$, which is disconnected (two components), so no isomorphism of covering groups $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\cong SO(n)\times\{\pm 1\}$ can exist. Concretely, $\xi\circ\gamma$ is the loop $t\mapsto\exp\!\big(t\,d_1\xi(e_1 e_2)\big)$ of rotations through angle $2t$ in the $e_1$–$e_2$ plane (Lemma 6, Step 2: $d_1\xi(e_1 e_2)$ has $e_1\mapsto 2e_2$), a full $2\pi$ rotation as $t : 0\to\pi$, whose lift $\gamma$ fails to close — the geometric signature of the double cover.
>
> **Therefore**, for every $n\ge 2$, $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\subset\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$ is a compact connected Lie group and $\xi : \mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)$ is a surjective two-sheeted covering homomorphism with central kernel $\{\pm 1\}$ and Lie algebra isomorphism $d\xi$, and the cover is nontrivial. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Quantum mechanics — the $2\pi$ sign of a spinor.** A spin-$\tfrac12$ state transforms under rotations by an element of $SU(2) = \mathrm{Spin}(3)$, and rotating by $2\pi$ multiplies the state by $-1$, not $+1$. The theorem applies because the rotation $R_\theta\in SO(3)$ about a fixed axis, traversed once as $\theta : 0\to 2\pi$, is the noncontractible loop whose lift to $\mathrm{Spin}(3)$ is the path $\gamma$ from $1$ to $-1$ of part (iv); the state, being a vector in the spinor representation, is multiplied by the endpoint $-1$. This is non-obvious because the *physics* (the state changes sign) looks like a puzzle until one sees it is exactly the failure of the lift to close, i.e. part (iv) in dimension three, made visible by the neutron-interferometry experiments that measured it.

**Numerical linear algebra — Householder factorisation as a Clifford word.** The QR algorithm factors an orthogonal matrix into Householder reflections $R_{v}(u) = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v$. The theorem's Lemma 2 (Cartan–Dieudonné) is precisely the statement that this factorisation terminates in at most $n$ steps, and Lemma 1 says each Householder step is twisted conjugation by the unit vector $v$ in $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$; a rotation (determinant $+1$) needs an even number of steps and so lifts to an element of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$. The application is non-obvious because a working numerical analyst rarely notices that the reflection vectors they compute are literally coordinates of a $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ element covering the orthogonal matrix being factored.

**Lie theory — recognising a coincidental cover.** Suppose one is handed a compact connected Lie group $G$, a surjective homomorphism $p : G\to SO(n)$, and told that $dp$ is an isomorphism and $\ker p$ has two elements. The theorem's Lemma 6 shows $p$ is then a two-sheeted covering, and since connected double covers of a connected group are unique up to isomorphism over the base, $G\cong\mathrm{Spin}(n)$. This is exactly the argument identifying $Sp(1)\cong\mathrm{Spin}(3)$ and $Sp(1)\times Sp(1)\cong\mathrm{Spin}(4)$ (**[[Thm - Spin Groups in Dimensions Three and Four via Quaternions]]**); the non-obvious point is that one verifies "double cover" purely infinitesimally (the differential) plus a kernel count, never touching the fundamental group.

# Bridges

- **[[Thm - Spin Groups in Dimensions Three and Four via Quaternions]]** — the low-dimensional models. There the quaternionic groups $Sp(1)$ and $Sp(1)\times Sp(1)$ are shown to be double covers of $SO(3)$ and $SO(4)$ by direct quaternion computations, and the Clifford isomorphisms $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$, $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$ (**[[Thm - Low-Dimensional Clifford Algebras and the Quaternionic Spinor Modules]]**) carry the abstract $\mathrm{Spin}(3),\mathrm{Spin}(4)$ of this page onto them, with $\xi$ becoming the quaternion-conjugation maps $q\mapsto(h\mapsto qh\bar q)$ and $(q_+,q_-)\mapsto(h\mapsto q_+ h\bar q_-)$. In particular the centrality of $\{\pm 1\}$ (Lemma 4) is "clear for $n = 3, 4$" precisely because $-1 = -1\in Sp(1)$ is a central quaternion scalar.

- **[[Thm - SU(2) is the Double Cover of SO(3)]]** — the matrix incarnation. Under $Sp(1)\cong SU(2)$ (right multiplication by $\bar i$ makes $\mathbb{H}$ a complex $2$-dimensional space), the $n = 3$ case of this theorem is exactly the classical statement that $SU(2)\to SO(3)$ is a nontrivial double cover; that page's $2\times 2$-matrix proof and this page's Clifford proof are the same theorem in two languages.

- **[[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator]]** — the reason the theorem is needed. A spinor field over an oriented Riemannian manifold is a section of a bundle associated to a lift of the frame bundle from $SO(n)$ to $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ along $\xi$; the isomorphism $\lambda : \mathfrak{so}(n)\to\mathfrak{spin}(n)$ of part (iii) is what transports the Levi-Civita connection to the spin connection, and hence what makes the Dirac operator writable. Without part (iii) there is no way to differentiate spinors.

- **The Pin group and $O(n)$.** Dropping the parity constraint from the definition — allowing products of *any* number of unit vectors — gives $\mathrm{Pin}(n)\subset\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$, and the *twisted* adjoint $\tilde\xi$ (which sees single reflections, Lemma 1) makes $\mathrm{Pin}(n)$ a double cover of the full orthogonal group $O(n)$. The present theorem is the restriction to the even part $\mathrm{Cl}^0$, where $\tilde\xi = \xi$ and the image is the identity component $SO(n)$; Cartan–Dieudonné (Lemma 2) governs both, with the parity of the number of reflections separating $SO(n)$ from $O(n)\setminus SO(n)$.

> [!tip] Spin structures and spinor bundles *(from Spin Geometry)*
> With $\xi$ established as a covering homomorphism, one can ask when the frame bundle of an oriented Riemannian manifold lifts along $\xi$ — the existence question for **spin structures** — and, given a lift, build the spinor bundle and the **[[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator|Dirac operator]]**. > [!tip] $\mathrm{Spin}^c(n)$ and Seiberg–Witten theory *(from Gauge Theory)*
> Enlarging $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ to $\mathrm{Spin}^c(n) = \mathrm{Spin}(n)\times U(1)/\{\pm 1\}$ — legitimate precisely because $\{\pm 1\}$ is the central kernel identified in part (ii) — yields the structure group of Seiberg–Witten theory (chapter XI), where the $U(1)$ factor carries the determinant line bundle and its connection. The centrality of $\{\pm 1\}$ proved in Lemma 4 is exactly what makes the diagonal quotient a group.
