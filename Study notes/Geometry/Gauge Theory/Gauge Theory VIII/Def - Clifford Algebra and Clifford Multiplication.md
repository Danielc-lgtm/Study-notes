# Notation

Throughout this page $U$ denotes a finite-dimensional real [[Def - Inner Product Space|Euclidean vector space]], that is, a real vector space equipped with a positive-definite inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$; we write $|u|^2 = \langle u,u\rangle$ for the squared length of $u\in U$. The model case is $U = \mathbb{R}^n$ with the standard inner product and standard orthonormal basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$, so that $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$. We write $TU = \mathbb{R}\oplus U\oplus (U\otimes U)\oplus\cdots = \bigoplus_{k\ge 0} U^{\otimes k}$ for the [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces|tensor algebra]] of $U$, with multiplication given by concatenation of tensors (the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|universal]] associative unital algebra generated by $U$), and $1\in\mathbb{R} = U^{\otimes 0}$ for its unit. The Clifford product is written by juxtaposition or with a dot, $u\cdot v$ or $uv$; it is never the inner product, which is always written $\langle u,v\rangle$. The symbol $\alpha$ denotes the grading automorphism defined below; $\mathrm{Cl}^0(U)$ and $\mathrm{Cl}^1(U)$ its two eigenspaces. For a subset $I = \{i_1 < \cdots < i_k\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ we write $e_I = e_{i_1}e_{i_2}\cdots e_{i_k}$ for the ordered Clifford product of the corresponding basis vectors, with $e_\emptyset = 1$. For the exterior-algebra example we write $\Lambda U^* = \bigoplus_k \Lambda^k U^*$ for the exterior algebra of the dual, $\iota_u$ for the [[Def - Interior Product (Contraction with a Vector Field)|interior product]] (contraction) with $u$, $u^\flat = \langle u,\cdot\rangle\in U^*$ for the metric dual $1$-form, and $\wedge$ for the exterior product. We write $\mathbb{H}$ for the [[Def - Quaternions|quaternions]] with conjugation $h\mapsto\bar h$ and $\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H} = \{h : \bar h = -h\}$ for the imaginary quaternions.

This is a compound page: it defines three interlocking notions — the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$, its canonical $\mathbb{Z}/2$-grading $\mathrm{Cl}(U) = \mathrm{Cl}^0(U)\oplus\mathrm{Cl}^1(U)$, and the modules over $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ (Clifford modules) — because they are inseparable in use: the algebra exists to act on modules, the grading is what splits a module into its chiral halves, and every later object of the chapter (spinor bundles, the Dirac operator, the spin groups) is assembled from these three at once.

> [!warning] Convention: the sign of the Clifford relation
> We follow Haydys and take the defining relation to be $u\cdot u = -|u|^2\cdot 1$ (equivalently $v^2 = -q(v)$ with $q$ positive-definite, the convention of Lawson–Michelsohn, *Spin Geometry*). The vault's [[Def - Clifford Algebra|general Clifford algebra page]] writes the relation as $\varphi(v)^2 = Q(v)\cdot 1$ for a possibly indefinite quadratic form $Q$. The two agree under the dictionary
> $$\mathrm{Cl}(U) \;=\; \mathrm{Cl}\big(U,\; Q\big) \quad\text{with}\quad Q(u) = -|u|^2,$$
> so that our $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ is the vault's Clifford algebra of the *negative-definite* form. In the alternative sign convention $v^2 = +|v|^2$ one obtains a genuinely different real algebra (for $U = \mathbb{R}^2$ one gets $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ rather than $\mathbb{H}$; see the Examples). Every page in this chapter that invokes the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] does so with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$, and every claim of the form "$v^2 = -|v|^2$" is read in our convention.

# Axiom Motivation

The Clifford algebra is the answer to a single, sharply posed question: **is there an associative algebra in which every vector is a square root of (minus) its own squared length?** The reason to want such a thing is Dirac's, and it is worth stating before the definition, because the definition is forced by the goal and can be reinvented from it.

The scalar Laplacian on $\mathbb{R}^n$, in the geometer's positive sign, is $\Delta = -\sum_{i=1}^n \partial_i^2$, a second-order operator. Dirac's idea was to factor it as the square of a *first-order* operator with constant coefficients,
$$D = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i\,\partial_i,$$
where the coefficients $e_1,\dots,e_n$ are as yet unknown symbols. Squaring formally and using that the second partial derivatives commute, $\partial_i\partial_j = \partial_j\partial_i$, we obtain
$$D^2 = \sum_{i,j} e_i e_j\,\partial_i\partial_j = \sum_{i<j}(e_ie_j + e_je_i)\,\partial_i\partial_j + \sum_i e_i^2\,\partial_i^2,$$
because in the double sum only the part of $e_ie_j$ symmetric in the exchange $i\leftrightarrow j$ survives against the symmetric $\partial_i\partial_j$. For this to equal the Laplacian $\Delta = -\sum_i\partial_i^2$ we need the cross terms to vanish and each square to be $-1$:
$$e_ie_j + e_je_i = 0 \quad (i\neq j), \qquad e_i^2 = -1.$$
These two families of relations are precisely $u\cdot u = -|u|^2$ read on the orthonormal basis, together with its polarisation. So the desideratum "a first-order square root of the Laplacian" and the desideratum "vectors square to minus their length" are the same desideratum, and the algebra generated by $U$ under exactly these relations is the smallest home in which $D$ can live. It has no name yet; the definition below simply gives it one.

We now ask what each ingredient of the relation is for, by removing it and seeing what breaks.

**Why the square is a scalar at all, and not zero.** The most tempting weaker relation is $u\cdot u = 0$. It is not idle: it is the defining relation of the *exterior algebra* $\Lambda U$, and it makes $D = \sum e_i\partial_i$ into (a version of) the exterior derivative $d$, for which $d^2 = 0$. But $d^2 = 0$ is exactly the failure we cannot tolerate: an operator whose square is zero is no square root of the Laplacian, and more generally an algebra in which vectors are nilpotent can never produce a second-order operator by squaring a first-order one. The scalar $-|u|^2$ on the right-hand side is what forces $D^2$ to be a genuine Laplacian rather than zero. The Clifford algebra is thus a deformation of the exterior algebra in which the quadratic form, previously invisible, is written into the multiplication.

**Why the inner product is needed.** Without a Euclidean structure on $U$ there is no quantity $|u|^2$ to put on the right, and no polarisation $uv + vu = -2\langle u,v\rangle$ relating distinct generators; one is left again with $u\cdot u = 0$, the exterior algebra, or with an ill-posed relation. The inner product is not decoration: it is the entire content of the multiplication beyond concatenation. Two Euclidean spaces of the same dimension give isomorphic Clifford algebras precisely because their inner products are isometric.

**Why the sign is minus, and what the plus sign gives instead.** The choice $u\cdot u = -|u|^2$ (rather than $+|u|^2$) is a genuine choice, not a normalisation one can absorb: the two signs produce non-isomorphic real algebras. With the minus sign the two-dimensional Clifford algebra is the quaternions $\mathbb{H}$ (a division algebra); with the plus sign it is the matrix algebra $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ (which has zero divisors), as the Examples verify. We take the minus sign for two reasons that recur throughout the chapter: it makes Clifford multiplication by a unit vector *skew-adjoint* rather than self-adjoint, hence makes the Dirac operator formally self-adjoint against the geometer's Laplacian $\Delta = -\sum\partial_i^2 = D^2$ (with the plus sign one would get $D^2 = +\sum\partial_i^2 = -\Delta$); and it makes the low-dimensional algebras the quaternionic ones from which the spin representations are built. A module on which vectors satisfy $u\cdot(u\cdot v) = +|u|^2 v$ is not a mistake — it is a module over the *other* Clifford algebra, and the Examples exhibit one so the distinction is concrete.

A reader who has internalised these three points could write the definition unaided: take the free associative unital algebra on $U$ (the tensor algebra), and impose the single relation that each vector squares to minus its length. Everything else — the polarisation, the anticommutation of distinct basis vectors, the dimension $2^n$, the grading — is a consequence, derived below.

# The Definition

## The algebra

Let $U$ be a Euclidean vector space. The **Clifford algebra** of $U$ is the quotient of the tensor algebra by the two-sided ideal generated by the elements $u\otimes u + |u|^2\cdot 1$:
$$\mathrm{Cl}(U) \;:=\; TU \,\big/\, \big\langle\, u\otimes u + |u|^2\cdot 1 \;:\; u\in U \,\big\rangle.$$
It is an associative, unital $\mathbb{R}$-algebra. The composite $U\hookrightarrow TU\twoheadrightarrow \mathrm{Cl}(U)$ is an injective linear map (injectivity is part of the dimension computation below), through which we regard $U$ as a subspace of $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$; on this subspace the image of the ideal is exactly the relation
$$u\cdot u = -|u|^2\cdot 1 \qquad\text{for all } u\in U. \tag{Cl}$$
Equivalently, and this is the working description, $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ is the associative unital $\mathbb{R}$-algebra generated by the vectors of $U$ subject only to the relations (Cl).

**Polarisation.** The relation (Cl) on a single vector is equivalent, in the presence of the vector-space structure, to a relation between distinct vectors. Applying (Cl) to the vector $u+v$ and expanding by bilinearity of the inner product and associativity of the product,
$$-|u+v|^2\cdot 1 = (u+v)(u+v) = u\cdot u + u\cdot v + v\cdot u + v\cdot v \qquad\text{(distributivity of the Clifford product)}$$
$$= -|u|^2\cdot 1 + u\cdot v + v\cdot u - |v|^2\cdot 1 \qquad\text{(by (Cl) applied to } u \text{ and to } v\text{)},$$
and since $|u+v|^2 = |u|^2 + 2\langle u,v\rangle + |v|^2$, cancelling the terms $-|u|^2\cdot 1$ and $-|v|^2\cdot 1$ from both sides leaves the **polarised Clifford relation**
$$u\cdot v + v\cdot u = -2\langle u,v\rangle\cdot 1 \qquad\text{for all } u,v\in U. \tag{Cl$'$}$$
Conversely (Cl$'$) with $v = u$ returns $2\,u\cdot u = -2|u|^2\cdot 1$, that is (Cl); so the two relations carry the same information.

**The relations on an orthonormal basis.** Take $U = \mathbb{R}^n$ with orthonormal basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$, so $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$. Then (Cl$'$) reads
$$e_i e_j + e_j e_i = -2\delta_{ij}\cdot 1,$$
which is the pair of relations
$$e_i^2 = -1 \quad (\text{the case } i = j), \qquad e_i e_j = -e_j e_i \quad (\text{the case } i\neq j).$$
Thus $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is generated by $1, e_1,\dots,e_n$ with each generator squaring to $-1$ and distinct generators anticommuting. Using $e_ie_j = -e_je_i$ any product of generators can be reordered into increasing index order (at the cost of a sign), and using $e_i^2 = -1$ any repeated index can be removed (at the cost of a sign); hence every element of $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is a real linear combination of the $2^n$ ordered monomials
$$e_I = e_{i_1}e_{i_2}\cdots e_{i_k}, \qquad I = \{i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\},\quad e_\emptyset = 1.$$
That these $2^n$ monomials are moreover linearly independent — so that $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ and the inclusion $U\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ is injective — is proved in the Examples via the exterior-algebra module, which is faithful.

## The $\mathbb{Z}/2$-grading

The linear map $U\to U$, $u\mapsto -u$, followed by the inclusion into $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$, is a linear map $\varphi:U\to\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ with $\varphi(u)^2 = (-u)(-u) = u\cdot u = -|u|^2\cdot 1$, so it satisfies the Clifford relation. By the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] (restated in the Categorical section below, applied with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$) there is a unique algebra homomorphism
$$\alpha:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\mathrm{Cl}(U), \qquad \alpha(u) = -u \ \text{ for } u\in U,$$
the **grading automorphism**. Since $\alpha^2$ is an algebra homomorphism restricting to the identity on the generating set $U$, uniqueness in the universal property forces $\alpha^2 = \operatorname{id}$; in particular $\alpha$ is an automorphism of order two. Its $\pm 1$ eigenspaces
$$\mathrm{Cl}^0(U) := \{a : \alpha(a) = a\}, \qquad \mathrm{Cl}^1(U) := \{a : \alpha(a) = -a\}$$
give a direct-sum decomposition $\mathrm{Cl}(U) = \mathrm{Cl}^0(U)\oplus\mathrm{Cl}^1(U)$ (every $a$ is the sum of $\tfrac12(a+\alpha(a))\in\mathrm{Cl}^0$ and $\tfrac12(a-\alpha(a))\in\mathrm{Cl}^1$, and the two summands meet only in $0$). Because $\alpha$ is an algebra homomorphism, $\alpha(ab) = \alpha(a)\alpha(b)$, so on eigenvectors the eigenvalues multiply:
$$\mathrm{Cl}^i(U)\cdot\mathrm{Cl}^j(U)\subseteq\mathrm{Cl}^{i+j\bmod 2}(U) \qquad (i,j\in\{0,1\}).$$
This makes $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ a **$\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded algebra** (a superalgebra). Elements of $\mathrm{Cl}^0(U)$ are called **even**, those of $\mathrm{Cl}^1(U)$ **odd**. On the monomial basis, $\alpha(e_I) = (-1)^{|I|}e_I$, so $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is spanned by the $e_I$ with $|I|$ even and $\mathrm{Cl}^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$ by those with $|I|$ odd; each half has dimension $2^{n-1}$. The even part $\mathrm{Cl}^0(U)$ is a subalgebra (it is closed under multiplication, being the $i = j = 0$ case above), and it is the home of the spin group defined on the page [[Def - Spin Group and the Double Cover of SO(n)]].

## Clifford modules

A **Clifford module** for $U$ is a real vector space $V$ together with a bilinear **Clifford multiplication**
$$U\otimes V \to V, \qquad u\otimes v \mapsto u\cdot v,$$
satisfying
$$u\cdot(u\cdot v) = -|u|^2\, v \qquad\text{for all } u\in U,\ v\in V. \tag{Mod}$$
Polarising (Mod) exactly as (Cl) was polarised gives the equivalent bilinear form $u\cdot(w\cdot v) + w\cdot(u\cdot v) = -2\langle u,w\rangle\, v$. The relation (Mod) is precisely the statement that the linear map $c:U\to\operatorname{End}(V)$, $c(u)v = u\cdot v$, satisfies $c(u)^2 = -|u|^2\operatorname{id}_V$, that is, the Clifford relation with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$. Hence, by the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] applied with target algebra $A = \operatorname{End}(V)$, giving $V$ the structure of a Clifford module is **the same data** as giving an algebra homomorphism
$$\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(V):$$
every Clifford multiplication extends uniquely to such a homomorphism (this is the universal property), and conversely every algebra homomorphism $\rho:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(V)$ restricts on $U\subseteq\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ to a Clifford multiplication, since $\rho(u)^2 = \rho(u\cdot u) = \rho(-|u|^2\cdot 1) = -|u|^2\operatorname{id}_V$. A module is called **faithful** when this homomorphism is injective.

A **complex Clifford module** is a *complex* vector space $V$ with an $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear Clifford multiplication satisfying (Mod) and commuting with multiplication by the complex scalar $i$ (equivalently, each $c(u)$ is $\mathbb{C}$-linear). Since $c(u)$ is then $\mathbb{C}$-linear, the associated homomorphism factors through the **complexified Clifford algebra**
$$\mathbb{C}\mathrm{l}(U) := \mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(U),$$
and a complex Clifford module is the same as a $\mathbb{C}$-algebra homomorphism $\mathbb{C}\mathrm{l}(U)\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)$. The complexification satisfies $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{l}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$, with the same monomial basis $\{e_I\}$ now taken over $\mathbb{C}$. The spinor modules built in [[Thm - Low-Dimensional Clifford Algebras and the Quaternionic Spinor Modules]] are complex Clifford modules of this kind.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The Clifford algebra is characterised, without reference to the tensor algebra or any basis, by a universal property, and this is the form in which it is used throughout the chapter (the grading automorphism above, and the module–homomorphism dictionary, are both immediate applications of it). We restate the vault's theorem in our sign convention.

> **Universal property (applied with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$).** Let $\iota:U\to\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ be the canonical inclusion, which satisfies $\iota(u)^2 = -|u|^2\cdot 1$. For every associative unital $\mathbb{R}$-algebra $A$ and every linear map $\varphi:U\to A$ with
> $$\varphi(u)^2 = -|u|^2\cdot 1_A \qquad\text{for all } u\in U,$$
> there exists a unique algebra homomorphism $\tilde\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to A$ with $\tilde\varphi\circ\iota = \varphi$.

The complete proof is on [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property]] (stated there for a general quadratic form $Q$; here $Q(u) = -|u|^2$); it constructs $\tilde\varphi$ as the map induced on the quotient $TU/\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle$ by the algebra homomorphism $TU\to A$ extending $\varphi$, checks it kills the defining ideal because $\varphi(u)^2 + |u|^2\cdot 1_A = 0$, and gets uniqueness because $U$ generates $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ as an algebra. Two consequences of that theorem are used here and stated for completeness: the Clifford algebra is unique up to a unique isomorphism (initial object in the category of pairs $(A,\varphi)$), and it is functorial — every isometry $f$ of Euclidean spaces (indeed every linear map preserving the quadratic form) extends to a unique algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(f)$, so the orthogonal group $O(U)$ acts on $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ by algebra automorphisms. The grading automorphism $\alpha$ is $\mathrm{Cl}(-\operatorname{id}_U)$, the extension of the isometry $u\mapsto -u$; this is why it exists and is unique.

The module picture is the structural payoff: because giving a Clifford module is giving an algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(V)$, the representation theory of the algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ *is* the theory of Clifford multiplications, and classifying the irreducible modules (done for $n = 3,4$ on [[Thm - Low-Dimensional Clifford Algebras and the Quaternionic Spinor Modules]]) classifies the possible spinor spaces.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The Clifford algebra sits between two algebras the reader already knows, and is best understood as an interpolation. Set the inner product to zero (formally, replace $|u|^2$ by $0$ in the relation) and $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ degenerates to the **exterior algebra** $\Lambda U$, in which $u\wedge u = 0$; keep the full quadratic form and one obtains an algebra of the same dimension $2^n$ but with an entirely different, non-nilpotent, multiplication. The exterior algebra and the Clifford algebra are canonically isomorphic *as filtered vector spaces* — the map $a\mapsto a\cdot 1$ into the exterior-algebra module, computed in the Examples, is exactly this isomorphism — but not as algebras. This is the precise sense in which "$\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ is the exterior algebra with the metric switched on," and it is why $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n = \dim\Lambda\mathbb{R}^n$.

In low dimensions the Clifford algebra reproduces the classical **division algebras**: $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^0) = \mathbb{R}$, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\cong\mathbb{C}$, and $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$ (Examples below, and [[Thm - Low-Dimensional Clifford Algebras and the Quaternionic Spinor Modules]]). This is not a coincidence but the beginning of the Clifford functor's role in organising the real division and matrix algebras; the full periodic pattern is the classification theorem, which this series deliberately does not use, proving instead by hand exactly the two dimensions ($n = 3,4$) it needs.

**True name.** Operationally, an element $u\in U$ of a Clifford algebra is *a square root of $-|u|^2$ that anticommutes with the perpendicular directions*. Everything the algebra does downstream flows from this one property: conjugation $u\mapsto v u v^{-1}$ by a unit vector $v$ is (minus) reflection in the hyperplane $v^\perp$, which is why even products of unit vectors realise rotations and generate the spin group; and Clifford multiplication on a module lets one build a first-order operator whose square is the Laplacian, which is the Dirac operator. The official definition, "quotient of the tensor algebra by an ideal," is the construction; "a metric-twisted square root of the negative length" is what it is for.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\cong\mathbb{C}$.** Here $U = \mathbb{R}^1$ with generator $e_1$, $|e_1| = 1$, and the only relation is $e_1^2 = -1$. Thus $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1) = \mathbb{R}\cdot 1\oplus\mathbb{R}\cdot e_1$ is two-dimensional, and the algebra homomorphism $\mathbb{C}\to\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)$ sending $1\mapsto 1$ and $i\mapsto e_1$ is well-defined (because $i^2 = -1 = e_1^2$), bijective (it matches bases), and multiplicative; so $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\cong\mathbb{C}$ as real algebras. The grading is $\mathrm{Cl}^0 = \mathbb{R}\cdot 1$, $\mathrm{Cl}^1 = \mathbb{R}\cdot e_1$, matching the real and imaginary axes.

**Is an instance — $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$.** With orthonormal basis $e_1,e_2$ the relations are $e_1^2 = e_2^2 = -1$ and $e_1e_2 = -e_2e_1$. Set $I = e_1$, $J = e_2$, $K = e_1e_2$. We verify the quaternion multiplication table clause by clause. First $I^2 = e_1^2 = -1$ and $J^2 = e_2^2 = -1$; next
$$K^2 = (e_1e_2)(e_1e_2) = e_1(e_2 e_1)e_2 = e_1(-e_1e_2)e_2 = -e_1^2 e_2^2 = -(-1)(-1) = -1 \qquad\text{(using } e_2e_1 = -e_1e_2 \text{, then } e_1^2 = e_2^2 = -1\text{)}.$$
Then $IJ = e_1e_2 = K$, and $JI = e_2e_1 = -e_1e_2 = -K$; and
$$IK = e_1(e_1e_2) = e_1^2 e_2 = -e_2 = -J, \qquad KI = (e_1e_2)e_1 = e_1(e_2e_1) = e_1(-e_1e_2) = -e_1^2 e_2 = e_2 = J.$$
$$JK = e_2(e_1e_2) = (e_2e_1)e_2 = -e_1e_2^2 = e_1 = I, \qquad KJ = (e_1e_2)e_2 = e_1e_2^2 = -e_1 = -I.$$
So $\{1,I,J,K\}$ satisfy exactly Hamilton's relations $I^2 = J^2 = K^2 = -1$, $IJ = K = -JI$, $JK = I = -KJ$, $KI = J = -IK$, and the linear map $\mathbb{H}\to\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$, $a + bi + cj + dk\mapsto a\cdot 1 + bI + cJ + dK$, is an algebra isomorphism onto the four-dimensional $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$. This is Haydys's E4.1.1 and the low-dimensional theorem's base case.

**Is NOT an instance — the opposite sign gives $M_2(\mathbb{R})$, not $\mathbb{H}$.** To make the sign convention concrete: the algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2, +|\cdot|^2)$ generated by $f_1,f_2$ with $f_1^2 = f_2^2 = +1$ and $f_1f_2 = -f_2f_1$ is *not* isomorphic to $\mathbb{H}$. The assignment $f_1\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $f_2\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ satisfies $f_1^2 = f_2^2 = \operatorname{id}$ and $f_1f_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix} = -f_2f_1$, and extends to an isomorphism onto $M_2(\mathbb{R})$. Since $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ has zero divisors and $\mathbb{H}$ is a division algebra, the two are not isomorphic; the sign in (Cl) is therefore load-bearing, not cosmetic. (A full verification is the drill [[Ex - The Clifford Algebras of R^1 and R^2]].)

**Is an instance — the exterior algebra $\Lambda U^*$ as a faithful $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$-module (Haydys E4.1.2, equation (110)).** Let $V = \Lambda U^*$ and define, for $u\in U$ and $\phi\in\Lambda U^*$,
$$u\cdot\phi := \iota_u\phi - u^\flat\wedge\phi,$$
where $\iota_u$ is the [[Def - Interior Product (Contraction with a Vector Field)|interior product]] with $u$ and $u^\flat = \langle u,\cdot\rangle$ is the metric-dual $1$-form. We verify the module relation (Mod), $u\cdot(u\cdot\phi) = -|u|^2\phi$, clause by clause. Write $\epsilon_u = u^\flat\wedge(\cdot)$ for exterior multiplication by $u^\flat$, so that Clifford multiplication is $c_u = \iota_u - \epsilon_u$. Then
$$c_u^2 = (\iota_u - \epsilon_u)^2 = \iota_u^2 - \iota_u\epsilon_u - \epsilon_u\iota_u + \epsilon_u^2 \qquad\text{(expanding the square; the middle terms do not commute, so both orders are kept)}.$$
The two outer terms vanish: $\iota_u^2 = 0$ because contracting twice with the same vector gives zero (the interior product is an antiderivation squaring to zero), and $\epsilon_u^2 = u^\flat\wedge u^\flat\wedge(\cdot) = 0$ because $u^\flat\wedge u^\flat = 0$. For the middle terms we use the standard anticommutation identity between interior and exterior multiplication by a $1$-form, which is itself an instance of the interior product being an antiderivation:
$$\iota_u(u^\flat\wedge\phi) = (\iota_u u^\flat)\,\phi - u^\flat\wedge\iota_u\phi = \langle u,u\rangle\,\phi - u^\flat\wedge\iota_u\phi \qquad\text{(antiderivation rule; } \iota_u u^\flat = u^\flat(u) = \langle u,u\rangle = |u|^2\text{)},$$
which rearranges to $\iota_u\epsilon_u\phi + \epsilon_u\iota_u\phi = |u|^2\phi$, that is $\iota_u\epsilon_u + \epsilon_u\iota_u = |u|^2\operatorname{id}$. Substituting the two vanishings and this anticommutator,
$$c_u^2 = -(\iota_u\epsilon_u + \epsilon_u\iota_u) = -|u|^2\operatorname{id} \qquad\text{(by } \iota_u^2 = \epsilon_u^2 = 0 \text{ and the anticommutator identity)}.$$
So $u\cdot(u\cdot\phi) = -|u|^2\phi$, and $\Lambda U^*$ is a Clifford module. This example is the workhorse of the whole page, because it is *faithful*, and faithfulness is what pins down the dimension.

**Corollary — $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$, and $U\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ is injective.**

> [!note]- Full proof of the dimension count
> **Goal.** We show the $2^n$ ordered monomials $\{e_I : I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\}$ are a basis of $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$; the spanning half was proved in The Definition, so it remains to prove linear independence.
>
> **Strategy: push the monomials into a space we understand.** Let $c:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)\to\operatorname{End}(\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^n)^*)$ be the algebra homomorphism associated (by the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]], applied with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$) to the faithful exterior-algebra module of the previous example, so $c(e_i) = c_{e_i} = \iota_{e_i} - e^i\wedge(\cdot)$ where $e^i = e_i^\flat$ is the dual basis $1$-form. We evaluate the operator $c(e_I)$ on the element $1\in\Lambda^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^* = \mathbb{R}$ and read off its leading term.
>
> **Step 1 — the action of a single generator on $1$.** For any $i$, $c_{e_i}(1) = \iota_{e_i}(1) - e^i\wedge 1 = 0 - e^i = -e^i$, using $\iota_{e_i}(1) = 0$ (contraction of a $0$-form is zero) and $e^i\wedge 1 = e^i$.
>
> **Step 2 — the action of an ordered monomial on $1$.** Fix $I = \{i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k\}$ and set $\eta = c(e_I)(1) = c_{e_{i_1}}c_{e_{i_2}}\cdots c_{e_{i_k}}(1)$. We claim
> $$c(e_I)(1) = (-1)^k\, e^{i_1}\wedge e^{i_2}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_k},$$
> proved by descending induction on the number of factors applied. **Base:** by Step 1, $c_{e_{i_k}}(1) = -e^{i_k} = (-1)^1 e^{i_k}$. **Inductive step:** suppose $c_{e_{i_{j+1}}}\cdots c_{e_{i_k}}(1) = (-1)^{k-j}\,e^{i_{j+1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_k} =: (-1)^{k-j}\omega$, a wedge of dual $1$-forms whose indices are all strictly greater than $i_j$. Apply $c_{e_{i_j}} = \iota_{e_{i_j}} - e^{i_j}\wedge(\cdot)$:
> $$c_{e_{i_j}}\big((-1)^{k-j}\omega\big) = (-1)^{k-j}\big(\iota_{e_{i_j}}\omega - e^{i_j}\wedge\omega\big).$$
> The contraction $\iota_{e_{i_j}}\omega$ vanishes: $\omega$ is a wedge of the $1$-forms $e^{i_{j+1}},\dots,e^{i_k}$, none of whose index equals $i_j$ (they are all larger), so $\iota_{e_{i_j}}e^{i_m} = \delta_{i_j,i_m} = 0$ for each factor, and by the antiderivation rule the whole contraction is zero. Hence
> $$c_{e_{i_j}}\big((-1)^{k-j}\omega\big) = -(-1)^{k-j}\, e^{i_j}\wedge\omega = (-1)^{k-j+1}\, e^{i_j}\wedge e^{i_{j+1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_k},$$
> which is the claim with $j$ in place of $j+1$. This completes the induction, and taking $j = 1$ gives $c(e_I)(1) = (-1)^k e^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_k}$.
>
> **Step 3 — independence.** The elements $\{e^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_k} : I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\}$ are, up to reindexing, the standard monomial basis of the exterior algebra $\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^n)^*$, hence linearly independent. Suppose $\sum_I \lambda_I\, e_I = 0$ in $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ with $\lambda_I\in\mathbb{R}$. Applying the algebra homomorphism $c$ and then evaluating at $1$,
> $$0 = c\Big(\sum_I \lambda_I e_I\Big)(1) = \sum_I \lambda_I\, c(e_I)(1) = \sum_I \lambda_I\,(-1)^{|I|}\, e^{i_1}\wedge\cdots \qquad\text{(by Step 2)},$$
> and since distinct $I$ give distinct (independent) exterior monomials, every coefficient $\lambda_I(-1)^{|I|}$, hence every $\lambda_I$, is zero. Therefore the $\{e_I\}$ are linearly independent.
>
> **Conclusion.** The $2^n$ monomials $\{e_I\}$ both span (from The Definition) and are independent (Steps 1–3), so they are a basis and $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$. In particular the degree-one monomials $e_1,\dots,e_n$ are independent, so the inclusion $U\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ is injective, and the computation also shows $c$ is injective, that is, the exterior-algebra module is faithful. $\blacksquare$

This corollary is the reason the vault proves the dimension by the exterior-algebra trick rather than by the classification of Clifford algebras over $\mathbb{R}$: the argument above is complete and elementary, whereas the classification is a substantial theorem the series does not need. The same computation is offered as the guided drill [[Ex - The Exterior Algebra is a Faithful Clifford Module]].

**Is an instance — a quaternionic vector space as a $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$-module (Haydys E4.1.3).** Identify $\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H} = \{h\in\mathbb{H} : \bar h = -h\}$ with $\mathbb{R}^3$, with its Euclidean inner product $|h|^2 = h\bar h$; the imaginary units $i,j,k$ form an orthonormal basis. Let $V$ be any left $\mathbb{H}$-vector space and define Clifford multiplication by quaternion multiplication, $h\cdot v = hv$ for $h\in\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}$. We verify (Mod): for $h\in\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}$,
$$h\cdot(h\cdot v) = h(hv) = h^2 v = -h\bar h\, v = -|h|^2 v \qquad\text{(since } \bar h = -h \text{ gives } h^2 = -h\bar h\text{, and } h\bar h = |h|^2\text{)}.$$
So every quaternionic vector space is a $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$-module. The smallest instance $V = \mathbb{H}$ itself is the two-complex-dimensional spinor module $\slashed{S}$ of dimension three, analysed on [[Thm - Low-Dimensional Clifford Algebras and the Quaternionic Spinor Modules]].

**Is an instance — $V\oplus V$ as a $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)$-module (Haydys E4.1.4, equation (112)).** Now identify $\mathbb{R}^4$ with all of $\mathbb{H}$ (not only the imaginary part), with $|h|^2 = h\bar h$. For a left $\mathbb{H}$-vector space $V$, define Clifford multiplication on $V\oplus V$ by
$$h\cdot(v_1, v_2) := (h v_2,\ -\bar h v_1) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & h \\ -\bar h & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad h\in\mathbb{H}.$$
We verify (Mod): applying the map twice,
$$h\cdot\big(h\cdot(v_1,v_2)\big) = h\cdot(h v_2,\ -\bar h v_1) = \big(h(-\bar h v_1),\ -\bar h(h v_2)\big) = (-h\bar h\, v_1,\ -\bar h h\, v_2) \qquad\text{(by the definition, applied to } (hv_2, -\bar h v_1)\text{)}$$
$$= (-|h|^2 v_1,\ -|h|^2 v_2) = -|h|^2 (v_1, v_2) \qquad\text{(since } h\bar h = \bar h h = |h|^2 \text{ for every quaternion)}.$$
So $V\oplus V$ is a $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)$-module. With $V = \mathbb{H}$ this is the four-dimensional spinor module $\slashed{S} = \slashed{S}^+\oplus\slashed{S}^-$ of dimension four, whose chiral splitting is exactly the two summands and whose analysis (irreducibility, the Hermitian structure, the interchange of the halves under Clifford multiplication) is carried out on [[Thm - Low-Dimensional Clifford Algebras and the Quaternionic Spinor Modules]]. The block-off-diagonal form of the matrix already shows the defining feature: Clifford multiplication by a vector sends the first summand to the second and vice versa, that is, it is an odd operator for the grading — a fact that becomes chirality on the spinor bundle.

**Is NOT an instance — a module for the wrong sign.** Let $U = \mathbb{R}^1$ with generator $e_1$, $|e_1| = 1$, and let $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with the candidate multiplication $e_1\cdot v = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} v$. Then $e_1\cdot(e_1\cdot v) = \begin{pmatrix} 0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}^2 v = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} v = +v = +|e_1|^2 v$, which is $+|e_1|^2 v$, not $-|e_1|^2 v$. This *fails* the module relation (Mod) and so $V$ is **not** a $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)$-module. It is, however, a perfectly good module over the opposite-sign Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1, +|\cdot|^2)\cong\mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}$; the sign in (Mod) is exactly what separates modules of $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ from modules of the other Clifford algebra, and mistaking one for the other is the standard way to land in the wrong spinor representation.

**Calibration check.** Three quick verifications the reader should be able to carry out from the page alone. First, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^0) = \mathbb{R}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1) = \mathbb{C}$ have real dimensions $1 = 2^0$ and $2 = 2^1$, agreeing with the corollary; and $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2) = 4 = \dim\mathbb{H}$, agreeing with $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$. Second, in $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$ the even part $\mathrm{Cl}^0 = \operatorname{span}\{1, e_1e_2\} = \operatorname{span}\{1, K\}\cong\mathbb{C}$ is a two-dimensional subalgebra, as the grading rule $\mathrm{Cl}^0\cdot\mathrm{Cl}^0\subseteq\mathrm{Cl}^0$ predicts, while the odd part $\mathrm{Cl}^1 = \operatorname{span}\{e_1, e_2\}$ is not a subalgebra (it does not contain $1$, and $e_1\cdot e_2\in\mathrm{Cl}^0$). Third, on the exterior-algebra module $\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^2)^*$ compute $e_1\cdot 1 = -e^1$ and $e_1\cdot e^2 = \iota_{e_1}e^2 - e^1\wedge e^2 = -e^1\wedge e^2$; the volume element $e_1e_2\in\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ therefore acts on $1$ as $e_1\cdot(e_2\cdot 1) = e_1\cdot(-e^2) = e^1\wedge e^2$, illustrating Step 2 of the dimension proof with $k = 2$, sign $(-1)^2 = +1$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The spin group inside the Clifford algebra *(from this chapter, §8.1)*
> The even part $\mathrm{Cl}^0(U)$ defined here is the home of the [[Def - Spin Group and the Double Cover of SO(n)|spin group]] $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, defined as the subgroup generated by even products of unit vectors. The Clifford relation (Cl) is what makes conjugation by a unit vector a reflection, and hence even products rotations; the grading is what selects the even elements.

> [!tip] The spinor modules in dimensions three and four *(from this chapter, §8.1)*
> The quaternionic modules of the last two examples become, once given their complex and Hermitian structures, the irreducible spinor representations $\slashed{S}$ (dimension three) and $\slashed{S}^+\oplus\slashed{S}^-$ (dimension four), classified on [[Thm - Low-Dimensional Clifford Algebras and the Quaternionic Spinor Modules]]. The off-diagonal block form of the four-dimensional action is the origin of chirality.

> [!tip] Bundles of Clifford modules and the Dirac operator *(from this chapter, §8.2)*
> Globalising the fibrewise notions of this page over an oriented Riemannian manifold gives the **Clifford bundle** and **Dirac bundles** on [[Def - Clifford Bundle and Bundle of Clifford Modules]] and [[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator]]; the module relation (Mod), read fibrewise, is exactly the condition that makes $D = \sum_i e_i\cdot\nabla_{e_i}$ a first-order square root of the Laplacian — the motivation of this page realised on a manifold.
