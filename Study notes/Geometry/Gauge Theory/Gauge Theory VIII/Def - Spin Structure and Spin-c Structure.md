# Notation

Throughout, $M$ is a smooth, oriented, Riemannian manifold of dimension $n \geq 3$ (the excluded low dimensions $n = 1, 2$ are discussed in the calibration check at the end, where the failure is genuine and instructive). We write $SO(n)$ for the special orthogonal group of the standard inner product on $\mathbb{R}^n$, and $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO} = \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}(M) \to M$ for the **oriented orthonormal frame bundle** of $M$: its fibre over $m \in M$ is the set of oriented orthonormal ordered bases of the tangent space $T_m M$, and it is a principal $SO(n)$-bundle, an element of the fibre being a linear isometry $u : \mathbb{R}^n \to T_m M$ that preserves orientation, with $SO(n)$ acting on the right by $u \cdot R := u \circ R$ (see [[Def - Frame Bundle of a Vector Bundle|the frame bundle of a vector bundle]]).

We write $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ for the spin group, the identity component of the group of units of the [[Def - Clifford Algebra|Clifford algebra]] generated by products of pairs of unit vectors, and
$$\xi : \operatorname{Spin}(n) \longrightarrow SO(n)$$
for the double-covering homomorphism (Haydys writes this map without a name; we call it $\xi$ throughout). By [[Thm - Spin(n) inside the Clifford Algebra is a Double Cover of SO(n)|the double-cover theorem]] — restated at each point of use below — $\xi$ is a surjective smooth homomorphism whose kernel is $\ker \xi = \{\pm 1\}$, the two central elements $+1$ and $-1$ of $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$, and $\{\pm 1\}$ lies in the centre of $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$.

The circle group is $U(1) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$, written multiplicatively. For a right principal $H$-bundle $Q \to M$ and a representation $\lambda : H \to GL(V)$ we write $Q \times_{H, \lambda} V = Q \times_\lambda V$ for the [[Def - Associated Bundle|associated bundle]] $(Q \times V)/H$ with $(q, v) \cdot h = (q \cdot h, \lambda(h)^{-1} v)$; for a subgroup $K \leq H$ we write $Q/K$ for the quotient of the total space by the restricted right action. All Lie groups act on principal bundles on the right, following the series convention. The symbol $\mathbb{S}$ denotes the complex spinor module — the distinguished $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$-module of dimension $2^{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor}$, often written with a slash through the letter $S$ in the literature; it is defined and used in full on the next page, [[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator]], and appears here only in the motivation.

> [!warning] Convention: Clifford sign, and the two definitions of $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$
> Following Haydys and the series convention, the Clifford algebra is generated by $v \cdot v = -|v|^2 \, 1$, so $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ in this series is the vault's $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n, -|\cdot|^2)$; the opposite sign convention $v \cdot v = +|v|^2$ exists and changes none of the group theory of this page. The vault's existing page [[Def - Spin Structure on a Manifold]] defines a spin structure in the language of transition functions lifting the frame cocycle; it is **the same notion** as the one below, and we prove the equivalence in the Relate section. Finally, some authors (notably Lawson–Michelsohn) write $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n) = \operatorname{Spin}(n) \times_{\mathbb{Z}/2} U(1)$ for the group we call $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n) = \operatorname{Spin}(n) \times U(1) / \{\pm 1\}$; the notations denote the identical group — the subscript $\mathbb{Z}/2$ names exactly the diagonal $\{\pm 1\}$ we quotient by.

This is a compound page: it defines several interlocking notions — a *spinnable* manifold, a *spin structure*, a *spin manifold*, the group $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ together with its two homomorphisms $\rho_0$ and $\rho_{\det}$ and the exact sequence (120), a *spin$^c$ structure*, and its *determinant line bundle* $L_{\det}$ with associated $U(1)$-bundle $P_{\det}$ — because they are one construction seen at successively refined resolutions. A spin structure is a lift of the frame bundle's structure group along $\xi$; the group $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ is the smallest enlargement of $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ that repairs the lift when the pure lift is obstructed; a spin$^c$ structure is the lift along the enlarged group; and $L_{\det}$ records the extra circle's worth of data that the enlargement carries. Separating them into distinct pages would sever a single definition at its joints.

# Axiom Motivation

The construction of the Dirac operator on a curved manifold — the central object of this chapter — needs a bundle of Clifford modules on which the Levi–Civita connection acts compatibly. The frame bundle $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ already carries the Levi–Civita connection, and the spinor module $\mathbb{S}$ is a $\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$-module, so one is tempted to form the associated bundle $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO} \times_{SO(n)} \mathbb{S}$ and be done. The obstruction is representation-theoretic and unavoidable: the spinor module $\mathbb{S}$ is a representation of $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$, **not** of $SO(n)$. The homomorphism $\xi : \operatorname{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$ is two-to-one, and the spinor representation does not descend through it — concretely, the element $-1 \in \operatorname{Spin}(n)$, which $\xi$ sends to the identity rotation, acts on $\mathbb{S}$ as $-\operatorname{id}$, not as $\operatorname{id}$. A rotation by $2\pi$, trivial in $SO(n)$, multiplies a spinor by $-1$. So there is no way to let $SO(n)$ act on $\mathbb{S}$, and $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO} \times_{SO(n)} \mathbb{S}$ is not even defined.

The desideratum, then, is a principal bundle over $M$ whose structure group is $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ rather than $SO(n)$, and which is compatible with the geometry already present — that is, which sits over $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ in the way $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ sits over $SO(n)$. This is precisely what a spin structure will be: a principal $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-bundle $P$ with a fibrewise double cover $\tau : P \to \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$, equivariant for the actions related by $\xi$. Given such a $P$, the spinor bundle $P \times_{\operatorname{Spin}(n)} \mathbb{S}$ *is* defined, because $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ does act on $\mathbb{S}$, and it is a bundle of Clifford modules by the associated-bundle construction. The whole of spin geometry follows once this lift exists.

Why must the map $\tau$ be a **fibrewise double covering**, and not merely a bundle map covering the identity? Drop the double-cover clause and require only that $\tau$ be $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-equivariant covering $\mathrm{id}_M$. Then nothing forces $\tau$ to be surjective or two-to-one on fibres: the constant-on-each-fibre "map" that collapses every point of $P_m$ to a single frame is equivariant in a degenerate sense only if the action is trivial, but more to the point, a fibre map that is not a covering carries no information relating the $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-torsor $P_m$ to the $SO(n)$-torsor $(\operatorname{Fr}_{SO})_m$ in the manner $\xi$ demands. The double-cover clause is exactly the statement that on each fibre $\tau$ is a copy of $\xi : \operatorname{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$ itself — a surjective two-to-one map — so that the two sheets of $P_m$ over each frame are the two spin lifts of that frame, the two elements of $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ over the given rotation. Without it, the identity $\rho(-1) = -\operatorname{id}$ on $\mathbb{S}$ has nowhere to live: the sign ambiguity of a spinor is *encoded* in which of the two sheets one stands on, and a $\tau$ that is not a double cover has no two sheets to encode it.

Why must $\tau$ be $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-**equivariant**, with $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ acting on $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ *through* $\xi$? Drop equivariance and keep a fibrewise double cover: then $\tau$ could double-cover each fibre in a way incompatible with the group actions, and the associated bundle $P \times_{\operatorname{Spin}(n)} \mathbb{S}$ would fail to relate to the tangent geometry — the Clifford multiplication by a tangent vector, which is defined through the frame identification, would not be well defined on $P \times_{\operatorname{Spin}(n)} \mathbb{S}$ because the frame that a point of $P$ determines (namely $\tau$ of it) would not transform correctly under the $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-action. Equivariance is the compatibility that makes "the frame underlying a spinor frame" a $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-natural notion. The precise requirement is $\tau(p \cdot g) = \tau(p) \cdot \xi(g)$ for all $p \in P$, $g \in \operatorname{Spin}(n)$.

Now the second, subtler problem, which forces the spin$^c$ enlargement. A spin structure need not exist: the lift of the structure group along $\xi$ is obstructed by a characteristic class of $M$ (the second Stiefel–Whitney class, discussed as bold context in the Relate section), and there are perfectly good four-manifolds, such as $\mathbb{CP}^2$, on which it fails. The remedy is not to abandon spinors but to enlarge the group just enough to remove the obstruction while keeping a spinor representation. If we take $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ and adjoin a central circle $U(1)$, identifying the two central $-1$'s of $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ and of the double cover $z \mapsto z^2$ of $U(1)$, we obtain a group $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ that still carries the spinor representation (because $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ sits inside it) but is a *larger*, less rigid target for the lifting problem. As Haydys puts it (Remark 125): in general neither the pure spinor bundle $\mathbb{S}$ nor the auxiliary line bundle $L_0$ whose square is the determinant line need exist globally, and yet the tensor product $\mathbb{S} \otimes L_0$ does; **a spin$^c$ structure is precisely the device that makes that globally-defined product rigorous even when neither factor is**. The determinant line bundle $L_{\det}$ is the global record of $L_0^{\,2}$, the one honest object among the three. A reader who has understood why the pure lift is obstructed, and why enlarging the fibre by a circle relaxes the obstruction from an integral lift to a torsion condition, could reconstruct the definition of $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ unaided.

# The Definition

We give the definitions in the order in which they build on one another: first the spin case, then the group $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ with its structural exact sequences proved in full, then the spin$^c$ case and its determinant line bundle.

**Spinnable manifolds, spin structures, spin manifolds.** Let $M$ be an oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold and let $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ act on the frame bundle $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ through the homomorphism $\xi : \operatorname{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$, that is, $p' \cdot g := p' \cdot \xi(g)$ for $p' \in \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ and $g \in \operatorname{Spin}(n)$.

> **Definition (spin structure).** $M$ is **spinnable** if there exists a principal $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-bundle $P \to M$ together with a map $\tau : P \to \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ such that
> 1. $\tau$ covers the identity of $M$ (it sends the fibre $P_m$ into the fibre $(\operatorname{Fr}_{SO})_m$ for each $m$);
> 2. $\tau$ is $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-equivariant: $\tau(p \cdot g) = \tau(p) \cdot \xi(g)$ for all $p \in P$, $g \in \operatorname{Spin}(n)$;
> 3. $\tau$ restricts on each fibre to a fibrewise double covering.
>
> A choice of such a pair $(P, \tau)$ is a **spin structure** on $M$. A manifold together with a chosen spin structure is a **spin manifold**.

A given $M$ may admit no spin structure, exactly one, or several inequivalent ones; the existence and classification are governed by the Stiefel–Whitney classes and are discussed as bold context below. For the geometry, the point is that once $(P, \tau)$ is fixed, $P$ is a genuine principal $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-bundle, and the spinor bundle and the spin Dirac operator are built from it on [[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator|the next page]].

**The group $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$.** By [[Thm - Spin(n) inside the Clifford Algebra is a Double Cover of SO(n)|the double-cover theorem]] — *$\xi : \operatorname{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $\{\pm 1\}$, and this kernel is central in $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$* — the two-element group $\{\pm 1\}$ sits centrally inside $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$. Embed $\{\pm 1\}$ diagonally into the product $\operatorname{Spin}(n) \times U(1)$ by $\epsilon \mapsto (\epsilon, \epsilon)$ (using $-1 \in U(1) \subset \mathbb{C}$); this diagonal copy is a central, hence normal, subgroup, because $\{\pm 1\}$ is central in each factor.

> **Definition ($\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$).** For $n \geq 3$ set
> $$\operatorname{Spin}^c(n) := \big(\operatorname{Spin}(n) \times U(1)\big)\big/\{\pm 1\}, \qquad \{\pm 1\} \text{ embedded diagonally as } \{(1,1),\,(-1,-1)\}.$$
> We write $[g, z]$ for the class of $(g, z) \in \operatorname{Spin}(n) \times U(1)$, so that $[g, z] = [g', z']$ if and only if $(g', z') = (g, z)$ or $(g', z') = (-g, -z)$, and the product is $[g, z] \cdot [g', z'] = [g g', z z']$. Define two homomorphisms
> $$\rho_0 : \operatorname{Spin}^c(n) \to SO(n), \quad \rho_0([g, z]) := \xi(g), \qquad\qquad \rho_{\det} : \operatorname{Spin}^c(n) \to U(1), \quad \rho_{\det}([g, z]) := z^2.$$

Both $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ and $U(1)$ include into $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$, by $g \mapsto [g, 1]$ and $z \mapsto [1, z]$ respectively, and the image of $U(1)$ is central (since $[1, z] [g, z'] = [g, z z'] = [g, z'] [1, z]$). We verify first that $\rho_0$ and $\rho_{\det}$ are well defined and are homomorphisms, and then prove the three exact sequences clause by clause.

> [!note]- Well-definedness and homomorphism property of $\rho_0$ and $\rho_{\det}$
> **Goal.** Show that $\rho_0$ and $\rho_{\det}$ are well defined on classes and respect the group law.
>
> **Well-definedness of $\rho_0$.** The formula $\rho_0([g,z]) = \xi(g)$ must give the same value on the two representatives $(g, z)$ and $(-g, -z)$ of a class. We compute $\xi(-g) = \xi((-1) g) = \xi(-1)\,\xi(g) = \xi(g)$, where the second equality is because $\xi$ is a homomorphism and the third is because $-1 \in \ker \xi$ (by the double-cover theorem, $\ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$, so $\xi(-1) = \mathbf{1}_{SO(n)}$). Hence $\rho_0$ is independent of the representative.
>
> **Well-definedness of $\rho_{\det}$.** On the two representatives, $z^2$ and $(-z)^2 = z^2$ agree. Hence $\rho_{\det}$ is independent of the representative.
>
> **Homomorphism property.** For classes $[g,z]$ and $[g',z']$, $\rho_0([g,z][g',z']) = \rho_0([gg', zz']) = \xi(gg') = \xi(g)\xi(g') = \rho_0([g,z])\rho_0([g',z'])$ (by the definition of the product, then $\xi$ a homomorphism). Likewise $\rho_{\det}([gg', zz']) = (zz')^2 = z^2 (z')^2 = \rho_{\det}([g,z])\rho_{\det}([g',z'])$ (using that $U(1)$ is abelian). Both maps are continuous, indeed smooth, as they are induced by the smooth maps $\xi \times (\,\cdot\,)$ and $(z \mapsto z^2)$ on the covering group $\operatorname{Spin}(n) \times U(1)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- The three exact sequences (proved clause by clause)
> We prove the two "coordinate" sequences and then the sequence (120). Recall that a sequence $1 \to A \xrightarrow{\iota} B \xrightarrow{\pi} C \to 1$ of groups is exact when $\iota$ is injective, $\pi$ is surjective, and $\operatorname{im}\iota = \ker\pi$.
>
> **Sequence 1:** $\displaystyle 1 \to U(1) \xrightarrow{\ z \mapsto [1,z]\ } \operatorname{Spin}^c(n) \xrightarrow{\ \rho_0\ } SO(n) \to 1.$
>
> *Injectivity of $z \mapsto [1,z]$.* Suppose $[1, z] = [1, 1]$. Then $(1, z)$ equals $(1,1)$ or $(-1, -1)$ (the two representatives of the identity class); the second is impossible since its first coordinate is $-1 \neq 1$, so $(1,z) = (1,1)$ and $z = 1$. Thus the inclusion is injective.
>
> *Surjectivity of $\rho_0$.* Let $R \in SO(n)$. By the double-cover theorem $\xi$ is surjective, so there is $g \in \operatorname{Spin}(n)$ with $\xi(g) = R$; then $\rho_0([g, 1]) = \xi(g) = R$. Thus $\rho_0$ is surjective.
>
> *$\operatorname{im}(U(1)) \subseteq \ker \rho_0$.* For any $z$, $\rho_0([1, z]) = \xi(1) = \mathbf{1}_{SO(n)}$, so $[1,z] \in \ker\rho_0$.
>
> *$\ker \rho_0 \subseteq \operatorname{im}(U(1))$.* Suppose $\rho_0([g,z]) = \xi(g) = \mathbf{1}_{SO(n)}$. Then $g \in \ker\xi = \{\pm 1\}$ (double-cover theorem). If $g = 1$, then $[g,z] = [1, z] \in \operatorname{im}(U(1))$. If $g = -1$, then $[g, z] = [-1, z] = [1, -z]$ (rewriting by the diagonal relation $(-1, z) \sim (1, -z)$), which lies in $\operatorname{im}(U(1))$. In both cases $[g,z] \in \operatorname{im}(U(1))$. Combining the two inclusions, $\operatorname{im}(U(1)) = \ker\rho_0$. The sequence is exact.
>
> **Sequence 2:** $\displaystyle 1 \to \operatorname{Spin}(n) \xrightarrow{\ g \mapsto [g,1]\ } \operatorname{Spin}^c(n) \xrightarrow{\ \rho_{\det}\ } U(1) \to 1.$
>
> *Injectivity of $g \mapsto [g,1]$.* Suppose $[g, 1] = [1,1]$. Then $(g, 1)$ equals $(1,1)$ or $(-1,-1)$; the second has second coordinate $-1 \neq 1$, so $(g,1) = (1,1)$ and $g = 1$. Injective.
>
> *Surjectivity of $\rho_{\det}$.* Let $w \in U(1)$. Since squaring is surjective on the circle, choose $z \in U(1)$ with $z^2 = w$ (for instance $z = e^{i\theta/2}$ if $w = e^{i\theta}$); then $\rho_{\det}([1, z]) = z^2 = w$. Surjective.
>
> *$\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{Spin}(n)) \subseteq \ker\rho_{\det}$.* For any $g$, $\rho_{\det}([g, 1]) = 1^2 = 1$, so $[g,1] \in \ker\rho_{\det}$.
>
> *$\ker\rho_{\det} \subseteq \operatorname{im}(\operatorname{Spin}(n))$.* Suppose $\rho_{\det}([g,z]) = z^2 = 1$, so $z \in \{\pm 1\}$. If $z = 1$, then $[g,z] = [g,1] \in \operatorname{im}(\operatorname{Spin}(n))$. If $z = -1$, then $[g, -1] = [-g, 1]$ (by $(g,-1) \sim (-g, 1)$), which lies in $\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{Spin}(n))$ since $-g \in \operatorname{Spin}(n)$. Hence $\operatorname{im}(\operatorname{Spin}(n)) = \ker\rho_{\det}$. The sequence is exact.
>
> **Sequence (120):** $\displaystyle 1 \to \{\pm 1\} \xrightarrow{\ \epsilon \mapsto [\epsilon, 1]\ } \operatorname{Spin}^c(n) \xrightarrow{\ (\rho_0,\, \rho_{\det})\ } SO(n) \times U(1) \to 1.$
>
> Here the map into $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ sends $+1 \mapsto [1,1]$ (the identity) and $-1 \mapsto [-1, 1] = [1, -1]$, the non-trivial central element.
>
> *Injectivity of $\epsilon \mapsto [\epsilon,1]$.* We must see $[-1, 1] \neq [1,1]$. If they were equal, $(-1, 1)$ would be $(1,1)$ or $(-1,-1)$; the first fails in the first coordinate, the second fails in the second coordinate ($1 \neq -1$). So $[-1,1] \neq [1,1]$ and the map is injective; its image is the two-element central subgroup $\{[1,1],\,[-1,1]\}$.
>
> *Surjectivity of $(\rho_0, \rho_{\det})$.* Given $(R, w) \in SO(n) \times U(1)$, choose $g$ with $\xi(g) = R$ (double-cover theorem) and $z$ with $z^2 = w$ (square root in $U(1)$); then $(\rho_0, \rho_{\det})([g, z]) = (\xi(g), z^2) = (R, w)$. Surjective.
>
> *$\operatorname{im}(\{\pm 1\}) \subseteq \ker(\rho_0,\rho_{\det})$.* $(\rho_0, \rho_{\det})([\epsilon, 1]) = (\xi(\epsilon), 1) = (\mathbf{1}, 1)$, since $\epsilon \in \{\pm 1\} = \ker\xi$. So both $[1,1]$ and $[-1,1]$ lie in the kernel.
>
> *$\ker(\rho_0, \rho_{\det}) \subseteq \operatorname{im}(\{\pm 1\})$.* Suppose $(\rho_0, \rho_{\det})([g,z]) = (\mathbf{1}, 1)$, i.e. $\xi(g) = \mathbf{1}_{SO(n)}$ and $z^2 = 1$. Then $g \in \{\pm 1\}$ and $z \in \{\pm 1\}$, so $(g, z)$ is one of $(1,1), (1,-1), (-1,1), (-1,-1)$. In $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ these give the classes $[1,1] = [-1,-1]$ and $[1,-1] = [-1,1]$ — exactly the two elements of $\operatorname{im}(\{\pm 1\})$. Hence $\ker(\rho_0,\rho_{\det}) = \{[1,1], [-1,1]\} = \operatorname{im}(\{\pm 1\})$. The sequence (120) is exact, and it exhibits $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ as a double cover of $SO(n) \times U(1)$. $\blacksquare$

Sequences 1 and 2 record the two ways of collapsing $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ onto one factor: killing the circle recovers $SO(n)$ through $\rho_0$, and killing the spin group recovers $U(1)$ through $\rho_{\det}$ (the subscript $\det$ anticipating that this circle will carry the determinant line). Sequence (120) fuses the two projections into one double cover $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n) \to SO(n) \times U(1)$, the analogue for the enlarged group of the covering $\xi$ itself.

**Spin$^c$ structures.** Let $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ act on $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ through $\rho_0 : \operatorname{Spin}^c(n) \to SO(n)$, that is, $p' \cdot [g, z] := p' \cdot \rho_0([g,z]) = p' \cdot \xi(g)$.

> **Definition (spin$^c$ structure).** A **spin$^c$ structure** on $M$ is a principal $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$-bundle $P \to M$ together with a $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$-equivariant map $\tau : P \to \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ covering $\mathrm{id}_M$ that induces an isomorphism of principal $SO(n)$-bundles
> $$P/U(1) \;\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }\; \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}.$$

Here $U(1) \leq \operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ is the central circle and $P/U(1)$ is the quotient of the total space by its restricted right action. That $P/U(1)$ is again a principal bundle uses the general fact — a standard part of principal-bundle theory, established in this series' chapter III material on quotients — that for a principal $G$-bundle $P$ and a closed normal subgroup $N \trianglelefteq G$, the quotient $P/N$ is a principal $G/N$-bundle over the same base, the residual $G/N$-action being $[p] \cdot (gN) := [p \cdot g]$ (well defined because $N$ is normal). Applying this with $G = \operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ and $N = U(1)$ gives, by Sequence 1, the structure group $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)/U(1) \cong SO(n)$; the definition asks that this $SO(n)$-bundle be identified with $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ through $\tau$.

**Isomorphisms and the set $\mathcal{S}(M)$.** Two spin$^c$ structures $(P_1, \tau_1)$ and $(P_2, \tau_2)$ on the same $M$ are **isomorphic** if there is an isomorphism of principal $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$-bundles $\Phi : P_1 \to P_2$ covering the identity of $M$ and compatible with the maps to the frame bundle, $\tau_2 \circ \Phi = \tau_1$ — equivalently, $\Phi$ covers $\mathrm{id}_{\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}}$ under the induced maps. We write $\mathcal{S}(M)$ for the set of isomorphism classes of spin$^c$ structures on $M$; the theorem [[Thm - Classification of Spin-c Structures]] shows that $\mathcal{S}(M)$, when non-empty, is a torsor under the group of Hermitian line bundles on $M$.

**The determinant line bundle.** Let $(P, \tau)$ be a spin$^c$ structure.

> **Definition (determinant line bundle).** The **determinant line bundle** of the spin$^c$ structure is the associated complex line bundle
> $$L_{\det} := P \times_{\rho_{\det}} \mathbb{C} = \big(P \times \mathbb{C}\big)\big/\operatorname{Spin}^c(n), \qquad (p, w) \cdot [g,z] = (p \cdot [g,z],\ z^{-2} w),$$
> a Hermitian line bundle whose associated principal $U(1)$-bundle is
> $$P_{\det} := P/\operatorname{Spin}(n).$$

By Sequence 2, $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)/\operatorname{Spin}(n) \cong U(1)$ through $\rho_{\det}$, so — again by the quotient-by-normal-subgroup fact, now with $N = \operatorname{Spin}(n)$, which is normal by Sequence 2 — $P_{\det} = P/\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ is a principal $U(1)$-bundle, and $L_{\det} = P_{\det} \times_{U(1)} \mathbb{C}$ is its associated line bundle. The two central subgroups $U(1)$ and $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ together generate $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ and intersect in $\{\pm 1\}$; the quotient by the joint $\{\pm 1\}$ is exactly the double cover (120). Concretely, applying the quotient fact once more with $N = \{\pm 1\}$ (central by the double-cover theorem, hence normal) and using the isomorphism $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)/\{\pm 1\} \cong SO(n) \times U(1)$ from (120), we obtain a two-to-one map
$$\tau : P \longrightarrow P/\{\pm 1\} \;=\; \operatorname{Fr}_{SO} \times_M P_{\det},$$
where the right-hand side is the fibre product of the two structure-reduced bundles and $\tau$ is the natural quotient projection. This is the double cover through which the Levi–Civita and determinant connections are pulled back to $P$ on [[Def - Spin-c Dirac Operator Twisted by a Connection|the twisted-Dirac page]]; we record it here because it is a structural feature of the definition, not of any later construction.

Existence and uniqueness for spin$^c$ structures parallel the spin case but are weaker: a spin$^c$ structure may fail to exist, and when it exists it is rarely unique. It is immediate from the inclusion $\operatorname{Spin}(n) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ that **every spin manifold is spin$^c$** — a fact we prove as a corollary below.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

Both notions are instances of one structural pattern: a **lift of the structure group along a group homomorphism**, the topological form of a lifting problem. Recall from [[Def - Reduction and Extension of the Structure Group|reduction and extension of the structure group]] that, given a homomorphism $\phi : H \to G$ and a principal $G$-bundle $Q \to M$, a *lift* (or reduction, when $\phi$ is an inclusion) of $Q$ along $\phi$ is a principal $H$-bundle $P \to M$ with an $H$-equivariant map $P \to Q$ covering $\mathrm{id}_M$, where $H$ acts on $Q$ through $\phi$; equivalently, it is a bundle map filling the square
$$\begin{array}{ccc} P & \longrightarrow & EH \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ Q & \longrightarrow & EG \end{array}$$
compatibly, or in cocycle terms a lift of the $G$-valued transition cocycle of $Q$ to an $H$-valued cocycle satisfying the same cocycle condition.

A **spin structure** is exactly a lift of the frame bundle $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ along the double cover $\xi : \operatorname{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$, with the added analytic condition — automatic here — that on fibres the equivariant map is the covering $\xi$ itself, hence a fibrewise double covering. A **spin$^c$ structure** is a lift of $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ along $\rho_0 : \operatorname{Spin}^c(n) \to SO(n)$; the requirement $P/U(1) \cong \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ is precisely the statement that $P$, with structure group cut down from $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ to its $\rho_0$-image, reproduces the frame bundle. In this language the spin$^c$ enlargement has a clean reading: $\xi$ and $\rho_0$ have the *same* image $SO(n)$, but $\rho_0$ has a larger kernel — a whole circle $U(1)$ rather than the finite $\{\pm 1\}$ — and a lifting problem is easier the larger the kernel of the map being lifted along, because the fibre $\ker$ over which one must patch local lifts is more flexible. The obstruction to a spin lift lives in $H^2(M; \{\pm 1\}) = H^2(M; \mathbb{Z}/2)$; the obstruction to a spin$^c$ lift lives in $H^3(M; \mathbb{Z})$ and is the image of the spin obstruction under a connecting map, so it is a torsion, and often vanishing, refinement.

The determinant line bundle then has a functorial reading: $\rho_{\det} : \operatorname{Spin}^c(n) \to U(1)$ is a one-dimensional representation, and $L_{\det} = P \times_{\rho_{\det}} \mathbb{C}$ is the line bundle *associated to the spin$^c$ structure through this representation*. Passing a spin$^c$ structure to its determinant line bundle is the map induced on associated bundles by $\rho_{\det}$; that this map is compatible with the torsor structure of $\mathcal{S}(M)$ (it doubles the acting line bundle) is the content of [[Thm - Classification of Spin-c Structures]].

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** The operational content of "$M$ is spinnable" is: *the transition functions of the tangent bundle, taking values in $SO(n)$, can be lifted to $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$ so that the cocycle condition survives the lift.* This is the vault's existing definition on [[Def - Spin Structure on a Manifold]], phrased in cocycles, and it is equivalent to the principal-bundle definition above. The equivalence is the general dictionary between principal bundles and their transition cocycles ([[Thm - Principal Bundles are Classified by Cocycles|principal bundles are classified by cocycles]]): a lift of the frame bundle to a principal $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-bundle $P$ with equivariant double cover $\tau$ produces, on a trivialising cover $\{U_\alpha\}$, lifts $\tilde{g}_{\alpha\beta} : U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \to \operatorname{Spin}(n)$ of the frame transition functions $g_{\alpha\beta} : U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \to SO(n)$ (choose a local section of $\tau$ over each frame section); conversely a lifted cocycle glues to a principal $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-bundle $P$ and the covering maps $\xi \circ \tilde{g}_{\alpha\beta} = g_{\alpha\beta}$ assemble $\tau$. So the two definitions describe the same objects; we use the principal-bundle form because it makes the spin$^c$ enlargement and the determinant line transparent.

**The Stiefel–Whitney obstruction (bold context; not proved in this series).** The classical answer to *when* a spin or spin$^c$ structure exists is stated in terms of the Stiefel–Whitney classes $w_i(M) \in H^i(M; \mathbb{Z}/2)$, following Lawson–Michelsohn. **An oriented manifold $M$ (so $w_1 = 0$) admits a spin structure if and only if $w_2(M) = 0$, and the set of spin structures is then a torsor under $H^1(M; \mathbb{Z}/2)$.** **It admits a spin$^c$ structure if and only if $w_2(M)$ lifts to an integral class — equivalently, the integral third Stiefel–Whitney class $W_3(M) = \beta w_2(M) \in H^3(M; \mathbb{Z})$ vanishes, where $\beta$ is the Bockstein of $0 \to \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot 2} \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \to 0$.** These criteria are stated here only as orientation; they are **not proved and not used in this series**, because their proofs require Steenrod squares, the Thom isomorphism, and obstruction theory, none of which the vault develops, and — crucially — no theorem of chapters VIII through XI depends on them: every later result takes a spin or spin$^c$ structure as given data. Haydys makes the same standing assumption, declining to treat existence and referring to [LM89]. In the same spirit, the following existence facts are recorded as **bold plain text, not as imported theorems, since nothing in this series is derived from them**: *every closed oriented four-manifold admits a spin$^c$ structure* (Hirzebruch–Hopf; a proof is in Morgan, *The Seiberg–Witten Equations*, Lemma 3.1.2, or Gompf–Stipsicz, *4-Manifolds and Kirby Calculus*, Prop. 2.4.16, via Wu's formula $v_2 = w_2$ for oriented manifolds, which forces $w_2$ to be characteristic for the mod-2 intersection form and hence integrally liftable), and *every closed oriented three-manifold is spin* (Stiefel; every such manifold is parallelisable, so all its Stiefel–Whitney classes vanish). These are the reason four-manifold Seiberg–Witten theory is available on every closed oriented four-manifold, but the theory of chapters VIII–XI is built assuming the structure, not proving its existence.

**Compression across fields.** The spin$^c$ enlargement is the differential-geometric instance of a recurring algebraic move: *when a representation you need lives on a double cover but not on the group itself, tensor the double cover with a circle and quotient by the shared centre, obtaining a group that carries the representation with a compensating "charge".* In quantum mechanics this is exactly how a charged particle of half-integer spin is described — the wavefunction is a section of $\mathbb{S} \otimes L_0$ where $L_0$ carries the electromagnetic $U(1)$-charge, and only the combined object is globally single-valued even when neither the spinor nor the charge line is; the determinant line bundle $L_{\det} = L_0^{\,2}$ is the observable field. In representation theory the same construction produces projective representations: $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n) \to SO(n) \times U(1)$ realises the spinor representation as an honest representation of a central $U(1)$-extension of $SO(n)$, trading a projective (double-valued) representation of $SO(n)$ for a linear one of $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the trivial spin structure on $\mathbb{R}^n$.** On $M = \mathbb{R}^n$ with its standard flat metric and orientation, the frame bundle is globally trivial, $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO} = \mathbb{R}^n \times SO(n)$, using the constant standard oriented orthonormal frame $\partial_1, \dots, \partial_n$ to identify each fibre with $SO(n)$. Take $P = \mathbb{R}^n \times \operatorname{Spin}(n)$ with the right $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-action $(x, g) \cdot h = (x, gh)$, and define $\tau(x, g) = (x, \xi(g))$. We verify the three clauses. *Clause 1 (covers $\mathrm{id}_M$):* $\tau$ sends $(x, g)$ to a frame over the same $x$, so it covers the identity. *Clause 2 (equivariance):* $\tau((x,g)\cdot h) = \tau(x, gh) = (x, \xi(gh)) = (x, \xi(g)\xi(h)) = (x, \xi(g)) \cdot \xi(h) = \tau(x,g) \cdot \xi(h)$ (using $\xi$ a homomorphism and the definition of the $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-action on $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ through $\xi$). *Clause 3 (fibrewise double cover):* on the fibre over $x$, $\tau$ is $g \mapsto \xi(g)$, the map $\xi : \operatorname{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$, which is a two-to-one surjective covering by the double-cover theorem. All three clauses hold, so $(P, \tau)$ is a spin structure. Since $H^1(\mathbb{R}^n; \mathbb{Z}/2) = 0$, it is the unique one.

**Is an instance — the homogeneous spin structure on $S^n$ (bold context).** For the round sphere $S^n = SO(n+1)/SO(n)$, the group $SO(n+1)$ acts transitively by isometries with the stabiliser of the north pole equal to $SO(n)$, and the oriented orthonormal frame bundle is $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}(S^n) \cong SO(n+1)$ as a principal $SO(n)$-bundle over $S^n$ (a group element carries the reference frame at the north pole to a frame at its image point; this is the standard frame-bundle identification for a Riemannian homogeneous space). The double cover $\xi_{n+1} : \operatorname{Spin}(n+1) \to SO(n+1)$ restricts on the stabiliser to the double cover $\operatorname{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$, so **$P := \operatorname{Spin}(n+1)$, viewed as a principal $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-bundle over $\operatorname{Spin}(n+1)/\operatorname{Spin}(n) = S^n$, together with $\tau := \xi_{n+1} : \operatorname{Spin}(n+1) \to SO(n+1) = \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}(S^n)$, is a spin structure on $S^n$.** We check the clauses: $\tau = \xi_{n+1}$ is $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-equivariant because it is a group homomorphism restricting to $\xi$ on the structure group ($\tau(p \cdot g) = \xi_{n+1}(pg) = \xi_{n+1}(p)\xi_{n+1}(g) = \tau(p)\cdot\xi(g)$ for $g \in \operatorname{Spin}(n)$); it covers the identity of $S^n$ because $\xi_{n+1}$ descends to the identity on the quotient $\operatorname{Spin}(n+1)/\operatorname{Spin}(n) = SO(n+1)/SO(n) = S^n$; and it is a fibrewise double cover because on each fibre it is a copy of $\operatorname{Spin}(n) \to SO(n)$. This exhibits the spin structure explicitly, as a principal-bundle object, complementing the existence-and-count argument via Stiefel–Whitney classes in the companion exercise [[Ex - Spin Structure on the Sphere S^n]] (which proves $w_i(TS^n) = 0$ by stable parallelisability and counts the structures by the torsor $H^1(S^n; \mathbb{Z}/2)$: unique for $n \geq 2$, two on $S^1$).

**Is an instance — the spin$^c$ structure induced by a spin structure and a $U(1)$-bundle.** Let $M$ be spin with spin structure $P_{\operatorname{Spin}}$, and let $P_0 \to M$ be any principal $U(1)$-bundle. Then
$$P := \big(P_{\operatorname{Spin}} \times_M P_0\big)\big/\{\pm 1\}$$
— where $\{\pm 1\}$ acts diagonally, by $-1$ on $P_{\operatorname{Spin}}$ through the central $-1 \in \operatorname{Spin}(n)$ and by $-1 \in U(1)$ on $P_0$ — is a principal $(\operatorname{Spin}(n) \times U(1))/\{\pm 1\} = \operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$-bundle, and is a spin$^c$ structure on $M$ with determinant line bundle $L_{\det} = L_0^{\,2}$, where $L_0 = P_0 \times_{U(1)} \mathbb{C}$. Its spin$^c$ spinor bundle is the twisted product $\mathbb{S} \otimes L_0$. The clause-by-clause verification — that $P$ is principal, that $P/U(1) \cong \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$, and that $L_{\det} = L_0^{\,2}$ — is carried out in the exercise [[Ex - Spin-c Structures on a Spin Manifold]], and is the base case of the classification [[Thm - Classification of Spin-c Structures]]. The corollary below records the special case $P_0$ trivial.

**Corollary — every spin manifold is spin$^c$.** If $M$ is spin, take $P_0 = M \times U(1)$ trivial in the construction above; then $P = P_{\operatorname{Spin}} \times_M (M \times U(1))/\{\pm 1\}$ is a spin$^c$ structure with $L_{\det} = L_0^{\,2}$ trivial. *Proof.* The inclusion $\iota : \operatorname{Spin}(n) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$, $g \mapsto [g, 1]$, is a group homomorphism with $\rho_0 \circ \iota = \xi$ (indeed $\rho_0([g,1]) = \xi(g)$). Extend the structure group of the principal $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-bundle $P_{\operatorname{Spin}}$ along $\iota$ (the extension of the structure group, [[Def - Reduction and Extension of the Structure Group|as on the extension page]]): $P := P_{\operatorname{Spin}} \times_\iota \operatorname{Spin}^c(n) = (P_{\operatorname{Spin}} \times \operatorname{Spin}^c(n))/\operatorname{Spin}(n)$, a principal $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$-bundle. Composing the spin structure's $\tau_{\operatorname{Spin}} : P_{\operatorname{Spin}} \to \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ with the extension gives a $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$-equivariant $\tau : P \to \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$; since $\rho_0 \circ \iota = \xi$, the induced map $P/U(1) \to \operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ is the isomorphism coming from $\tau_{\operatorname{Spin}}$ being a spin structure. Hence $(P, \tau)$ is a spin$^c$ structure. That $P \cong P_{\operatorname{Spin}} \times_M (M \times U(1))/\{\pm 1\}$ and $L_{\det}$ is trivial is the $P_0$-trivial case of the previous example. $\blacksquare$

**Is NOT an instance — the fibrewise-constant candidate.** Not every principal $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-bundle equipped with a map to $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ is a spin structure; clause 3 genuinely constrains. On $\mathbb{R}^n$ take again $P = \mathbb{R}^n \times \operatorname{Spin}(n)$, but define $\tau'(x, g) := (x, \mathbf{1}_{SO(n)})$, the constant frame. This covers the identity of $M$, but it fails clause 3: on each fibre $\tau'$ is the constant map $\operatorname{Spin}(n) \to \{\mathbf{1}\} \subset SO(n)$, which is not surjective and not a two-to-one covering. It also fails clause 2 unless the action were trivial: $\tau'((x,g)\cdot h) = (x, \mathbf{1})$ while $\tau'(x,g)\cdot \xi(h) = (x, \xi(h))$, and these disagree whenever $\xi(h) \neq \mathbf{1}$. So $(P, \tau')$ is not a spin structure — the pair "principal $\operatorname{Spin}(n)$-bundle plus a map down to the frame bundle" is much weaker than a spin structure, and the equivariant-double-cover clauses are exactly what pins the extra structure down.

**Is NOT an instance — $\mathbb{CP}^2$ is not spin (bold context).** The complex projective plane $\mathbb{CP}^2$, with its standard orientation, is a closed oriented four-manifold that admits **no** spin structure: **its second Stiefel–Whitney class is non-zero, $w_2(\mathbb{CP}^2) \neq 0$** (the generator of $H^2(\mathbb{CP}^2; \mathbb{Z}/2) = \mathbb{Z}/2$), which by the Stiefel–Whitney criterion above obstructs the lift of $\operatorname{Fr}_{SO}$ along $\xi$. It is nonetheless spin$^c$ — as every closed oriented four-manifold is, and as every almost-complex manifold is canonically (the $U(1)$-bundle being the anticanonical line bundle) — so it carries a Dirac operator after the enlargement even though the pure spin lift does not exist. This is the standard example separating the two notions; we state it as bold context because verifying $w_2(\mathbb{CP}^2) \neq 0$ requires the Stiefel–Whitney machinery that this series does not develop.

**Calibration check.** First, confirm from the exact sequences that $\operatorname{Spin}^c(n)$ has centre containing $U(1)$ and that the two projections $\rho_0, \rho_{\det}$ restrict on the central circle $\{[1,z]\}$ to $\rho_0([1,z]) = \mathbf{1}$ and $\rho_{\det}([1,z]) = z^2$ — so the central circle is invisible to $SO(n)$ and seen with weight two by the determinant. Second, check that the diagonal relation gives $[-1, 1] = [1, -1]$, the single non-trivial element of the kernel of (120), so that (120) is a genuine double cover and not a larger quotient. Third, verify the excluded dimensions: for $n = 2$, $SO(2) = U(1)$ has $\pi_1(SO(2)) = \mathbb{Z}$ rather than $\mathbb{Z}/2$, so its universal-type double cover is again a circle and the finite kernel $\{\pm 1\}$ of the $n \geq 3$ theory is replaced by an infinite one; this is exactly why Haydys, and this page, assume $n \geq 3$ (see also Remark 129 on $\mathbb{R}^2$ on [[Thm - Weitzenbock Formula for the Dirac Operator|the Weitzenböck page]]).

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Spinor bundle and the spin Dirac operator *(from this chapter, §8.2–§8.3)*
> With a spin structure $(P, \tau)$ in hand, the spinor bundle $\mathbb{S} := P \times_{\operatorname{Spin}(n), \rho} \mathbb{S}$ is defined, and the Levi–Civita connection pulled back along $\tau$ makes it a Dirac bundle, yielding the spin Dirac operator on [[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator]].

> [!tip] Spin$^c$ Dirac operator twisted by a connection *(from this chapter, §8.3)*
> A connection $A$ on the determinant $U(1)$-bundle $P_{\det}$, together with the Levi–Civita connection, pulls back through the double cover $\tau : P \to \operatorname{Fr}_{SO} \times_M P_{\det}$ to a connection on the spin$^c$ bundle $P$, giving the spin$^c$ Dirac operator $D_A$ on [[Def - Spin-c Dirac Operator Twisted by a Connection]]. The weight-two appearance of the central circle in $\rho_{\det}$ is the source of the factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in the connection dependence of $D_A$.

> [!tip] Classification of spin$^c$ structures *(from this chapter, §8.3)*
> The set $\mathcal{S}(M)$ of isomorphism classes is a torsor under the group of Hermitian line bundles, hence under $H^2(M; \mathbb{Z})$ once that is available: [[Thm - Classification of Spin-c Structures]]. This is why the Seiberg–Witten invariant of chapter XI is a function on $H^2(M;\mathbb{Z})$.

> [!tip] The groups $\operatorname{Spin}^c(3)$ and $\operatorname{Spin}^c(4)$ *(from this chapter, §8.3)*
> In the dimensions that matter for gauge theory the enlarged group is familiar: $\operatorname{Spin}^c(3) \cong U(2)$ and $\operatorname{Spin}^c(4) \cong \{(A_+, A_-) \in U(2) \times U(2) : \det A_+ = \det A_-\}$, computed on **Thm - Spin-c(3) is U(2) and Spin-c(4) is the Fibre Product of Two Copies of U(2)**, which turns the abstract quotient of this page into concrete matrix groups.
