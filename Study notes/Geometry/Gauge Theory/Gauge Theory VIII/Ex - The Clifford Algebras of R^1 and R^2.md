# Problem Statement

Let $U$ be a Euclidean vector space (finite-dimensional real, with a positive-definite inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ and norm $|u|^2 = \langle u,u\rangle$), and let $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ be its Clifford algebra in the sign convention $u\cdot u = -|u|^2\cdot 1$ used throughout this series. Prove the following identifications of real associative algebras.

1. **Dimension one.** $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\cong\mathbb{C}$, by an explicit isomorphism, with the Clifford relation checked.
2. **Dimension two.** $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$, by an explicit isomorphism, with the Clifford relation checked.
3. **The opposite sign convention.** Let $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2)$ denote the Clifford algebra of $\mathbb{R}^2$ built with the *opposite* relation $u\cdot u = +|u|^2\cdot 1$ (equivalently, the vault's $\mathrm{Cl}(2,0)$). Show that $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$, the algebra of real $2\times 2$ matrices — and observe that the two sign conventions therefore produce genuinely different algebras (a division algebra in one case, an algebra with zero divisors in the other).

This is the drill A-E4.1.1 of Haydys, *Introduction to Gauge Theory*, p. 35, with part 3 added to make concrete the warning that the sign of the Clifford relation is not cosmetic.

> [!warning] Convention: the sign of the Clifford relation
> The vault's [[Def - Clifford Algebra|Clifford algebra]] page and Lawson–Michelsohn write the defining relation as $v\cdot v = Q(v)\cdot 1$ for a quadratic form $Q$ that may be indefinite, whereas this series fixes $Q = -|\cdot|^2$ and writes the relation as $u\cdot u = -|u|^2\cdot 1$. The dictionary is $\mathrm{Cl}(U) = \mathrm{Cl}(U, -|\cdot|^2) = \mathrm{Cl}(0,n)$ for $\dim U = n$. In this exercise, part 3's $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2)$ is the *other* one, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2, +|\cdot|^2) = \mathrm{Cl}(2,0)$; we write a prime to keep the two apart on the page.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the Clifford algebra of a Euclidean space, its universal property, and the quaternions.

![[Def - Clifford Algebra and Clifford Multiplication#The Definition]]

For $\mathbb{R}^n$ with its standard orthonormal basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$, the [[Def - Clifford Algebra and Clifford Multiplication|Clifford algebra]] $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is the associative unital $\mathbb{R}$-algebra generated by $1,e_1,\dots,e_n$ subject to $e_i\cdot e_i = -1$ and $e_i\cdot e_j = -e_j\cdot e_i$ for $i\neq j$; the monomials $e_I = e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_k}$ with $1\le i_1<\cdots<i_k\le n$ (and $e_\varnothing = 1$) form a basis, so $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ (proved on the definition page, and re-derived in [[Ex - The Exterior Algebra is a Faithful Clifford Module]]). In particular $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1) = 2$ with basis $\{1,e_1\}$, and $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2) = 4$ with basis $\{1,e_1,e_2,e_1e_2\}$.

![[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property#Statement]]

The [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]], in the form we use it: for a quadratic vector space $(U,Q)$, any associative unital $\mathbb{R}$-algebra $A$, and any $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $\varphi:U\to A$ satisfying $\varphi(u)^2 = Q(u)\cdot 1_A$ for every $u\in U$, there is a unique algebra homomorphism $\widetilde\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(U,Q)\to A$ with $\widetilde\varphi(u) = \varphi(u)$ for $u\in U$. For the series' $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ we take $Q(u) = -|u|^2$; for the primed algebra $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2)$ of part 3 we take $Q(u) = +|u|^2$. The condition to be checked is always the *unpolarised* one, $\varphi(u)^2 = Q(u)\cdot 1_A$ for all $u$, which already encodes anticommutation of the basis images by polarisation.

![[Def - Quaternions#The Definition]]

The [[Def - Quaternions|quaternions]] $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}i\oplus\mathbb{R}j\oplus\mathbb{R}k$ satisfy $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = -1$ and $ij = k = -ji$, $jk = i = -kj$, $ki = j = -ik$; they form a $4$-dimensional real division algebra.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is an *identify-a-concretely-presented-algebra* problem: we are handed an algebra by generators and relations and must recognise it as a familiar one. The recognisable trigger is that the generators-and-relations presentation of $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is *exactly* the presentation of a small classical algebra ($\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}$, a matrix algebra), so the whole task reduces to matching relations and then verifying that the match is a bijection. No cleverness of construction is required; the difficulty, such as it is, lives entirely in checking the Clifford relation for *every* vector, not merely for the basis vectors.

**Assumption pattern.** The only structural fact we use about $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ beyond its defining relations is its dimension, $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$. This is what upgrades a surjective homomorphism into an isomorphism: a surjective linear map between real vector spaces of the same finite dimension is automatically injective. So the pattern is "build a homomorphism, show it is onto, count dimensions".

**Theorem routing.** The route is fixed and the same three times. First define a linear map $\varphi$ from $\mathbb{R}^n$ into the target algebra $A$ on the basis vectors $e_i$; second verify $\varphi(u)^2 = Q(u)\cdot 1_A$ for *all* $u\in\mathbb{R}^n$ (the polarised anticommutation is a consequence, not an extra hypothesis); third invoke the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] to extend $\varphi$ to an algebra homomorphism $\widetilde\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n,Q)\to A$; fourth check that $\widetilde\varphi$ is surjective by hitting a spanning set of $A$; fifth compare dimensions ($\dim\mathrm{Cl} = 2^n = \dim A$) to conclude bijectivity.

**Key decision point.** The one place a careless argument goes wrong is the second step. It is tempting to check the Clifford relation only on the basis vectors $e_i$ — that $\varphi(e_i)^2 = Q(e_i)\cdot 1$ — and declare victory. But the universal property demands $\varphi(u)^2 = Q(u)\cdot 1$ for *every* $u$, and for a general $u = \sum a_i e_i$ this expands to $\varphi(u)^2 = \sum_i a_i^2\varphi(e_i)^2 + \sum_{i<j}a_ia_j(\varphi(e_i)\varphi(e_j) + \varphi(e_j)\varphi(e_i))$; the cross terms vanish only if the images $\varphi(e_i)$ *anticommute* in $A$. So the genuine content of every part is the pair of checks "$\varphi(e_i)^2 = Q(e_i)$" and "$\varphi(e_i)\varphi(e_j) = -\varphi(e_j)\varphi(e_i)$", the second of which is precisely where the target algebra's own multiplication table is used ($ij = -ji$ in $\mathbb{H}$; $E_1E_2 = -E_2E_1$ for the matrices).

# Legal Operations Used

The solution deploys the following legal operations from the topic page's Legal Operations (named descriptively here; the topic page reconciles the numbering).

1. **Present a Clifford algebra by generators and relations.** Replace the abstract quotient $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = T(\mathbb{R}^n)/\langle u\otimes u + |u|^2\rangle$ by the working description "generated by $e_1,\dots,e_n$ with $e_i^2 = -1$ and $e_ie_j = -e_je_i$", which is what one actually computes with.

2. **Derive anticommutation of distinct generators from the unpolarised relation.** From $(e_i + e_j)\cdot(e_i + e_j) = -|e_i + e_j|^2 = -2$ for orthonormal $e_i,e_j$, expand and cancel $e_i^2 = e_j^2 = -1$ to read off $e_ie_j + e_je_i = 0$. (In part 3 the same computation with the $+$ sign gives $e_ie_j + e_je_i = 0$ again, since $|e_i + e_j|^2 = 2 = e_i^2 + e_j^2$.)

3. **Extend a relation-respecting linear map to an algebra homomorphism via the universal property.** Once a linear $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^n\to A$ satisfies $\varphi(u)^2 = Q(u)\cdot 1_A$ for all $u$, the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] hands us a unique algebra homomorphism extending it.

4. **Upgrade a surjection to an isomorphism by dimension count.** A surjective $\mathbb{R}$-linear map between vector spaces of equal finite dimension is bijective; combined with $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ this closes each identification.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Each of the three algebras $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}$, $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ has a natural set of elements that square to $\pm 1$ and anticommute with one another. Find them, and send the Clifford generators $e_i$ there.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> For part 1, $i\in\mathbb{C}$ has $i^2 = -1$, and $\mathbb{R}^1$ has a single generator $e_1$ with $e_1^2 = -1$. For part 2, the pair $(i,j)\in\mathbb{H}$ has $i^2 = j^2 = -1$ and $ij = -ji$, matching $(e_1,e_2)$. For part 3, look for two real $2\times 2$ matrices $E_1,E_2$ with $E_1^2 = E_2^2 = I$ and $E_1E_2 = -E_2E_1$ — a diagonal reflection and an off-diagonal swap will do.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Do not stop at checking the relation on the basis vectors. The universal property needs $\varphi(u)^2 = Q(u)\cdot 1$ for *every* $u = \sum a_i e_i$. Expand $\varphi(u)^2$; the cross terms carry the anticommutator $\varphi(e_i)\varphi(e_j) + \varphi(e_j)\varphi(e_i)$. Verify this anticommutator vanishes in the target, so the cross terms drop out and only $\sum a_i^2\varphi(e_i)^2 = -\bigl(\sum a_i^2\bigr) = -|u|^2$ (respectively $+|u|^2$) survives.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> After the universal property gives an algebra homomorphism $\widetilde\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n,Q)\to A$, check it is onto by exhibiting the image of the standard basis $\{1,e_1,\dots\}$ and confirming it spans $A$; then compare $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ with $\dim A$ to conclude the homomorphism is a bijection.

# Solution

The three parts run through the identical machine: choose target elements matching the Clifford generators, verify the relation for all vectors (which is where anticommutation in the target is used), extend by the universal property, and finish by a dimension count. We carry the parts out in turn, then collect the whole argument in one clean pass.

**Step 1: $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\cong\mathbb{C}$.**

Send the single generator $e_1$ to $i\in\mathbb{C}$; this respects the Clifford relation and extends to an algebra isomorphism.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)$ is generated by $1,e_1$ with the single relation $e_1\cdot e_1 = -1$, and by the recalled dimension formula it has real dimension $2^1 = 2$, with basis $\{1,e_1\}$.
>
> **Define the linear map on the generator.** Let $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^1\to\mathbb{C}$ be the $\mathbb{R}$-linear map with $\varphi(e_1) = i$; that is, $\varphi(a e_1) = a i$ for $a\in\mathbb{R}$.
>
> **Verify the Clifford relation for every vector.** A general vector of $\mathbb{R}^1$ is $u = a e_1$ with $a\in\mathbb{R}$, and $Q(u) = -|u|^2 = -a^2$. We compute
> $$\varphi(u)^2 = (a i)^2 = a^2 i^2 = -a^2 = Q(u) \qquad (\text{since } i^2 = -1 \text{ in } \mathbb{C}).$$
> Thus $\varphi(u)^2 = Q(u)\cdot 1_{\mathbb{C}}$ for all $u\in\mathbb{R}^1$.
>
> **Extend by the universal property.** By the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$, the map $\varphi$ extends to a unique algebra homomorphism $\widetilde\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\to\mathbb{C}$ with $\widetilde\varphi(e_1) = i$ and $\widetilde\varphi(1) = 1$.
>
> **Surjectivity and dimension count.** The image contains $\widetilde\varphi(1) = 1$ and $\widetilde\varphi(e_1) = i$, which span $\mathbb{C}$ over $\mathbb{R}$; hence $\widetilde\varphi$ is surjective. Since $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1) = 2 = \dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}$ (by operation 4, the dimension count), a surjective linear map between equidimensional finite-dimensional real spaces is injective. Therefore $\widetilde\varphi$ is an isomorphism of real algebras, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\cong\mathbb{C}$.

**Step 2: $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$.**

Send $e_1\mapsto i$ and $e_2\mapsto j$; the anticommutation $ij = -ji$ in $\mathbb{H}$ is exactly what makes the relation hold for all vectors, and the extension is an isomorphism.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ is generated by $1,e_1,e_2$ with $e_1^2 = e_2^2 = -1$; by the recalled dimension formula it has real dimension $2^2 = 4$, with basis $\{1,e_1,e_2,e_1e_2\}$.
>
> **Record anticommutation from the unpolarised relation.** Applying $u\cdot u = -|u|^2$ to $u = e_1 + e_2$, which has $|e_1 + e_2|^2 = |e_1|^2 + 2\langle e_1,e_2\rangle + |e_2|^2 = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2$ (the $e_i$ being orthonormal), gives
> $$-2 = (e_1 + e_2)^2 = e_1^2 + e_1e_2 + e_2e_1 + e_2^2 = -1 + e_1e_2 + e_2e_1 - 1 \qquad (\text{expanding, and } e_1^2 = e_2^2 = -1),$$
> whence $e_1e_2 + e_2e_1 = 0$, i.e. $e_1e_2 = -e_2e_1$ (operation 2).
>
> **Define the linear map on generators.** Let $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{H}$ be the $\mathbb{R}$-linear map with $\varphi(e_1) = i$ and $\varphi(e_2) = j$, so $\varphi(a e_1 + b e_2) = a i + b j$.
>
> **Verify the Clifford relation for every vector.** For $u = a e_1 + b e_2$ we have $Q(u) = -|u|^2 = -(a^2 + b^2)$, and
> $$\varphi(u)^2 = (a i + b j)^2 = a^2 i^2 + a b\,(ij + ji) + b^2 j^2 \qquad (\text{expanding the square in } \mathbb{H}).$$
> Now $i^2 = j^2 = -1$ and $ij + ji = k + (-k) = 0$ (since $ij = k$ and $ji = -k$ in $\mathbb{H}$), so the cross term vanishes and
> $$\varphi(u)^2 = -a^2 - b^2 = -(a^2 + b^2) = Q(u) \qquad (\text{the anticommutator } ij + ji = 0 \text{ killed the } ab \text{ term}).$$
> This is the decisive computation: the vanishing of $ij + ji$ in the target mirrors the Clifford anticommutation $e_1e_2 + e_2e_1 = 0$, and without it the relation would fail for non-basis vectors.
>
> **Extend by the universal property.** By the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$, $\varphi$ extends to a unique algebra homomorphism $\widetilde\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\to\mathbb{H}$ with $\widetilde\varphi(e_1) = i$, $\widetilde\varphi(e_2) = j$.
>
> **Surjectivity and dimension count.** Because $\widetilde\varphi$ is an algebra homomorphism, $\widetilde\varphi(e_1e_2) = \widetilde\varphi(e_1)\widetilde\varphi(e_2) = ij = k$. Hence the image contains $\widetilde\varphi(1) = 1$, $\widetilde\varphi(e_1) = i$, $\widetilde\varphi(e_2) = j$, $\widetilde\varphi(e_1e_2) = k$, which span $\mathbb{H}$ over $\mathbb{R}$; so $\widetilde\varphi$ is surjective. Since $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2) = 4 = \dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}$ (operation 4), the surjection is a bijection, and $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$ as real algebras.

**Step 3: with the opposite sign, $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$.**

Now the generators must square to $+1$; a diagonal reflection and an off-diagonal swap do the job, and the resulting four images are a basis of $M_2(\mathbb{R})$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The algebra $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2) = \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2, +|\cdot|^2)$ is generated by $1,e_1,e_2$ with $e_1\cdot e_1 = e_2\cdot e_2 = +1$; its basis is again $\{1,e_1,e_2,e_1e_2\}$ (the spanning argument that produces the basis uses only $e_i^2 = \pm 1$ and anticommutation, both of which hold here), so $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2) = 4$.
>
> **Anticommutation from the $+$ relation.** With $Q(u) = +|u|^2$, applying $u\cdot u = |u|^2$ to $u = e_1 + e_2$ gives
> $$2 = (e_1 + e_2)^2 = e_1^2 + e_1e_2 + e_2e_1 + e_2^2 = 1 + e_1e_2 + e_2e_1 + 1 \qquad (\text{expanding, and } e_1^2 = e_2^2 = +1),$$
> so once again $e_1e_2 + e_2e_1 = 0$.
>
> **Choose target matrices.** In $A = M_2(\mathbb{R})$ set
> $$E_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$
> and let $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^2\to M_2(\mathbb{R})$ be the linear map with $\varphi(e_1) = E_1$, $\varphi(e_2) = E_2$.
>
> **Verify the Clifford relation for every vector.** Directly,
> $$E_1^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I, \qquad E_2^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = I \qquad (\text{squaring the two matrices}),$$
> and
> $$E_1E_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_2E_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = -E_1E_2 \qquad (\text{multiplying in each order}),$$
> so $E_1E_2 + E_2E_1 = 0$. For $u = a e_1 + b e_2$ with $Q(u) = +|u|^2 = a^2 + b^2$,
> $$\varphi(u)^2 = (aE_1 + bE_2)^2 = a^2 E_1^2 + ab(E_1E_2 + E_2E_1) + b^2 E_2^2 = a^2 I + 0 + b^2 I = (a^2 + b^2)I = Q(u)\cdot I,$$
> the middle term vanishing by $E_1E_2 + E_2E_1 = 0$.
>
> **Extend by the universal property and count dimensions.** By the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] with $Q = +|\cdot|^2$, $\varphi$ extends to a unique algebra homomorphism $\widetilde\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2)\to M_2(\mathbb{R})$. Its values on the basis are
> $$\widetilde\varphi(1) = I,\quad \widetilde\varphi(e_1) = E_1,\quad \widetilde\varphi(e_2) = E_2,\quad \widetilde\varphi(e_1e_2) = E_1E_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
> These four matrices are linearly independent over $\mathbb{R}$: a relation $\alpha I + \beta E_1 + \gamma E_2 + \delta E_1E_2 = 0$ reads entrywise as $\begin{pmatrix}\alpha+\beta & \gamma+\delta \\ \gamma-\delta & \alpha-\beta\end{pmatrix} = 0$, forcing $\alpha+\beta = \alpha-\beta = \gamma+\delta = \gamma-\delta = 0$, hence $\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\delta=0$. As $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}M_2(\mathbb{R}) = 4$, they are a basis, so $\widetilde\varphi$ is surjective; and $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2) = 4 = \dim_{\mathbb{R}}M_2(\mathbb{R})$ makes it a bijection. Therefore $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$.

> [!warning] Illegal but tempting: reading off the algebra from the basis relations alone
> One might argue "both $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ and $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2)$ are $4$-dimensional with basis $\{1,e_1,e_2,e_1e_2\}$ and anticommuting generators, so they are the same algebra." This is false: the two algebras differ precisely in $e_i^2 = -1$ versus $e_i^2 = +1$, and this single sign changes the isomorphism type from $\mathbb{H}$ (a division algebra: every non-zero element is invertible) to $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ (which has zero divisors, e.g. $\tfrac12(1+e_1)\cdot\tfrac12(1-e_1) = \tfrac14(1 - e_1^2) = \tfrac14(1-1) = 0$ with the $+$ sign). The extra condition that *would* legitimise a comparison is an explicit algebra homomorphism matching the squares of the generators; there is none between $\mathbb{H}$ and $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ because the former has no zero divisors and the latter does.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** As real associative algebras, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\cong\mathbb{C}$, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$, and $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$, where $\mathrm{Cl}$ uses $u^2 = -|u|^2$ and $\mathrm{Cl}'$ uses $u^2 = +|u|^2$.
>
> Throughout we use the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]]: for a quadratic space $(U,Q)$, a linear $\varphi:U\to A$ into an associative unital algebra with $\varphi(u)^2 = Q(u)\cdot 1_A$ for all $u\in U$ extends uniquely to an algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(U,Q)\to A$. We also use $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$, so a surjective linear map from $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ to an algebra of dimension $2^n$ is an isomorphism.
>
> *Part 1.* Take $A = \mathbb{C}$, $Q(u) = -|u|^2$, and $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^1\to\mathbb{C}$, $\varphi(ae_1) = ai$. For $u = ae_1$, $\varphi(u)^2 = a^2 i^2 = -a^2 = Q(u)$. The extension $\widetilde\varphi$ has image containing $1$ and $i$, hence is onto; $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1) = 2 = \dim\mathbb{C}$, so $\widetilde\varphi$ is an isomorphism.
>
> *Part 2.* Take $A = \mathbb{H}$, $Q(u) = -|u|^2$, and $\varphi:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{H}$, $\varphi(ae_1 + be_2) = ai + bj$. For $u = ae_1 + be_2$, using $i^2 = j^2 = -1$ and $ij + ji = k - k = 0$, $\varphi(u)^2 = a^2 i^2 + ab(ij+ji) + b^2 j^2 = -(a^2+b^2) = Q(u)$. The extension $\widetilde\varphi$ satisfies $\widetilde\varphi(e_1e_2) = ij = k$, so its image contains $1,i,j,k$ and is onto; $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2) = 4 = \dim\mathbb{H}$, so $\widetilde\varphi$ is an isomorphism.
>
> *Part 3.* Take $A = M_2(\mathbb{R})$, $Q(u) = +|u|^2$, and $\varphi(e_1) = E_1 = \operatorname{diag}(1,-1)$, $\varphi(e_2) = E_2 = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Then $E_1^2 = E_2^2 = I$ and $E_1E_2 = -E_2E_1$, so for $u = ae_1 + be_2$, $\varphi(u)^2 = a^2 E_1^2 + ab(E_1E_2 + E_2E_1) + b^2 E_2^2 = (a^2+b^2)I = Q(u)\cdot I$. The extension $\widetilde\varphi$ maps the basis $\{1,e_1,e_2,e_1e_2\}$ to $\{I,E_1,E_2,E_1E_2\}$, which are linearly independent (hence a basis of the $4$-dimensional $M_2(\mathbb{R})$); so $\widetilde\varphi$ is onto, and by the dimension count $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$.
>
> The sign of the defining relation is therefore essential: $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2) = \mathbb{H}$ is a division algebra while $\mathrm{Cl}'(\mathbb{R}^2) = M_2(\mathbb{R})$ has zero divisors, so no algebra isomorphism between them exists. $\blacksquare$

**Independent sanity check.** The isomorphism types are consistent with the general real classification recorded on the definition page: $\mathrm{Cl}(0,1) = \mathbb{C}$, $\mathrm{Cl}(0,2) = \mathbb{H}$, $\mathrm{Cl}(2,0) = M_2(\mathbb{R})$. One can also confirm dimensions by a different route: $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}$, $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ have real dimensions $2,4,4$, matching $2^1, 2^2, 2^2$; and $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ is forced to be a matrix algebra rather than a division algebra because $\tfrac12(1+e_1)$ is a non-trivial idempotent ($\bigl(\tfrac12(1+e_1)\bigr)^2 = \tfrac14(1 + 2e_1 + e_1^2) = \tfrac14(2 + 2e_1) = \tfrac12(1+e_1)$ using $e_1^2 = +1$), and division algebras have no idempotents other than $0$ and $1$.

# Key Takeaways

**To identify a Clifford algebra with a classical algebra, the whole task is a relation match plus a dimension count — and the relation must be checked on all vectors, not just the basis.** The reusable principle is that $\mathrm{Cl}(U,Q)$ is defined by a universal property, so a map *out* of it is manufactured by finding, in the target algebra, a copy of the vector space $U$ whose elements square to the right scalars; the universal property then does the extending for free. The trigger condition is a presentation "generators with $e_i^2 = c_i$ and $e_ie_j = -e_je_i$" together with a candidate target holding matching elements. The transferable diagnostic is the reminder that the universal property's hypothesis is $\varphi(u)^2 = Q(u)$ for *every* $u$; expanding $\varphi(\sum a_ie_i)^2$ shows the cross terms carry the anticommutator $\varphi(e_i)\varphi(e_j) + \varphi(e_j)\varphi(e_i)$, so verifying anticommutation in the target is not optional bookkeeping but the load-bearing step. Once the homomorphism exists, equal finite dimension turns any surjection into an isomorphism, which is why $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ is the only structural input needed.

**The sign of the Clifford relation is a genuine invariant, not a convention one can wave away.** The same abstract data — a two-dimensional space, orthonormal generators, anticommutation — produces $\mathbb{H}$ with $e_i^2 = -1$ and $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ with $e_i^2 = +1$, and these are not isomorphic: one is a division algebra and the other has zero divisors and non-trivial idempotents. This is exactly why the series fixes $u\cdot u = -|u|^2$ once and states the dictionary $\mathrm{Cl}(U) = \mathrm{Cl}(0,n)$ on every page, and why the vault's $\mathrm{Cl}(2,0)$ and the series' $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ must be kept distinct. The recurring lesson for spaced practice: whenever two algebras look "the same up to a sign", test for zero divisors or idempotents before believing they are isomorphic; a single square that flips sign can change the isomorphism type completely. The full real periodicity — the eightfold pattern of $\mathrm{Cl}(0,n)$ through the classical algebras — is the systematic version of this sensitivity, and the low-dimensional cases computed here are its first entries.

**Quaternions are not an accident of dimension two; they are the two-generator Clifford algebra, and this is the seed of the spinor story.** Recognising $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2) = \mathbb{H}$ is the first hint that quaternion multiplication *is* Clifford multiplication in low dimension, which the chapter develops into the statements $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$ and, through them, into the quaternionic models of the spinor representations of $Spin(3)$ and $Spin(4)$ on [[Thm - Low-Dimensional Clifford Algebras and the Quaternionic Spinor Modules|the low-dimensional Clifford algebras page]]. The trigger to keep in mind is that any time a computation in dimension three or four asks for elements squaring to $-1$ and anticommuting, the quaternion units are the concrete answer, and the abstract Clifford relations become the familiar $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = -1$, $ij = -ji$. This exercise is the base case on which those later, heavier identifications rest; the companion drill [[Ex - The Exterior Algebra is a Faithful Clifford Module]] supplies the other half of the foundation, the general dimension formula $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ that we invoked here.
