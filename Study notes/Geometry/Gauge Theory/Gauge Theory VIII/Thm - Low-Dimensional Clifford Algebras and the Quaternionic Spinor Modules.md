# Notation

Throughout, $U$ is a finite-dimensional real vector space with a Euclidean inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ and norm $|u|^2 = \langle u,u\rangle$, and $\mathbb{R}^n$ carries its standard inner product with orthonormal basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$. The **Clifford algebra** $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ is the associative unital real algebra generated by $U$ subject to $u\cdot u = -|u|^2\,1$ for every $u\in U$; polarising this relation gives $u\cdot v + v\cdot u = -2\langle u,v\rangle\,1$, so on the standard basis $e_i^2 = -1$ and $e_ie_j = -e_je_i$ for $i\ne j$. We write $e_I = e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_r}$ for a strictly increasing multi-index $I=(i_1<\cdots<i_r)$; the family $\{e_I\}$ is a real basis of $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ and $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ (proved on [[Def - Clifford Algebra and Clifford Multiplication]]). The **grading automorphism** $\alpha:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ is the unique algebra automorphism extending $u\mapsto -u$; its $+1$- and $-1$-eigenspaces are the **even** and **odd** parts $\mathrm{Cl}(U) = \mathrm{Cl}^0(U)\oplus\mathrm{Cl}^1(U)$, where $\mathrm{Cl}^0(U)$ is spanned by products of an even number of vectors. The **volume element** is $\omega_n = e_1\cdots e_n \in\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$.

A **Clifford module** is a real vector space $V$ with a bilinear Clifford multiplication $U\otimes V\to V$, $u\otimes v\mapsto u\cdot v$, satisfying $u\cdot(u\cdot v) = -|u|^2 v$; equivalently, by the universal property, an algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{R}}(V)$. A **complex** Clifford module is one whose underlying space carries a complex structure that Clifford multiplication respects, equivalently a module over the complexified algebra $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(U)$.

The [[Def - Quaternions|quaternions]] $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}i\oplus\mathbb{R}j\oplus\mathbb{R}k$ have $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$, conjugation $\overline{a+bi+cj+dk} = a-bi-cj-dk$, norm $|q|^2 = q\bar q = \bar q q$, and imaginary part $\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}i\oplus\mathbb{R}j\oplus\mathbb{R}k$; for $h\in\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}$ we have $\bar h = -h$ and $h^2 = -|h|^2$. We write $Sp(1) = \{q\in\mathbb{H} : |q| = 1\}$ for the group of unit quaternions. $M_k(\mathbb{C})$ and $M_k(\mathbb{H})$ are the algebras of $k\times k$ matrices with complex, respectively quaternionic, entries, acting on column vectors by left multiplication; $E_{ij}$ are the matrix units ($E_{ij}E_{lm} = \delta_{jl}E_{im}$, $\sum_i E_{ii} = 1$). The [[Def - The Pauli Matrices|Pauli matrices]] are $\sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $\sigma_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}$, $\sigma_3 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$; they satisfy $\sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}I + i\,\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$. The spinor module is denoted $\slashed{S}$; its two-dimensional chiral halves in dimension four are $\slashed{S}^{\pm}$.

> [!warning] Convention: the Clifford sign
> This series uses the geometers' sign $u\cdot u = -|u|^2$ (Haydys; Lawson–Michelsohn), so that $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is generated by anticommuting square roots of $-1$. The vault page [[Def - Clifford Algebra]] states the universal property for a general quadratic form $Q$ with $v^2 = Q(v)$; the object of this page is the special case $\mathrm{Cl}(U) = \mathrm{Cl}(U,\,-|\cdot|^2)$, i.e. $Q = -|\cdot|^2$. With the opposite sign $v\cdot v = +|v|^2$ one obtains the *other* Clifford algebra: for instance $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2,\,+|\cdot|^2)\cong M_2(\mathbb{R})$ rather than $\mathbb{H}$. Every invocation of the universal property below applies [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property]] with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$.

> [!warning] Convention: orientation and the identification $\mathbb{R}^4 = \mathbb{H}$
> We orient $\mathbb{R}^n$ by declaring the ordered basis $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ positive, and we identify $\mathbb{R}^4$ with $\mathbb{H}$ by $e_1\mapsto 1$, $e_2\mapsto i$, $e_3\mapsto j$, $e_4\mapsto k$ and $\mathbb{R}^3$ with $\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}$ by $e_1\mapsto i$, $e_2\mapsto j$, $e_3\mapsto k$. These choices fix the sign with which the volume element acts on the chiral spinor modules; the sign is recorded explicitly at the point where it is computed (Part (iii), Step 6). With the opposite orientation the labels $\slashed{S}^{+}$ and $\slashed{S}^{-}$ are exchanged.

# Statement

> **Theorem (low-dimensional Clifford algebras and their spinor modules).** With the conventions above:
>
> **(i) Dimensions one and two.** There are isomorphisms of real algebras
> $$\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\cong\mathbb{C}, \qquad \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}.$$
>
> **(ii) Dimension three.** There are isomorphisms of real algebras
> $$\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}, \qquad \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}, \qquad \mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_2(\mathbb{C}).$$
> The space $\slashed{S} := \mathbb{H}$, with Clifford multiplication $\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}\otimes\slashed{S}\to\slashed{S}$, $h\otimes v\mapsto hv$ (left quaternion multiplication), is a $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$-module. Right multiplication by $\bar i = -i$ commutes with this action and makes $\slashed{S}$ a complex two-dimensional space; with the Hermitian inner product $\langle v,w\rangle := \pi(\bar v w)$, where $\pi:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}i$ is the projection along $\mathbb{R}j\oplus\mathbb{R}k$, Clifford multiplication by a unit vector is unitary. As a complex $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$-module $\slashed{S}$ is irreducible, and **every** irreducible complex $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$-module is isomorphic either to $\slashed{S}$ or to its conjugate $\slashed{S}'$ (the same space with the action precomposed by the grading automorphism $u\mapsto -u$); the two are distinguished by the action of the volume element $\omega_3 = e_1e_2e_3$, which is central, squares to $+1$, and acts as $-1$ on $\slashed{S}$ and as $+1$ on $\slashed{S}'$.
>
> **(iii) Dimension four.** There are isomorphisms of real algebras
> $$\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}, \qquad \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_2(\mathbb{H}), \qquad \mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_4(\mathbb{C}).$$
> The space $\slashed{S} := \slashed{S}^{+}\oplus\slashed{S}^{-}$ with $\slashed{S}^{\pm} = \mathbb{H}$, with Clifford multiplication $\mathbb{H}\otimes\slashed{S}\to\slashed{S}$, $h\cdot(v_1,v_2) = (hv_2,\,-\bar h v_1)$, is a $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)$-module; with the complex structure given by right multiplication by $\bar i$ on each summand and the Hermitian inner product $\langle(v_1,v_2),(w_1,w_2)\rangle := \pi(\bar v_1 w_1) + \pi(\bar v_2 w_2)$, it is a complex four-dimensional Hermitian Clifford module on which Clifford multiplication by unit vectors is unitary. It is irreducible and is the **unique** irreducible complex $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)$-module up to isomorphism. Clifford multiplication by a vector maps $\slashed{S}^{\pm}$ to $\slashed{S}^{\mp}$, and the volume element $\omega_4 = e_1e_2e_3e_4$ squares to $+1$, commutes with the even subalgebra and anticommutes with vectors (so it is the chirality operator, not a central element), and acts as $-1$ on $\slashed{S}^{+}$ and as $+1$ on $\slashed{S}^{-}$ (that is, as $\mp 1$ on $\slashed{S}^{\pm}$, with the orientation fixed above).

This page supplies, in the dimensions the series uses, exactly what a general "classification of Clifford algebras over $\mathbb{R}$" would give; the general classification is neither stated nor invoked anywhere in the series, and no proof leans on it.

# Motivation

The Dirac operator, the central object of this chapter, is built by choosing a bundle whose fibres are modules over the Clifford algebra of the tangent space and contracting a covariant derivative against Clifford multiplication. Before any of that can be globalised, one has to know, fibre by fibre, what the Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ actually *is* and what its modules look like — otherwise "the spinor bundle" is a name without an object. In the two dimensions that four-manifold gauge theory cares about, $n=3$ and $n=4$, this theorem answers both questions at once and answers them concretely: the algebra is a matrix algebra over the quaternions, and its distinguished module is the column space of quaternions on which those matrices act.

The pay-off of computing this by hand, rather than quoting the general classification of real Clifford algebras, is threefold. First, it makes the spinor representation *literal*: to Clifford-multiply a spinor is to multiply quaternions, an operation one can carry out on the back of an envelope, and this is what turns the Seiberg–Witten equations from a formal system into something one can differentiate and estimate. Second, it exhibits the two features that make dimension four special — the splitting of the spinors into chiral halves $\slashed{S}^{+}\oplus\slashed{S}^{-}$ and the appearance of *two* copies of $Sp(1)$ — as immediate consequences of the algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$ and its even part $\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$. Third, the existence and uniqueness of the irreducible module, which the general theory (Lawson–Michelsohn, Theorems I.5.7–5.8) delivers for all $n$ through Wedderburn's structure theorem, here follows from two short lemmas about modules over $M_k(\mathbb{C})$ and over a direct product of algebras, with no structure theory quoted at all.

The reader is assumed to know what an associative algebra, a module, an idempotent, and a tensor product of vector spaces are, and to be comfortable with the quaternions and the Pauli matrices; everything else — the Clifford relation, the universal property, the dimension count — is recalled at the point of use.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening).** The literal hypothesis of the theorem is trivial (it fixes $n\le 4$), so the useful "source" question is: which computations, when they arise, are secretly instances of this identification and can therefore be delegated to it?

The first disguised source is **any calculation with Dirac gamma-matrices in three or four Euclidean dimensions.** Whenever one is handed matrices $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_n$ with $\gamma_i\gamma_j + \gamma_j\gamma_i = -2\delta_{ij}$ acting on a complex vector space, one is being handed a complex $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$-module, and the classification part of this theorem says that in dimensions three and four such a representation is forced — up to isomorphism, and up to the choice of chirality in dimension three — to be $\slashed{S}$. The non-obvious bridge is that the *anticommutation relations alone* pin down the representation; one never has to guess a basis. *Example problem:* show that any two irreducible sets of $4\times 4$ Euclidean gamma-matrices in dimension four are conjugate, by identifying each with the unique irreducible module over $\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_4(\mathbb{C})$.

The second disguised source is **a real representation of $Sp(1)$ or of $Sp(1)\times Sp(1)$ on a quaternionic vector space.** The isomorphisms $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$ mean that the even Clifford algebra — the one that carries the spin group — *is* the quaternions (or a pair of copies), so a module over it is quaternionic linear-algebra data. The bridge is that "quaternionic vector space" and "$\mathrm{Cl}^0$-module" are the same category in these dimensions, so a construction phrased in one language transfers verbatim to the other. *Example problem:* deduce that the fundamental representation of $Sp(1)$ is the unique irreducible complex representation extending to $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$ from the fact that $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$ acts on $\mathbb{H}$ by left multiplication.

The third disguised source is **a central element of order two inside an associative algebra.** The splitting $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$ is produced entirely by the volume element $\omega_3$, which is central and satisfies $\omega_3^2 = 1$; any central square-one element $z$ manufactures complementary idempotents $\tfrac12(1\pm z)$ and hence a direct-product decomposition of the algebra and of every module over it. The bridge — recognising that a distinguished central involution is present — is exactly what separates the odd-dimensional case (with its two inequivalent spinor modules) from the even-dimensional one. *Example problem:* explain why $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)$ does *not* split as a direct product, by checking that although $\omega_4^2 = 1$, the element $\omega_4$ *anticommutes* with each vector and so is not central, hence the idempotents $\tfrac12(1\pm\omega_4)$ do not give two-sided ideals — the true reason being that $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$ is already simple.

**Targets (Output Amplification).** The bare identifications combine with further ingredients to produce the structural facts the rest of the chapter runs on.

Combine part (ii) with **the double cover $Sp(1)\to SO(3)$.** Once $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$ is known and $\slashed{S} = \mathbb{H}$ is the left-multiplication module, the spin group $Spin(3)\subset\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)$ is identified with $Sp(1)$ and its action on $\slashed{S}$ with the fundamental representation; feeding this into [[Thm - Spin Groups in Dimensions Three and Four via Quaternions]] yields the concrete description $Spin(3)\cong Sp(1)$ acting on $\mathbb{H}$. The payoff is that the abstract spin representation acquires an explicit matrix (indeed quaternion) form.

Combine part (iii) with **the self-dual/anti-self-dual splitting of two-forms.** The decomposition $\slashed{S} = \slashed{S}^{+}\oplus\slashed{S}^{-}$ together with $\mathfrak{so}(4)\cong\mathfrak{so}(3)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(3)$ lets one show that self-dual two-forms act on $\slashed{S}^{+}$ through $\mathfrak{su}(\slashed{S}^{+})$ while anti-self-dual two-forms act as zero; this is the linear-algebra backbone of the Seiberg–Witten curvature equation and is developed in [[Thm - Spinor Representations of Spin(4) and the Self-Dual Forms]]. The extra ingredient is the Hodge star; the payoff is the chiral Weitzenböck calculus.

Combine part (iii) with **a Hermitian metric and a compatible connection** to obtain a Dirac bundle. The theorem verifies exactly Haydys's condition (2), $\langle v\cdot s_1, v\cdot s_2\rangle = |v|^2\langle s_1,s_2\rangle$, at the level of the fibre; adding a metric connection satisfying the Leibniz compatibility gives the spin Dirac operator its formal self-adjointness. The extra ingredient is the Levi-Civita connection; the payoff is the elliptic, self-adjoint spin Dirac operator [[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator|constructed here]].

# Why Is It True

Strip away the bookkeeping and the whole theorem rests on one arithmetic coincidence: the quaternions are a four-dimensional real division algebra in which every imaginary unit squares to $-1$, and the Clifford relation asks for exactly that — anticommuting square roots of $-1$. So the generators of a low-dimensional Clifford algebra can be *realised* as quaternions (or as quaternion matrices), and once they are, a Clifford module is nothing more exotic than a quaternionic vector space with the generators acting by multiplication.

> **In dimensions three and four the Clifford algebra is a matrix algebra over the quaternions, and the spinor module is the column space $\mathbb{H}$ (in dimension three) or $\mathbb{H}^2$ (in dimension four) on which those matrices act — so quaternion multiplication is literally the spinor representation.**

The two dimensions differ in exactly one structural respect, and that difference is visible in the shape of the answer. In dimension three the top product $\omega_3 = e_1e_2e_3$ is *central* (it commutes past each generator with a sign that cancels because there are an even number of the others to pass) and squares to $+1$; a central involution splits the algebra into two pieces, $\tfrac12(1+\omega_3)$ and $\tfrac12(1-\omega_3)$ times the algebra, and each piece is a copy of $\mathbb{H}$. That is why there are two inequivalent irreducible modules: a module must choose an eigenvalue $\pm1$ for $\omega_3$, and the two choices are genuinely different representations, swapped by the reflection $u\mapsto -u$. In dimension four the top product $\omega_4$ squares to $+1$ but is *no longer central* — it anticommutes with each vector, because there are now three other generators to pass, contributing $(-1)^3 = -1$ — so it cannot split the algebra; instead $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$ is a *simple* algebra, with no non-trivial two-sided ideals, so there is only one irreducible module, and the eigenvalue of $\omega_4$ merely labels the two chiral halves *within* that single module rather than separating two modules.

The uniqueness statement, finally, is not a deep fact once the algebra has been named. A module over a matrix algebra $M_k(\mathbb{C})$ is completely determined by its dimension: the matrix units $E_{ij}$ let one carry any vector in the first "coordinate slot" to any other slot, so the module is a direct sum of copies of the single standard module $\mathbb{C}^k$. A module over a product $A_1\oplus A_2$ splits along the two central idempotents. Chaining these two observations through the complexifications $\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})$ and $\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_4(\mathbb{C})$ gives "two irreducibles in dimension three, one in dimension four" with no appeal to Wedderburn.

# What Makes This Hard

The genuine subtlety is not any single computation but keeping three algebras straight at once: the *real* algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, its *even* subalgebra $\mathrm{Cl}^0$, and its *complexification* $\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ — the classification of complex modules lives on the third, while the spin group lives in the second and Clifford multiplication acts on the first. A common error is to conflate "irreducible as a real module" with "irreducible as a complex module"; the module $\slashed{S} = \mathbb{H}$ has real dimension four but complex dimension two, and it is the complex structure (right multiplication by $\bar i$, chosen precisely so that it commutes with the *left* Clifford action) that makes the counting come out to the standard two-dimensional module of a single $M_2(\mathbb{C})$. The second recurring slip concerns the sign and centrality of the volume element: $\omega_n$ is central only in odd dimensions, and its square depends on $n$ modulo four; getting either wrong wrecks both the direct-product decomposition in dimension three and the chirality sign in dimension four. The third is to invoke Wedderburn's theorem out of habit — it is not needed here and is not available in the series.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** Realise each low-dimensional Clifford algebra as a concrete quaternion(-matrix) algebra by producing the generators as quaternions and invoking the universal property, then match dimensions to upgrade surjectivity to isomorphism. Complexify the results with the single input $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$. Finally classify complex modules using only two elementary lemmas — modules over $M_k(\mathbb{C})$ and over $A_1\oplus A_2$ — and read off irreducibility and uniqueness.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Realise $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)$ and $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$.**
   - *Hint:* Send $e_1\mapsto i$ (dimension one), and $e_1\mapsto i$, $e_2\mapsto j$ (dimension two); check the target relations and count dimensions.
   - *Why needed:* $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$ is the base case, and it packages the quaternion universal property used everywhere below.

2. **Even part in dimension three.**
   - *Hint:* $e_2e_3, e_3e_1, e_1e_2$ square to $-1$ and anticommute; apply the quaternion universal property.
   - *Why needed:* $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$ carries the spin group and is the model for $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)$.

3. **Full algebra in dimension three via the volume element.**
   - *Hint:* Show $\omega_3$ is central with $\omega_3^2 = 1$; use the idempotents $\tfrac12(1\pm\omega_3)$ and the map $a\mapsto \tfrac12(1\pm\omega_3)a$ on the even part.
   - *Why needed:* It gives $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$ and, after complexifying, the two-summand algebra whose two modules are $\slashed{S}$ and $\slashed{S}'$.

4. **The spinor module and its Hermitian structure.**
   - *Hint:* Left multiplication by imaginary quaternions gives the Clifford action; right multiplication by $\bar i$ gives a commuting complex structure; the $\mathbb{C}$-part of $\bar v w$ gives the metric.
   - *Why needed:* It exhibits the module concretely and verifies the Dirac-bundle unitarity condition (2).

5. **Classify complex modules.**
   - *Hint:* Lemma 1 (modules over $M_k(\mathbb{C})$) and Lemma 2 (modules over $A_1\oplus A_2$), applied to the complexified algebras.
   - *Why needed:* It yields irreducibility, the count (two in dimension three, one in dimension four), and the volume-element criterion.

6. **Dimension four.**
   - *Hint:* $e_i\mapsto e_ie_4$ realises $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)$; the module map $h\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&h\\-\bar h&0\end{pmatrix}$ realises $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$; complexify to $M_4(\mathbb{C})$.
   - *Why needed:* It produces the chiral module $\slashed{S}^{+}\oplus\slashed{S}^{-}$, its uniqueness, and the volume-element sign.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Modules over a complex matrix algebra
> **Statement:** Let $V$ be a (unital, left) $M_k(\mathbb{C})$-module. Then $V\cong(\mathbb{C}^k)^{\oplus m}$ for some cardinal $m$, where $\mathbb{C}^k$ is the standard column module; $\mathbb{C}^k$ is irreducible; and every irreducible $M_k(\mathbb{C})$-module is isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}^k$. When $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V = k$, $V\cong\mathbb{C}^k$ and the structure map $M_k(\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)$ is an isomorphism.
>
> **Hint:** Use the matrix units $E_{ij}$: the $E_{ii}$ are orthogonal idempotents summing to $1$, and $E_{i1}$ carries $E_{11}V$ isomorphically onto $E_{ii}V$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the entire content of "irreducible complex Clifford modules are unique of the expected dimension"; every classification statement on the page reduces to it.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Goal.** Decompose an arbitrary $M_k(\mathbb{C})$-module and identify the irreducibles.
> >
> > **Step 1 — the idempotent decomposition.** The matrix units satisfy $E_{ij}E_{lm} = \delta_{jl}E_{im}$ and $\sum_{i=1}^k E_{ii} = 1$. Hence the $E_{ii}$ are idempotents ($E_{ii}E_{ii} = E_{ii}$) that are mutually orthogonal ($E_{ii}E_{jj} = 0$ for $i\ne j$) and sum to the identity. Set $V_i := E_{ii}V$. For any $v\in V$, $v = 1\cdot v = \sum_i E_{ii}v \in\sum_i V_i$ (since $\sum_i E_{ii} = 1$), and if $\sum_i w_i = 0$ with $w_i = E_{ii}w_i\in V_i$ then applying $E_{jj}$ gives $w_j = E_{jj}\sum_i w_i = 0$ (by orthogonality, $E_{jj}w_i = E_{jj}E_{ii}w_i = 0$ for $i\ne j$ and $E_{jj}w_j = w_j$). Therefore $V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^k V_i$.
> >
> > **Step 2 — the slots are isomorphic.** Define $E_{i1}:V_1\to V_i$ by $v\mapsto E_{i1}v$; this lands in $V_i$ because $E_{ii}(E_{i1}v) = E_{i1}v$ (as $E_{ii}E_{i1} = E_{i1}$). Its candidate inverse is $E_{1i}:V_i\to V_1$. For $v\in V_1$, $E_{1i}E_{i1}v = E_{11}v = v$ (since $v\in V_1$ means $E_{11}v = v$); for $w\in V_i$, $E_{i1}E_{1i}w = E_{ii}w = w$. So $E_{i1}:V_1\to V_i$ is a $\mathbb{C}$-linear isomorphism.
> >
> > **Step 3 — assemble standard submodules.** Choose a $\mathbb{C}$-basis $\{v_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}$ of $V_1$. For each $\alpha$ put $W_\alpha := \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{E_{i1}v_\alpha : 1\le i\le k\}$. This is a submodule: $E_{jl}\cdot E_{i1}v_\alpha = \delta_{li}E_{j1}v_\alpha \in W_\alpha$ (by the matrix-unit product), and a general element of $M_k(\mathbb{C})$ is a $\mathbb{C}$-combination of the $E_{jl}$. The map $\mathbb{C}^k\to W_\alpha$ sending the $i$-th standard basis vector to $E_{i1}v_\alpha$ is a module isomorphism, because $E_{jl}$ acts on $\mathbb{C}^k$ by sending the $l$-th basis vector to the $j$-th and the others to $0$, matching the displayed relation. So $W_\alpha\cong\mathbb{C}^k$.
> >
> > **Step 4 — the $W_\alpha$ fill $V$.** By Step 2, $V_i = E_{i1}V_1 = \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{E_{i1}v_\alpha\}_\alpha$, so $\sum_\alpha W_\alpha \supseteq \sum_i V_i = V$. The sum is direct: within each fixed slot $V_i$ the vectors $\{E_{i1}v_\alpha\}_\alpha$ form a basis (image of a basis under the isomorphism $E_{i1}$), and each $W_\alpha$ meets $V_i$ exactly in $\mathbb{C}\,E_{i1}v_\alpha$, so a relation $\sum_\alpha w_\alpha = 0$ with $w_\alpha\in W_\alpha$ projects, slot by slot, to independent relations forcing every component to vanish. Hence $V = \bigoplus_{\alpha\in A}W_\alpha\cong(\mathbb{C}^k)^{\oplus|A|}$ with $|A| = \dim_{\mathbb{C}}V_1$.
> >
> > **Step 5 — irreducibility and uniqueness.** The standard module $\mathbb{C}^k$ is irreducible: if $U\subseteq\mathbb{C}^k$ is a non-zero submodule and $0\ne u = \sum_l c_l\varepsilon_l\in U$ (with $\varepsilon_l$ the standard basis and some $c_j\ne0$), then $E_{ij}u = c_j\varepsilon_i\in U$, so $\varepsilon_i = c_j^{-1}E_{ij}u\in U$ for every $i$, whence $U = \mathbb{C}^k$. By Step 4 any module is a direct sum of copies of $\mathbb{C}^k$, so an irreducible module is a single copy, i.e. isomorphic to $\mathbb{C}^k$; and any irreducible is therefore unique up to isomorphism.
> >
> > **Step 6 — the equidimensional case.** If $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}V = k$, then $|A|\cdot k = k$ forces $|A| = 1$, so $V\cong\mathbb{C}^k$. The structure map $\rho:M_k(\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)$ is a non-zero (unital) homomorphism from a simple algebra, hence injective; as $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}M_k(\mathbb{C}) = k^2 = \dim_{\mathbb{C}}\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(V)$, it is an isomorphism. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Modules over a direct product of algebras
> **Statement:** Let $A = A_1\oplus A_2$ be a direct product of unital algebras, with central idempotents $\varepsilon_1 = (1_{A_1},0)$ and $\varepsilon_2 = (0,1_{A_2})$. Every $A$-module $V$ splits as $V = \varepsilon_1 V\oplus\varepsilon_2 V$, where $\varepsilon_i V$ is an $A_i$-module on which $A_{3-i}$ acts as zero. Consequently $V$ is irreducible if and only if one summand is zero and the other is an irreducible module over the corresponding $A_i$.
>
> **Hint:** $\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 = 1$, $\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2 = 0$, $\varepsilon_i^2 = \varepsilon_i$, and both are central.
>
> **Why needed:** It reduces modules over $\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})$ to modules over a single $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, producing exactly two irreducibles.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Goal.** Split an arbitrary module over $A_1\oplus A_2$ and characterise irreducibility.
> >
> > **Step 1 — the two projections.** In $A = A_1\oplus A_2$ one has $\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2 = (1_{A_1},1_{A_2}) = 1_A$, $\varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2 = 0 = \varepsilon_2\varepsilon_1$, and $\varepsilon_i^2 = \varepsilon_i$; both are central because each $A_i$-factor is an ideal and $\varepsilon_i$ acts as the identity on its factor and zero on the other. For a module $V$, $v = 1_A v = \varepsilon_1 v + \varepsilon_2 v$, and if $\varepsilon_1 u = \varepsilon_2 u'$ for some $u,u'$ then applying $\varepsilon_1$ gives $\varepsilon_1 u = \varepsilon_1\varepsilon_2 u' = 0$; hence $V = \varepsilon_1 V\oplus\varepsilon_2 V$.
> >
> > **Step 2 — the summands are modules over the factors.** Since $\varepsilon_i$ is central, $\varepsilon_i V$ is a submodule: for $a\in A$, $a(\varepsilon_i v) = \varepsilon_i(a v)\in\varepsilon_i V$. On $\varepsilon_1 V$ the idempotent $\varepsilon_2$ acts as $\varepsilon_2\varepsilon_1 = 0$, so the whole factor $A_2 = \varepsilon_2 A$ acts as zero; thus $\varepsilon_1 V$ is a module over $A/\!\ker \cong A_1$, that is, an $A_1$-module. Symmetrically for $\varepsilon_2 V$.
> >
> > **Step 3 — irreducibility.** If both summands are non-zero, each is a proper non-zero submodule, so $V$ is reducible. If exactly one summand is non-zero, say $V = \varepsilon_1 V$, then a submodule of $V$ is the same thing as an $A_1$-submodule, so $V$ is irreducible over $A$ if and only if it is irreducible over $A_1$. If both are zero then $V = 0$, which is not irreducible by convention. This is the stated characterisation. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: Universal property of the quaternions
> **Statement:** Let $B$ be an associative unital $\mathbb{R}$-algebra and $I,J\in B$ elements with $I^2 = J^2 = -1$ and $IJ = -JI$. Then there is a unique algebra homomorphism $\Psi:\mathbb{H}\to B$ with $\Psi(i) = I$, $\Psi(j) = J$; necessarily $\Psi(k) = IJ$.
>
> **Hint:** $\mathbb{H}\cong\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ by part (i), and this is precisely the Clifford universal property with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the tool that turns "these elements square to $-1$ and anticommute" into an honest algebra homomorphism, used for $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Goal.** Produce and pin down the homomorphism out of $\mathbb{H}$.
> >
> > **Step 1 — identify $\mathbb{H}$ with a Clifford algebra.** By part (i) of the theorem (Part (i) of the Formal Proof, which does not use this lemma), the map $e_1\mapsto i$, $e_2\mapsto j$ extends to an algebra isomorphism $\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\xrightarrow{\sim}\mathbb{H}$, sending $e_1e_2\mapsto k$.
> >
> > **Step 2 — apply the Clifford universal property.** Define the linear map $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to B$ by $f(e_1) = I$, $f(e_2) = J$. For $u = ae_1 + be_2$,
> > $$f(u)^2 = (aI + bJ)^2 = a^2 I^2 + ab(IJ + JI) + b^2 J^2 = -a^2 + 0 - b^2 = -(a^2+b^2) = -|u|^2 \qquad \text{(using } I^2 = J^2 = -1,\ IJ = -JI\text{).}$$
> > By [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property]] applied with the quadratic form $Q = -|\cdot|^2$ — for a linear map $f:U\to B$ into an associative unital algebra with $f(u)^2 = Q(u)\,1_B$ there is a unique algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(U,Q)\to B$ extending $f$ — there is a unique algebra homomorphism $\tilde f:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\to B$ with $\tilde f(e_1) = I$, $\tilde f(e_2) = J$.
> >
> > **Step 3 — transport and conclude.** Set $\Psi := \tilde f\circ\varphi^{-1}:\mathbb{H}\to B$. Then $\Psi(i) = \tilde f(e_1) = I$, $\Psi(j) = \tilde f(e_2) = J$, and $\Psi(k) = \tilde f(e_1e_2) = IJ$. Uniqueness: any algebra homomorphism $\mathbb{H}\to B$ is determined by its values on $i,j$ because these generate $\mathbb{H}$ as an algebra (with $1$ and $k = ij$), and $\varphi^{-1}$ is fixed; so $\Psi$ is the unique such map. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 4: Complexifying the quaternions and quaternion matrices
> **Statement:** There are isomorphisms of complex algebras $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$ and, for every $n$, $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}M_n(A)\cong M_n(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}A)$ for any real algebra $A$; consequently $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}M_2(\mathbb{H})\cong M_4(\mathbb{C})$.
>
> **Hint:** For the first, send $1,i,j,k$ to $I,\,i\sigma_3,\,i\sigma_2,\,i\sigma_1$; for the last, combine the first with $M_2(M_2(\mathbb{C}))\cong M_4(\mathbb{C})$.
>
> **Why needed:** It converts the real identifications of Parts (ii) and (iii) into the complex matrix algebras whose modules Lemma 1 classifies.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Goal.** Establish the three displayed isomorphisms.
> >
> > **Step 1 — $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})$.** Define the real-linear map $\rho:\mathbb{H}\to M_2(\mathbb{C})$ on the basis by $\rho(1) = I$, $\rho(i) = i\sigma_3$, $\rho(j) = i\sigma_2$, $\rho(k) = i\sigma_1$, extended $\mathbb{R}$-linearly. This is an algebra homomorphism: using $\sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}I + i\epsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$,
> > $$\rho(i)^2 = (i\sigma_3)^2 = -\sigma_3^2 = -I,\qquad \rho(i)\rho(j) = (i\sigma_3)(i\sigma_2) = -\sigma_3\sigma_2 = -(-i\sigma_1) = i\sigma_1 = \rho(k) \qquad \text{(since } \sigma_3\sigma_2 = i\epsilon_{321}\sigma_1 = -i\sigma_1\text{),}$$
> > and $\rho(j)^2 = -\sigma_2^2 = -I$, $\rho(i)\rho(j) = -\rho(j)\rho(i)$ (as $\sigma_3\sigma_2 = -\sigma_2\sigma_3$), so the images satisfy the quaternion relations $\rho(i)^2 = \rho(j)^2 = -I$, $\rho(i)\rho(j) = -\rho(j)\rho(i) = \rho(k)$; by Lemma 3, $\rho$ is an algebra homomorphism. Its complex-linear extension $\rho_{\mathbb{C}}:\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}\to M_2(\mathbb{C})$, $z\otimes q\mapsto z\,\rho(q)$, is a homomorphism of complex algebras. It is surjective because $\{\rho(1),\rho(i),\rho(j),\rho(k)\} = \{I,i\sigma_3,i\sigma_2,i\sigma_1\}$ spans $M_2(\mathbb{C})$ over $\mathbb{C}$ (the $I,\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\sigma_3$ are a $\mathbb{C}$-basis of $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, and multiplying three of them by the invertible scalar $i$ preserves spanning). Since $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}) = \dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H} = 4 = \dim_{\mathbb{C}}M_2(\mathbb{C})$, a surjection between equidimensional spaces is a bijection, so $\rho_{\mathbb{C}}$ is an isomorphism. (This is the identification drilled in [[Ex - Pauli Matrices Generate Cl(R^3)]].)
> >
> > **Step 2 — complexification commutes with matrix algebras.** For any real algebra $A$, the natural map $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}M_n(A)\to M_n(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}A)$, $z\otimes(a_{pq})\mapsto (z\otimes a_{pq})$, is well-defined, $\mathbb{C}$-linear, and multiplicative (matrix multiplication is computed entrywise from the algebra product, which the map respects). It is a bijection because $M_n(A) = A\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}M_n(\mathbb{R})$ and $\otimes$ is associative and commutative: $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}(A\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}M_n(\mathbb{R})) \cong (\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}A)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}M_n(\mathbb{R}) = M_n(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}A)$, using the universal property of the tensor product ([[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product]]).
> >
> > **Step 3 — assemble the four-dimensional case.** By Step 2 with $A = \mathbb{H}$, $n = 2$, then Step 1, then the block identification $M_2(M_2(\mathbb{C}))\cong M_4(\mathbb{C})$ (a $2\times 2$ matrix of $2\times 2$ blocks is a $4\times 4$ matrix, and this respects block multiplication),
> > $$\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}M_2(\mathbb{H}) \cong M_2(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}) \cong M_2(M_2(\mathbb{C})) \cong M_4(\mathbb{C}). \qquad \blacksquare$$

> [!note]- Lemma 5: The volume element in dimensions three and four
> **Statement:** In $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$ the element $\omega_3 = e_1e_2e_3$ is central and $\omega_3^2 = 1$. In $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)$ the element $\omega_4 = e_1e_2e_3e_4$ satisfies $\omega_4^2 = 1$, commutes with the even subalgebra, and anticommutes with each vector, so it is *not* central. If $z$ is a central element of a unital algebra with $z^2 = 1$, then $p_{\pm} := \tfrac12(1\pm z)$ are central idempotents with $p_+ + p_- = 1$ and $p_+ p_- = 0$.
>
> **Hint:** Count sign changes as $\omega_n$ is commuted past a generator; for the square, reverse one factor block.
>
> **Why needed:** Centrality with square one is what splits $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$ as $\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$; the failure of centrality in dimension four is exactly why $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)$ does not split, and the square-one property is what gives $\omega_4$ eigenvalues $\pm 1$ on the chiral halves.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Goal.** Verify centrality, the square, and the idempotent construction.
> >
> > **Step 1 — centrality in odd dimension three.** For a generator $e_m$, commuting it past each of the three factors of $\omega_3 = e_1e_2e_3$ produces one sign $-1$ per factor different from $e_m$, and passing $e_m$ across the factor equal to itself produces no sign (they coincide). There are two factors different from any given $e_m$, so $e_m\omega_3 = (-1)^2\,\omega_3 e_m = \omega_3 e_m$. Concretely, $e_1\omega_3 = e_1e_1e_2e_3 = -e_2e_3$ and $\omega_3 e_1 = e_1e_2e_3e_1 = e_1(e_2e_3e_1) = e_1(e_1e_2e_3) = -e_2e_3$ (moving $e_1$ leftward past $e_2,e_3$ costs two signs, so $e_2e_3e_1 = e_1e_2e_3$); hence $e_1\omega_3 = \omega_3 e_1$, and by symmetry $\omega_3$ commutes with every generator, so with all of $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$.
> >
> > **Step 2 — the square in dimension three.** Using anticommutation to reverse the second block,
> > $$\omega_3^2 = (e_1e_2e_3)(e_1e_2e_3) = -(e_1e_2e_3)(e_3e_2e_1) = -e_1e_2(e_3e_3)e_2e_1 = -e_1e_2(-1)e_2e_1 = e_1(e_2e_2)e_1 = e_1(-1)e_1 = -e_1e_1 = 1,$$
> > where reversing $e_1e_2e_3$ to $e_3e_2e_1$ costs the sign $(-1)^{3(3-1)/2} = (-1)^3 = -1$ (that is the first equality), and each $e_m^2 = -1$ is used in turn.
> >
> > **Step 3 — dimension four.** For $\omega_4 = e_1e_2e_3e_4$ and a generator $e_m$: passing $e_m$ across the three factors different from itself costs $(-1)^3 = -1$, and across the equal factor costs nothing, so $e_m\omega_4 = -\omega_4 e_m$ — $\omega_4$ *anticommutes* with each generator, hence is **not** central (its commutant contains only the even subalgebra: a product of two generators $e_ae_b$ collects two signs, $(e_ae_b)\omega_4 = (-1)^2\omega_4(e_ae_b) = \omega_4(e_ae_b)$, so $\omega_4$ commutes with even elements and anticommutes with odd ones). For the square, reversing $e_1e_2e_3e_4$ to $e_4e_3e_2e_1$ costs $(-1)^{4(4-1)/2} = (-1)^6 = 1$, so
> > $$\omega_4^2 = (e_1e_2e_3e_4)(e_1e_2e_3e_4) = (e_1e_2e_3e_4)(e_4e_3e_2e_1) = (-1)^4 = 1,$$
> > each $e_m^2 = -1$ contributing once as the blocks collapse from the middle outward.
> >
> > **Step 4 — the idempotents.** For a central $z$ with $z^2 = 1$, $p_{\pm}^2 = \tfrac14(1\pm 2z + z^2) = \tfrac14(2\pm 2z) = p_{\pm}$, $p_+ + p_- = 1$, and $p_+ p_- = \tfrac14(1 - z^2) = 0$; centrality of $p_{\pm}$ is inherited from that of $z$. (This is applied only to $z = \omega_3$; in dimension four $\omega_4$ is not central, so no such splitting is available — consistent with $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)$ being simple.) $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] The volume element $\omega_4$ is the chirality operator, not a central element.
> The careful statement is that $\omega_n\in\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is central exactly when $n$ is odd; for even $n$ it anticommutes with vectors and commutes with the even subalgebra. In dimension four, $\omega_4$ therefore plays the role a central element cannot: it commutes with the $Spin(4)$-action (which lands in the even subalgebra) and so acts as a scalar on each irreducible $Spin(4)$-summand, while anticommuting with Clifford multiplication by a vector, which is exactly why that multiplication exchanges the two eigenspaces $\slashed{S}^{\pm}$. The sign statement in the theorem rests only on the explicit computation $\Phi(\omega_4) = \operatorname{diag}(-1,1)$ in $M_2(\mathbb{H})$ (Part (iii), Step 6), which needs neither centrality nor any abstract input beyond $\omega_4^2 = 1$.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> We prove the three parts in turn. Throughout, "$\dim$" means real dimension unless a subscript $\mathbb{C}$ says otherwise, and we use freely that $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ with basis $\{e_I\}$ (proved on [[Def - Clifford Algebra and Clifford Multiplication]]) and the Clifford universal property [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property]] applied with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$: a linear map $f:U\to B$ into an associative unital algebra with $f(u)^2 = -|u|^2\,1_B$ for all $u$ extends uniquely to an algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to B$.
>
> ---
>
> ## Part (i): dimensions one and two
>
> **Dimension one.** $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)$ is spanned by $1,e_1$ with $e_1^2 = -1$, so $\dim = 2$. The linear map $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}$, $e_1\mapsto i$, has $f(ae_1)^2 = a^2 i^2 = -a^2 = -|ae_1|^2$, so it extends to an algebra homomorphism $\tilde f:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\to\mathbb{C}$ with $\tilde f(1) = 1$, $\tilde f(e_1) = i$. It is surjective (its image contains $1$ and $i$) and both spaces have real dimension $2$, so $\tilde f$ is an isomorphism: $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\cong\mathbb{C}$.
>
> **Dimension two.** $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ has basis $\{1,e_1,e_2,e_1e_2\}$, so $\dim = 4$. Define $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{H}$ by $f(e_1) = i$, $f(e_2) = j$. For $u = ae_1 + be_2$,
> $$f(u)^2 = (ai + bj)^2 = a^2 i^2 + ab(ij + ji) + b^2 j^2 = -a^2 + ab\cdot 0 - b^2 = -(a^2 + b^2) = -|u|^2 \qquad \text{(since } i^2 = j^2 = -1,\ ij = -ji\text{).}$$
> By the universal property $f$ extends to an algebra homomorphism $\tilde f:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\to\mathbb{H}$ with $\tilde f(e_1e_2) = ij = k$. Its image contains $1,i,j,k$, hence is all of $\mathbb{H}$; both algebras have real dimension $4$, so $\tilde f$ is an isomorphism: $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$. (This is the isomorphism Lemma 3 depends on; Lemma 3 is not used here, so there is no circularity.)
>
> ---
>
> ## Part (ii): dimension three
>
> **Step 1 — the even part is $\mathbb{H}$.** Put $I = e_2e_3$, $J = e_3e_1$, $K = e_1e_2$; these lie in $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)$, which has basis $\{1, e_2e_3, e_3e_1, e_1e_2\}$, so $\dim\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3) = 4$. We compute
> $$I^2 = e_2e_3e_2e_3 = e_2(e_3e_2)e_3 = e_2(-e_2e_3)e_3 = -e_2^2 e_3^2 = -(-1)(-1) = -1 \qquad \text{(anticommutation, then } e_m^2 = -1\text{),}$$
> and identically $J^2 = -1$. Next
> $$IJ = (e_2e_3)(e_3e_1) = e_2(e_3e_3)e_1 = e_2(-1)e_1 = -e_2e_1 = e_1e_2 = K,$$
> $$JI = (e_3e_1)(e_2e_3) = e_3e_1e_2e_3 = -e_3e_2e_1e_3 = e_2e_3e_1e_3 = -e_2e_1e_3e_3 = e_1e_2(-1)\cdot(-1)\ \text{—}\ \text{recompute below},$$
> so we redo $JI$ carefully: $JI = e_3e_1e_2e_3$; move the leading $e_3$ rightward past $e_1,e_2$ (two anticommutations, sign $+1$) to get $JI = e_1e_2e_3e_3 = e_1e_2(-1) = -e_1e_2 = -K$. Hence $IJ = K = -JI$, so $IJ = -JI$ and $I^2 = J^2 = -1$. By Lemma 3 there is an algebra homomorphism $\Psi:\mathbb{H}\to\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)$ with $\Psi(i) = I$, $\Psi(j) = J$, $\Psi(k) = IJ = K$. Its image contains $1,I,J,K$, which span $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)$; both sides have dimension $4$, so $\Psi$ is an isomorphism: $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$.
>
> **Step 2 — the full algebra splits.** By Lemma 5, $\omega_3 = e_1e_2e_3$ is central with $\omega_3^2 = 1$, giving central idempotents $p_{\pm} = \tfrac12(1\pm\omega_3)$ with $p_+ + p_- = 1$, $p_+ p_- = 0$. Set $A_{\pm} = p_{\pm}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$; because $p_{\pm}$ are central complementary idempotents, these are two-sided ideals, each a unital algebra with unit $p_{\pm}$, and $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3) = A_+\oplus A_-$ as algebras. Consider the map $\theta_+:\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\to A_+$, $a\mapsto p_+ a$. It is an algebra homomorphism onto $A_+\cap(\text{even-generated part})$: for even $a,b$, $\theta_+(a)\theta_+(b) = p_+ a p_+ b = p_+^2 ab = p_+ ab = \theta_+(ab)$ (using centrality of $p_+$), and $\theta_+(1) = p_+$. It is injective: if $p_+ a = 0$ with $a$ even, then $a = -\omega_3 a$; but $a$ is even and $\omega_3 a$ is odd (as $\omega_3$ has odd degree $3$), so both sides lie in complementary graded pieces and must vanish, giving $a = 0$. Now $\dim A_+ = \dim\theta_+(\mathrm{Cl}^0) = \dim\mathrm{Cl}^0 = 4$ once we know $\theta_+$ is *onto* $A_+$; and it is, because $\dim A_+ + \dim A_- = \dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3) = 8$ while the identical map $\theta_-:a\mapsto p_- a$ is also injective on $\mathrm{Cl}^0$, forcing $\dim A_{\pm}\ge 4$, hence $\dim A_{\pm} = 4$ and $\theta_{\pm}$ are isomorphisms onto $A_{\pm}$. Therefore $A_{\pm}\cong\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$ (by Step 1), and
> $$\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3) = A_+\oplus A_- \cong\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}.$$
>
> **Step 3 — complexification.** Complexifying the previous line and using Lemma 4,
> $$\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3) \cong \mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}) = (\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H})\oplus(\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}) \cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_2(\mathbb{C}) \qquad \text{(} \mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\text{, Lemma 4).}$$
>
> **Step 4 — the spinor module.** Let $\slashed{S} = \mathbb{H}$ and define Clifford multiplication by $\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}$ via left multiplication: for $h\in\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}$ and $v\in\slashed{S}$, $h\cdot v := hv$. Then, for $h$ imaginary, $\bar h = -h$ and $h^2 = -h\bar h = -|h|^2$, so
> $$h\cdot(h\cdot v) = h(hv) = h^2 v = -|h|^2 v.$$
> Thus $L:\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{H})$, $h\mapsto (v\mapsto hv)$, satisfies $L_h^2 = -|h|^2$, and (universal property, $Q = -|\cdot|^2$, on $U = \operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}\cong\mathbb{R}^3$) extends to an algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{H})$, so $\slashed{S}$ is a $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$-module. Under the identification $e_1\mapsto i$, $e_2\mapsto j$, $e_3\mapsto k$ of the orientation convention, $e_1\cdot v = iv$, $e_2\cdot v = jv$, $e_3\cdot v = kv$.
>
> **Complex structure.** For $\lambda = s + ti\in\mathbb{C}$ define $\lambda\cdot v := v\bar\lambda = v(s - ti)$ (right multiplication by the quaternion $\bar\lambda$; note $v\bar i = -vi$). This is a complex-vector-space structure: $i\cdot(i\cdot v) = (v\bar i)\bar i = v\bar i^2 = v(-i)(-i) = v i^2 = -v$, and real scalars act as usual. It commutes with Clifford multiplication because left and right quaternion multiplications commute by associativity: $h\cdot(\lambda\cdot v) = h(v\bar\lambda) = (hv)\bar\lambda = \lambda\cdot(h\cdot v)$. Hence Clifford multiplication is complex-linear, and $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\slashed{S} = \tfrac12\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H} = 2$.
>
> **Hermitian structure.** Let $\pi:\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}\oplus\mathbb{R}i$ be the projection killing the $j$- and $k$-components, and set $\langle v,w\rangle := \pi(\bar v w)$. Then:
> - *Conjugate symmetry:* $\langle w,v\rangle = \pi(\bar w v) = \pi(\overline{\bar v w}) = \overline{\pi(\bar v w)} = \overline{\langle v,w\rangle}$, since $\pi$ commutes with conjugation (both keep the $1$-part and negate the $i$-part).
> - *Complex linearity in the first slot:* left multiplication by $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}$ preserves the decomposition $\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}j$, so $\pi(\lambda q) = \lambda\pi(q)$; hence $\langle\lambda\cdot v, w\rangle = \pi(\overline{v\bar\lambda}\,w) = \pi(\lambda\bar v w) = \lambda\langle v,w\rangle$ (using $\overline{v\bar\lambda} = \lambda\bar v$). Conjugate linearity in the second slot follows by conjugate symmetry.
> - *Positive definiteness:* $\langle v,v\rangle = \pi(\bar v v) = \pi(|v|^2) = |v|^2 > 0$ for $v\ne 0$.
>
> So $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is a Hermitian inner product. Clifford multiplication by a unit vector is unitary — Haydys's Dirac-bundle condition (2): for $h\in\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H} = \mathbb{R}^3$,
> $$\langle h\cdot v, h\cdot w\rangle = \pi(\overline{hv}\,hw) = \pi(\bar v\bar h h w) = \pi(\bar v |h|^2 w) = |h|^2\pi(\bar v w) = |h|^2\langle v,w\rangle \qquad \text{(using } \bar h h = |h|^2\text{),}$$
> which for $|h| = 1$ says $\langle h\cdot v, h\cdot w\rangle = \langle v,w\rangle$.
>
> **Step 5 — irreducibility and classification.** A complex $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$-module is a module over $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})$ (Step 3). By Lemma 2 any such module $V$ splits as $V = V_+\oplus V_-$ with $V_{\pm}$ a module over the respective $M_2(\mathbb{C})$-summand, and $V$ is irreducible if and only if one summand is zero and the other is irreducible over its $M_2(\mathbb{C})$; by Lemma 1 the unique irreducible $M_2(\mathbb{C})$-module is $\mathbb{C}^2$. Hence there are **exactly two** irreducible complex $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$-modules up to isomorphism, one for each summand. The two summands are the $\pm 1$-eigenspaces of the central involution $\omega_3$ (which acts as $\omega_3 p_{\pm} = p_{\pm}$, i.e. as $+1$ on $A_+$ and $-1$ on $A_-$, and likewise on the complexification), so the two irreducibles are distinguished by whether $\omega_3$ acts as $+1$ or as $-1$.
>
> Now $\slashed{S}$ is one of them: it has $\dim_{\mathbb{C}} = 2$, and
> $$\omega_3\cdot v = e_1\cdot(e_2\cdot(e_3\cdot v)) = i(j(kv)) = (ijk)v = -v,$$
> so $\omega_3$ acts as $-1$ on $\slashed{S}$; therefore $\slashed{S}$ is a module over the $\omega_3 = -1$ summand $M_2(\mathbb{C})$, of complex dimension $2$, hence (Lemma 1, equidimensional case) the standard module — irreducible. Its conjugate $\slashed{S}'$ is defined as the same complex space with the action precomposed by the grading automorphism $\alpha$ (that is $x\cdot' v := \alpha(x)\cdot v$); since $\alpha(\omega_3) = (-e_1)(-e_2)(-e_3) = -\omega_3$, on $\slashed{S}'$ the volume element acts as $\omega_3\cdot' v = \alpha(\omega_3)\cdot v = -\omega_3\cdot v = -(-v) = v$, i.e. as $+1$. So $\slashed{S}'$ realises the other summand. Consequently every irreducible complex $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$-module is isomorphic to $\slashed{S}$ or to $\slashed{S}'$, the two being distinguished by the action of $\omega_3 = e_1e_2e_3$ ($=\mp 1$ on $\slashed{S}$, $\slashed{S}'$ respectively). This completes Part (ii).
>
> ---
>
> ## Part (iii): dimension four
>
> **Step 1 — the even part.** Define $g:\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)$ by $g(e_i) = e_i e_4$ for $i = 1,2,3$. Each image is even, and
> $$(e_ie_4)^2 = e_ie_4e_ie_4 = -e_ie_ie_4e_4 = -(-1)(-1) = -1 = -|e_i|^2,$$
> while for $i\ne j$, $(e_ie_4)(e_je_4) = e_ie_4e_je_4 = -e_ie_je_4e_4 = e_ie_j$ and $(e_je_4)(e_ie_4) = e_je_i = -e_ie_j$, so the images anticommute; thus $g(u)^2 = -|u|^2$ for all $u\in\mathbb{R}^3$ (polarising: cross terms are the anticommutators, which vanish). By the universal property $g$ extends to an algebra homomorphism $\tilde g:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\to\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)$. It is surjective: $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)$ has basis $\{1\}\cup\{e_ae_b: a<b\}\cup\{e_1e_2e_3e_4\}$ ($1 + 6 + 1 = 8$ elements), and the image contains $1$; $g(e_i) = e_ie_4$ for $i=1,2,3$ (three of the $e_ae_b$); $g(e_ie_j) = (e_ie_4)(e_je_4) = e_ie_j$ for $i<j\le 3$ (the other three); and $g(e_1e_2e_3) = (e_1e_4)(e_2e_4)(e_3e_4) = e_1e_2e_3e_4$ (using $(e_ie_4)(e_je_4) = e_ie_j$ twice). Since $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3) = 8 = \dim\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)$, $\tilde g$ is an isomorphism. Combining with Part (ii),
> $$\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}.$$
>
> **Step 2 — the module and the map to $M_2(\mathbb{H})$.** Let $\slashed{S} = \mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H} = \slashed{S}^{+}\oplus\slashed{S}^{-}$, regarded as a *right* $\mathbb{H}$-module by componentwise right multiplication. Under $\mathbb{R}^4\cong\mathbb{H}$ ($e_1\mapsto 1$, $e_2\mapsto i$, $e_3\mapsto j$, $e_4\mapsto k$) define Clifford multiplication by $h\in\mathbb{H}$ as the operator
> $$c(h)\begin{pmatrix}v_1\\v_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & h\\ -\bar h & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_1\\v_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}hv_2\\ -\bar h v_1\end{pmatrix}.$$
> Then $c(h)^2\binom{v_1}{v_2} = c(h)\binom{hv_2}{-\bar h v_1} = \binom{h(-\bar h v_1)}{-\bar h(hv_2)} = \binom{-|h|^2 v_1}{-|h|^2 v_2} = -|h|^2\binom{v_1}{v_2}$ (using $h\bar h = \bar h h = |h|^2$), so $c(h)^2 = -|h|^2$, and by the universal property $c$ extends to an algebra homomorphism $\Phi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{H}}(\slashed{S})$. Each $c(h)$ is right-$\mathbb{H}$-linear (left multiplication by $h$ or $\bar h$ commutes with right multiplication by any quaternion, by associativity), so it is a genuine element of $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{H}}(\slashed{S}) = M_2(\mathbb{H})$ (endomorphisms of the right module $\mathbb{H}^2$ are left multiplications by $2\times 2$ quaternion matrices).
>
> **Step 3 — $\Phi$ is an isomorphism.** We show $\Phi$ is surjective onto $M_2(\mathbb{H})$; since $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4) = 16 = \dim_{\mathbb{R}}M_2(\mathbb{H})$, surjectivity gives bijectivity. First,
> $$c(e_1) = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad c(e_2) = \begin{pmatrix}0&i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad c(e_3) = \begin{pmatrix}0&j\\j&0\end{pmatrix},\quad c(e_4) = \begin{pmatrix}0&k\\k&0\end{pmatrix},$$
> using $-\bar{1} = -1$ and $-\bar i = i$, $-\bar j = j$, $-\bar k = k$. Compute the images of some products (quaternion-matrix multiplication, minding the order of factors):
> $$\Phi(e_1e_2) = c(e_1)c(e_2) = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix},$$
> $$\Phi(e_3e_4) = c(e_3)c(e_4) = \begin{pmatrix}0&j\\j&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&k\\k&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}jk&0\\0&jk\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix},$$
> and therefore
> $$\Phi(\omega_4) = \Phi(e_1e_2)\Phi(e_3e_4) = \begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}i^2&0\\0&-i\cdot i\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = -E_{11} + E_{22}.$$
> Since $\Phi(1) = I$, we recover the matrix units: $E_{11} = \tfrac12(I - \Phi(\omega_4))$ and $E_{22} = \tfrac12(I + \Phi(\omega_4))$ lie in the image. Then $E_{11}c(e_1) = E_{11}(E_{12} - E_{21}) = E_{12}$ and $E_{22}c(e_1) = E_{22}(E_{12} - E_{21}) = -E_{21}$ lie in the image, so $E_{12}, E_{21}\in\operatorname{im}\Phi$ (here $c(e_1) = E_{12} - E_{21}$). For the quaternion scalars, $\Phi(e_1e_2)E_{11} = \operatorname{diag}(i,-i)E_{11} = iE_{11}$, and likewise $\Phi(e_1e_3) = c(e_1)c(e_3) = \operatorname{diag}(j,-j)$ gives $jE_{11}$ and $\Phi(e_1e_4) = c(e_1)c(e_4) = \operatorname{diag}(k,-k)$ gives $kE_{11}$; together with $E_{11}$ itself, all of $\mathbb{H}E_{11}$ lies in the image. Finally, for any $q\in\mathbb{H}$,
> $$qE_{12} = (qE_{11})E_{12},\qquad qE_{21} = E_{21}(qE_{11}),\qquad qE_{22} = (qE_{21})E_{12},$$
> so every $qE_{ab}$ ($a,b\in\{1,2\}$, $q\in\mathbb{H}$) is in $\operatorname{im}\Phi$; these span $M_2(\mathbb{H})$, so $\Phi$ is surjective and therefore an isomorphism:
> $$\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_2(\mathbb{H}).$$
>
> **Step 4 — complexification.** By Lemma 4, $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}M_2(\mathbb{H})\cong M_4(\mathbb{C})$.
>
> **Step 5 — complex and Hermitian structure, chirality.** Give each summand $\slashed{S}^{\pm} = \mathbb{H}$ the complex structure of Part (ii) (scalar $\lambda$ acts by right multiplication by $\bar\lambda$), so $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\slashed{S} = 4$; this commutes with $\Phi$ as before (right-scalar multiplication commutes with the left-multiplication entries of $c(h)$). The block form $c(h) = \begin{pmatrix}0&h\\-\bar h&0\end{pmatrix}$ is off-diagonal, so $c(h)(\slashed{S}^{+}) = c(h)\{(v_1,0)\} = \{(0,-\bar h v_1)\}\subseteq\slashed{S}^{-}$ and likewise $c(h)(\slashed{S}^{-})\subseteq\slashed{S}^{+}$: Clifford multiplication by a vector interchanges the chiral halves. Define $\langle(v_1,v_2),(w_1,w_2)\rangle := \pi(\bar v_1 w_1) + \pi(\bar v_2 w_2)$. It is Hermitian and positive definite (sum of two copies of the Part (ii) form) with $\slashed{S}^{+}\perp\slashed{S}^{-}$, and it satisfies condition (2): for $h\in\mathbb{R}^4 = \mathbb{H}$,
> $$\langle h\cdot v, h\cdot w\rangle = \pi(\overline{hv_2}\,hw_2) + \pi(\overline{-\bar h v_1}\,(-\bar h w_1)) = \pi(\bar v_2|h|^2 w_2) + \pi(\bar v_1|h|^2 w_1) = |h|^2\langle v,w\rangle,$$
> where $\overline{hv_2} = \bar v_2\bar h$ and $\overline{-\bar h v_1} = -\bar v_1 h$, and $\bar h h = h\bar h = |h|^2$ are used. So $\slashed{S}$ is a complex four-dimensional Hermitian Clifford module on which unit vectors act unitarily.
>
> **Step 6 — irreducibility, uniqueness, volume element.** A complex $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)$-module is a module over $\mathbb{C}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_4(\mathbb{C})$ (Step 4). By Lemma 1 the unique irreducible $M_4(\mathbb{C})$-module is $\mathbb{C}^4$, and any module of complex dimension $4$ on which $M_4(\mathbb{C})$ acts faithfully is the standard module. Our $\slashed{S}$ has $\dim_{\mathbb{C}} = 4$, and the complexified action $M_4(\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb{C}}(\slashed{S})$ is a non-zero homomorphism from the simple algebra $M_4(\mathbb{C})$, hence injective, hence (equal dimensions $16$) an isomorphism; thus $\slashed{S}\cong\mathbb{C}^4$ is the standard module — irreducible — and it is the unique irreducible complex $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)$-module up to isomorphism (Lemma 1). Finally, from Step 3, $\Phi(\omega_4) = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1)$, which acts as $-1$ on the first summand $\slashed{S}^{+}$ and as $+1$ on the second summand $\slashed{S}^{-}$; that is, $\omega_4 = e_1e_2e_3e_4$ acts as $\mp 1$ on $\slashed{S}^{\pm}$, with the orientation and identification $\mathbb{R}^4 = \mathbb{H}$ fixed in the Notation. (Reversing the orientation replaces $\omega_4$ by $-\omega_4$ and swaps the two chiralities.) This completes Part (iii), and the theorem. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Representation theory of finite and Lie groups.** The pair of inequivalent irreducible $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)$-modules distinguished by $\omega_3 = \pm 1$ is a clean model of the general phenomenon that a *central* element forces a representation to choose an eigenvalue, splitting the representation category. Ask a student to redo the classification for the group algebra $\mathbb{C}[\mathbb{Z}/2] = \mathbb{C}\oplus\mathbb{C}$ and see the same two-summand structure and the same "one module per summand" conclusion from Lemma 2. The theorem applies because both algebras are products; the non-obvious part is recognising the Clifford volume element as the analogue of the group involution.

**Quantum mechanics of spin-$\tfrac12$ systems.** The identification $\mathbb{C}\otimes\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong M_2(\mathbb{C})\oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})$ with $\slashed{S} = \mathbb{C}^2$ is exactly the statement that the Pauli spin operators generate all $2\times 2$ observables and that the two-component spinor is the smallest faithful carrier. A good exercise is to show that any set of three Hermitian operators $S_1,S_2,S_3$ on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space with $\{S_a,S_b\} = 2\delta_{ab}$ decomposes the space into copies of $\mathbb{C}^2$; this is Lemma 1 in physical dress. It is non-obvious because physicists usually *posit* the two-dimensionality rather than deriving it.

**Division algebras and Bott periodicity.** The fact that $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1),\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$ are $\mathbb{C},\mathbb{H}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong M_2(\mathbb{H})$ is the first stretch of the length-eight periodic table of real Clifford algebras. Ask a student to continue the ladder using the two isomorphisms $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cong\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})$ and the tensor identities for $\mathbb{H}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{H}\cong M_4(\mathbb{R})$, and to locate exactly where the pattern repeats with a shift of a matrix size. The present theorem provides the base cases; the non-obviousness is that periodicity is an *algebraic* fact provable by hand, independent of its topological shadow.

# Bridges

- **[[Def - SU(2) Action on Spinors]].** The concrete module $\slashed{S} = \mathbb{H}$ with $Sp(1)$ acting by left multiplication *is* the two-dimensional spinor representation of $SU(2)$ once one uses the isomorphism $Sp(1)\cong SU(2)$ (a unit quaternion $a+bi+cj+dk$ corresponds to the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}a+bi & c+di\\ -c+di & a-bi\end{pmatrix}$, from [[Def - Quaternions]]). Under this correspondence the complex structure "right multiplication by $\bar i$" becomes the standard complex structure on $\mathbb{C}^2$, and the Clifford action of $\operatorname{Im}\mathbb{H}$ becomes multiplication by $i\sigma_a$; this is the bridge along which the quaternionic picture of this page and the matrix picture of the spinor-representation page are the same object.

- **[[Def - Weyl Spinor]].** The chiral splitting $\slashed{S} = \slashed{S}^{+}\oplus\slashed{S}^{-}$ produced in Part (iii) is the Euclidean, four-dimensional counterpart of the left- and right-handed Weyl spinors of Lorentzian four-dimensional physics. In both settings the two halves are exchanged by Clifford multiplication with a vector and are eigenspaces of a chirality operator (here $\omega_4$, there $\gamma_5$); the construction transfers by replacing the Euclidean form with the Minkowski form and $Sp(1)\times Sp(1)$ with $\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$, so the same quaternion-versus-matrix dictionary organises both.

- **[[Thm - Spin Groups in Dimensions Three and Four via Quaternions]].** This page identifies the *algebras*; that page identifies the *groups* sitting inside them. The isomorphisms $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$ and $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^4)\cong\mathbb{H}\oplus\mathbb{H}$ restrict to $Spin(3)\cong Sp(1)$ and $Spin(4)\cong Sp(1)\times Sp(1)$, and the spinor modules of this page become the representations $\alpha$ and $\beta$ there; the bridge is that the spin group is by definition the even-unit-vector products, which live in exactly the even subalgebra this page computes.

- **[[Thm - Spinor Representations of Spin(4) and the Self-Dual Forms]].** That page takes the chiral modules $\slashed{S}^{\pm}$ built here and works out how two-forms act on them, obtaining the isomorphisms $\Lambda^2_+\cong\mathfrak{su}(\slashed{S}^{+})$ that drive Seiberg–Witten theory. The bridge is the block-off-diagonal form of $c(h)$ from Part (iii), Step 5, together with the volume-element sign of Step 6: those two facts are exactly what lets one compute the action of $e_ae_b$ on each half.
