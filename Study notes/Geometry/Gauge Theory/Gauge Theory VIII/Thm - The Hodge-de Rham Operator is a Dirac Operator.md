# Notation

Throughout, $(M, g)$ is a smooth oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold, $\Omega^k(M) = \Gamma(\Lambda^k T^*M)$ denotes the smooth $k$-forms, and $\Omega^\bullet(M) = \bigoplus_{k=0}^n \Omega^k(M)$ the full exterior algebra of forms. We write $\nabla$ for the [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita connection]] of $g$ on $TM$ and, by the same symbol, for the [[Def - Induced Connection on Tensor Bundles|connection it induces]] on $T^*M$ and on every $\Lambda^k T^*M$; when we must distinguish it from other connections we write $\nabla^{LC}$. The [[Def - Metric-Compatible Connection|musical isomorphisms]] $\flat : TM \to T^*M$, $v \mapsto v^\flat := g(v, \cdot)$, and its inverse $\sharp : T^*M \to TM$ raise and lower indices. The metric $g$ induces a fibre metric $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ on each $\Lambda^k T^*M$, characterised by $\langle e^I, e^J\rangle = \delta_{IJ}$ on the basis $\{e^I = e^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_k} : i_1 < \cdots < i_k\}$ dual to an oriented orthonormal frame $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ of $TM$, where $(e^i)$ is the dual coframe and $e^i = e_i^\flat$. For a vector $v \in T_mM$ the [[Def - Interior Product (Contraction with a Vector Field)|interior product]] $\iota_v : \Lambda^k T_m^*M \to \Lambda^{k-1} T_m^*M$ is contraction with $v$. The exterior derivative is $d : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k+1}(M)$; the [[Def - The Codifferential|codifferential]] is $d^* = \delta : \Omega^k(M) \to \Omega^{k-1}(M)$; the $\star$ is the [[Def - The Hodge Star Operator|Hodge star]]; $\mathrm{vol} = \mathrm{vol}_g$ is the [[Def - Riemannian Volume Form|Riemannian volume form]]. The $L^2$ pairing of forms is $\langle\alpha, \beta\rangle_{L^2} = \int_M \langle\alpha, \beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$. The full symbol registry for the chapter is on the parent page **Gauge Theory VIII — Clifford Algebras, Spin Structures, and Dirac Operators**.

This page depends on three standing conventions, which we state before using them.

> [!warning] Convention: sign of Clifford multiplication on forms
> We give $E := \Lambda T^*M$ the **[[Def - Clifford Bundle and Bundle of Clifford Modules|bundle-of-Clifford-modules]] structure** in which a tangent vector $v \in T_mM$ acts on a form $\phi \in \Lambda^\bullet T_m^*M$ by
> $$v \cdot \phi := v^\flat \wedge \phi - \iota_v \phi.$$
> This is the Lawson–Michelsohn convention adopted for the whole series, and it is the sign for which the Dirac operator comes out as $d + d^*$ with no overall minus sign. Haydys's equation (110) instead writes the action as $u \cdot \phi = \iota_u\phi - u^\flat\wedge\phi$, the **opposite** sign; the two differ by the Clifford-algebra automorphism $v \mapsto -v$, and both satisfy the [[Def - Clifford Algebra and Clifford Multiplication|Clifford relation]] $v \cdot (v \cdot \phi) = -|v|^2\phi$ (verified in the proof). With Haydys's sign the Dirac operator would be $-(d + d^*)$; adopting the series sign gives exactly $d + d^*$, as in his Example 114 (which cites [LM89, Thm 5.12], where the series sign is used).

> [!warning] Convention: sign of the codifferential
> The vault's [[Def - The Codifferential|codifferential]] is $d^* = \delta = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d\star$ on $k$-forms in the Riemannian case, with the [[Def - The Hodge Star Operator|Hodge star]] fixed by $\omega \wedge \eta = \langle\star\omega, \eta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$ (Bär's convention, so that $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)}$ on $k$-forms in Riemannian signature). Haydys and the theorem statement below write the same operator as $(-1)^{n(k-1)+1}\star d\star$. The two exponents differ by $n(k-1) - n(k+1) = -2n$, an even number, so $(-1)^{n(k-1)+1} = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1}$: the formulas are identical, and we use whichever exponent is convenient, noting the parity agreement where it matters.

> [!warning] Convention: the Clifford relation and $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$
> The series' Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ of a Euclidean space $U$ is generated by $U$ with $v \cdot v = -|v|^2\,1$; in the vault's general notation this is $\mathrm{Cl}(U, -|\cdot|^2) = \mathrm{Cl}(0, n)$. A [[Def - Clifford Bundle and Bundle of Clifford Modules|bundle of Clifford modules]] over $M$ is a vector bundle $E \to M$ with a bundle map $\mathrm{Cl} : TM \otimes E \to E$, $(v, e) \mapsto v \cdot e$, satisfying $v \cdot (v \cdot e) = -|v|^2 e$.

# Statement

> **Theorem (the Hodge–de Rham operator is a Dirac operator).** Let $(M, g)$ be an oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold. Equip $E := \Lambda T^*M = \bigoplus_{k=0}^n \Lambda^k T^*M$ with
> - the Clifford multiplication $v \cdot \phi = v^\flat\wedge\phi - \iota_v\phi$, making $E$ a [[Def - Clifford Bundle and Bundle of Clifford Modules|bundle of Clifford modules]];
> - the fibre metric $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ induced by $g$;
> - the [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita connection]] $\nabla^{LC}$.
>
> Then $E$ is a **[[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator|Dirac bundle]]**: it satisfies the three Dirac-bundle conditions
> 1. $\nabla^{LC}$ is metric on $E$;
> 2. $\langle v\cdot\phi_1, v\cdot\phi_2\rangle = |v|^2\langle\phi_1, \phi_2\rangle$ for all $v \in T_mM$, $\phi_1, \phi_2 \in E_m$;
> 3. $\nabla^{LC}(\phi\cdot s) = (\nabla^{LC}\phi)\cdot s + \phi\cdot\nabla^{LC}s$ for $\phi \in \Gamma(\mathrm{Cl}(M))$, $s \in \Gamma(E)$.
>
> Its **[[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator|Dirac operator]]** $D = \sum_i e_i\cdot\nabla^{LC}_{e_i}$ (any local oriented orthonormal frame $(e_i)$) is the **Hodge–de Rham operator**
> $$D = d + d^*,$$
> where $d$ is the [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold|exterior derivative]] and $d^* = \delta = (-1)^{n(k-1)+1}\star d\star$ (equivalently $(-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d\star$) on $k$-forms is the [[Def - The Codifferential|codifferential]]. Explicitly, in any local oriented orthonormal frame,
> $$d = \sum_{i=1}^n e^i\wedge\nabla^{LC}_{e_i}, \qquad\qquad d^* = -\sum_{i=1}^n \iota_{e_i}\nabla^{LC}_{e_i}.$$

The proof follows Lawson–Michelsohn, *Spin Geometry*, Theorem II.5.12 (their Lemma II.5.13 gives the two frame formulas), with every step supplied in full.

# Motivation

The Dirac operator is the central object of this chapter, but on a general manifold it is defined abstractly: choose a bundle of Clifford modules, put on it a compatible metric and connection, and form $D = \mathrm{Cl}\circ\nabla$. The construction is clean, but for a reader meeting it for the first time it can seem to conjure a first-order elliptic operator out of nothing. This theorem grounds the abstraction in an operator every geometer already knows. The exterior algebra $\Lambda T^*M$ is a bundle of Clifford modules for free — no spin structure, no representation theory, nothing beyond the Riemannian metric — and the Dirac operator it carries is exactly $d + d^*$, the operator whose square is the [[Def - Hodge Laplacian|Hodge Laplacian]] and whose kernel computes the de Rham cohomology by the Hodge theorem. So the whole apparatus of Dirac operators is not new: it is the invariant, Clifford-algebraic repackaging of the exterior derivative and its adjoint.

The point cuts both ways. On one side, it certifies the Dirac-bundle axioms against a case where the answer is known: if the three conditions did not hold for $\Lambda T^*M$, or if the frame formula did not reproduce $d + d^*$, the definition would be wrong. On the other side, it exports every general theorem about Dirac operators to differential forms at no cost. Formal self-adjointness of $D$ (proved for all Dirac bundles on [[Thm - Dirac Operators are Formally Self-Adjoint|the self-adjointness page]]) becomes the mutual $L^2$-adjointness of $d$ and $d^*$; the Weitzenböck formula $D^2 = \nabla^*\nabla + \mathcal{R}$ becomes, on $1$-forms, the Bochner formula $dd^* + d^*d = \nabla^*\nabla + \operatorname{Ric}$; ellipticity of $D$ becomes ellipticity of the de Rham complex. The theorem is the bridge over which the analytic power of the Dirac formalism reaches classical Hodge theory, and it is the reason the two subjects are one.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The literal hypothesis is only "$(M, g)$ oriented Riemannian", which is as weak as a hypothesis gets, so the real skill is recognising when a problem is secretly asking for the Dirac bundle $\Lambda T^*M$ or its operator $d + d^*$.

The first disguised source is **an elliptic complex equipped with a fibre metric that one wishes to roll up into a single first-order operator**. Property $B$: a cochain complex $(\Gamma(E^\bullet), D_k)$ with $D_{k+1}D_k = 0$, each $E^k$ carrying a metric, over a closed manifold. The bridge is that the rolled-up operator $D + D^*$ on $\bigoplus_k E^k$ is formally self-adjoint and elliptic exactly when the symbol sequence is a Koszul (Clifford) complex, and $(\Lambda^\bullet T^*M, d)$ is the model case: its rolled-up operator is $d + d^*$, which this theorem identifies as the Dirac operator of a Dirac bundle. Recognising the de Rham complex as an instance lets one transport the Hodge decomposition to the abstract complex. *Example problem:* show that for the Dolbeault complex $(\Omega^{0,\bullet}, \bar\partial)$ on a Kähler manifold the operator $\sqrt2(\bar\partial + \bar\partial^*)$ is again a Dirac operator, by exhibiting the Clifford module structure that the metric and complex structure supply.

The second disguised source is **a first-order operator on forms whose principal symbol is $\xi \mapsto \xi^\flat\wedge - \iota_{\xi^\sharp}$**. Property $B$: an operator $P : \Omega^\bullet \to \Omega^\bullet$ with $Ps = \sum_i (e^i\wedge - \iota_{e_i})\nabla_{e_i}s + (\text{zeroth order})$ for some metric connection $\nabla$. The bridge is that "Dirac type" is a condition on the symbol alone, and the symbol here is precisely Clifford multiplication on $\Lambda T^*M$; the leading part of $P$ therefore agrees with $d + d^*$ up to the choice of connection and a zeroth-order term, and the theorem's frame formulas identify it. *Example problem:* a physicist writes a fermionic action whose kinetic operator on $p$-form fields is $\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu$ with $\gamma^\mu = dx^\mu\wedge - \iota_{\partial_\mu}$; recognise it as $d + d^*$ and read off the equations of motion as the harmonic-form equations.

The third disguised source is **a twisted or graded version of the exterior algebra**. Property $B$: a bundle of the form $\Lambda T^*M \otimes F$ for a Hermitian bundle $F$ with a unitary connection, or the even/odd splitting $\Lambda^{\mathrm{ev}} \oplus \Lambda^{\mathrm{odd}}$. The bridge is [[Ex - The Twisted Bundle of a Dirac Bundle is a Dirac Bundle|the tensoring construction]]: tensoring a Dirac bundle with an auxiliary bundle-with-connection produces a Dirac bundle, so once $\Lambda T^*M$ is known to be a Dirac bundle (this theorem), $\Lambda T^*M \otimes F$ is one too, with Dirac operator the twisted $d_F + d_F^*$. *Example problem:* show that the signature operator $d + d^*$ acting between the $\pm 1$-eigenbundles of the involution $\tau = i^{p(p-1)+\ell}\star$ on a closed oriented $4\ell$-manifold is the Dirac operator of the corresponding graded Dirac bundle, so that its index is the signature of $M$.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The bare conclusion is the identity $D = d + d^*$ on $\Lambda T^*M$. Combined with the general theory it yields far more.

Combine the conclusion with **the general Weitzenböck formula** $D^2 = \nabla^*\nabla + \mathcal{R}$ for Dirac bundles ([[Thm - Weitzenbock Formula for the Dirac Operator|Weitzenböck page]]). Since $D = d + d^*$ gives $D^2 = (d + d^*)^2 = dd^* + d^*d = \Delta$, the [[Def - Hodge Laplacian|Hodge Laplacian]], the general formula specialises to $\Delta = \nabla^*\nabla + \mathcal{R}$ on forms. Computing $\mathcal{R}$ on $1$-forms with [[Def - Curvature Endomorphism of a Dirac Bundle|the curvature endomorphism]] yields the classical **Bochner formula** $\Delta = \nabla^*\nabla + \operatorname{Ric}$ (worked out in [[Ex - Bochner's Formula for 1-Forms from the Weitzenbock Formula|the Bochner exercise]]), and thence the vanishing of harmonic $1$-forms under positive Ricci curvature. The extra ingredient is the identification of the zeroth-order Weitzenböck term with a curvature of $M$; the payoff is a topological obstruction (a bound on $b_1$) from a curvature hypothesis.

Combine the conclusion with **the four-dimensional chirality splitting**. On an oriented Riemannian $4$-manifold the Hodge star squares to $+1$ on $\Omega^2$, splitting it into self-dual and anti-self-dual parts $\Omega^2 = \Omega^2_+ \oplus \Omega^2_-$ ([[Def - Self-Dual and Anti-Self-Dual Forms|self-dual forms]]). The operator $d + d^*$ restricted to the subcomplex $\Omega^0 \oplus \Omega^2_+ \xleftarrow{} \Omega^1 \xrightarrow{} \dots$ becomes the operator $d^* \oplus d^+ : \Omega^1 \to \Omega^0 \oplus \Omega^2_+$ that Atiyah–Hitchin–Singer use as the deformation operator of the anti-self-dual moduli space in chapter XI; the theorem says this is a Dirac operator restricted to a subbundle, so its index theory is governed by the Dirac index theorem. The extra ingredient is the self-dual/anti-self-dual decomposition; the payoff is that instanton deformation theory is Dirac-operator index theory.

Combine the conclusion with **ellipticity and the Hodge theorem**. A Dirac operator is elliptic ([[Thm - Dirac Operators are Formally Self-Adjoint|self-adjoint]] and, in chapter IX, elliptic), so on a closed manifold $\ker(d + d^*)$ is finite-dimensional, and formal self-adjointness gives the $L^2$-orthogonal decomposition $\Omega^\bullet = \ker D \oplus \operatorname{im} D$. Since $\ker(d + d^*) = \ker\Delta$ is the harmonic forms, this is the Hodge decomposition, and the Euler characteristic $\chi(M) = \sum_k (-1)^k b_k$ appears as the index of $d + d^*$ between even and odd forms. The extra ingredient is the elliptic package; the payoff is that the alternating sum of Betti numbers is an index, hence a Dirac index-theorem computation.

# Why Is It True

Forget the Dirac formalism and look at what $d$ does in an orthonormal frame. The exterior derivative of a function is $df = \sum_i (e_i f)\,e^i = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}f$, and one expects, and it is true, that the same recipe — differentiate in each frame direction $e_i$ and wedge on the covector $e^i$ — computes $d$ on every form: $d = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}$. The only content in this is that the connection terms $\nabla_{e_i}$ contribute nothing beyond $d$, and that is exactly the statement that the Levi-Civita connection is **torsion-free**: torsion is the antisymmetric part of $\nabla$, and $d$ is the antisymmetrisation of $\nabla$, so the two agree precisely when the torsion vanishes. Dually, the metric adjoint of $e^i\wedge$ is $\iota_{e_i}$, so the metric adjoint of $d = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}$ is $-\sum_i \iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}$ — the sign coming from integration by parts moving the derivative across — and this must be the codifferential $d^*$, because $d^*$ is *defined* to be the adjoint of $d$.

Now recall the Clifford multiplication on forms, $v\cdot = v^\flat\wedge - \iota_v$. It is built out of exactly the two operators that appear above: wedging and contracting. So the Dirac operator, which sums $e_i\cdot\nabla_{e_i}$, splits into a wedging part and a contracting part,
$$\sum_i e_i\cdot\nabla_{e_i} = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i} \;-\; \sum_i \iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i} = d + d^*.$$
The wedging part raises degree and is $d$; the contracting part lowers degree and is $d^*$. There is nothing more to it than the observation that Clifford multiplication on the exterior algebra is "wedge minus contract", and that these are respectively the exterior derivative and (up to sign) its adjoint once they are paired with the covariant derivative.

> **The mechanism in one sentence.** Clifford multiplication on $\Lambda T^*M$ is *wedge minus interior product*; paired with the covariant derivative and summed over an orthonormal frame, the wedge half reassembles the exterior derivative (by torsion-freeness) and the interior-product half reassembles its metric adjoint (by integration by parts), so the Dirac operator is $d + d^*$.

---

# What Makes This Hard

Three steps carry all the difficulty. The first is the identity $d = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}$: it is quickly verified on functions and readily *guessed* in general, but the honest proof requires knowing that $d$ is characterised as the unique antiderivation extending $f \mapsto df$, and that the candidate $\sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}$ is an antiderivation that annihilates exact one-forms — and the annihilation is exactly where torsion-freeness enters, through the symmetry of the Hessian $\nabla^2 f$. A reader who omits torsion-freeness has not proved the formula; on a connection with torsion, $\sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}$ is *not* $d$. The second hard step is that the frame formula $-\sum_i\iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}$ equals $d^*$ and not merely "an adjoint of $d$": one must both show it is a formal adjoint (an integration-by-parts computation that produces a divergence) and invoke the uniqueness of formal adjoints, then reconcile with the explicit $\star d\star$ formula through the parity of the sign exponents. The third, easily skipped, is verifying the Dirac-bundle conditions at all: in particular that Clifford multiplication $v\cdot = v^\flat\wedge - \iota_v$ is skew-adjoint, which rests on the mutual adjointness of $v^\flat\wedge$ and $\iota_v$ — a piece of exterior-algebra bookkeeping that is true but must be checked, not asserted.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** First verify that $\Lambda T^*M$ with $v\cdot = v^\flat\wedge - \iota_v$, the induced metric, and the Levi-Civita connection satisfies the three Dirac-bundle conditions; the only non-formal input is the mutual adjointness of wedging and contracting. Then split the frame formula $D = \sum_i e_i\cdot\nabla_{e_i}$ into its degree-raising and degree-lowering halves, identify the raising half with $d$ (an antiderivation agreeing with $d$ on generators, using torsion-freeness), and the lowering half with $d^*$ (a formal adjoint of $d$, unique, and equal to the codifferential by the vault's adjoint theorem).

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Mutual adjointness of wedge and contraction.** Show $\langle v^\flat\wedge\alpha, \beta\rangle = \langle\alpha, \iota_v\beta\rangle$ pointwise for all $v$, $\alpha \in \Lambda^{k-1}$, $\beta \in \Lambda^k$.
   - *Hint:* By linearity in $v$, reduce to $v = e_j$ in an orthonormal frame, then check on the orthonormal basis $\{e^I\}$; both sides are non-zero for the same pair of multi-indices, with matching sign.
   - *Why needed:* It gives condition (2) (skew-adjointness of $v\cdot$) and the integration-by-parts step for $d^*$.

2. **The Dirac-bundle conditions.** Show $\nabla^{LC}$ is metric on $\Lambda T^*M$ (condition 1), that $v\cdot$ is skew-adjoint hence condition (2) holds, and that $\nabla^{LC}$ is a derivation over Clifford multiplication (condition 3).
   - *Hint:* Conditions (1) and (3) follow from the induced connection commuting with the metric, with contraction, and with the wedge product; (2) follows from subgoal 1 and the Clifford relation.
   - *Why needed:* Without them $\Lambda T^*M$ is not a Dirac bundle and $D$ is not its Dirac operator.

3. **The exterior derivative frame formula.** Show $d = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}$.
   - *Hint:* The right side is a frame-independent antiderivation; it equals $df$ on functions; it annihilates $d(df)$ because the Hessian $\nabla^2 f$ is symmetric, which is torsion-freeness. Two antiderivations agreeing on functions and exact one-forms agree.
   - *Why needed:* It identifies the degree-raising half of $D$ with $d$.

4. **The divergence integrates to zero.** Show $\int_M \operatorname{div}(V)\,\mathrm{vol} = 0$ for compactly supported $V$.
   - *Hint:* $\operatorname{div}(V)\,\mathrm{vol} = d(\iota_V\mathrm{vol})$, then Stokes.
   - *Why needed:* It is the analytic engine turning a pointwise identity into an $L^2$-adjoint identity.

5. **The lowering half is a formal adjoint of $d$.** Show $\int_M\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int_M\langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$ for $\delta := -\sum_i\iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}$ and compactly supported forms.
   - *Hint:* Show the pointwise identity $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle - \langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle = \operatorname{div}(W)$ for an explicit $W$, computing in a synchronous frame; integrate using subgoal 4.
   - *Why needed:* It is half of "$\delta = d^*$".

6. **Uniqueness of formal adjoints, and reconciliation.** Show a formal adjoint of $d$ is unique, and that the codifferential $(-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d\star$ is a formal adjoint of $d$; conclude $\delta = d^*$ and $(-1)^{n(k-1)+1} = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1}$.
   - *Hint:* Uniqueness is the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations; the codifferential's adjointness is the vault's [[Thm - Codifferential is the Adjoint of d|adjoint theorem]].
   - *Why needed:* It upgrades "$\delta$ is a formal adjoint" to "$\delta$ is *the* codifferential $d^*$".

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Wedging by $v^\flat$ and contracting by $v$ are mutually adjoint
> **Statement:** Let $(V, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an $n$-dimensional real inner-product space with induced inner product on $\Lambda^\bullet V^*$. For every $v \in V$, $\alpha \in \Lambda^{k-1}V^*$, and $\beta \in \Lambda^k V^*$,
> $$\langle v^\flat\wedge\alpha, \beta\rangle = \langle\alpha, \iota_v\beta\rangle.$$
>
> **Hint:** Both sides are linear in $v$, so reduce to $v = e_j$ for an orthonormal basis $(e_i)$; then check on the orthonormal monomial basis $\{e^I\}$, comparing when each side is non-zero and its sign.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the exterior-algebra fact underlying condition (2) of the Dirac bundle (skew-adjointness of $v\cdot$) and the integration-by-parts step identifying $d^*$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **We must show** the displayed identity for all $v, \alpha, \beta$. Fix an orthonormal basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ of $V$ with dual basis $(e^1, \dots, e^n)$ of $V^*$; then $e_j^\flat = e^j$, and $\{e^I : |I| = k\}$ (increasing multi-indices) is an orthonormal basis of $\Lambda^k V^*$ because $\langle e^I, e^J\rangle = \det(\langle e^{i_a}, e^{j_b}\rangle) = \det(\delta_{i_a j_b}) = \delta_{IJ}$ (the determinant characterisation of the induced inner product on decomposable forms).
> >
> > **Reduction to $v = e_j$.** Both sides of the identity are linear in $v$: $v \mapsto v^\flat\wedge\alpha$ and $v \mapsto \iota_v\beta$ are linear. Writing $v = \sum_j v^j e_j$ and expanding, it suffices to prove $\langle e^j\wedge\alpha, \beta\rangle = \langle\alpha, \iota_{e_j}\beta\rangle$ for each $j$. By bilinearity in $(\alpha, \beta)$, it suffices to check this on basis monomials $\alpha = e^I$ ($|I| = k-1$) and $\beta = e^J$ ($|J| = k$).
> >
> > **Case $j \notin J$.** Then $\iota_{e_j}e^J = 0$ (the monomial $e^J$ contains no factor $e^j$ to contract), so the right side is $\langle e^I, 0\rangle = 0$. On the left, $e^j\wedge e^I$ is either $0$ (if $j \in I$) or $\pm e^{\{j\}\cup I}$; in the latter case $\langle e^{\{j\}\cup I}, e^J\rangle = 0$ because $\{j\}\cup I \neq J$ as sets (since $j \in \{j\}\cup I$ but $j \notin J$). So the left side is $0$ as well, and both sides agree.
> >
> > **Case $j \in J$.** Write $J = \{j_1 < \cdots < j_k\}$ and let $p$ be the position with $j_p = j$. By the definition of the interior product on a monomial,
> > $$\iota_{e_j}e^J = \sum_{q=1}^k (-1)^{q-1}e^{j_q}(e_j)\,e^{J\setminus\{j_q\}} = (-1)^{p-1}e^{J\setminus\{j\}} \qquad \text{(only } q = p \text{ has } e^{j_q}(e_j) = \delta_{j_q j} = 1\text{).}$$
> > Hence the right side is $\langle e^I, (-1)^{p-1}e^{J\setminus\{j\}}\rangle = (-1)^{p-1}\,\delta_{I,\,J\setminus\{j\}}$, non-zero exactly when $I = J\setminus\{j\}$, where it equals $(-1)^{p-1}$.
> > On the left, $e^j\wedge e^I$ is non-zero only when $j \notin I$, and then $\langle e^j\wedge e^I, e^J\rangle \neq 0$ requires $\{j\}\cup I = J$ as sets, i.e. $I = J\setminus\{j\}$. In that case $e^j\wedge e^I = e^{j}\wedge e^{J\setminus\{j\}}$; reordering the factors $e^{j}, e^{j_1}, \dots, \widehat{e^{j}}, \dots, e^{j_k}$ into the increasing order $e^{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{j_k} = e^J$ moves $e^{j} = e^{j_p}$ across the $p-1$ factors preceding its sorted position, contributing the sign $(-1)^{p-1}$, so $e^j\wedge e^I = (-1)^{p-1}e^J$ and $\langle e^j\wedge e^I, e^J\rangle = (-1)^{p-1}$.
> > Thus both sides vanish unless $I = J\setminus\{j\}$, in which case both equal $(-1)^{p-1}$.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** The identity holds on all basis pairs for each $v = e_j$, hence by bilinearity and linearity in $v$ for all $v, \alpha, \beta$. Therefore $v^\flat\wedge$ and $\iota_v$ are mutually adjoint on $\Lambda^\bullet V^*$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: $\Lambda T^*M$ with the Levi-Civita connection satisfies the Dirac-bundle conditions
> **Statement:** With $v\cdot = v^\flat\wedge - \iota_v$, the induced fibre metric, and the Levi-Civita connection $\nabla^{LC}$: (1) $\nabla^{LC}$ is metric on $\Lambda T^*M$; (2) $\langle v\cdot\phi_1, v\cdot\phi_2\rangle = |v|^2\langle\phi_1, \phi_2\rangle$; equivalently $v\cdot$ is skew-adjoint, $\langle v\cdot\phi_1, \phi_2\rangle = -\langle\phi_1, v\cdot\phi_2\rangle$; (3) $\nabla^{LC}(\phi\cdot s) = (\nabla^{LC}\phi)\cdot s + \phi\cdot\nabla^{LC}s$.
>
> **Hint:** Conditions (1) and (3) follow because the induced connection commutes with the metric, with contraction, and with the wedge product, and because $\sharp$ is parallel; condition (2) follows from Lemma 1 and the Clifford relation.
>
> **Why needed:** These three conditions are exactly what makes $\Lambda T^*M$ a Dirac bundle, so that $D = \sum_i e_i\cdot\nabla_{e_i}$ is a genuine Dirac operator.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **We must show** the three conditions. Throughout, $\nabla = \nabla^{LC}$ denotes the [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita connection]] and its [[Def - Induced Connection on Tensor Bundles|induced connection]] on $T^*M$ and $\Lambda^\bullet T^*M$, and $X$ is a vector field on $M$.
> >
> > **Preliminary — the induced structure is compatible with $\flat, \sharp$, contraction, and $\wedge$.** Because $\nabla$ is [[Def - Metric-Compatible Connection|metric-compatible]] on $TM$, the musical isomorphism is parallel: $\nabla_X(Y^\flat) = (\nabla_X Y)^\flat$ for all vector fields $Y$ (this is the statement that $g$, viewed as a section of $T^*M\otimes T^*M$, is parallel). By the defining property of the induced connection on tensor bundles, $\nabla_X$ commutes with every contraction, in particular $\nabla_X(\iota_v\psi) = \iota_{\nabla_X v}\psi + \iota_v\nabla_X\psi$ for a vector field $v$ and a form $\psi$; and $\nabla_X$ is a derivation of the wedge product, $\nabla_X(\theta\wedge\psi) = (\nabla_X\theta)\wedge\psi + \theta\wedge\nabla_X\psi$, because the wedge product is the antisymmetrisation of the (parallel) tensor product and $\nabla_X$ is a derivation of $\otimes$.
> >
> > **Condition (1): $\nabla$ is metric on $\Lambda^k T^*M$.** Fix $m \in M$ and choose, by [[Thm - Existence of Synchronous Orthonormal Frames|the existence of synchronous orthonormal frames]], an oriented orthonormal frame $(e_i)$ of $TM$ near $m$ with $\nabla e_i(m) = 0$. The dual coframe then satisfies $\nabla e^i(m) = 0$ (differentiate $e^i(e_j) = \delta^i_j$: $0 = (\nabla_X e^i)(e_j) + e^i(\nabla_X e_j)$, and at $m$ the second term vanishes for all $j$, so $\nabla_X e^i(m) = 0$), and hence $\nabla e^I(m) = 0$ for every increasing multi-index (derivation of $\wedge$). For forms $\alpha = \sum_I \alpha_I e^I$, $\beta = \sum_J \beta_J e^J$ of degree $k$, orthonormality of $\{e^I\}$ gives $\langle\alpha, \beta\rangle = \sum_I \alpha_I\beta_I$. At $m$,
> > $$X\langle\alpha, \beta\rangle = \sum_I \big((X\alpha_I)\beta_I + \alpha_I(X\beta_I)\big) \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule for the vector field } X \text{ on functions).}$$
> > Also at $m$, since $\nabla e^I(m) = 0$, $\nabla_X\alpha(m) = \sum_I (X\alpha_I)\,e^I$, so $\langle\nabla_X\alpha, \beta\rangle(m) = \sum_I (X\alpha_I)\beta_I$, and likewise $\langle\alpha, \nabla_X\beta\rangle(m) = \sum_I \alpha_I(X\beta_I)$. Adding, $\langle\nabla_X\alpha, \beta\rangle + \langle\alpha, \nabla_X\beta\rangle = X\langle\alpha, \beta\rangle$ at $m$. Both sides are values of frame-independent expressions and $m$ was arbitrary, so the identity holds on all of $M$: $\nabla$ is metric on $\Lambda^k T^*M$.
> >
> > **Condition (2).** By Lemma 1, $\langle v^\flat\wedge\phi_1, \phi_2\rangle = \langle\phi_1, \iota_v\phi_2\rangle$ and (applying Lemma 1 with the roles of the arguments swapped) $\langle\iota_v\phi_1, \phi_2\rangle = \langle\phi_1, v^\flat\wedge\phi_2\rangle$. Hence
> > $$\langle v\cdot\phi_1, \phi_2\rangle = \langle v^\flat\wedge\phi_1, \phi_2\rangle - \langle\iota_v\phi_1, \phi_2\rangle = \langle\phi_1, \iota_v\phi_2\rangle - \langle\phi_1, v^\flat\wedge\phi_2\rangle = -\langle\phi_1, v\cdot\phi_2\rangle,$$
> > so $v\cdot$ is skew-adjoint. Next, the Clifford relation: using $(v^\flat\wedge)^2 = 0$, $(\iota_v)^2 = 0$, and $\iota_v(v^\flat\wedge\psi) = (\iota_v v^\flat)\psi - v^\flat\wedge\iota_v\psi = |v|^2\psi - v^\flat\wedge\iota_v\psi$ (the interior-product Leibniz rule, with $\iota_v v^\flat = v^\flat(v) = g(v,v) = |v|^2$),
> > $$v\cdot(v\cdot\psi) = (v^\flat\wedge - \iota_v)(v^\flat\wedge - \iota_v)\psi = -v^\flat\wedge\iota_v\psi - \iota_v(v^\flat\wedge\psi) = -v^\flat\wedge\iota_v\psi - |v|^2\psi + v^\flat\wedge\iota_v\psi = -|v|^2\psi.$$
> > Combining skew-adjointness with the Clifford relation,
> > $$\langle v\cdot\phi_1, v\cdot\phi_2\rangle = -\langle\phi_1, v\cdot(v\cdot\phi_2)\rangle = -\langle\phi_1, -|v|^2\phi_2\rangle = |v|^2\langle\phi_1, \phi_2\rangle \qquad \text{(skew-adjointness; then the Clifford relation).}$$
> > This is condition (2).
> >
> > **Condition (3).** The Clifford bundle $\mathrm{Cl}(M)$ is generated fibrewise by degree-one elements, i.e. by $T^*M \cong TM$, and the induced connection $\nabla^{LC}$ on $\mathrm{Cl}(M)$ is a derivation of the fibrewise Clifford product ([[Def - Clifford Bundle and Bundle of Clifford Modules|Clifford-bundle page]]). Both sides of condition (3) are derivations in $\phi$ over the Clifford product, so it suffices to verify it for $\phi = \theta$ a one-form, where Clifford multiplication is $\theta\cdot\psi = \theta\wedge\psi - \iota_{\theta^\sharp}\psi$. Using the derivation property over $\wedge$, the commutation with contraction, and the parallelism of $\sharp$ (so $\nabla_X(\theta^\sharp) = (\nabla_X\theta)^\sharp$),
> > $$\nabla_X(\theta\cdot\psi) = \nabla_X(\theta\wedge\psi) - \nabla_X(\iota_{\theta^\sharp}\psi) = (\nabla_X\theta)\wedge\psi + \theta\wedge\nabla_X\psi - \iota_{(\nabla_X\theta)^\sharp}\psi - \iota_{\theta^\sharp}\nabla_X\psi.$$
> > Grouping the terms with $\nabla_X\theta$ and the terms with $\nabla_X\psi$,
> > $$\nabla_X(\theta\cdot\psi) = \big((\nabla_X\theta)\wedge\psi - \iota_{(\nabla_X\theta)^\sharp}\psi\big) + \big(\theta\wedge\nabla_X\psi - \iota_{\theta^\sharp}\nabla_X\psi\big) = (\nabla_X\theta)\cdot\psi + \theta\cdot\nabla_X\psi,$$
> > which is condition (3) for one-forms, hence for all $\phi \in \Gamma(\mathrm{Cl}(M))$.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** All three Dirac-bundle conditions hold, so $(\Lambda T^*M, v\cdot, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle, \nabla^{LC})$ is a Dirac bundle. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The exterior derivative in a frame, $d = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}$
> **Statement:** For any local oriented orthonormal frame $(e_i)$ of $TM$ with dual coframe $(e^i)$, the operator $\tilde d := \sum_{i=1}^n e^i\wedge\nabla^{LC}_{e_i}$ on $\Omega^\bullet(M)$ is independent of the frame and equals the [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold|exterior derivative]] $d$.
>
> **Hint:** Show $\tilde d$ is frame-independent, an antiderivation of degree $+1$, equal to $d$ on functions, and annihilating $d(df)$ — the last using the symmetry of the Hessian, which is torsion-freeness. Then two antiderivations agreeing on functions and on exact one-forms coincide.
>
> **Why needed:** It identifies the degree-raising half of the Dirac operator with $d$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **We must show** $\tilde d = d$. We proceed in four steps.
> >
> > **Step 0 — frame independence.** Let $(\tilde e_a) = (\sum_i e_i O^i_{\ a})$ be a second oriented orthonormal frame, with $O = (O^i_{\ a}) : U \to SO(n)$; the dual coframes are related by $\tilde e^a = \sum_i O^i_{\ a} e^i$ (since $O$ is orthogonal, $O^{-1} = O^{\mathsf T}$). Then, using $C^\infty$-linearity of $\nabla$ in the direction slot,
> > $$\sum_a \tilde e^a\wedge\nabla_{\tilde e_a} = \sum_a \sum_{i, j} O^i_{\ a} O^j_{\ a}\, e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_j} = \sum_{i, j}\Big(\sum_a O^i_{\ a}O^j_{\ a}\Big) e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_j} = \sum_{i, j}\delta_{ij}\, e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_j} = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i},$$
> > where $\sum_a O^i_{\ a}O^j_{\ a} = (OO^{\mathsf T})_{ij} = \delta_{ij}$. So $\tilde d$ does not depend on the frame.
> >
> > **Step 1 — $\tilde d$ is an antiderivation of degree $+1$.** For $\alpha \in \Omega^p$, $\beta \in \Omega^q$, using that $\nabla_{e_i}$ is a derivation of $\wedge$ (Lemma 2, preliminary) and that $e^i$ has degree $1$,
> > $$\tilde d(\alpha\wedge\beta) = \sum_i e^i\wedge\big((\nabla_{e_i}\alpha)\wedge\beta + \alpha\wedge\nabla_{e_i}\beta\big) = \Big(\sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}\alpha\Big)\wedge\beta + (-1)^p\alpha\wedge\Big(\sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}\beta\Big),$$
> > since moving $e^i$ (degree $1$) past $\alpha$ (degree $p$) costs the sign $(-1)^p$. Thus $\tilde d(\alpha\wedge\beta) = \tilde d\alpha\wedge\beta + (-1)^p\alpha\wedge\tilde d\beta$: $\tilde d$ is an antiderivation, and it visibly raises degree by $1$.
> >
> > **Step 2 — $\tilde d = d$ on functions.** For $f \in \Omega^0 = C^\infty(M)$, $\nabla_{e_i}f = e_i f = df(e_i)$, so $\tilde d f = \sum_i (df(e_i))\,e^i = df$, because $\sum_i df(e_i) e^i$ is the expansion of the one-form $df$ in the coframe $(e^i)$.
> >
> > **Step 3 — $\tilde d$ annihilates exact one-forms.** Let $f \in C^\infty(M)$. The second covariant derivative (Hessian) $\nabla^2 f(X, Y) := (\nabla_X df)(Y) = X(Yf) - (\nabla_X Y)f$ is **symmetric** in $X, Y$: using torsion-freeness of the [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita connection]], which by definition of the [[Def - Torsion Tensor|torsion tensor]] reads $\nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X = [X, Y]$,
> > $$\nabla^2 f(X, Y) - \nabla^2 f(Y, X) = X(Yf) - (\nabla_X Y)f - Y(Xf) + (\nabla_Y X)f = [X, Y]f - (\nabla_X Y - \nabla_Y X)f = [X, Y]f - [X, Y]f = 0.$$
> > Now compute, writing $\nabla_{e_i}(df) = \sum_j \nabla^2 f(e_i, e_j)\,e^j$,
> > $$\tilde d(df) = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}(df) = \sum_{i, j}\nabla^2 f(e_i, e_j)\,e^i\wedge e^j = 0,$$
> > because $\nabla^2 f(e_i, e_j)$ is symmetric in $(i, j)$ while $e^i\wedge e^j$ is antisymmetric, so the double sum vanishes. Hence $\tilde d$ kills every exact one-form $df$.
> >
> > **Step 4 — conclusion by the antiderivation characterisation.** Both $d$ and $\tilde d$ are antiderivations of degree $+1$ on $\Omega^\bullet(M)$ (for $d$ this is part of [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold|its definition]]; for $\tilde d$ it is Step 1), and both agree on $\Omega^0$ (Step 2) and on exact one-forms $df$ (Step 3, where $d(df) = 0 = \tilde d(df)$). Locally every $k$-form is a finite sum $\sum f_0\,df_1\wedge\cdots\wedge df_k$; an antiderivation is determined on such a term by its values on the functions $f_0$ and on the exact one-forms $df_j$ through the Leibniz rule,
> > $$D(f_0\,df_1\wedge\cdots\wedge df_k) = Df_0\wedge df_1\wedge\cdots\wedge df_k + f_0\sum_{r=1}^k (-1)^{r}\,df_1\wedge\cdots\wedge D(df_r)\wedge\cdots\wedge df_k,$$
> > so $d$ and $\tilde d$, agreeing on all $f_0$ and all $df_j$, agree on every such term and hence on all forms. Therefore $\tilde d = d$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 4: A divergence integrates to zero
> **Statement:** Let $(M, g)$ be an oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold without boundary and $V$ a smooth compactly supported vector field. With $\operatorname{div}V := \sum_i\langle\nabla^{LC}_{e_i}V, e_i\rangle$ in any local orthonormal frame (independent of the frame, being the trace of $X \mapsto \nabla_X V$),
> $$\operatorname{div}(V)\,\mathrm{vol} = d(\iota_V\mathrm{vol}), \qquad\text{hence}\qquad \int_M \operatorname{div}(V)\,\mathrm{vol} = 0.$$
>
> **Hint:** Compute at a point in a synchronous frame, where the coframe is parallel and $de^i = 0$; then use [[Thm - Stokes' Theorem on Manifolds|Stokes]].
>
> **Why needed:** It converts the pointwise identity of Lemma 5 into an integrated $L^2$-adjoint identity.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **We must show** the two displayed statements. Fix $m \in M$ and a synchronous oriented orthonormal frame $(e_i)$ at $m$ ([[Thm - Existence of Synchronous Orthonormal Frames|synchronous frames]]), so $\nabla e_i(m) = 0$, hence $\nabla e^i(m) = 0$; by [[Thm - Cartan's First Structural Equation|Cartan's first structural equation]] and torsion-freeness, $de^i = -\sum_k \omega^i_{\ k}\wedge e^k$ with connection forms $\omega^i_{\ k}$ vanishing at $m$, so $de^i(m) = 0$. Write $V = \sum_j V^j e_j$ and $\mathrm{vol} = e^1\wedge\cdots\wedge e^n$. Then
> > $$\iota_V\mathrm{vol} = \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{j-1}V^j\, e^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\widehat{e^j}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^n,$$
> > where $\widehat{\phantom{e}}$ denotes omission. Taking $d$ and evaluating at $m$, the terms in which $d$ hits a coframe factor $e^\ell$ vanish (since $de^\ell(m) = 0$), leaving
> > $$d(\iota_V\mathrm{vol})(m) = \sum_j (-1)^{j-1}\, dV^j\wedge e^1\wedge\cdots\widehat{e^j}\cdots\wedge e^n.$$
> > In $dV^j = \sum_k (e_k V^j)e^k$, only the $k = j$ term survives the wedge with $e^1\wedge\cdots\widehat{e^j}\cdots\wedge e^n$, and $(-1)^{j-1}e^j\wedge e^1\wedge\cdots\widehat{e^j}\cdots\wedge e^n = e^1\wedge\cdots\wedge e^n = \mathrm{vol}$ (moving $e^j$ into its sorted position costs $(-1)^{j-1}$). Hence
> > $$d(\iota_V\mathrm{vol})(m) = \sum_j (e_j V^j)\,\mathrm{vol}.$$
> > On the other hand, in the synchronous frame at $m$, $\operatorname{div}V(m) = \sum_i\langle\nabla_{e_i}V, e_i\rangle(m) = \sum_i\langle\nabla_{e_i}(\textstyle\sum_j V^j e_j), e_i\rangle(m) = \sum_{i, j}\big((e_i V^j)\langle e_j, e_i\rangle + V^j\langle\nabla_{e_i}e_j, e_i\rangle\big)(m) = \sum_j (e_j V^j)(m)$, using $\nabla e_j(m) = 0$ and $\langle e_j, e_i\rangle = \delta_{ij}$. Therefore $d(\iota_V\mathrm{vol})(m) = \operatorname{div}V(m)\,\mathrm{vol}$; as both sides are values of frame-independent expressions and $m$ was arbitrary, $\operatorname{div}(V)\,\mathrm{vol} = d(\iota_V\mathrm{vol})$ on $M$.
> >
> > Finally, since $V$ has compact support, $\iota_V\mathrm{vol}$ is a compactly supported $(n-1)$-form, and by [[Thm - Stokes' Theorem on Manifolds|Stokes' theorem]] on the boundaryless $M$,
> > $$\int_M \operatorname{div}(V)\,\mathrm{vol} = \int_M d(\iota_V\mathrm{vol}) = \int_{\partial M}\iota_V\mathrm{vol} = 0,$$
> > the last integral being over the empty boundary. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 5: The lowering operator $\delta = -\sum_i\iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}$ is a formal adjoint of $d$
> **Statement:** Define $\delta := -\sum_{i}\iota_{e_i}\nabla^{LC}_{e_i}$ in a local orthonormal frame (it is frame-independent, as the degree-lowering half of the frame-independent Dirac operator). For $\alpha \in \Omega^{k-1}(M)$ and $\beta \in \Omega^k(M)$ with at least one compactly supported,
> $$\int_M\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int_M\langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}.$$
> Precisely, the pointwise identity $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle - \langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle = \operatorname{div}(W)$ holds for the vector field $W := \sum_i\langle\alpha, \iota_{e_i}\beta\rangle\,e_i$.
>
> **Hint:** Compute $\operatorname{div}W$ at a point in a synchronous frame using metric compatibility (Lemma 2(1)) and the mutual adjointness of Lemma 1; recognise the two resulting sums as $\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle$ (via Lemma 3) and $-\langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle$. Then integrate with Lemma 4.
>
> **Why needed:** It is one half of the identification $\delta = d^*$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **We must show** the pointwise divergence identity; the integrated identity then follows from Lemma 4. Note first that $W$ is a well-defined smooth vector field: $\langle\alpha, \iota_{e_i}\beta\rangle$ transforms as the components of a genuine one-form (equivalently, $W$ is the metric dual of the one-form $X \mapsto \langle\alpha, \iota_X\beta\rangle$, which is $C^\infty$-linear in $X$), and $W$ inherits compact support from $\alpha$ or $\beta$.
> >
> > Fix $m \in M$ and a synchronous oriented orthonormal frame at $m$, so $\nabla e_i(m) = 0$. Writing $W = \sum_i w_i e_i$ with $w_i = \langle\alpha, \iota_{e_i}\beta\rangle$, the computation in the proof of Lemma 4 gives, at $m$, $\operatorname{div}W(m) = \sum_i (e_i w_i)(m)$. Expand each derivative by the metric compatibility of $\nabla$ on $\Lambda^\bullet T^*M$ (Lemma 2(1)):
> > $$e_i w_i = e_i\langle\alpha, \iota_{e_i}\beta\rangle = \langle\nabla_{e_i}\alpha, \iota_{e_i}\beta\rangle + \langle\alpha, \nabla_{e_i}(\iota_{e_i}\beta)\rangle \qquad \text{(metric compatibility).}$$
> > At $m$, $\nabla_{e_i}(\iota_{e_i}\beta) = \iota_{\nabla_{e_i}e_i}\beta + \iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}\beta = \iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}\beta$ (the induced connection commutes with contraction, and $\nabla_{e_i}e_i(m) = 0$). Summing over $i$ at $m$,
> > $$\operatorname{div}W(m) = \sum_i\langle\nabla_{e_i}\alpha, \iota_{e_i}\beta\rangle + \sum_i\langle\alpha, \iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}\beta\rangle.$$
> > For the first sum, apply the mutual adjointness of Lemma 1, $\langle\nabla_{e_i}\alpha, \iota_{e_i}\beta\rangle = \langle e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}\alpha, \beta\rangle$, and then Lemma 3, $\sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}\alpha = d\alpha$:
> > $$\sum_i\langle\nabla_{e_i}\alpha, \iota_{e_i}\beta\rangle = \Big\langle\sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}\alpha,\ \beta\Big\rangle = \langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle.$$
> > For the second sum, $\sum_i\langle\alpha, \iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}\beta\rangle = \big\langle\alpha,\ \sum_i\iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}\beta\big\rangle = \langle\alpha, -\delta\beta\rangle = -\langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle$, by the definition $\delta = -\sum_i\iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}$. Therefore
> > $$\operatorname{div}W(m) = \langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle(m) - \langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle(m).$$
> > Both sides are values of frame-independent quantities, and $m$ was arbitrary, so $\operatorname{div}W = \langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle - \langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle$ everywhere on $M$. Integrating and applying Lemma 4 (with $W$ compactly supported),
> > $$\int_M\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} - \int_M\langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int_M\operatorname{div}(W)\,\mathrm{vol} = 0,$$
> > which is the claimed adjoint identity. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 6: Formal adjoints are unique
> **Statement:** Let $E, F \to M$ be Euclidean vector bundles over a Riemannian manifold and $L : \Gamma(E) \to \Gamma(F)$ a linear operator. If $A, B : \Gamma(F) \to \Gamma(E)$ both satisfy $\int_M\langle Ls, t\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int_M\langle s, At\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int_M\langle s, Bt\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$ for all compactly supported $s \in \Gamma(E)$, $t \in \Gamma(F)$, then $A = B$.
>
> **Hint:** $\int_M\langle s, (A - B)t\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = 0$ for all compactly supported $s$; use a bump-function argument (the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations) to conclude $(A - B)t = 0$.
>
> **Why needed:** It upgrades "$\delta$ is a formal adjoint of $d$" (Lemma 5) and "the codifferential is a formal adjoint of $d$" (vault theorem) to "$\delta$ equals the codifferential".
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **We must show** $A = B$. Fix a compactly supported $t \in \Gamma(F)$ and set $\gamma := (A - B)t \in \Gamma(E)$. Subtracting the two adjoint identities, $\int_M\langle s, \gamma\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = 0$ for every compactly supported $s \in \Gamma(E)$.
> >
> > Suppose, for contradiction, that $\gamma \neq 0$; then $\gamma(m_0) \neq 0$ for some $m_0$, so $\langle\gamma, \gamma\rangle(m_0) = |\gamma(m_0)|^2 > 0$. By continuity of $\langle\gamma, \gamma\rangle$ there is an open neighbourhood $U \ni m_0$ with $\langle\gamma, \gamma\rangle > 0$ on $U$. Choose a smooth bump function $\chi \geq 0$ with support a compact subset of $U$ and $\chi(m_0) > 0$, and set $s := \chi\gamma \in \Gamma(E)$, which is compactly supported. Then
> > $$0 = \int_M\langle s, \gamma\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int_M \chi\,\langle\gamma, \gamma\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int_U \chi\,|\gamma|^2\,\mathrm{vol} > 0,$$
> > since the integrand $\chi|\gamma|^2$ is non-negative, continuous, and strictly positive at $m_0$. This contradicts the vanishing of the integral. Hence $\gamma = 0$, i.e. $(A - B)t = 0$ for every compactly supported $t$. As $M$ is covered by such $t$ locally (multiply any section by a bump function), $A - B = 0$ on all of $\Gamma(F)$, so $A = B$. $\blacksquare$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $(M, g)$ be an oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold and $E = \Lambda T^*M$ with the structure of the Statement.
>
> **Step 0 — the objects are well-posed.** The Clifford multiplication $v\cdot\phi = v^\flat\wedge\phi - \iota_v\phi$ is a smooth bundle map $TM \otimes E \to E$ satisfying $v\cdot(v\cdot\phi) = -|v|^2\phi$ (proved in Lemma 2, condition (2) block), so $E$ is a [[Def - Clifford Bundle and Bundle of Clifford Modules|bundle of Clifford modules]]. The induced metric on each $\Lambda^k T^*M$ is well-defined, and the [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita connection]] induces a connection on $E$.
>
> **Step 1 — $E$ is a Dirac bundle.** By Lemma 2, the induced connection $\nabla^{LC}$ is metric on $E$ (condition 1), Clifford multiplication satisfies $\langle v\cdot\phi_1, v\cdot\phi_2\rangle = |v|^2\langle\phi_1, \phi_2\rangle$ (condition 2), and $\nabla^{LC}$ is a derivation over Clifford multiplication (condition 3). Hence $E$ is a **[[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator|Dirac bundle]]**, and its Dirac operator is, in any local oriented orthonormal frame $(e_i)$,
> $$D = \sum_{i=1}^n e_i\cdot\nabla^{LC}_{e_i},$$
> a frame-independent first-order operator (well-definedness on the Dirac-operator page; independently, the two halves below are each frame-independent).
>
> **Step 2 — split $D$ into raising and lowering halves.** By the definition of Clifford multiplication, in the frame $(e_i)$ (with $e^i = e_i^\flat$),
> $$D = \sum_i e_i\cdot\nabla_{e_i} = \sum_i\big(e^i\wedge - \iota_{e_i}\big)\nabla_{e_i} = \underbrace{\sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}}_{=: \ \tilde d} \;+\; \underbrace{\Big(-\sum_i\iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}\Big)}_{=: \ \delta}.$$
> The operator $\tilde d$ raises the form degree by $1$ and $\delta$ lowers it by $1$; both are frame-independent (Lemma 3 for $\tilde d$; $\delta = D - \tilde d$ is a difference of frame-independent operators).
>
> **Step 3 — the raising half is $d$.** By Lemma 3, $\tilde d = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i} = d$, the [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold|exterior derivative]]. (This is the step that uses torsion-freeness of the Levi-Civita connection, through the symmetry of the Hessian.)
>
> **Step 4 — the lowering half is the codifferential $d^*$.** By Lemma 5, $\delta = -\sum_i\iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}$ is a formal adjoint of $d$: for all compactly supported forms, $\int_M\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int_M\langle\alpha, \delta\beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$. The vault's adjoint theorem supplies a second formal adjoint:
> > **[[Thm - Codifferential is the Adjoint of d|Theorem (codifferential is the adjoint of $d$)]].** On a closed oriented Riemannian $n$-manifold, the codifferential $d^* = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d\star : \Omega^k \to \Omega^{k-1}$ satisfies $\int_M\langle d\alpha, \beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol} = \int_M\langle\alpha, d^*\beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$ for all $\alpha \in \Omega^{k-1}$, $\beta \in \Omega^k$.
>
> That theorem's page carries a complete Formal Proof (integration by parts via Stokes on $\alpha\wedge\star\beta$, with the sign fixed by the double-star formula), so it may be invoked here. By Lemma 6 (uniqueness of formal adjoints, applied with $L = d$), two operators that are both formal adjoints of $d$ on compactly supported forms coincide; hence
> $$\delta = d^*.$$
> For a general oriented Riemannian manifold this equality of operators is local and holds pointwise: both $\delta$ and $d^* = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d\star$ are given by frame/coordinate formulas independent of compactness, and they agree on every closed manifold containing a given coordinate chart, hence they agree as differential operators. Finally, the sign exponents in the two written forms of the codifferential satisfy
> $$(-1)^{n(k-1)+1} = (-1)^{n(k+1)+1} \qquad \text{because } n(k-1) - n(k+1) = -2n \text{ is even},$$
> so Haydys's form $(-1)^{n(k-1)+1}\star d\star$ and the vault's form $(-1)^{n(k+1)+1}\star d\star$ denote the same operator $d^*$.
>
> **Step 5 — conclude.** Combining Steps 2, 3, 4,
> $$D = \tilde d + \delta = d + d^*.$$
> In particular the frame formulas $d = \sum_i e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}$ (Step 3) and $d^* = \delta = -\sum_i\iota_{e_i}\nabla_{e_i}$ (Step 4) hold in every local oriented orthonormal frame. Therefore the Dirac bundle $\Lambda T^*M$ has Dirac operator the Hodge–de Rham operator $d + d^*$. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Euclidean space and the coefficient Laplacian.** On $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the flat metric, the frame formulas give $d = \sum_i dx^i\wedge\partial_i$ and $d^* = -\sum_i\iota_{\partial_i}\partial_i$, and one can compute $(d + d^*)^2 = dd^* + d^*d = -\sum_i\partial_i^2$ acting coefficientwise on each $\Omega^k(\mathbb{R}^n)$ (worked in [[Ex - d plus d-star on Euclidean Space Squares to the Laplacian on Coefficients|the Euclidean squaring exercise]]). The application of the theorem is that the abstract statement "$D^2$ is a Laplacian" is here a bare computation with the anticommutator $[\iota_{\partial_i}, dx^j\wedge] = \delta_{ij}$; the non-obvious part is that the mixed cross-terms cancel in pairs, leaving only the diagonal $-\sum\partial_i^2$, which is exactly the flat Weitzenböck identity $\mathcal{R} = 0$.

**Kähler geometry and the Dolbeault operator.** On a Kähler manifold the complex structure and metric make $\Lambda^{0,\bullet}T^*M \otimes K$ (with $K$ a holomorphic bundle) into a Dirac bundle whose Dirac operator is $\sqrt2(\bar\partial + \bar\partial^*)$, and the Kähler identities relate its square to $\tfrac12(d + d^*)^2$. The theorem here is the real-form prototype that makes the identification "the $\bar\partial$-complex rolled up is a Dirac operator" precise: one first knows $\Lambda T^*M$ is a Dirac bundle, then refines the Clifford module along the complex structure. The application is non-obvious because it requires the metric to be Kähler for the two Laplacians to be proportional; on a general Hermitian manifold the torsion of the Chern connection breaks the clean identity, exactly as a torsionful connection breaks $d = \sum e^i\wedge\nabla_{e_i}$ in Lemma 3.

**Topology and the Gauss–Bonnet / Euler characteristic.** Grading $\Lambda T^*M = \Lambda^{\mathrm{ev}} \oplus \Lambda^{\mathrm{odd}}$, the Hodge–de Rham operator restricts to $d + d^* : \Omega^{\mathrm{ev}} \to \Omega^{\mathrm{odd}}$, an elliptic operator whose index is $\sum_k (-1)^k \dim\mathcal{H}^k = \chi(M)$, the Euler characteristic. The theorem certifies that this is a Dirac operator, so the Atiyah–Singer index theorem computes $\chi(M)$ as the integral of a curvature polynomial (the Pfaffian), recovering Gauss–Bonnet. The application is non-obvious because it turns a homotopy invariant into the index of a metric-dependent operator, the metric dependence washing out in the index.

# Bridges

- **[[Def - Hodge Laplacian|Hodge Laplacian]]** — squaring the theorem's conclusion gives $D^2 = (d + d^*)^2 = dd^* + d^*d = \Delta$, since $d^2 = 0$ and $(d^*)^2 = 0$ kill the cross-terms $d^2$ and $(d^*)^2$. So the Hodge Laplacian is the square of the Hodge–de Rham Dirac operator; this is the form-analogue of "the Dirac operator is a square root of the Laplacian", and it is the identity that makes $D$ the natural first-order operator whose kernel (harmonic forms) computes de Rham cohomology on a closed manifold.

- **[[Thm - Weitzenbock Formula for the Dirac Operator|Weitzenböck formula]]** — the general Dirac-bundle identity $D^2 = \nabla^*\nabla + \mathcal{R}$ specialises, via $D^2 = \Delta$, to $\Delta = \nabla^*\nabla + \mathcal{R}$ on forms. On $1$-forms the curvature endomorphism $\mathcal{R}$ is the Ricci tensor, giving the Bochner formula $\Delta = \nabla^*\nabla + \operatorname{Ric}$; the construction is carried out by computing [[Def - Curvature Endomorphism of a Dirac Bundle|the curvature endomorphism]] of $\Lambda T^*M$ against the [[Def - Riemann Curvature Tensor|Riemann tensor]] and tracing, and it yields the vanishing of harmonic $1$-forms in positive Ricci curvature.

- **[[Thm - Dirac Operators are Formally Self-Adjoint|Formal self-adjointness]]** — the general theorem that every Dirac operator is formally self-adjoint specialises here to the mutual $L^2$-adjointness of $d$ and $d^*$: $\langle(d + d^*)s_1, s_2\rangle_{L^2} = \langle s_1, (d + d^*)s_2\rangle_{L^2}$ unwinds to $\langle d s_1, s_2\rangle = \langle s_1, d^* s_2\rangle$ and its transpose. The bridge is that the abstract self-adjointness proof (skew-adjoint Clifford multiplication plus a metric connection, difference a divergence) is, on $\Lambda T^*M$, exactly the integration-by-parts proof of Lemma 5.

- **Ellipticity and the Hodge theorem** — because Clifford multiplication by a non-zero covector is invertible (its square is $-|v|^2$), the principal symbol of $D = d + d^*$ is invertible off the zero section, so $D$ is elliptic; on a closed manifold elliptic self-adjoint operators have finite-dimensional kernel and closed range, giving the $L^2$-orthogonal decomposition $\Omega^\bullet = \ker D \oplus \operatorname{im} d \oplus \operatorname{im} d^*$. Since $\ker D = \ker\Delta$ is the harmonic forms, this is the Hodge decomposition; the theorem is what places de Rham–Hodge theory inside the elliptic theory of Dirac operators.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The Signature Operator *(from Index Theory)*
> Twisting the grading of $\Lambda T^*M$ by the involution $\tau = i^{k(k-1)+2\ell}\star$ on a closed oriented $4\ell$-manifold turns $d + d^*$ into the **signature operator**, whose index is the signature $\sigma(M)$. Because this theorem identifies $d + d^*$ as a Dirac operator, the Atiyah–Singer index theorem computes $\sigma(M)$ as the integral of the Hirzebruch $L$-polynomial, giving the **Hirzebruch signature theorem**. In dimension four this is $\sigma(M) = \tfrac13\int_M p_1$, the input to the intersection-form constraints of chapter XIII.

> [!tip] Twisted Dirac Operators on Forms *(from Gauge Theory)*
> Once $\Lambda T^*M$ is a Dirac bundle, tensoring with a Hermitian bundle $F$ carrying a unitary connection $\nabla^F$ gives ([[Ex - The Twisted Bundle of a Dirac Bundle is a Dirac Bundle|the twisting construction]]) a Dirac bundle $\Lambda T^*M \otimes F$ whose Dirac operator is the coupled $d_F + d_F^*$. This is the operator whose deformation complex controls the moduli space of anti-self-dual connections in Donaldson theory, and its ellipticity and index are again Dirac-operator index theory.
