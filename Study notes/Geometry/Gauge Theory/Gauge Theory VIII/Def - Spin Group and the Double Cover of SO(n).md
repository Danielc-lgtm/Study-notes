# Notation

Throughout, $n$ is a positive integer, $\mathbb{R}^n$ carries its standard Euclidean inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ with $|u|^2 = \langle u,u\rangle$, and $e_1,\dots,e_n$ is its standard orthonormal basis. We write $S^{n-1} = \{v\in\mathbb{R}^n : |v| = 1\}$ for the unit sphere. The **Clifford algebra** of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, the associative real algebra with unit $1$ generated by the vectors of $\mathbb{R}^n$ subject to the Clifford relation

$$u\cdot u = -|u|^2\, 1 \qquad (u\in\mathbb{R}^n),$$

whose polarised form (obtained by expanding $(u+w)\cdot(u+w) = -|u+w|^2$ and subtracting $u\cdot u$ and $w\cdot w$) is

$$u\cdot w + w\cdot u = -2\langle u,w\rangle\, 1 \qquad (u,w\in\mathbb{R}^n).$$

On the standard basis this reads $e_i\cdot e_i = -1$ and $e_i\cdot e_j = -e_j\cdot e_i$ for $i\ne j$. All of this, together with the grading, is set up on **[[Def - Clifford Algebra and Clifford Multiplication]]**, whose notation and conventions this page adopts wholesale; we recall only what is used here.

The algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is $\mathbb{Z}/2$-graded, $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = \mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)\oplus\mathrm{Cl}^1(\mathbb{R}^n)$, where $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ is spanned by products of an even number of vectors and $\mathrm{Cl}^1$ by products of an odd number. The **grading automorphism** (also called the parity or main automorphism) is the unique algebra automorphism

$$\alpha : \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)\to\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n), \qquad \alpha(u) = -u \ \ (u\in\mathbb{R}^n),$$

which exists because $u\mapsto -u$ preserves the Clifford relation ($(-u)\cdot(-u) = u\cdot u = -|u|^2$) and so extends by the universal property (**[[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property]]**); on homogeneous elements $\alpha(x) = (-1)^{\deg x}x$, so $\alpha = +\mathrm{id}$ on $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ and $\alpha = -\mathrm{id}$ on $\mathrm{Cl}^1$. We write $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$ for the group of invertible elements and $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times = \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times\cap\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for the even invertibles.

We use $SO(n) = \{T\in GL(\mathbb{R}^n) : T^{\mathsf T}T = I,\ \det T = 1\}$ for the rotation group and $\mathfrak{so}(n) = \{A\in\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{R}^n) : A^{\mathsf T} = -A\}$ for its Lie algebra of skew-symmetric matrices, with the commutator bracket $[A,B] = AB - BA$. The matrix units $E_{ij}\in\mathfrak{gl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ have a $1$ in row $i$, column $j$, and zeros elsewhere.

> [!warning] Convention: the sign in the Clifford relation
> This series (following Haydys) uses $u\cdot u = -|u|^2$. The vault's **[[Def - Clifford Algebra]]** and **[[Def - Pin and Spin Groups]]** are written for a general quadratic space $(V,Q)$ with $v\cdot v = Q(v)$, so that the series' algebra is the vault's $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n, -|\cdot|^2)$. The dictionary is a single sign: a unit vector of this page satisfies $v\cdot v = -1$, whereas a unit vector for the vault's Euclidean $\mathrm{Spin}(n) = \mathrm{Spin}(\mathbb{R}^n, |\cdot|^2)$ satisfies $v\cdot v = +1$. The twisted adjoint action $a,u\mapsto\alpha(a)ua^{-1}$, the identification of a single unit vector's action with a reflection, and the resulting groups $\mathrm{Pin}(n)$ and $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ are the same in either sign; only the intermediate scalar $v\cdot v = \mp 1$ changes, and every formula below carries its own sign so the reader never has to translate. We therefore use "the vault's $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$" and this page's $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ interchangeably as abstract Lie groups.

# Axiom Motivation

The rotation group $SO(n)$ is the symmetry group of Euclidean geometry, and one might think it the last word on rotations. It is not, and the reason is topological. For $n\ge 3$ the group $SO(n)$ is connected but *not* simply connected: a loop of rotations can wind in a way that cannot be contracted to a point. The precise statement, established in this series in chapter XII and used here only as orienting context, is that **$\pi_1(SO(n))\cong\mathbb{Z}/2$ for every $n\ge 3$** — there is exactly one nontrivial class of loops, and traversing such a loop twice gives a contractible loop. The single most familiar witness is the "plate trick" or "belt trick": a rotation by $2\pi$ about a fixed axis is a nontrivial loop, while a rotation by $4\pi$ can be undone continuously.

This has a decisive consequence for physics and for geometry. Quantum mechanics represents symmetries only up to phase, so it sees not $SO(3)$ but its double cover; a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle returns to its original state only after a $4\pi$ rotation, precisely because the state lives on the cover and a $2\pi$ rotation is the nontrivial loop. Differential geometry meets the same object whenever it tries to define spinor fields on a manifold: the transition functions of the tangent bundle take values in $SO(n)$, and to build a bundle of spinors one must lift those transition functions to the double cover. There is thus a genuine, unavoidable group sitting one level above $SO(n)$, and we need to name it and get our hands on it.

Because $SO(n)$ is connected and has fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n\ge 3$, covering-space theory (chapter XII) guarantees a **connected double cover**, unique up to isomorphism, which is moreover simply connected and is a Lie group with a smooth two-to-one covering homomorphism onto $SO(n)$. **This abstract characterisation is Haydys's definition of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$:** the simply connected Lie group with a double-covering homomorphism $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)$ (Haydys, unlabelled definition, p. 34; the topological input $\pi_1(SO(n))\cong\mathbb{Z}/2$ is Haydys's I4.1.1). It is a clean characterisation, but it is useless for computation: it tells us the group exists and is unique, not what its elements *are*, how they multiply, or how they act on $\mathbb{R}^n$. We cannot differentiate a group we cannot write down, and we will need to differentiate this one to build the Dirac operator.

The desideratum, then, is a **concrete model**: an explicit set of elements, sitting inside a concrete algebra, closed under an explicit multiplication, together with an explicit formula for the map to $SO(n)$. The Clifford algebra $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ supplies exactly this. Its unit vectors are honest algebra elements; products of unit vectors are again algebra elements; and there is a natural way to make an algebra element act on $\mathbb{R}^n\subset\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, namely by conjugation. The whole construction below is the discovery that

- conjugation (suitably twisted) by a *unit vector* is a *reflection* of $\mathbb{R}^n$, so
- conjugation by a product of *evenly many* unit vectors is a *rotation* (a composite of an even number of reflections), and
- the only such products that act trivially are the two scalars $\pm 1$.

That is precisely a two-to-one map onto $SO(n)$ from a group of even Clifford elements, and it will turn out to reproduce the abstract double cover. The virtue of insisting on this concrete model — rather than resting on the abstract characterisation — is that everything downstream (the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{spin}(n)$, the spinor representation, the Dirac operator) is then a matter of explicit algebra, and no fundamental-group computation is ever invoked inside the chapter.

Why unit vectors, and why an *even* number of them? A single unit vector $v$ conjugates $\mathbb{R}^n$ by a reflection, and a reflection has determinant $-1$: it lands in $O(n)$ but not $SO(n)$. Dropping the parity constraint would give the larger group $\mathrm{Pin}(n)$ covering all of $O(n)$, which is the right object for orientation-reversing symmetries but is disconnected and is not what a spinor bundle over an *oriented* manifold requires. Keeping only even products selects the orientation-preserving part and, as we will see, exactly the connected piece. Dropping the *unit-norm* constraint would spoil the map to the orthogonal group: a general nonzero vector $v$ conjugates by a reflection rescaled by $|v|^{-2}$ or $|v|^2$, which is not orthogonal unless $|v| = 1$; the norm-one condition is what makes conjugation land in $O(n)$ at all. Finally, why *conjugation* and not some other action? Because conjugation by an invertible element is automatically an algebra automorphism, hence in particular linear and multiplicative, so it has a chance of preserving the quadratic form $u\mapsto u\cdot u = -|u|^2$; and preserving that form is exactly being orthogonal. The definition below is the minimal package that makes all three observations precise.

# The Definition

Fix $n\ge 1$ and work inside $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ with the conventions of the Notation section.

**The spin group.** The **spin group** $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is the subset of $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$ consisting of products of an even number of unit vectors:

$$\mathrm{Spin}(n) := \{v_1\cdot v_2\cdots v_{2k} : k\ge 0,\ v_i\in S^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^n\}\ \subset\ \mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times.$$

Here $k = 0$ gives the empty product $1$. This is the definition adopted for the series (Haydys R4.1.1 and I4.1.2, p. 35: "the subgroup of $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ generated by elements $v_1\cdots v_{2k}$ of an even number of unit vectors is isomorphic to $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$"). It is the even-parity part of the pin group of **[[Def - Pin and Spin Groups]]**, transported to the sign convention $v\cdot v = -1$.

We must first check that this set is a group. A unit vector $v$ satisfies $v\cdot v = -1$, hence is invertible with $v^{-1} = -v$. Therefore an even product $a = v_1\cdots v_{2k}$ is invertible, with

$$a^{-1} = v_{2k}^{-1}\cdots v_1^{-1} = (-v_{2k})\cdots(-v_1) = (-1)^{2k}\, v_{2k}\cdots v_1 = v_{2k}\cdots v_1,$$

which is again a product of $2k$ unit vectors, hence again in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$. The product of two even products of unit vectors is an even product of unit vectors, so the set is closed under multiplication; it contains the identity $1$ (empty product); and we have just shown it is closed under inverses. Thus $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a subgroup of $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$. That it is in addition a *Lie* subgroup — compact, and for $n\ge 2$ connected — is proved on **[[Thm - Spin(n) inside the Clifford Algebra is a Double Cover of SO(n)]]** using the [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem|closed subgroup theorem]] and the Cartan–Dieudonné bound on the number of factors; we take that structure for granted only where the present page says so.

**The (untwisted) adjoint action.** For $a\in\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$ define the conjugation map

$$\xi(a) : \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)\to\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n), \qquad \xi(a)x := a\cdot x\cdot a^{-1}.$$

Each $\xi(a)$ is an algebra automorphism, and $\xi(ab) = \xi(a)\xi(b)$, so $\xi$ is a homomorphism from $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$ into the automorphism group of the algebra. When $\xi(a)$ preserves the subspace $\mathbb{R}^n\subset\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ we regard it as an element of $GL(\mathbb{R}^n)$; the point of the construction is that for $a\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ it does, and lands in $SO(n)$.

**The twisted adjoint action.** For $a\in\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$ define the **twisted adjoint action** (using the grading automorphism $\alpha$)

$$\tilde\xi(a) : \mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)\to\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n), \qquad \tilde\xi(a)x := \alpha(a)\cdot x\cdot a^{-1}.$$

Since $\alpha$ is an algebra homomorphism, $\tilde\xi(ab)x = \alpha(ab)\,x\,(ab)^{-1} = \alpha(a)\big(\alpha(b)\,x\,b^{-1}\big)a^{-1} = \tilde\xi(a)\big(\tilde\xi(b)x\big)$, so $\tilde\xi$ is also a homomorphism. The two actions are defined so that $\tilde\xi$ is the natural one on odd elements (unit vectors) while $\xi$ is the natural one on even elements; the following proposition makes both statements precise and shows they agree on $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$.

> [!note] Proposition (reflection formula, and $\tilde\xi = \xi$ on the even part)
> Let $v\in S^{n-1}$ be a unit vector and $u\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Then
> $$\tilde\xi(v)u = -v\cdot u\cdot v^{-1} = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle\, v,$$
> which is the reflection of $u$ across the hyperplane $v^\perp = \{w : \langle w,v\rangle = 0\}$. Consequently $\tilde\xi(v)$ preserves $\mathbb{R}^n$ and lies in $O(n)$ with $\det\tilde\xi(v) = -1$. Moreover, for every even element $a\in\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$ — in particular for every $a\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ — one has $\tilde\xi(a) = \xi(a)$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **What is to be shown.** Three claims: (i) the displayed identity $\tilde\xi(v)u = -v\cdot u\cdot v^{-1} = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v$; (ii) that this is the reflection across $v^\perp$, and hence orthogonal with determinant $-1$; (iii) that $\tilde\xi = \xi$ on $\mathrm{Cl}^0$.
> >
> > **Step 1 — rewrite the twisted conjugation of a vector.** Since $v\in\mathbb{R}^n\subset\mathrm{Cl}^1$ is odd, $\alpha(v) = -v$. A unit vector satisfies $v\cdot v = -1$, so $v^{-1} = -v$. Substituting both facts into the definition of $\tilde\xi$,
> > $$\tilde\xi(v)u = \alpha(v)\cdot u\cdot v^{-1} = (-v)\cdot u\cdot(-v) = v\cdot u\cdot v \qquad \text{(} \alpha(v) = -v,\ v^{-1} = -v \text{)}.$$
> > Equivalently, writing the first two signs the other way, $\tilde\xi(v)u = -v\cdot u\cdot v^{-1}$ (using $v^{-1} = -v$ once more), which is the first displayed equality.
> >
> > **Step 2 — apply the polarised Clifford relation.** By the polarised relation $v\cdot u + u\cdot v = -2\langle u,v\rangle 1$ (Notation section) we have $v\cdot u = -u\cdot v - 2\langle u,v\rangle$. Multiplying on the right by $v$ and using $v\cdot v = -1$,
> > $$v\cdot u\cdot v = \big(-u\cdot v - 2\langle u,v\rangle\big)\cdot v = -u\cdot(v\cdot v) - 2\langle u,v\rangle\, v = -u\cdot(-1) - 2\langle u,v\rangle\, v = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle\, v \qquad \text{(polarised relation; } v\cdot v = -1 \text{).}$$
> > Combining with Step 1 gives $\tilde\xi(v)u = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v$, the second displayed equality. In particular $\tilde\xi(v)u\in\mathbb{R}^n$, so $\tilde\xi(v)$ preserves $\mathbb{R}^n$.
> >
> > **Step 3 — identify the map as a reflection.** The linear map $R_v(u) := u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v$ fixes every $u\in v^\perp$ (there $\langle u,v\rangle = 0$, so $R_v(u) = u$) and sends $v$ to $v - 2\langle v,v\rangle v = v - 2v = -v$ (since $|v| = 1$). A linear map fixing the hyperplane $v^\perp$ pointwise and negating the normal direction $v$ is by definition the reflection across $v^\perp$. Such a reflection is orthogonal: for any $u,w$,
> > $$\langle R_v u, R_v w\rangle = \langle u,w\rangle - 2\langle u,v\rangle\langle v,w\rangle - 2\langle w,v\rangle\langle u,v\rangle + 4\langle u,v\rangle\langle w,v\rangle\langle v,v\rangle = \langle u,w\rangle \qquad \text{(expand; } |v|^2 = 1 \text{; the three correction terms cancel).}$$
> > In the orthonormal basis $(v, \text{basis of } v^\perp)$ the matrix of $R_v$ is $\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,\dots,1)$, so $\det R_v = -1$. Hence $\tilde\xi(v) = R_v\in O(n)\setminus SO(n)$.
> >
> > **Step 4 — agreement on the even part.** Let $a\in\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$. Because $\alpha$ acts as the identity on $\mathrm{Cl}^0$ (Notation section), $\alpha(a) = a$, and therefore for every $x$,
> > $$\tilde\xi(a)x = \alpha(a)\cdot x\cdot a^{-1} = a\cdot x\cdot a^{-1} = \xi(a)x \qquad \text{(} \alpha|_{\mathrm{Cl}^0} = \mathrm{id} \text{)}.$$
> > Since $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\subset\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)^\times$, in particular $\tilde\xi = \xi$ on $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** For a unit vector, twisted conjugation is exactly the reflection across the orthogonal hyperplane, an orthogonal map of determinant $-1$; and on the even part of the Clifford algebra the twist is invisible, so $\xi$ and $\tilde\xi$ coincide there. $\blacksquare$

**The map $\xi : \mathrm{Spin}(n)\to GL(\mathbb{R}^n)$.** For $a = v_1\cdots v_{2k}\in\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ we have, by the Proposition and the homomorphism property of $\tilde\xi$,

$$\xi(a) = \tilde\xi(a) = \tilde\xi(v_1)\circ\cdots\circ\tilde\xi(v_{2k}) = R_{v_1}\circ\cdots\circ R_{v_{2k}},$$

a composite of $2k$ reflections. Each $R_{v_i}$ preserves $\mathbb{R}^n$ and lies in $O(n)$, so $\xi(a)$ preserves $\mathbb{R}^n$ and lies in $O(n)$; and $\det\xi(a) = (-1)^{2k} = +1$, so in fact $\xi(a)\in SO(n)$. We therefore regard $\xi$ as a group homomorphism

$$\xi : \mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n), \qquad \xi(a)u = a\cdot u\cdot a^{-1}.$$

That $\xi$ is *surjective*, that its *kernel is exactly* $\{\pm 1\}$ (which is central in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$), and that it is a *two-sheeted covering map*, are the substantive facts; they are stated and proved in full on **[[Thm - Spin(n) inside the Clifford Algebra is a Double Cover of SO(n)]]**. On the present page $\xi$ is *defined* and shown to *land in* $SO(n)$; the double-cover theorem then earns it the name "the double cover".

**The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{spin}(n)$.** Define the linear subspace of $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$

$$\mathfrak{spin}(n) := \operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{e_i\cdot e_j : 1\le i < j\le n\}\ \subset\ \mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n),$$

equipped with the commutator bracket $[x,y] = x\cdot y - y\cdot x$ inherited from the algebra. It has dimension $\binom{n}{2}$, which equals $\dim\mathfrak{so}(n)$. This is, concretely, the Lie algebra of the Lie group $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ in the sense of **[[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group]]**: the tangent space at the identity, with the bracket of the ambient associative algebra restricting to the Lie bracket (as it always does for a matrix or subalgebra Lie group). The identification $\mathfrak{spin}(n) = T_1\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, and the fact that $\{e_i\cdot e_j\}_{i<j}$ is a basis, are proved on the double-cover page; here $\mathfrak{spin}(n)$ is *defined* by the explicit span, and the bracket is the concrete commutator.

**The isomorphism $\mathfrak{so}(n)\to\mathfrak{spin}(n)$.** The differential $d\xi : \mathfrak{spin}(n)\to\mathfrak{so}(n)$ of the covering homomorphism is a Lie algebra isomorphism (both spaces have dimension $\binom{n}{2}$, and $\xi$ is a local diffeomorphism). Its inverse is the explicit map

$$\lambda : \mathfrak{so}(n)\to\mathfrak{spin}(n), \qquad \lambda(A) = \tfrac14\sum_{i,j=1}^n \langle A e_i, e_j\rangle\, e_i\cdot e_j.$$

We record the formula here because it is what downstream constructions (the spin connection, the Dirac operator) actually use to turn a skew-symmetric curvature or connection matrix into a Clifford element. That $\lambda$ is a well-defined linear isomorphism, that it is a Lie algebra homomorphism, and that $d\xi\circ\lambda = \mathrm{id}_{\mathfrak{so}(n)}$, are all proved on **[[Thm - Spin(n) inside the Clifford Algebra is a Double Cover of SO(n)]]**; this page states the map and, in the Examples, verifies it in the smallest case $n = 2$.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

There are two structural ways to see $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, and they answer two different questions.

The first is the **universal-cover / lift** viewpoint, which is the one the Axiom Motivation started from. For $n\ge 3$ the group $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is the universal cover of $SO(n)$: among all connected Lie groups $\widetilde{G}$ admitting a covering homomorphism $\widetilde{G}\to SO(n)$, it is the initial one, meaning every connected covering of $SO(n)$ factors uniquely through $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)$. Equivalently, $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is characterised up to unique isomorphism (over $SO(n)$) by being connected, simply connected, and equipped with a nontrivial homomorphism to $SO(n)$ that is a local diffeomorphism. This universal property is what makes the construction canonical: any two people who build "the double cover of $SO(n)$" build isomorphic groups, and the isomorphism respecting the maps to $SO(n)$ is unique. The concrete Clifford model of this page is one construction of this universal object, and **[[Thm - Spin(n) inside the Clifford Algebra is a Double Cover of SO(n)]]** verifies that it has the defining properties.

The second is the **twisted-torsor / extension** viewpoint, which is the one that generalises. The homomorphism $\xi$ fits into a short exact sequence of groups

$$1\longrightarrow \{\pm 1\}\longrightarrow \mathrm{Spin}(n)\xrightarrow{\ \xi\ } SO(n)\longrightarrow 1,$$

with $\{\pm 1\}$ central. This exhibits $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ as a central extension of $SO(n)$ by $\mathbb{Z}/2$; the extension is nontrivial (it is not the product $SO(n)\times\{\pm1\}$) precisely because $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is connected while a product would be disconnected. Central $\mathbb{Z}/2$-extensions of a group $G$ are classified by the cohomology group $H^2(G;\mathbb{Z}/2)$, and $\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)$ is the nonzero class. From this angle a *spin structure* on a manifold, defined in §8.3, is exactly a lift of the structure group of the frame bundle along this extension — a torsor for the extension over each fibre — which is why $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ and not $SO(n)$ is the group a spinor bundle is associated to. The exact sequence displayed here is the local, linear-algebra shadow of that global construction; its proof (surjectivity, kernel, centrality) is again on the double-cover page.

Both viewpoints are self-contained given the double-cover theorem: the first says *what* $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is up to isomorphism, the second says *how* it sits over $SO(n)$ and how it is used.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** Officially $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is "the set of even products of unit vectors in the Clifford algebra". Operationally it is something sharper: *$\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is the group of Clifford elements that conjugate $\mathbb{R}^n$ into itself by rotations, one level of double cover above $SO(n)$.* The defining formula is a recipe for building elements (multiply unit vectors in pairs); the operational characterisation is what those elements *do* (act on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by rotations through $\xi$, with a two-to-one ambiguity of sign). Almost every use of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ downstream is through the operational form: one has a rotation, one wants a spinor to transform, and one lifts the rotation to $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ up to the sign $\pm 1$ — and that unresolved sign is the entire physical and geometric content of "spin".

The construction is an instance of a pattern that recurs across mathematics: **realising a symmetry group inside an algebra so that conjugation reproduces the symmetry.** In linear representation theory a group acts on a vector space and one studies the action; here the logic runs backwards — one starts with the space $\mathbb{R}^n$ and its symmetry $SO(n)$, builds the Clifford algebra as an enlargement of the space, and *recovers* the symmetry as inner automorphisms of the enlargement. The same move underlies the realisation of $SU(2)$ acting on $\mathbb{C}^2$ as the unit quaternions conjugating $\mathrm{Im}\,\mathbb{H}\cong\mathbb{R}^3$ (the $n = 3$ case here, worked out on **[[Thm - Spin Groups in Dimensions Three and Four via Quaternions]]**), and, one field over, the realisation of the Lorentz group's double cover $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ acting on Minkowski space by conjugation on Hermitian $2\times 2$ matrices. In each case the double cover is not an accident bolted on afterwards; it is forced by the fact that conjugation by $a$ and by $-a$ give the same automorphism, so the algebra sees the symmetry group only up to a sign it cannot resolve.

A second compression: the *reflection formula* $\tilde\xi(v)u = u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v$ is the Householder reflection of numerical linear algebra written in Clifford language. Cartan–Dieudonné's theorem — every element of $O(n)$ is a product of at most $n$ reflections — is then the statement that every orthogonal map is $\tilde\xi$ of a product of at most $n$ unit vectors, which is exactly why $\mathrm{Pin}(n)$ (all products) covers $O(n)$ and $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ (even products) covers $SO(n)$. The Clifford algebra turns the geometric fact "rotations are even products of reflections" into the algebraic fact "$\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is the even part of the group generated by unit vectors", and the passage between them is a single sign twist.

# Examples / Corollaries

**The case $n = 2$: $\mathrm{Spin}(2)\cong U(1)$ and $\xi$ is $z\mapsto z^2$ — computed in full.** Write $\omega := e_1\cdot e_2\in\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^2)$. First, $\omega$ squares to $-1$: using $e_1\cdot e_2 = -e_2\cdot e_1$ and $e_i\cdot e_i = -1$,

$$\omega^2 = e_1 e_2 e_1 e_2 = -e_1 e_1 e_2 e_2 = -(-1)(-1) = -1 \qquad \text{(anticommutation, then } e_i\cdot e_i = -1\text{)}.$$

(Here and below we drop the dot for products of basis vectors.) Because $\{1,\omega\}$ spans $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^2)$ and $\omega^2 = -1$, the even subalgebra is $\mathbb{R}[\omega]\cong\mathbb{C}$, with $\omega$ playing the role of $i$. Now I claim

$$\mathrm{Spin}(2) = \{\cos t + \sin t\,\omega : t\in\mathbb{R}\},$$

which under $\cos t + \sin t\,\omega\mapsto e^{it}$ is exactly the circle group $U(1)$. To see the right-hand set is contained in $\mathrm{Spin}(2)$, take the two unit vectors $v_1 = e_1$ and $v_2 = \cos t\,e_1 + \sin t\,e_2$ and compute their product:

$$e_1\cdot(\cos t\,e_1 + \sin t\,e_2) = \cos t\,(e_1 e_1) + \sin t\,(e_1 e_2) = -\cos t + \sin t\,\omega \qquad \text{(} e_1 e_1 = -1 \text{)}.$$

As $t$ ranges over $\mathbb{R}$ the pair $(-\cos t,\sin t)$ traces out the entire unit circle in the $(1,\omega)$-plane, so every element $\cos s + \sin s\,\omega$ arises as such a product $e_1\cdot v_2$ of two unit vectors and hence lies in $\mathrm{Spin}(2)$. Conversely every product of $2k$ unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ lies in the unit circle of $\mathbb{R}[\omega]\cong\mathbb{C}$ (a product of $2k$ unit-modulus complex numbers has modulus one), i.e. has the form $\cos s + \sin s\,\omega$. Thus the two sets are equal and $\mathrm{Spin}(2)\cong U(1)$.

Now compute $\xi$ on $a = \cos t + \sin t\,\omega$. Its inverse is $a^{-1} = \cos t - \sin t\,\omega$ (check: $a\cdot a^{-1} = \cos^2 t - \sin^2 t\,\omega^2 = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$, using $\omega^2 = -1$). First,

$$a\cdot e_1 = \cos t\, e_1 + \sin t\,(e_1 e_2 e_1) = \cos t\, e_1 + \sin t\,e_2 \qquad \text{(} e_1 e_2 e_1 = -e_1 e_1 e_2 = e_2\text{)},$$

and then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xi(a)e_1 = (a e_1)a^{-1} &= (\cos t\, e_1 + \sin t\, e_2)(\cos t - \sin t\,\omega)\\
&= \cos^2 t\, e_1 - \cos t\sin t\,(e_1 e_1 e_2) + \sin t\cos t\, e_2 - \sin^2 t\,(e_2 e_1 e_2)\\
&= \cos^2 t\, e_1 + \cos t\sin t\, e_2 + \sin t\cos t\, e_2 - \sin^2 t\, e_1 \qquad \text{(} e_1 e_1 e_2 = -e_2,\ e_2 e_1 e_2 = e_1\text{)}\\
&= (\cos^2 t - \sin^2 t)\,e_1 + 2\sin t\cos t\, e_2 = \cos 2t\, e_1 + \sin 2t\, e_2.
\end{aligned}
$$

An identical computation, or the fact that $\xi(a)\in SO(2)$ once one coordinate is known, gives $\xi(a)e_2 = -\sin 2t\,e_1 + \cos 2t\,e_2$. Hence $\xi(a)$ is the rotation of $\mathbb{R}^2$ through angle $2t$. Under the isomorphisms $\mathrm{Spin}(2)\cong U(1)$, $a\mapsto e^{it}$, and $SO(2)\cong U(1)$, $R_\theta\mapsto e^{i\theta}$, the homomorphism $\xi$ becomes

$$U(1)\to U(1), \qquad z = e^{it}\longmapsto e^{2it} = z^2,$$

the squaring map, which is the standard nontrivial double cover of the circle by itself. Its kernel is $\{z : z^2 = 1\} = \{\pm 1\}$, matching the general kernel $\{\pm 1\}$; in Clifford terms these are $a = 1$ (at $t = 0$) and $a = \cos\pi + \sin\pi\,\omega = -1$ (at $t = \pi$).

**Corollary — the path from $1$ to $-1$ that makes the cover nontrivial.** The curve $\gamma(t) = \cos t + \sin t\,\omega$, $t\in[0,\pi]$, lies in $\mathrm{Spin}(2)$ and joins $\gamma(0) = 1$ to $\gamma(\pi) = -1$. Because both endpoints lie in the kernel of $\xi$, the loop $\xi\circ\gamma$ in $SO(2)$ is a full $2\pi$ rotation (as $t : 0\to\pi$, the angle $2t : 0\to 2\pi$), yet its lift $\gamma$ does not close up — it ends at $-1\ne 1$. This is the concrete manifestation of the double cover: the noncontractible loop downstairs lifts to a *path* upstairs, and only running around it twice ($t : 0\to 2\pi$, angle $0\to 4\pi$) returns to the identity. The same curve $\cos t + \sin t\,e_1 e_2$ lies in $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ for every $n\ge 2$ and joins $1$ to $-1$ there too, which is how connectedness (and nontriviality of the cover) is established for all $n\ge 2$ on the double-cover page.

**The degenerate case $n = 1$: $\mathrm{Spin}(1) = \{\pm 1\}$, disconnected, $\xi$ trivial.** In $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^1)\cong\mathbb{C}$ the only unit vectors are $\pm e_1$, with $e_1\cdot e_1 = -1$. A product of $2k$ of them is $(\pm 1)^{\,\text{signs}}\,e_1^{2k} = \pm(-1)^k\in\{\pm 1\}$, so

$$\mathrm{Spin}(1) = \{\pm 1\}\cong\mathbb{Z}/2,$$

a two-element discrete group. It is *not connected*. The map $\xi$ acts on the one-dimensional space $\mathbb{R} e_1$ by $\xi(\pm 1)u = (\pm 1)u(\pm 1)^{-1} = u$, i.e. $\xi$ is trivial, consistent with $SO(1) = \{1\}$ being the trivial group; the kernel is all of $\mathrm{Spin}(1)$, not merely $\{\pm 1\}$ as a proper subgroup, because upstairs and downstairs have collapsed. This is exactly why the hypothesis $n\ge 2$ appears everywhere connectedness is claimed: in dimension one there are simply not enough directions to rotate, the "path from $1$ to $-1$" of the previous corollary does not exist, and $\mathrm{Spin}(1)$ falls apart into two points. For $n\ge 2$ the plane spanned by $e_1, e_2$ already supplies the connecting curve, and $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is connected.

**The case $n = 3$ (stated; computed on the next pages).** Here $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^3)\cong\mathbb{H}$, the quaternions, under $e_2 e_3\mapsto i$, $e_3 e_1\mapsto j$, $e_1 e_2\mapsto k$, and this isomorphism carries $\mathrm{Spin}(3)$ onto the group $Sp(1)$ of unit quaternions, with $\xi$ becoming the conjugation action $q,h\mapsto q h\bar q$ on $\mathrm{Im}\,\mathbb{H}\cong\mathbb{R}^3$. Thus $\mathrm{Spin}(3)\cong Sp(1)\cong SU(2)$, the double cover of $SO(3)$. The full computation is on **[[Thm - Spin Groups in Dimensions Three and Four via Quaternions]]**, which uses the quaternionic model of the Clifford algebra from **[[Thm - Low-Dimensional Clifford Algebras and the Quaternionic Spinor Modules]]**; the resulting map is the same as the matrix double cover proved on **[[Thm - SU(2) is the Double Cover of SO(3)]]**. We flag it here only to show the definition reproduces the familiar object in the dimension the reader already knows.

**Is NOT an instance — a general (non-unit) vector, or an odd product.** The recipe "products of unit vectors" cannot be relaxed. If $v$ has $|v|\ne 1$ then $v\cdot v = -|v|^2$ and $v^{-1} = -v/|v|^2$, so $\tilde\xi(v)u = \alpha(v)uv^{-1} = (-v)u(-v/|v|^2) = (v u v)/|v|^2 = (u - 2\langle u,v\rangle v/|v|^2)$ — this is still the reflection across $v^\perp$, but note that $\xi(v) = v u v^{-1}$ (untwisted) then differs from $-R_v$ by nothing new; the genuine failure is that a *scalar multiple* $c\cdot v$ of a unit vector gives $\xi(cv) = \xi(v)$, so allowing non-unit vectors adds no new orthogonal maps but destroys the clean statement "kernel $= \{\pm 1\}$", replacing it by all nonzero scalars $\mathbb{R}^\times$. Likewise an *odd* product $v_1\cdots v_{2k+1}$ gives $\xi = R_{v_1}\cdots R_{v_{2k+1}}$, a product of an odd number of reflections, which has determinant $-1$ and so lies in $O(n)\setminus SO(n)$: it is an element of $\mathrm{Pin}(n)$ but not of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, and it does not act on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by a rotation. Both non-instances confirm the two constraints in the definition — unit norm (to pin the kernel down to $\pm 1$) and even parity (to land in $SO(n)$).

**Calibration check.** Three quick verifications the reader can carry out from this page alone. (1) In $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)$, check directly from $\tilde\xi(e_1)u = u - 2\langle u,e_1\rangle e_1$ that $\tilde\xi(e_1)e_1 = -e_1$ and $\tilde\xi(e_1)e_2 = e_2$, confirming that $\tilde\xi(e_1)$ is the reflection across the hyperplane $e_1^\perp = \mathbb{R}e_2$ — it negates the $e_1$-component and fixes the $e_2$-component. (2) Verify $\dim\mathfrak{spin}(n) = \binom{n}{2} = \dim\mathfrak{so}(n)$ by counting basis elements $e_i e_j$ with $i<j$. (3) Check the inverse-differential formula in the smallest case: for $n = 2$, $\mathfrak{so}(2)$ is spanned by $A$ with $Ae_1 = e_2$, $Ae_2 = -e_1$, so $\langle Ae_1, e_2\rangle = 1$, $\langle Ae_2, e_1\rangle = -1$, and all other entries vanish; then

$$\lambda(A) = \tfrac14\big(\langle Ae_1,e_2\rangle\, e_1 e_2 + \langle Ae_2,e_1\rangle\, e_2 e_1\big) = \tfrac14\big(e_1 e_2 - e_2 e_1\big) = \tfrac14\big(e_1 e_2 + e_1 e_2\big) = \tfrac12\, e_1 e_2 = \tfrac12\,\omega.$$

Compare this with the $n = 2$ computation above: differentiating $\xi(\cos t + \sin t\,\omega) = R_{2t}$ at $t = 0$ gives $d\xi(\omega) = 2A$ (the angle doubles), so $d\xi(\tfrac12\omega) = A$, i.e. $\lambda(A) = \tfrac12\omega$ is indeed the inverse of $d\xi$ on this generator — the factor $\tfrac14$ in $\lambda$ is exactly what compensates the antisymmetric double-count in the sum and the factor $2$ in the double cover. If all three checks come out, the reader has understood how the definition, the reflection formula, and the Lie-algebra map fit together.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The double-cover theorem *(from this chapter)*
> With $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, $\xi$, and $\mathfrak{spin}(n)$ in hand, the next page **[[Thm - Spin(n) inside the Clifford Algebra is a Double Cover of SO(n)]]** proves that $\xi : \mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)$ is a surjective two-sheeted covering homomorphism with central kernel $\{\pm 1\}$, that $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ is a compact connected Lie group for $n\ge 2$, and that $d\xi : \mathfrak{spin}(n)\to\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is a Lie algebra isomorphism with inverse $\lambda$. Every use of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ downstream rests on that theorem.

> [!tip] Spinor representations and the Dirac operator *(from §8.2–§8.3)*
> Once $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ sits inside $\mathrm{Cl}^0(\mathbb{R}^n)$, a complex $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$-module restricts to a representation of $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ — the **spinor representation** — and, associated to a lift of the frame bundle to $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$, gives the **spinor bundle** carrying the Dirac operator. The Lie-algebra map $\lambda$ is what feeds the Levi-Civita connection matrix into Clifford multiplication to build the **spin connection**. These are constructed on **[[Def - Spinor Bundle and Dirac Operator]]** and **[[Def - Spin Structure and Spin-c Structure]]**.

> [!tip] Spin structures on manifolds *(from §8.3)*
> The central extension $1\to\{\pm1\}\to\mathrm{Spin}(n)\to SO(n)\to 1$ is precisely what a **[[Def - Spin Structure and Spin-c Structure|spin structure]]** lifts the frame bundle along; the obstruction to lifting is the second Stiefel–Whitney class, and the group $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ of this page is the fibre of that lifting problem.
