# Problem Statement

Let $U$ be a Euclidean vector space of dimension $n$, with positive-definite inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, norm $|u|^2 = \langle u,u\rangle$, and orthonormal basis $e_1,\dots,e_n$. Let $\Lambda U^* = \bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\Lambda^k U^*$ be the exterior algebra of the dual space $U^*$, with dual basis $\epsilon^1,\dots,\epsilon^n$ (so $\epsilon^i(e_j) = \delta^i_j$). For $u\in U$ write $u^\flat = \langle u,\cdot\rangle\in U^*$ for the covector metrically dual to $u$; on the orthonormal basis $e_i^\flat = \epsilon^i$. Define, for each $u\in U$, the endomorphism of $\Lambda U^*$
$$c(u) := \iota_u - u^\flat\wedge\,,\qquad c(u):\Lambda^k U^*\to\Lambda^{k-1}U^*\oplus\Lambda^{k+1}U^*,$$
where $\iota_u$ is contraction with $u$ (interior product) and $u^\flat\wedge$ is left exterior multiplication by the $1$-form $u^\flat$. This is Haydys's Clifford-module structure on $\Lambda U^*$, equation (110), $u\otimes\phi\mapsto\iota_u\phi - \langle u,\cdot\rangle\wedge\phi$. Prove the following.

1. **The Clifford relation holds.** For every $u\in U$, $c(u)^2 = -|u|^2\cdot\operatorname{id}_{\Lambda U^*}$. Consequently, by the universal property, $c$ extends to an algebra homomorphism $\gamma:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(\Lambda U^*)$, so $\Lambda U^*$ is a $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$-module.
2. **The module is faithful.** The homomorphism $\gamma:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(\Lambda U^*)$ is injective. Prove this by evaluating on $1\in\Lambda^0 U^*$ and using the *symbol map* $a\mapsto\gamma(a)(1)$ to show that the monomials $\{e_I\}$ are linearly independent in $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$.
3. **The dimension formula.** Deduce that $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$, and that $\{e_I : I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\}$ is a basis.

This is the drill A-E4.1.2 of Haydys, *Introduction to Gauge Theory*, p. 35 (equation (110)), carried out in full: it is the standard argument by which the dimension of a Clifford algebra is pinned down *without* invoking the classification of Clifford algebras over $\mathbb{R}$.

> [!warning] Convention: sign of the Clifford relation, and the linear-algebra interior product
> This series uses $u\cdot u = -|u|^2\cdot 1$; the dictionary to the vault's [[Def - Clifford Algebra|Clifford algebra]] page (which writes $v\cdot v = Q(v)$) is $\mathrm{Cl}(U) = \mathrm{Cl}(U,-|\cdot|^2) = \mathrm{Cl}(0,n)$. The sign in $c(u) = \iota_u - u^\flat\wedge$ is chosen to realise exactly this convention, as part 1 verifies; the opposite Clifford sign is realised by $\iota_u + u^\flat\wedge$. The [[Def - Interior Product (Contraction with a Vector Field)|interior product]] is stated on that page for differential forms on a manifold; here we use only its purely algebraic, pointwise restriction to the single exterior algebra $\Lambda U^*$, where all of its listed properties (linearity, the graded anti-derivation identity, $\iota_u\circ\iota_u = 0$) hold verbatim with $M$ replaced by the fixed space $U$.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the Clifford algebra of a Euclidean space with its universal property, the interior product, and exterior multiplication.

![[Def - Clifford Algebra and Clifford Multiplication#The Definition]]

For $\mathbb{R}^n$ the [[Def - Clifford Algebra and Clifford Multiplication|Clifford algebra]] $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is generated by $1,e_1,\dots,e_n$ subject to $e_i\cdot e_i = -1$ and $e_i\cdot e_j = -e_j\cdot e_i$ for $i\neq j$. A *Clifford module* is a vector space $V$ with a map $U\otimes V\to V$, $u\otimes v\mapsto u\cdot v$, satisfying $u\cdot(u\cdot v) = -|u|^2 v$; equivalently (by the universal property below) an algebra homomorphism $\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(V)$. The module is **faithful** when this homomorphism is injective. For a subset $I = \{i_1<\cdots<i_k\}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ we write $e_I = e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_k}\in\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ (with $e_\varnothing = 1$), and correspondingly $\epsilon^I = \epsilon^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\epsilon^{i_k}\in\Lambda^k U^*$.

![[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property#Statement]]

The [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] we use: for a quadratic space $(U,Q)$, an associative unital algebra $A$, and a linear map $\varphi:U\to A$ with $\varphi(u)^2 = Q(u)\cdot 1_A$ for all $u\in U$, there is a unique algebra homomorphism $\widetilde\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(U,Q)\to A$ extending $\varphi$. Here $A = \operatorname{End}(\Lambda U^*)$ and $Q = -|\cdot|^2$.

![[Def - Interior Product (Contraction with a Vector Field)#The Definition]]

Restricted to the fixed exterior algebra $\Lambda U^*$, the [[Def - Interior Product (Contraction with a Vector Field)|interior product]] $\iota_u:\Lambda^k U^*\to\Lambda^{k-1}U^*$ is the $\mathbb{R}$-linear contraction with $u\in U$, determined by $\iota_u(1) = 0$ on scalars, $\iota_u(\alpha) = \alpha(u)$ for a $1$-form $\alpha\in U^*$, and the **graded anti-derivation** rule
$$\iota_u(\alpha\wedge\beta) = (\iota_u\alpha)\wedge\beta + (-1)^{\deg\alpha}\,\alpha\wedge(\iota_u\beta)\qquad(\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda U^*),$$
and it satisfies $\iota_u\circ\iota_u = 0$. In particular $\iota_u(\epsilon^j) = \epsilon^j(u) = \langle e_j,u\rangle$ evaluated via $u^\flat$; for a basis vector, $\iota_{e_i}(\epsilon^j) = \delta^j_i$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *represent-then-detect-injectivity* problem: we build a concrete linear representation of an abstractly presented algebra on a familiar space and then read off information about the algebra (here, its dimension) from the representation. It belongs to the same family as showing a group acts faithfully on a set, or that a Lie algebra has a faithful matrix representation. The distinctive feature is that the representation is *chosen* so that a single evaluation — applying an algebra element to the vector $1\in\Lambda^0$ — recovers a basis-sized amount of information.

**Assumption pattern.** Two structural inputs are used. First, the graded-anti-derivation identity for $\iota_u$ together with $\iota_u^2 = 0$ and $(u^\flat\wedge)^2 = 0$; these are what make $c(u)^2$ collapse to a scalar. Second, the spanning half of the basis statement — that the ordered monomials $e_I$ span $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ — which comes for free from the defining relations and gives the upper bound $\dim\le 2^n$. The exercise supplies the matching lower bound.

**Theorem routing.** The route is: (i) compute $c(u)^2$ by expanding $(\iota_u - u^\flat\wedge)^2$ into four terms and evaluating each — two vanish, and the two cross terms assemble into the *canonical anticommutation relation* $\iota_u(u^\flat\wedge\phi) + u^\flat\wedge(\iota_u\phi) = |u|^2\phi$; (ii) feed $c(u)^2 = -|u|^2$ into the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] to get the algebra homomorphism $\gamma$; (iii) evaluate $\gamma(e_I)$ on $1$ and prove by induction that $\gamma(e_I)(1) = (-1)^{|I|}\epsilon^I$; (iv) since the $\epsilon^I$ are a basis of $\Lambda U^*$, conclude that the $e_I$ are linearly independent and $\gamma$ is injective; (v) combine independence ($\dim\ge 2^n$) with spanning ($\dim\le 2^n$) to get equality.

**Key decision point.** The crux is the anticommutation identity $\{\iota_u,\,u^\flat\wedge\} = |u|^2$. Everything hinges on getting the sign right in the graded-anti-derivation rule: contracting $u^\flat\wedge\phi$ produces $(\iota_u u^\flat)\phi - u^\flat\wedge(\iota_u\phi)$, and the *minus* sign is what lets $\iota_u u^\flat = |u|^2$ survive when the two operators are added. The second decision point is recognising that the map $\gamma$ can be probed by a single vector: because $c(e_i)$ carries $1$ to $-\epsilon^i$ and thereafter each further Clifford generator wedges on a new covector (its interior-product part dying because the index is fresh), the entire monomial $e_I$ is faithfully recorded as $\pm\epsilon^I$. That the evaluation-at-$1$ map is injective on the span of the $e_I$ is the whole content of faithfulness.

# Legal Operations Used

The solution deploys the following legal operations from the topic page's Legal Operations (named descriptively; the topic page reconciles the numbering).

1. **Realise a Clifford generator as an operator built from creation and annihilation.** Represent $u\in U$ by $c(u) = \iota_u - u^\flat\wedge$, a difference of an "annihilation" operator $\iota_u$ (lowers degree) and a "creation" operator $u^\flat\wedge$ (raises degree); the Clifford relation becomes an operator identity.

2. **Collapse a squared operator using the canonical anticommutation relations.** Expand $c(u)^2$ and use $\iota_u^2 = 0$, $(u^\flat\wedge)^2 = 0$, and $\{\iota_u, u^\flat\wedge\} = |u|^2$ (proved from the graded anti-derivation identity) to reduce it to a scalar.

3. **Extend a relation-respecting linear map to an algebra homomorphism via the universal property.** With $c(u)^2 = -|u|^2$ verified, the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] delivers $\gamma:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(\Lambda U^*)$.

4. **Detect linear independence by an evaluation (symbol) map.** Compose $\gamma$ with evaluation at $1\in\Lambda^0 U^*$; if the images $\gamma(e_I)(1)$ are linearly independent in $\Lambda U^*$, so are the $e_I$ in $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$, and $\gamma$ is injective.

5. **Combine spanning and independence to fix a dimension.** The defining relations give spanning ($\dim\le 2^n$); the symbol map gives independence ($\dim\ge 2^n$); together they force $\dim = 2^n$.

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Expand $c(u)^2 = (\iota_u - u^\flat\wedge)^2$ into four operator products. Two of them are squares of a single operator — what do you know about $\iota_u\circ\iota_u$ and about wedging the same $1$-form twice?

> [!note]- Hint 2
> The two surviving terms are $\iota_u\circ(u^\flat\wedge)$ and $(u^\flat\wedge)\circ\iota_u$, with a minus sign. Add them: apply the graded anti-derivation rule to $\iota_u(u^\flat\wedge\phi)$. You should find the mixed terms combine into a multiple of $\phi$; the coefficient is $\iota_u(u^\flat) = u^\flat(u)$. What scalar is that?

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For faithfulness, feed the module structure into the universal property to get $\gamma:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(\Lambda U^*)$. Then look at what $\gamma(e_i)$ does to the constant $1$: $\iota_{e_i}(1) = 0$, so $c(e_i)(1) = -\epsilon^i$. Now apply a *second* generator $e_j$ with $j\neq i$ — why does the interior-product part of $c(e_j)$ kill $\epsilon^i$?

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Prove by induction on $|I|$ that for $I = \{i_1<\cdots<i_k\}$, $\gamma(e_I)(1) = (-1)^k\,\epsilon^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\epsilon^{i_k}$. The point is that when you apply $c(e_{i_l})$ with $i_l$ smaller than all indices already present, $\iota_{e_{i_l}}$ annihilates the accumulated wedge (no $\epsilon^{i_l}$ factor is there yet), leaving only the creation term $-\epsilon^{i_l}\wedge(\cdots)$. Since the $\epsilon^I$ are independent, so are the $e_I$; independence plus spanning gives $\dim = 2^n$.

# Solution

The argument has two movements. The first is a short operator computation showing that $c(u) = \iota_u - u^\flat\wedge$ squares to the scalar $-|u|^2$, which is exactly the Clifford relation and hence, by the universal property, promotes $\Lambda U^*$ to a $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$-module. The second is the faithfulness argument: evaluating a Clifford monomial $e_I$ on the constant $1$ produces $\pm\epsilon^I$, so the linearly independent covectors $\epsilon^I$ certify that the $e_I$ are themselves independent, which both proves $\gamma$ injective and, combined with the elementary spanning bound, computes $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$.

**Step 1: The three building-block identities.**

We record $\iota_u^2 = 0$, $(u^\flat\wedge)^2 = 0$, and the anticommutation relation $\iota_u(u^\flat\wedge\phi) + u^\flat\wedge(\iota_u\phi) = |u|^2\phi$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **Annihilation squares to zero.** By the recalled property of the [[Def - Interior Product (Contraction with a Vector Field)|interior product]], $\iota_u\circ\iota_u = 0$ on $\Lambda U^*$.
>
> **Creation squares to zero.** For any $\phi\in\Lambda U^*$, associativity of the wedge product gives $(u^\flat\wedge)(u^\flat\wedge\phi) = (u^\flat\wedge u^\flat)\wedge\phi$, and $u^\flat\wedge u^\flat = 0$ because the wedge of any $1$-form with itself vanishes (antisymmetry of $\wedge$ on $1$-forms: $\alpha\wedge\alpha = -\alpha\wedge\alpha$, so $\alpha\wedge\alpha = 0$). Hence $(u^\flat\wedge)^2 = 0$.
>
> **The anticommutation relation.** Apply the graded anti-derivation rule to $\alpha = u^\flat$ (which has degree $\deg\alpha = 1$) and $\beta = \phi$:
> $$\iota_u(u^\flat\wedge\phi) = (\iota_u u^\flat)\wedge\phi + (-1)^{1}\,u^\flat\wedge(\iota_u\phi) = (\iota_u u^\flat)\,\phi - u^\flat\wedge(\iota_u\phi) \qquad (\text{graded anti-derivation, } \deg u^\flat = 1),$$
> where $\iota_u u^\flat$ is a scalar (contraction of a $1$-form) and so multiplies $\phi$. That scalar is
> $$\iota_u u^\flat = u^\flat(u) = \langle u,u\rangle = |u|^2 \qquad (\text{definition } \iota_u\alpha = \alpha(u), \text{ and } u^\flat = \langle u,\cdot\rangle).$$
> Rearranging the displayed identity,
> $$\iota_u(u^\flat\wedge\phi) + u^\flat\wedge(\iota_u\phi) = |u|^2\,\phi \qquad\text{for all } \phi\in\Lambda U^*,$$
> that is, $\{\iota_u,\,u^\flat\wedge\} = |u|^2\cdot\operatorname{id}$ as operators on $\Lambda U^*$.

**Step 2: The Clifford relation $c(u)^2 = -|u|^2$.**

Expanding the square and using the three identities of Step 1 gives the Clifford relation directly.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Expand the operator square, writing $e_{u^\flat} := u^\flat\wedge$ for brevity:
> $$c(u)^2 = (\iota_u - e_{u^\flat})^2 = \iota_u^2 - \iota_u e_{u^\flat} - e_{u^\flat}\iota_u + e_{u^\flat}^2 \qquad (\text{expanding, keeping the operator order since these do not commute}).$$
> By Step 1 the first and last terms vanish, $\iota_u^2 = 0$ and $e_{u^\flat}^2 = 0$, leaving
> $$c(u)^2 = -\bigl(\iota_u e_{u^\flat} + e_{u^\flat}\iota_u\bigr) = -\{\iota_u,\,e_{u^\flat}\} = -|u|^2\cdot\operatorname{id} \qquad (\text{by the anticommutation relation of Step 1}).$$
> Thus $c(u)^2 = -|u|^2\cdot\operatorname{id}_{\Lambda U^*}$ for every $u\in U$, which is exactly the defining relation of $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ in the series' sign convention $u\cdot u = -|u|^2$.

**Step 3: The module structure $\gamma$.**

The Clifford relation lets the universal property extend $c$ to an algebra homomorphism.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The assignment $u\mapsto c(u) = \iota_u - u^\flat\wedge$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear in $u$ (both $\iota_u$ and $u^\flat$ depend linearly on $u$: $\iota_{au+bv} = a\iota_u + b\iota_v$ by $C^\infty$-linearity of contraction in the vector slot, restricted to constants, and $(au+bv)^\flat = au^\flat + bv^\flat$ since the metric dual is linear). By Step 2 this linear map $c:U\to\operatorname{End}(\Lambda U^*)$ satisfies $c(u)^2 = -|u|^2\cdot\operatorname{id} = Q(u)\cdot\operatorname{id}$ with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$. By the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] there is a unique algebra homomorphism
> $$\gamma:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(\Lambda U^*),\qquad \gamma(u) = c(u)\ \text{for } u\in U,\quad \gamma(1) = \operatorname{id}.$$
> This is precisely the statement that $\Lambda U^*$ is a $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$-module, with $a\cdot\phi := \gamma(a)\phi$; part 1 is proved.

**Step 4: The symbol map records each monomial as $\pm\epsilon^I$.**

Evaluating $\gamma(e_I)$ on the constant $1$ returns $(-1)^{|I|}\epsilon^I$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> Define the **symbol map** $\sigma:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\Lambda U^*$ by $\sigma(a) := \gamma(a)(1)$, where $1\in\Lambda^0 U^* = \mathbb{R}$ is the constant. We first record two facts about $c(e_j) = \gamma(e_j)$.
>
> *Action on the constant.* Since $\iota_{e_j}(1) = 0$ (contraction annihilates scalars) and $e_j^\flat\wedge 1 = \epsilon^j$,
> $$c(e_j)(1) = \iota_{e_j}(1) - e_j^\flat\wedge 1 = 0 - \epsilon^j = -\epsilon^j \qquad (e_j^\flat = \epsilon^j \text{ for orthonormal } e_j).$$
>
> *Action on a wedge missing the index $j$.* Let $J\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$ with $j\notin J$ and consider $\epsilon^J = \epsilon^{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\epsilon^{j_m}$. Iterating the graded anti-derivation rule,
> $$\iota_{e_j}(\epsilon^J) = \sum_{l=1}^{m}(-1)^{l-1}\,\iota_{e_j}(\epsilon^{j_l})\;\epsilon^{j_1}\wedge\cdots\widehat{\epsilon^{j_l}}\cdots\wedge\epsilon^{j_m},$$
> and every coefficient $\iota_{e_j}(\epsilon^{j_l}) = \delta^{j_l}_{j} = 0$ because $j\notin J$ means $j\neq j_l$ for all $l$; hence $\iota_{e_j}(\epsilon^J) = 0$. Therefore
> $$c(e_j)(\epsilon^J) = \iota_{e_j}(\epsilon^J) - \epsilon^j\wedge\epsilon^J = -\epsilon^j\wedge\epsilon^J \qquad (j\notin J).$$
>
> *Induction on $|I|$.* We claim that for $I = \{i_1<i_2<\cdots<i_k\}$,
> $$\sigma(e_I) = \gamma(e_{i_1})\gamma(e_{i_2})\cdots\gamma(e_{i_k})(1) = (-1)^{k}\,\epsilon^{i_1}\wedge\epsilon^{i_2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\epsilon^{i_k} = (-1)^{|I|}\epsilon^I.$$
> The base case $k = 0$ is $\sigma(1) = \gamma(1)(1) = 1 = (-1)^0\epsilon^\varnothing$. Assume the claim for the ordered tuple $(i_2,\dots,i_k)$, so that $\gamma(e_{i_2})\cdots\gamma(e_{i_k})(1) = (-1)^{k-1}\epsilon^{i_2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\epsilon^{i_k}$; call this $(-1)^{k-1}\epsilon^{J}$ with $J = \{i_2,\dots,i_k\}$. Since the indices are strictly increasing, $i_1 < i_2 \le\cdots$, so $i_1\notin J$. Applying $c(e_{i_1})$ and the "wedge missing the index" computation,
> $$\sigma(e_I) = c(e_{i_1})\bigl((-1)^{k-1}\epsilon^J\bigr) = (-1)^{k-1}\bigl(-\epsilon^{i_1}\wedge\epsilon^J\bigr) = (-1)^{k}\,\epsilon^{i_1}\wedge\epsilon^{i_2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\epsilon^{i_k},$$
> completing the induction. So $\sigma(e_I) = (-1)^{|I|}\epsilon^I$ for every ordered $I$.

**Step 5: Faithfulness of $\gamma$ and the dimension formula.**

Linear independence of $\{\epsilon^I\}$ transfers through $\sigma$ to the $e_I$, giving injectivity of $\gamma$ and, with the spanning bound, $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **Independence of the monomials.** Suppose $\sum_{I}a_I\,e_I = 0$ in $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ (finite sum over ordered subsets $I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}$, coefficients $a_I\in\mathbb{R}$). Apply $\sigma = \gamma(\cdot)(1)$, which is linear because $\gamma$ is a homomorphism and evaluation at $1$ is linear:
> $$0 = \sigma\Bigl(\sum_I a_I e_I\Bigr) = \sum_I a_I\,\sigma(e_I) = \sum_I a_I\,(-1)^{|I|}\epsilon^I \qquad (\text{by Step 4}).$$
> The forms $\{\epsilon^I : I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\}$ are the standard basis of $\Lambda U^*$, hence linearly independent; so every coefficient $a_I(-1)^{|I|} = 0$, i.e. $a_I = 0$ for all $I$. Therefore the $\{e_I\}$ are linearly independent in $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$.
>
> **Injectivity of $\gamma$.** By the defining relations, the ordered monomials $\{e_I\}$ *span* $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$: any product of generators $e_{j_1}\cdots e_{j_m}$ can be brought to an ordered monomial by repeatedly applying $e_ae_b = -e_be_a$ to sort the indices (each transposition contributing a sign) and $e_a^2 = -1$ to delete repeated indices, so every element of $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is a real combination of the $e_I$. Thus any $a\in\mathrm{Cl}(U)$ is $a = \sum_I a_I e_I$. If $\gamma(a) = 0$ then in particular $\sigma(a) = \gamma(a)(1) = 0$, so by the independence just shown all $a_I = 0$ and $a = 0$. Hence $\ker\gamma = \{0\}$: the module $\Lambda U^*$ is faithful, proving part 2.
>
> **The dimension.** The spanning statement gives $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)\le \#\{I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\} = 2^n$; the independence statement gives $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)\ge 2^n$. Therefore $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ and $\{e_I : I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}\}$ is a basis, proving part 3.

> [!warning] Illegal but tempting: concluding $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ from spanning alone
> The reduction argument (sort indices, delete repeats) shows only that the $2^n$ monomials $e_I$ *span*, giving $\dim\le 2^n$. It does not by itself prove they are independent — a priori the relations $e_i^2 = -1$, $e_ie_j = -e_je_i$ could conspire to make $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ collapse to something smaller (as happens for other quotients of the tensor algebra). The faithful module is exactly what rules this out: exhibiting a representation on which the $e_I$ act as visibly independent operators forces $\dim\ge 2^n$. Skipping the module and asserting the basis "by counting the obvious monomials" is the tempting error; the extra ingredient that makes it legitimate is a faithful representation, which is what this exercise constructs. (This is why the definition page proves the dimension via this module rather than by the classification theorem.)

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** With $c(u) = \iota_u - u^\flat\wedge$ on $\Lambda U^*$: (1) $c(u)^2 = -|u|^2\operatorname{id}$, so $c$ induces an algebra homomorphism $\gamma:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(\Lambda U^*)$; (2) $\gamma$ is injective; (3) $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$.
>
> *Part 1.* The interior product satisfies $\iota_u^2 = 0$ and the graded anti-derivation rule $\iota_u(\alpha\wedge\beta) = (\iota_u\alpha)\wedge\beta + (-1)^{\deg\alpha}\alpha\wedge(\iota_u\beta)$; and $u^\flat\wedge u^\flat = 0$, so $(u^\flat\wedge)^2 = 0$. Taking $\alpha = u^\flat$, $\beta = \phi$ gives $\iota_u(u^\flat\wedge\phi) = (u^\flat(u))\phi - u^\flat\wedge\iota_u\phi = |u|^2\phi - u^\flat\wedge\iota_u\phi$, i.e. $\{\iota_u, u^\flat\wedge\} = |u|^2\operatorname{id}$. Hence
> $$c(u)^2 = \iota_u^2 - \{\iota_u, u^\flat\wedge\} + (u^\flat\wedge)^2 = -|u|^2\operatorname{id}.$$
> As $c$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear in $u$ and $c(u)^2 = Q(u)\operatorname{id}$ with $Q = -|\cdot|^2$, the [[Thm - Clifford Algebra Universal Property|universal property]] yields a unique algebra homomorphism $\gamma:\mathrm{Cl}(U)\to\operatorname{End}(\Lambda U^*)$ with $\gamma(u) = c(u)$.
>
> *Part 2.* Let $\sigma(a) = \gamma(a)(1)$ for $a\in\mathrm{Cl}(U)$, $1\in\Lambda^0 U^*$. Then $c(e_j)(1) = -\epsilon^j$, and for $j\notin J$, $\iota_{e_j}\epsilon^J = 0$ so $c(e_j)\epsilon^J = -\epsilon^j\wedge\epsilon^J$. By induction on $|I|$, for $I = \{i_1<\cdots<i_k\}$, $\sigma(e_I) = (-1)^k\epsilon^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\epsilon^{i_k} = (-1)^{|I|}\epsilon^I$. If $\sum_I a_I e_I = 0$ then $\sum_I a_I(-1)^{|I|}\epsilon^I = \sigma(0) = 0$; the $\epsilon^I$ are independent, so all $a_I = 0$: the $e_I$ are independent. The $e_I$ span $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)$ by sorting-and-deleting via $e_ie_j = -e_je_i$, $e_i^2 = -1$; so any $a = \sum a_I e_I$, and $\gamma(a) = 0 \Rightarrow \sigma(a) = 0 \Rightarrow$ all $a_I = 0 \Rightarrow a = 0$. Thus $\gamma$ is injective and $\Lambda U^*$ is a faithful $\mathrm{Cl}(U)$-module.
>
> *Part 3.* Spanning gives $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n)\le 2^n$; independence gives $\dim\ge 2^n$; hence $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ with basis $\{e_I\}_{I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}}$. $\blacksquare$
>
> **Consistency check ($n = 2$).** On $\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^2)^*$ (a $4$-dimensional space, basis $1,\epsilon^1,\epsilon^2,\epsilon^1\wedge\epsilon^2$), the operators $\gamma(1),\gamma(e_1),\gamma(e_2),\gamma(e_1e_2)$ send $1$ to $1,-\epsilon^1,-\epsilon^2,\epsilon^1\wedge\epsilon^2$ respectively, which are independent — matching $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2) = 4$ and the identification $\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^2)\cong\mathbb{H}$ of [[Ex - The Clifford Algebras of R^1 and R^2]].

# Key Takeaways

**A faithful representation is the standard, classification-free way to compute the dimension of an algebra given by generators and relations.** When an algebra $A$ is presented as a quotient of a free (or tensor) algebra, listing the "obvious" monomials shows only that they span, hence bounds $\dim A$ from above; the relations might secretly force extra collapse. The reusable principle is that a *faithful* module — a representation $\gamma:A\to\operatorname{End}(V)$ with trivial kernel — certifies independence from below, because independent operators can only come from independent algebra elements. The trigger condition is precisely a "$\dim\le N$ from spanning, need $\dim\ge N$" gap for a relations-presented algebra. The transferable diagnostic is to look for a natural space on which the generators act by recognisable operators whose products are visibly independent; here that space is $\Lambda U^*$ and the operators are the creation–annihilation pair $\iota_u \mp u^\flat\wedge$. This is exactly how one proves $\dim\mathrm{Cl}(\mathbb{R}^n) = 2^n$ without the eightfold classification of real Clifford algebras, and the same move computes dimensions of universal enveloping algebras (Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt via the polynomial representation) and of Weyl and Heisenberg algebras.

**Clifford multiplication on forms is a difference of a creation and an annihilation operator, and its square collapses precisely because of the canonical anticommutation relations.** The mechanism worth remembering is $c(u) = \iota_u - u^\flat\wedge$ with $\iota_u^2 = 0$, $(u^\flat\wedge)^2 = 0$, and $\{\iota_u, u^\flat\wedge\} = |u|^2$; squaring $c(u)$ leaves only $-\{\iota_u, u^\flat\wedge\} = -|u|^2$. This is the fermionic Fock-space picture: $u^\flat\wedge$ "creates a particle in mode $u$", $\iota_u$ "annihilates" one, they anticommute, and the Clifford relation is the statement that a mode occupied twice is annihilated. The trigger to recall this identification is any operator that mixes a degree-raising and a degree-lowering piece on a graded space; checking whether the two pieces anticommute to a scalar tells you immediately whether you are looking at a Clifford (or CAR) structure. The sign in $c(u) = \iota_u - u^\flat\wedge$ is what selects $u\cdot u = -|u|^2$; the choice $\iota_u + u^\flat\wedge$ gives the opposite Clifford algebra, the same sign sensitivity seen concretely in [[Ex - The Clifford Algebras of R^1 and R^2]].

**Evaluation at the vacuum vector $1$ turns an algebra element into its "symbol", and this is the bridge between the Clifford algebra and the exterior algebra.** The computation $\gamma(e_I)(1) = (-1)^{|I|}\epsilon^I$ is the seed of the Chevalley identification $\mathrm{Cl}(U)\cong\Lambda U^*$ as filtered vector spaces: the symbol map $\sigma(a) = \gamma(a)(1)$ is a linear isomorphism, and it intertwines the Clifford product with the exterior product up to lower-order (contraction) corrections. The reusable diagnostic is that whenever an algebra acts on a space with a distinguished cyclic vector (a "vacuum"), evaluating at that vector often gives a linear isomorphism onto the space, converting hard multiplicative questions into easy questions about the module. Here it lets us both prove faithfulness and read off the basis; downstream, the same $\Lambda T^*M$ construction globalises to make the exterior bundle a bundle of Clifford modules (with $v\cdot\phi = v^\flat\wedge\phi - \iota_v\phi$), which is how the Hodge–de Rham operator $d + d^*$ is recognised as a Dirac-type operator. The companion drill [[Ex - The Clifford Algebras of R^1 and R^2]] fixes the low-dimensional isomorphism types; this exercise supplies the general dimension count that underwrites all of them.
