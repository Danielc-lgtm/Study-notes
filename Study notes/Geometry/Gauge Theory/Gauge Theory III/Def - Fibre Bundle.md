# Notation

Throughout this series a **manifold** is a smooth ($C^\infty$), Hausdorff, second-countable manifold, possibly with boundary, and "smooth map" means $C^\infty$ map; see [[Def - Smooth Manifold]] and [[Def - Smooth Map between Manifolds|smooth map]]. A **diffeomorphism** is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse, [[Def - Diffeomorphism]]. We write $\operatorname{pr}_1 : X \times Y \to X$ and $\operatorname{pr}_2 : X \times Y \to Y$ for the two projections of a Cartesian product.

The objects of this page are named as follows. The map whose fibres are to be understood is $\pi : E \to B$, with $E$ the **total space**, $B$ the **base**, and $\pi$ the **projection**. The letter $F$ denotes the **typical fibre**, a fixed manifold. For $x \in B$ we write $E_x := \pi^{-1}(x) \subseteq E$ for the **fibre over $x$**, and for an open set $U \subseteq B$ we write $E|_U := \pi^{-1}(U)$ for the **restriction of $E$ to $U$**. A **local trivialisation over $U$** is a diffeomorphism $\psi_U : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times F$ with $\operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi_U = \pi|_{\pi^{-1}(U)}$. For a smooth vector bundle we write $\Gamma(E)$ for the $C^\infty(B)$-module of its smooth sections, and $0_x \in E_x$ for the zero of the vector-space fibre over $x$. The scalar field is $\mathbb{K} \in \{\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}\}$.

> [!warning] Convention: which maps count as bundle isomorphisms
> Bär's Definition 2.1.6 defines an isomorphism of two bundles $(E,\pi,B)$ and $(E',\pi',B')$ by a diffeomorphism $\psi : E \to E'$ making the triangle over a **single** base commute, that is with $\pi' \circ \psi = \pi$; so as literally written it compares bundles over the *same* base $B = B'$ and covers the identity of $B$. This is the notion we use by default and call "isomorphism over $B$". We also record the more general notion, used from chapter IV onward, of a **bundle map covering a diffeomorphism** $f : B \to B'$ of bases: a diffeomorphism $\psi : E \to E'$ with $\pi' \circ \psi = f \circ \pi$. The default notion is the special case $f = \operatorname{id}_B$. Haydys works with the same conventions; where the two sources' section theory diverges (principal-bundle equivariance, the associated-bundle sign) the relevant page carries its own convention callout.

This is a compound page: it fixes the several interlocking notions of the first section of the bundle chapter — fibre bundle, base, total space, typical fibre, local trivialisation, fibre, restriction, isomorphism, triviality, the special case of a vector bundle, section, zero section, and pull-back — because they are one circle of ideas and each is defined by its relation to the others, so splitting them across pages would force every one to restate the rest.

# Axiom Motivation

The question this definition answers is: *what is the right notion of a family of manifolds smoothly parametrised by the points of a base?* One has in mind, at each point $x$ of a base manifold $B$, a manifold $E_x$ — a copy of a fixed model $F$, but attached to $x$ and possibly glued to its neighbours with a twist — and one wants a single smooth object $E$ that assembles all the $E_x$ at once, together with the bookkeeping map $\pi : E \to B$ that remembers which fibre a point lives in. The tangent bundle of a manifold, in which $E_x = T_x B$, is the archetype; the Möbius strip, a smoothly varying family of open intervals over the circle glued back with a flip, is the archetype of a family that is genuinely twisted and not a product. The definition must be permissive enough to admit both and precise enough that "smoothly varying" has content.

The desiderata are three. First, $E$ should be a single manifold and $\pi$ a smooth map, so that calculus is available on the total space and the assignment $x \mapsto E_x$ is smooth in the only sense manifolds understand, namely that $E$ is smooth and $\pi$ is smooth. Second, every fibre $E_x$ should be a copy of one fixed model $F$, so that the family has a constant fibre type and phrases like "the bundle with fibre $S^1$" mean something. Third, and decisively, the family should be **locally a product**: near any point of the base the bundle should be indistinguishable from the trivial family $U \times F \to U$, so that all the twisting is in how the local products are glued, never in the local structure itself. These three are captured by asking that $\pi$ be a surjective smooth map admitting, around every point of $B$, a diffeomorphism $\psi_U : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times F$ that respects $\pi$.

Consider what each clause excludes and why dropping it breaks the theory. Suppose we drop **surjectivity** of $\pi$. Then a point $x \in B$ with $\pi^{-1}(x) = \varnothing$ has an empty fibre, which is not a copy of $F$, and "the fibre over $x$" is a copy of nothing; worse, a local trivialisation $\psi_U : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times F$ over a neighbourhood of such an $x$ cannot exist, because the left side omits the slice $\{x\} \times F$ that the right side contains. Surjectivity is exactly the demand that every point of the base actually carries a fibre. Suppose next we drop **local triviality** but keep $\pi$ a surjective submersion. This is the sharpest failure, because a surjective submersion looks locally like a projection $\mathbb{R}^{m} \to \mathbb{R}^{n}$ but need not have constant fibre. The concrete witness, which we verify in full below, is the map $\varphi : (0, 4\pi) \to S^1$, $t \mapsto e^{it}$: it is a surjective submersion, yet the fibre over $1 \in S^1$ is a single point while every nearby fibre has two points, so no neighbourhood of $1$ admits a trivialisation with a fixed model fibre. Local triviality is precisely the condition that rules out such jumps in the fibre; it is not implied by $\pi$ being a submersion. Finally, suppose we weaken the **local model** by letting the fibre type vary from point to point — asking only that each $\pi^{-1}(x)$ be *some* manifold. Then "the typical fibre $F$" is undefined, all the fibres over different points may be non-diffeomorphic, and the objects that make the theory usable — a well-defined structure group, transition functions valued in one automorphism group, characteristic classes — have nothing constant to be built from. Requiring one fixed $F$ is what makes the family a *bundle* rather than a mere disjoint indexed collection of manifolds.

There is a temptation to strengthen the definition to demand **global** triviality, that $E$ be diffeomorphic to $B \times F$ over $B$. This is the wrong strengthening, and understanding why is the whole point of the subject. Global triviality would exclude the Möbius strip, the tangent bundle of the two-sphere, and the Hopf bundle — that is, it would exclude every example whose twisting is the reason bundles are interesting — and would leave only Cartesian products, on which nothing happens that ordinary calculus on $B$ and $F$ separately does not already see. The correct condition is the local one: strong enough that smoothness of sections and the transition-function calculus make sense, weak enough that twisted families survive. A reader handed only the three desiderata and the failure analysis above could reconstruct the definition, because each clause is forced: surjectivity by "every point has a fibre", the fixed model $F$ by "constant fibre type", and local triviality (not mere submersion) by "smoothly a product near each point, but allowed to be globally twisted".

# The Definition

**Fibre bundle.** Let $E$, $B$, $F$ be smooth manifolds and let $\pi : E \to B$ be a surjective smooth map. The triple $(E, \pi, B)$ is a **fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$** if each point $x \in B$ has an open neighbourhood $U \subseteq B$ for which there exists a diffeomorphism
$$\psi_U : \pi^{-1}(U) \longrightarrow U \times F \qquad \text{such that} \qquad \operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi_U = \pi|_{\pi^{-1}(U)},$$
that is, the diagram formed by $\psi_U$, the projection $\pi$ on $\pi^{-1}(U)$, and $\operatorname{pr}_1$ on $U \times F$ commutes. The manifold $B$ is the **base**, $E$ the **total space**, and $\psi_U$ a **local trivialisation over $U$**. (This is Bär, Definition 2.1.1, transcribed with the series' spelling "fibre".)

**Fibre.** For $x \in B$ the **fibre over $x$** is $E_x := \pi^{-1}(x)$. The **restriction** of the bundle to an open set $U \subseteq B$ is $E|_U := \pi^{-1}(U)$ with the projection $\pi|_{\pi^{-1}(U)} : E|_U \to U$.

**Isomorphism and triviality.** Two fibre bundles $(E, \pi, B)$ and $(E', \pi', B)$ over the same base $B$ are **isomorphic** if there is a diffeomorphism $\psi : E \to E'$ with $\pi' \circ \psi = \pi$ (Bär, Definition 2.1.6). A fibre bundle is **trivial** if it is isomorphic to the product bundle $(B \times F, \operatorname{pr}_1, B)$; equivalently — as proved below — if it admits a **global trivialisation**, a local trivialisation with $U = B$.

**Vector bundle as a fibre bundle.** A fibre bundle $(E, \pi, B)$ with typical fibre $\mathbb{K}^n$ ($\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$) is a **(real or complex) vector bundle of rank $n$** if each fibre $E_x$ carries the structure of a $\mathbb{K}$-vector space and the local trivialisations may be chosen so that, for every $x \in U$, the restriction $\psi_U|_{E_x} : E_x \to \{x\} \times \mathbb{K}^n \cong \mathbb{K}^n$ is a $\mathbb{K}$-linear isomorphism (Bär, Definition 2.1.7). We show in **Examples / Corollaries** that this notion coincides, in both directions, with the vector bundle of [[Def - Vector Bundle]].

**Section and zero section.** A **section** of a fibre bundle $(E, \pi, B)$ is a smooth map $s : B \to E$ with $\pi \circ s = \operatorname{id}_B$; sections are always taken smooth unless stated otherwise (Bär, Definition 2.1.9 and its convention). For a vector bundle $(V, \pi, B)$ the **zero section** is $s_0(x) := 0_x \in V_x$ for every $x \in B$.

**Pull-back.** Let $(E, \pi, B)$ be a fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$ and let $\lambda : B' \to B$ be a smooth map. Put
$$E' := \{ (b', p) \in B' \times E : \lambda(b') = \pi(p) \}, \qquad \pi' := \operatorname{pr}_1|_{E'} : E' \to B'.$$
The triple $\lambda^*(E, \pi, B) := (E', \pi', B')$ is the **pull-back of $(E, \pi, B)$ along $\lambda$** (Bär, Definition 2.1.11). By construction the square with $\operatorname{pr}_2 : E' \to E$ across the top, $\pi'$ down the left, $\lambda$ across the bottom, and $\pi$ down the right commutes, $\pi \circ \operatorname{pr}_2 = \lambda \circ \pi'$. That $\lambda^* E$ is again a fibre bundle with fibre $F$, and that $\operatorname{pr}_2$ identifies $E'_{b'}$ with $E_{\lambda(b')}$, is proved on **[[Thm - The Pull-Back of a Fibre Bundle is a Fibre Bundle]]**; on this page the pull-back is a definition only.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

A fibre bundle is precisely a **locally trivial family of manifolds over $B$ with model fibre $F$**, and this phrasing is worth making exact because it is what organises the whole chapter. A *family of manifolds over $B$* is a submersion $\pi : E \to B$; the fibres $E_x$ are then manifolds by the [[Thm - Regular Value Theorem on Manifolds|regular value theorem]] — every point of $B$ is a regular value of a submersion, so each $E_x$ is an [[Def - Embedded Submanifold|embedded submanifold]] of $E$. A family is *locally trivial with model $F$* if it is locally, over an open cover of $B$, isomorphic as a family to the constant family $U \times F \to U$. The definition above is the statement that a fibre bundle is exactly a locally trivial family with a fixed model, and the corollary in the next section that $\pi$ is a submersion shows the two descriptions of "family" agree.

Fixing the base $B$, the fibre bundles over $B$ are the objects of a **category $\mathbf{Bun}_B$**. A morphism from $(E, \pi, B)$ to $(E', \pi', B)$ is a smooth map $\Phi : E \to E'$ covering the identity of $B$, meaning $\pi' \circ \Phi = \pi$, so that $\Phi$ carries each fibre $E_x$ into the same-base fibre $E'_x$. Composition is composition of smooth maps and the identity morphism is the identity map, so the category axioms hold trivially. The isomorphisms of $\mathbf{Bun}_B$ are exactly the bundle isomorphisms defined above: a morphism $\Phi$ with a two-sided inverse in $\mathbf{Bun}_B$ is a diffeomorphism $\Phi$ with $\pi' \circ \Phi = \pi$, and conversely. In this language, a bundle is **trivial** precisely when it is isomorphic in $\mathbf{Bun}_B$ to the product $B \times F$, and the pull-back along $\lambda : B' \to B$ is a functor $\lambda^* : \mathbf{Bun}_B \to \mathbf{Bun}_{B'}$ (its action on morphisms is $\Phi \mapsto \operatorname{id}_{B'} \times_\lambda \Phi$; the functoriality and the identification of fibres are established on [[Thm - The Pull-Back of a Fibre Bundle is a Fibre Bundle]]). The vector bundles of rank $n$ form the full subcategory whose objects carry fibrewise $\mathbb{K}$-vector-space structures with linear trivialisations, and whose morphisms are additionally fibrewise linear; this is the category studied in [[Def - Vector Bundle]]. The principal bundles of [[Def - Principal G-Bundle|the next section]] are, in the same spirit, the fibre bundles carrying a fibrewise-free-and-transitive right group action with equivariant trivialisations. So "fibre bundle" is the common genus of which vector bundles, principal bundles, and associated bundles are the species distinguished by extra fibrewise structure preserved by the trivialisations.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The idea "locally a product, globally perhaps twisted" recurs across mathematics under different names, and recognising the fibre bundle inside each is a genuine compression. In **algebraic topology** a continuous surjection with the homotopy lifting property is a *fibration*, [[Def - Fibration]]; every smooth fibre bundle over a paracompact base is a fibration, which is why the long exact homotopy sequence (chapter XII) applies to it, and it is the tool that first detects the non-triviality of the Hopf bundle. In **covering space theory** a covering map is exactly a fibre bundle with discrete typical fibre; local triviality is the "evenly covered neighbourhood" condition, and the deck group plays the role of the structure group. In **differential geometry** the tangent, cotangent, and tensor bundles are the fibre bundles built fibrewise by multilinear algebra, and Riemannian geometry is calculus on their sections. In **theoretical physics** a bundle is the geometric home of a field: a field on spacetime $B$ is a section of a bundle over $B$, and the requirement that the field's components transform correctly under a change of local frame is exactly the requirement that they patch into a global section, so the "gauge freedom" of physics is the freedom in choosing local trivialisations.

**True name.** Operationally, a fibre bundle is *the data of local product charts $\psi_U : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times F$ together with the smooth maps that glue them on overlaps*. When one meets a bundle in practice one does not verify surjectivity and submersion by hand; one produces an open cover and the trivialisations over it, and one reads off from the overlaps the **transition functions** $\psi_U \circ \psi_V^{-1}(x, f) = (x, g_{UV}(x)\cdot f)$, which encode all the twisting and are the subject of [[Def - Transition Functions and the Cocycle Condition|the cocycle description]]. The official definition — a surjective submersion that is locally a product — is the invariant statement; the transition functions are the working presentation, and the two are equivalent because a cocycle can be reconstructed into a bundle and a bundle produces a cocycle from any trivialising cover.

# Examples / Corollaries

We first draw the two structural corollaries the definition owes — that the fibres are all copies of $F$, and that $\pi$ is a submersion and an open map — then verify instances and one non-instance clause by clause, then the vector-bundle equivalence, and close with the calibration checks.

**Corollary — all fibres are diffeomorphic to $F$ (Bär, Remark 2.1.2).** Let $(E, \pi, B)$ be a fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$ and let $x \in B$. Choose a local trivialisation $\psi_U : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times F$ over a neighbourhood $U \ni x$. Because $\operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi_U = \pi$, a point $p \in \pi^{-1}(U)$ lies in $E_x = \pi^{-1}(x)$ if and only if $\operatorname{pr}_1(\psi_U(p)) = x$, that is if and only if $\psi_U(p) \in \{x\} \times F$; hence
$$E_x = \psi_U^{-1}\big(\{x\} \times F\big) \qquad \text{(since } \operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi_U = \pi \text{ and } \psi_U \text{ is a bijection).}$$
Now $\{x\} \times F$ is an embedded submanifold of $U \times F$ diffeomorphic to $F$ via $\operatorname{pr}_2|_{\{x\}\times F}$, and $\psi_U$ is a diffeomorphism, so its restriction
$$\psi_U|_{E_x} : E_x \longrightarrow \{x\} \times F$$
is a diffeomorphism (the restriction of a diffeomorphism to the preimage of an embedded submanifold is a diffeomorphism onto that submanifold). Composing with $\operatorname{pr}_2|_{\{x\}\times F} : \{x\} \times F \to F$, which is a diffeomorphism, gives a diffeomorphism $E_x \cong F$. **Therefore** every fibre of the bundle is diffeomorphic to the typical fibre $F$, which is what justifies the name "typical fibre". $\blacksquare$

**Corollary — $\pi$ is a submersion and an open map.** Let $p \in E$ and set $x := \pi(p)$; choose a local trivialisation $\psi_U : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times F$ over a neighbourhood $U \ni x$. On $\pi^{-1}(U)$ we have $\pi = \operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi_U$ (by the defining property of $\psi_U$). The map $\operatorname{pr}_1 : U \times F \to U$ is a surjective submersion (in product charts its differential is the coordinate projection, which is surjective at every point), and $\psi_U$ is a diffeomorphism, so $d\pi_p = d(\operatorname{pr}_1)_{\psi_U(p)} \circ d(\psi_U)_p$ is a composition of a surjective linear map with a linear isomorphism, hence surjective (by the chain rule and surjectivity of $d(\operatorname{pr}_1)$). As $p$ was arbitrary, $\pi$ is a submersion in the sense of [[Def - Immersion, Submersion, and Embedding|submersion]]. For openness, let $W \subseteq E$ be open; then $\psi_U(W \cap \pi^{-1}(U))$ is open in $U \times F$ (image of an open set under the diffeomorphism $\psi_U$), and $\operatorname{pr}_1$ is an open map (a projection of a product is open), so
$$\pi(W \cap \pi^{-1}(U)) = \operatorname{pr}_1\big(\psi_U(W \cap \pi^{-1}(U))\big) \qquad \text{(since } \pi = \operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi_U \text{ on } \pi^{-1}(U)\text{)}$$
is open in $U$, hence open in $B$. Since $\pi(W)$ is the union of such sets as $U$ ranges over a trivialising cover, $\pi(W)$ is open. **Therefore** $\pi$ is both a submersion and an open map. (This is the technical fact that lets us treat a fibre bundle as a locally trivial family in the sense of the structural definition; it is also the input to the transversality argument that proves the pull-back is a bundle.) $\blacksquare$

**Is an instance — the product (trivial) bundle (Bär, Example 2.1.3.1).** For any manifolds $B$ and $F$ the triple $(B \times F, \operatorname{pr}_1, B)$ is a fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$. We check each clause. The map $\operatorname{pr}_1 : B \times F \to B$ is smooth and surjective (for any $x \in B$ and any chosen $f \in F$, $\operatorname{pr}_1(x, f) = x$). Local triviality holds globally: take $U = B$ and $\psi_B = \operatorname{id}_{B \times F}$, which is a diffeomorphism with $\operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi_B = \operatorname{pr}_1 = \pi$. Every clause holds, so the product is a fibre bundle, and the identity is a global trivialisation, so it is trivial. Its fibre over $x$ is $\{x\} \times F$.

**Is an instance — the tangent bundle and its tensor constructions (Bär, Example 2.1.8).** For a smooth $m$-manifold $B$, the tangent bundle $TB$ with its foot-point projection is a fibre bundle with typical fibre $\mathbb{R}^m$ (indeed a rank-$m$ vector bundle): its construction and local trivialisations from coordinate charts are given on [[Def - The Tangent Bundle]], and the local trivialisation over a chart domain $U$ sends $v^i \, \partial/\partial x^i|_p$ to $(p, v^1, \dots, v^m)$, a diffeomorphism commuting with the projection and linear on each fibre. The bundles obtained from $TB$ by applying a linear-algebra construction fibrewise are again fibre bundles (vector bundles): the cotangent bundle $T^*B$ (see [[Def - Cotangent Space and Cotangent Bundle]]), the exterior powers $\Lambda^k T^*B$, the symmetric powers $\odot^k T^*B$, and the mixed tensor bundles $\bigotimes^k TB \otimes \bigotimes^l T^*B$. That each of these is a bundle, with trivialisations and transition functions induced functorially from those of $TB$, is the content of [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles|the operations on vector bundles]]; we do not re-derive it here but record that it produces exactly the fibre bundles whose sections are the standard fields of geometry.

**Is an instance — sections of these bundles are the classical fields (Bär, Remark 2.1.10 table).** The point of naming the tensor bundles is that their sections are objects one already knows, and this is a dictionary rather than a computation:

- a section of $TB$ is a **[[Def - Vector Field on a Manifold|vector field]]** on $B$;
- a section of $T^*B$ is a **differential $1$-form**;
- a section of $\Lambda^k T^*B$ is a **differential $k$-form**;
- a section of $\bigotimes^k TB \otimes \bigotimes^l T^*B$ is a **[[Def - Tensor Field on a Manifold|$(k, l)$-tensor field]]**.

Each identification is immediate from the definitions: a smooth map $s : B \to TB$ with $\pi \circ s = \operatorname{id}_B$ assigns to every $x$ a tangent vector $s(x) \in T_x B$ smoothly, which is exactly a vector field, and the other three rows read off the same way with the corresponding fibre.

**Is an instance — the Möbius strip (Bär, Example 2.1.5).** The Möbius strip is the total space of a fibre bundle over $S^1$ with typical fibre the open interval $(-1, 1)$, obtained by gluing the two ends of $[0,1] \times (-1,1)$ by the flip $x \mapsto -x$. Its construction as the mapping torus of the diffeomorphism $x \mapsto -x$, the verification that the projection to $S^1$ is a fibre bundle, and the proof that it is **not** trivial, are the subject of **[[Def - Sphere Bundles and Mapping Tori]]** and the theorem that follows it there; we record it here as the standing example of a non-trivial fibre bundle and forward the reader to that page.

**Is NOT an instance — a surjective submersion whose fibres jump (the non-example the spec demands).** Consider the smooth map
$$\varphi : (0, 4\pi) \longrightarrow S^1 \subseteq \mathbb{C}, \qquad \varphi(t) = e^{it},$$
with $S^1 = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1 \}$. We verify, clause by clause, that $\varphi$ is a surjective submersion and yet **not** a fibre bundle, so that "surjective submersion" is strictly weaker than "fibre bundle". *Surjective:* every $z = e^{i\theta}$ with $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ equals $\varphi(\theta + 2\pi)$ and $\theta + 2\pi \in (0, 4\pi)$, so $\varphi$ is onto. *Submersion:* identifying $T_z S^1$ with $iz\,\mathbb{R}$, we have $\varphi'(t) = i e^{it} \neq 0$ for all $t$, so $d\varphi_t$ has rank $1 = \dim S^1$ and is surjective; hence $\varphi$ is a submersion. *Not a fibre bundle:* compute the fibres. For $z = 1 = e^{i\cdot 0}$,
$$\varphi^{-1}(1) = \{ t \in (0, 4\pi) : e^{it} = 1 \} = \{ t \in (0,4\pi) : t \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z} \} = \{ 2\pi \} \qquad \text{(since } 0, 4\pi \notin (0,4\pi)\text{)},$$
a single point. But for $z = e^{i\theta}$ with $0 < \theta < 2\pi$,
$$\varphi^{-1}(e^{i\theta}) = \{ t \in (0,4\pi) : t \equiv \theta \ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi) \} = \{ \theta, \, \theta + 2\pi \} \qquad \text{(both lie in } (0,4\pi)\text{)},$$
a two-point set. If $\varphi$ were a fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$, then by the first corollary above every fibre would be diffeomorphic to $F$, in particular all fibres would have the same cardinality; but the fibre over $1$ has one point while every fibre over a nearby $e^{i\theta}$, $0 < \theta < 2\pi$, has two points. This contradicts the constancy of the fibre. **Therefore** $\varphi$ is a surjective submersion that is not a fibre bundle: the jump in fibre cardinality at $z = 1$ is exactly the failure of local triviality that the definition forbids, and it shows the local-triviality clause is not a consequence of the submersion clause.

**Is NOT an instance — the zero section is not always available (Bär, Remark 2.1.10).** Every **vector** bundle $(V, \pi, B)$ has at least one smooth global section, the **zero section** $s_0(x) = 0_x \in V_x$: it is smooth because in any local trivialisation $\psi_U(s_0(x)) = (x, 0)$, a smooth map, and it satisfies $\pi \circ s_0 = \operatorname{id}_B$ because $s_0(x) \in V_x = \pi^{-1}(x)$. General fibre bundles, by contrast, need not admit **any** continuous section. The standing witness is the **[[Def - The Hopf Bundle|Hopf bundle]]** $\pi : S^3 \to S^2$ (a fibre bundle with typical fibre $S^1$, in fact a principal $U(1)$-bundle): it has no global section at all, as proved on **[[Thm - The Hopf Bundle is Nontrivial]]**, where a global section is shown to force a diffeomorphism $S^3 \cong S^2 \times S^1$ that is excluded by first de Rham cohomology. This is the clean structural difference between vector bundles and general fibre bundles: the linear fibre supplies a canonical section, a bare fibre supplies none.

**Corollary — a bundle is trivial if and only if it admits a global trivialisation (Bär, Definition 2.1.6).** We prove both directions of this equivalence, which the definition asserts.

> [!note]- Proof that triviality $\iff$ global trivialisation
> Let $(E, \pi, B)$ be a fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$. We must show it is isomorphic to $(B \times F, \operatorname{pr}_1, B)$ over $B$ if and only if it admits a local trivialisation defined on all of $B$.
>
> **Direction 1 ($\Leftarrow$): a global trivialisation gives an isomorphism to the product.** Assume there is a global trivialisation $\psi_B : \pi^{-1}(B) \to B \times F$, that is a diffeomorphism $\psi_B : E \to B \times F$ (since $\pi^{-1}(B) = E$) with $\operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi_B = \pi$. Then $\psi_B$ is, by the very definition of isomorphism over $B$, an isomorphism $(E, \pi, B) \to (B \times F, \operatorname{pr}_1, B)$: it is a diffeomorphism $E \to B \times F$ and it satisfies $\operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi_B = \pi$ (the required intertwining of projections). **Hence** the bundle is trivial.
>
> **Direction 2 ($\Rightarrow$): triviality gives a global trivialisation.** Assume $(E, \pi, B)$ is trivial, so there is a diffeomorphism $\psi : E \to B \times F$ with $\operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi = \pi$ (the definition of isomorphism over $B$ applied to the product bundle, whose projection is $\operatorname{pr}_1$). Take $U := B$; then $\psi$ is a diffeomorphism $\pi^{-1}(B) = E \to B \times F$ with $\operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi = \pi$, which is exactly a local trivialisation over $U = B$, that is a global trivialisation. **Hence** the bundle admits a global trivialisation.
>
> **Conclusion.** The two conditions are equivalent, so "trivial" and "admits a global trivialisation" name the same class of bundles, as the definition claims. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary — the fibre-bundle vector bundle coincides with the vector bundle of the geometry chapter, in both directions.** The definition above says a vector bundle is a fibre bundle with fibre $\mathbb{K}^n$ carrying fibrewise linear structure and linear trivialisations. We must reconcile this with the primary definition [[Def - Vector Bundle]], which builds a vector bundle directly from a total space, a fibrewise-linear projection, and linear local triviality. We prove the two definitions describe the same objects.

> [!note]- Proof that the two definitions of a vector bundle agree
> Fix $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$; the complex case is identical after identifying $\mathbb{C}^n$ with $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ and $\mathbb{K}$-linear with $\mathbb{C}$-linear maps, which is the standing convention of [[Def - Vector Bundle]]. Recall the primary definition: a rank-$n$ real vector bundle over $B$ is a smooth manifold $E$, a smooth surjection $\pi : E \to B$, and a real vector-space structure of dimension $n$ on each fibre $E_x = \pi^{-1}(x)$, such that every $x \in B$ has a neighbourhood $U$ with a diffeomorphism $\Phi : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfying $\operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \Phi = \pi$ and, for each $q \in U$, $\Phi|_{E_q} : E_q \to \{q\} \times \mathbb{R}^n$ linear. We show the two definitions have identical hypotheses.
>
> **Direction 1: a fibre-bundle vector bundle is a primary vector bundle.** Let $(E, \pi, B)$ be a fibre bundle with typical fibre $\mathbb{R}^n$, with each $E_x$ a real $n$-dimensional vector space and the trivialisations $\psi_U$ chosen linear on fibres. We verify every clause of the primary definition. The total space $E$ is a smooth manifold and $\pi : E \to B$ is a smooth surjection: both are hypotheses of "fibre bundle" (surjectivity is part of Definition 2.1.1). Each fibre $E_x$ carries a real vector-space structure of dimension $n$: given, and the dimension is $n$ because $\psi_U|_{E_x}$ is a linear isomorphism onto $\{x\} \times \mathbb{R}^n \cong \mathbb{R}^n$. The local-triviality clause of the primary definition is literally the local-triviality clause of "fibre bundle with fibre $\mathbb{R}^n$" together with the chosen linearity of $\psi_U$ on fibres: $\psi_U : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times \mathbb{R}^n$ is a diffeomorphism with $\operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \psi_U = \pi$ and $\psi_U|_{E_q}$ linear for each $q \in U$. **Hence** every clause of [[Def - Vector Bundle]] holds, and $(E, \pi, B)$ is a primary vector bundle.
>
> **Direction 2: a primary vector bundle is a fibre-bundle vector bundle.** Let $\pi : E \to B$ be a rank-$n$ vector bundle in the primary sense. We verify it is a fibre bundle with fibre $\mathbb{R}^n$ carrying linear structure and linear trivialisations. It is a fibre bundle with typical fibre $\mathbb{R}^n$: $E$ is a smooth manifold, $\pi$ is smooth, $\pi$ is surjective (each fibre is a nonempty vector space, containing at least $0_x$, so every $x$ is hit), and each $x$ has a neighbourhood $U$ with a diffeomorphism $\Phi : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times \mathbb{R}^n$ satisfying $\operatorname{pr}_1 \circ \Phi = \pi$ — this is exactly Definition 2.1.1 with $F = \mathbb{R}^n$. The extra data of the vector-bundle notion is present: each fibre $E_x$ is a real $n$-dimensional vector space (given), and the trivialisations $\Phi$ are linear on each fibre (given), so they may be chosen linear as required. **Hence** $(E, \pi, B)$ is a vector bundle in the fibre-bundle sense of Definition 2.1.7.
>
> **Conclusion.** The two definitions have the same hypotheses read in the two orders, so they define the same class of objects, and every result proved on [[Def - Vector Bundle]] — local frames, transition functions in $\mathrm{GL}(n, \mathbb{R})$, the operations of [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles]] — applies verbatim to the vector bundles of this chapter. $\blacksquare$

**Calibration check.** Three quick verifications, each doable from the page. *(i) Restriction to an open set.* For a fibre bundle $(E, \pi, B)$ with fibre $F$ and an open $U \subseteq B$, check that $E|_U = \pi^{-1}(U)$ with $\pi|_{\pi^{-1}(U)}$ is a fibre bundle over $U$ with the same typical fibre $F$: any trivialisation $\psi_V$ of $E$ over $V \subseteq B$ restricts to a trivialisation of $E|_U$ over $V \cap U$, and these cover $U$, so local triviality survives restriction. *(ii) A bundle over a point.* Verify that a fibre bundle over a one-point base $B = \{*\}$ is nothing but a manifold diffeomorphic to $F$: local triviality over $B = \{*\}$ gives a diffeomorphism $E = \pi^{-1}(*) \to \{*\} \times F \cong F$, so the whole theory collapses to the choice of the fibre, as it must. *(iii) Composition of projections, product case only.* We do **not** claim in general that if $E \xrightarrow{\pi} B$ is a bundle with fibre $F$ and $E' \xrightarrow{\rho} E$ is a bundle with fibre $F'$ then $\pi \circ \rho : E' \to B$ is a bundle — this needs extra hypotheses (for instance compact $F$). Check only the product case: for $E = B \times F$ and $E' = E \times F' = B \times F \times F'$ with the two coordinate projections, the composite $B \times F \times F' \to B$ is the fibre bundle with typical fibre $F \times F'$, trivial with global trivialisation the identity. If you can also see why the general statement can fail without compactness — the local products over $B$ and over $E$ need not assemble to a local product over $B$ when the intermediate fibre is non-compact — you have understood the role of local triviality.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Principal Bundle *(from Gauge Theory III)*
> A **[[Def - Principal G-Bundle|principal $G$-bundle]]** is a fibre bundle whose typical fibre is a Lie group $G$, equipped with a free right $G$-action that is transitive on the fibres and with $G$-equivariant local trivialisations. It is the fibre bundle that records "all the frames at once" for a structure group $G$, and connections on it are the geometric objects that Yang–Mills theory varies. Everything on this page — local triviality, the corollary that $\pi$ is a submersion, triviality by a global section — specialises to it.

> [!tip] Associated Bundle *(from Gauge Theory III)*
> Given a principal $G$-bundle $P$ and a left action of $G$ on a manifold $F$, the **[[Def - Associated Bundle|associated bundle]]** $P \times_G F$ is the fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$ obtained by gluing $P \times F$ with $G$ acting diagonally. Vector bundles arise this way from the frame bundle and a representation, which is why a fibre bundle is the right common genus in which to phrase the constructions.

> [!tip] Transition Functions and Classification *(from Gauge Theory III)*
> The trivialisations of this page, compared on overlaps, produce the **[[Def - Transition Functions and the Cocycle Condition|transition-function cocycle]]**, and the classification theorem shows that a bundle is recovered from its cocycle up to isomorphism by a coboundary. This turns the geometric question "is this bundle trivial?" into the algebraic question "is this cocycle a coboundary?", the door to characteristic classes.
