# Notation

This is a compound page: it defines two interlocking constructions of fibre bundles — the **unit sphere bundle** $S(E)$ of a Euclidean vector bundle $E$, and the **mapping torus** $E_\phi$ of a diffeomorphism $\phi$ of a fixed manifold $F$ — because they are the two standard families of concrete, often nontrivial, fibre bundles that a first encounter with the subject can actually build by hand: one produced from a bundle by throwing away all but the unit-length vectors in each fibre, the other produced from a single self-map by gluing the two ends of a cylinder with a twist. Both are examples of the notion defined on [[Def - Fibre Bundle]], and each is worth isolating because it is the ancestor of a construction used throughout the series (the sphere bundle is the geometric shadow of the orthonormal frame bundle, the mapping torus is the model for every bundle over the circle).

Throughout, all manifolds are smooth, Hausdorff, and second countable, and "smooth" means $C^\infty$; the standing conventions of the series apply.

A **[[Def - Fibre Bundle|fibre bundle]]** is a triple $(E,\pi,B)$ of smooth manifolds together with a surjective smooth map $\pi\colon E\to B$ such that every point of the base $B$ has an open neighbourhood $U\subset B$ over which the bundle looks like a product: there is a diffeomorphism $\psi_U\colon\pi^{-1}(U)\to U\times F$, called a **local trivialisation**, with $\operatorname{pr}_1\circ\psi_U=\pi$, where $F$ is a fixed manifold called the **typical fibre** and $\operatorname{pr}_1\colon U\times F\to U$ is the projection onto the first factor. The **fibre** over $x\in B$ is $E_x:=\pi^{-1}(x)$; the condition $\operatorname{pr}_1\circ\psi_U=\pi$ forces $\psi_U$ to restrict to a diffeomorphism $E_x\cong\{x\}\times F\cong F$, so every fibre is diffeomorphic to $F$. The bundle is **trivial** if a single local trivialisation can be taken over all of $B$, equivalently if it is isomorphic to the product bundle $(B\times F,\operatorname{pr}_1,B)$.

For the first construction, $E\to B$ is a real **[[Def - Euclidean Vector Bundle and Metric Connection|Euclidean vector bundle]]** of rank $r$: a rank-$r$ real [[Def - Vector Bundle|vector bundle]] $\pi\colon E\to B$ equipped with a Euclidean structure, that is, a smooth family $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle=\{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_x\}_{x\in B}$ of inner products, one $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_x$ on each fibre $E_x$, varying smoothly in the sense that $x\mapsto\langle s_1(x),s_2(x)\rangle_x$ is smooth for all smooth sections $s_1,s_2$. The associated fibre norm is $\lvert v\rvert:=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{\pi(v)}}$ for $v\in E$. A **local orthonormal frame** over $U\subset B$ is an $r$-tuple $e=(e_1,\dots,e_r)$ of smooth sections of $E$ over $U$ with $\langle e_i(x),e_j(x)\rangle_x=\delta_{ij}$ for all $x\in U$; such frames exist over a neighbourhood of every point by applying the [[Thm - Gram-Schmidt Procedure|Gram–Schmidt procedure]] to an arbitrary local frame. The round sphere is $S^{r-1}:=\{a=(a_1,\dots,a_r)\in\mathbb R^r:\sum_{i=1}^r a_i^2=1\}$.

For the second construction, $F$ is any fixed smooth manifold and $\phi\colon F\to F$ is a diffeomorphism; $\phi^k$ denotes the $k$-fold composite for $k\ge0$ (with $\phi^0=\operatorname{id}_F$) and the $\lvert k\rvert$-fold composite of $\phi^{-1}$ for $k<0$, so that $\phi^k\circ\phi^l=\phi^{k+l}$ for all $k,l\in\mathbb Z$. The integers $\mathbb Z$ form a **[[Def - Discrete Group and Properly Discontinuous Action|discrete group]]** under addition (a zero-dimensional Lie group). We write $\mathbb Z\backslash X$ for the orbit space of a left $\mathbb Z$-action on $X$, with quotient map sending a point to its orbit; the circle is realised as $S^1\cong\mathbb Z\backslash\mathbb R$, the orbit space of $\mathbb Z$ acting on $\mathbb R$ by translation $(k,t)\mapsto t+k$. The full symbol registry for the chapter is on [[Gauge Theory III — Fibre Bundles, Principal Bundles, and Associated Bundles]].

> [!warning] Convention: Bär's unit sphere bundle is stated for the tangent bundle
> Bär (Example 2.1.3.2) defines the unit sphere bundle only for the tangent bundle of a Riemannian manifold $(B,g)$, namely $\{X\in TB:\lVert X\rVert_g=1\}$, whose fibre is $S^{n-1}$ with $n=\dim B$. The series states the construction for an arbitrary Euclidean vector bundle $E$ of rank $r$, giving fibre $S^{r-1}$; Bär's case is recovered by taking $E=TB$ with the Riemannian metric as its Euclidean structure (then $r=n$). The verification below is identical in the two cases, because it uses only the fibrewise inner product, not any special feature of the tangent bundle.

# Axiom Motivation

The definition of a fibre bundle on [[Def - Fibre Bundle]] is at this point supported by exactly two families of examples: the trivial product bundle $B\times F$, which is a bundle for the uninteresting reason that it was assembled as one, and the vector bundles inherited from differential geometry (the tangent bundle and its tensor relatives), whose local triviality is a repackaging of the fact that a manifold is locally Euclidean. Neither family exhibits the phenomenon that makes bundle theory necessary in the first place: a space that is *locally* a product but *globally* not one. Until we have produced a bundle that is provably not trivial, the definition is a solution in search of a problem. The two constructions on this page exist to supply the missing examples cheaply, before the machinery of principal bundles, transition functions, and characteristic classes is available to detect nontriviality in general. The sphere bundle takes a bundle we already have and shaves each fibre down to a sphere, keeping the twisting; the mapping torus manufactures the simplest possible twisting from a single diffeomorphism.

**The unit sphere bundle: keeping the directions, forgetting the lengths.** Given a Euclidean vector bundle $E\to B$, a great deal of geometry concerns not the vectors themselves but their *directions* — the unit vectors. The unit tangent bundle of a Riemannian manifold, whose points are pairs (position, unit velocity), is the natural home for geodesic flow and for the study of the sphere at infinity; the unit normal bundle governs tubular neighbourhoods. The desideratum is to make "the unit vectors of $E$" into a fibre bundle in its own right, with the sphere $S^{r-1}$ as typical fibre, so that the whole apparatus of bundle theory applies to it. The one ingredient this needs, and the reason the construction is stated for a *Euclidean* vector bundle rather than a bare vector bundle, is a fibre metric: without an inner product on each fibre the phrase "unit vector" is meaningless, since a general vector space has no distinguished length function. If one drops the Euclidean structure and tries to take "$\lvert v\rvert=1$" in a bundle whose fibres are only vector spaces, there is no well-defined locus to take — the set carved out depends on an arbitrary choice of norm, and different local frames give incompatible answers, so no fibre bundle results. The metric is exactly what makes the unit locus canonical and its local description (via orthonormal frames) coherent across overlaps.

Two further clauses of the construction earn their place by the same test. First, the trivialisations of $S(E)$ are required to come from *orthonormal* local frames, not arbitrary ones: an arbitrary local frame $e$ trivialises $E$ as $U\times\mathbb R^r$, but sends the fibre unit sphere to the unit sphere of a possibly skew inner product, an ellipsoid in $\mathbb R^r$ coordinates, which is diffeomorphic to $S^{r-1}$ but not equal to it, so the model fibre would drift from chart to chart. Insisting the frame be orthonormal pins the model fibre to the standard round $S^{r-1}$ once and for all. Second, one must check that the unit locus is a submanifold at all; this is where the requirement $r\ge1$ hides (for $r=0$ the "fibre" is empty), and where the regular-value argument below is doing real work rather than bookkeeping.

**The mapping torus: the simplest nontrivial gluing.** For the second construction the guiding question is: what are the fibre bundles over the circle $S^1$? A bundle over an interval is always trivial (an interval is contractible, so there is nothing for the bundle to twist around), and $S^1$ is obtained from the interval $[0,1]$ by identifying its two endpoints. It is therefore natural to build a bundle over $S^1$ by taking the trivial bundle $[0,1]\times F$ and gluing the fibre over $1$ back onto the fibre over $0$ — but with a twist, prescribed by a diffeomorphism $\phi\colon F\to F$ that says how a point of the fibre over $1$ is to be matched with a point of the fibre over $0$. When $\phi$ is the identity the two ends are glued straight and the result is the product $S^1\times F$; when $\phi$ is some nontrivial symmetry of $F$ the ends are glued with a shear, and the result can fail to be a product. The construction packages this gluing into an action of $\mathbb Z$ on the infinite cylinder $\mathbb R\times F$, one integer step advancing along $\mathbb R$ and simultaneously applying $\phi$: the quotient $\mathbb Z\backslash(\mathbb R\times F)$ realises the gluing, and the projection to $\mathbb Z\backslash\mathbb R=S^1$ is the bundle map.

The single indispensable hypothesis is that $\phi$ be a **diffeomorphism**, and this is the clause whose failure the non-example below exhibits. Two things break at once if $\phi$ is merely a smooth map. First, at the level of the group action: the formula $(k,(t,f))\mapsto(t+k,\phi^k(f))$ requires $\phi^k$ for *negative* $k$, that is $\phi^{-1}$, so if $\phi$ is not invertible there is no $\mathbb Z$-action to quotient by — at best a monoid $\mathbb N$ acts, and monoid quotients are not manifolds. Second, even if one abandons the group-theoretic packaging and glues $[0,1]\times F$ to itself by an identification $(0,f)\sim(1,\phi(f))$ directly, a non-injective $\phi$ collapses distinct points of the fibre over $1$ onto a single point of the fibre over $0$, so the total space fails to be locally Euclidean along the seam and is not a manifold, let alone a bundle. The requirement that $\phi$ be a diffeomorphism is precisely what guarantees a free, properly discontinuous $\mathbb Z$-action, and hence (by the quotient theorem invoked below) a smooth manifold $E_\phi$ carrying the desired bundle. A reader who has absorbed why the metric is needed for $S(E)$ and why invertibility is needed for $E_\phi$ could reconstruct both definitions from the desiderata alone.

# The Definition

### The unit sphere bundle

Let $(E,\pi,B)$ be a **[[Def - Euclidean Vector Bundle and Metric Connection|Euclidean vector bundle]]** of rank $r\ge1$ over $B$, with fibre metric $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ and fibre norm $\lvert v\rvert=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle_{\pi(v)}}$. The **unit sphere bundle** of $E$ is
$$S(E):=\{v\in E:\lvert v\rvert=1\},\qquad \pi_S:=\pi\big|_{S(E)}\colon S(E)\to B,$$
the set of unit vectors in each fibre, with projection the restriction of $\pi$. It is a fibre bundle $(S(E),\pi_S,B)$ with typical fibre the round sphere $S^{r-1}$; the fibre over $x\in B$ is the unit sphere $S(E)_x=\{v\in E_x:\lvert v\rvert=1\}$ of the inner-product space $(E_x,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_x)$. When $E=TB$ carries a Riemannian metric $g$, the resulting bundle $S(TB)$ is the **unit tangent bundle** of $(B,g)$, with fibre $S^{n-1}$, $n=\dim B$. The proof that $S(E)$ is a fibre bundle, and the explicit trivialisations, are carried out in the Examples section below.

### The mapping torus

Let $F$ be a smooth manifold and $\phi\colon F\to F$ a diffeomorphism. Define a left action of the **[[Def - Discrete Group and Properly Discontinuous Action|discrete group]]** $\mathbb Z$ on the product manifold $\mathbb R\times F$ by
$$\Theta\colon\mathbb Z\times(\mathbb R\times F)\to\mathbb R\times F,\qquad k\cdot(t,f):=(t+k,\phi^k(f)).$$
The **mapping torus** of $\phi$ is the orbit space
$$E_\phi:=\mathbb Z\backslash(\mathbb R\times F),$$
with the smooth structure it inherits from the quotient (justified below). The projection $\operatorname{pr}_1\colon\mathbb R\times F\to\mathbb R$, $(t,f)\mapsto t$, is constant on $\mathbb Z$-orbits up to the corresponding translation, and descends to a smooth map
$$\pi\colon E_\phi\to\mathbb Z\backslash\mathbb R\cong S^1,\qquad \pi\big([t,f]\big):=[t],$$
where $[t,f]$ denotes the orbit of $(t,f)$ and $[t]$ the orbit of $t$. The triple $(E_\phi,\pi,S^1)$ is a fibre bundle with typical fibre $F$; that it is a bundle is the content of the next theorem, [[Thm - The Mapping Torus of a Diffeomorphism is a Fibre Bundle over the Circle]]. Geometrically, $E_\phi$ is obtained from the trivial bundle $[0,1]\times F\to[0,1]$ by gluing the fibre $\{1\}\times F$ to the fibre $\{0\}\times F$ via $(1,f)\mapsto(0,\phi(f))$.

### The Möbius strip

The **(open) Möbius strip** is the mapping torus $E_\phi$ of the reflection
$$F=(-1,1),\qquad \phi\colon(-1,1)\to(-1,1),\qquad \phi(x)=-x,$$
a fibre bundle over $S^1$ with fibre the open interval $(-1,1)$. It is the total space of the tautological nontrivial line bundle over the circle; its nontriviality is proved in [[Ex - The Möbius Strip as a Mapping Torus is a Nontrivial Bundle]].

### The two constructions are well-posed

The unit sphere bundle is well-defined once we exhibit $S(E)$ as an embedded submanifold of $E$ and the orthonormal-frame maps as trivialisations, which is done in the Examples section. The mapping torus requires that $E_\phi=\mathbb Z\backslash(\mathbb R\times F)$ carry a smooth structure at all; this is guaranteed by the following theorem from chapter I, whose hypothesis (proper discontinuity of the $\mathbb Z$-action $\Theta$) we verify in the Examples section.

> **Theorem (quotient by a properly discontinuous action; restated from [[Thm - Quotient by a Properly Discontinuous Action is a Manifold and a Covering Map]]).** If a [[Def - Discrete Group and Properly Discontinuous Action|discrete group]] $\Gamma$ acts smoothly and properly discontinuously on a manifold $M$, then the orbit space $\Gamma\backslash M$ carries a unique smooth manifold structure for which the quotient map $q\colon M\to\Gamma\backslash M$ is a smooth covering map, in particular a local diffeomorphism; and $q$ has the universal property that every smooth map $M\to N$ constant on $\Gamma$-orbits factors uniquely through a smooth map $\Gamma\backslash M\to N$.

Applied with $\Gamma=\mathbb Z$, $M=\mathbb R\times F$, and the action $\Theta$ (shown properly discontinuous below), this theorem endows $E_\phi$ with a smooth manifold structure and makes $\mathbb R\times F\to E_\phi$ a smooth covering map; the descended projection $\pi$ above is smooth because $\operatorname{pr}_1$ followed by the covering $\mathbb R\to\mathbb Z\backslash\mathbb R$ is constant on orbits and the universal property applies.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

Both constructions are instances of the **associated-bundle** functor, and seeing them this way explains why each is automatically a fibre bundle and locates them in the machinery of the chapter; the associated bundle itself is defined on [[Def - Associated Bundle]] and here we only record the structural reading.

The unit sphere bundle is the bundle **associated to the orthonormal frame bundle**. A Euclidean vector bundle $E$ of rank $r$ has an orthonormal frame bundle $O(E)$ (defined on [[Def - Frame Bundle of a Vector Bundle]]), a principal $O(r)$-bundle whose fibre over $x$ is the set of orthonormal bases of $(E_x,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_x)$. The group $O(r)$ acts on the standard sphere $S^{r-1}\subset\mathbb R^r$ by its defining orthogonal action, and
$$S(E)\;\cong\;O(E)\times_{O(r)}S^{r-1},$$
the identification sending the orbit $[e,a]$ of an orthonormal frame $e=(e_1,\dots,e_r)$ and a point $a\in S^{r-1}$ to the unit vector $\sum_i a_i e_i\in S(E)$. This exhibits $S(E)$ as a fibre bundle with fibre $S^{r-1}$ and structure group $O(r)$ for free, and is the true reason the trivialisations must come from orthonormal frames: they are the local sections of $O(E)$.

The mapping torus is the bundle **associated to the universal covering, viewed as a principal $\mathbb Z$-bundle**. The covering map $\mathbb R\to\mathbb Z\backslash\mathbb R=S^1$ is a principal $\mathbb Z$-bundle (the deck group $\mathbb Z$ acts freely and properly discontinuously by translation, by [[Thm - Quotient by a Properly Discontinuous Action is a Manifold and a Covering Map]]), and the diffeomorphism $\phi$ is exactly a homomorphism $\mathbb Z\to\operatorname{Diff}(F)$, $k\mapsto\phi^k$, that is, an action of $\mathbb Z$ on the fibre $F$. The mapping torus is the associated fibre bundle
$$E_\phi\;=\;\mathbb R\times_{\mathbb Z}F,$$
the quotient of $\mathbb R\times F$ by the diagonal $\mathbb Z$-action combining deck transformations on $\mathbb R$ with $\phi$ on $F$. In this reading the "monodromy" of a bundle over $S^1$ is precisely the deck-group action on the fibre, and the classification of such bundles by isotopy classes of $\phi$ (foreshadowed in the next theorem) is the associated-bundle classification specialised to the circle.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name of the sphere bundle: sphericalisation of a metric bundle.** Operationally, $S(E)$ is what remains of $E$ after one imposes scale invariance fibrewise — it is the *projectivisation for spheres*, remembering a vector's direction and orientation but not its length. The map $E\setminus\{\text{zero section}\}\to S(E)$, $v\mapsto v/\lvert v\rvert$, is a deformation retraction, so $S(E)$ carries all the homotopy-theoretic twisting of $E$ minus the trivial radial direction; this is why sphere bundles are the natural setting for Euler classes and Gysin sequences, where the top cohomology of the fibre $S^{r-1}$ is exactly the obstruction data. In physics the unit tangent bundle $S(TB)$ is the phase space of a free particle constrained to move on $B$ at unit speed, and its geodesic flow is the Hamiltonian flow of the kinetic energy restricted to that energy level.

**True name of the mapping torus: the suspension of a dynamical system.** In dynamics $E_\phi$ is the **suspension flow** (or "mapping torus") of the diffeomorphism $\phi$: the flow on $E_\phi$ translating the $\mathbb R$-coordinate at unit speed has first-return map to the fibre $F$ over $[0]\in S^1$ equal to $\phi$ itself. This is the standard device converting a discrete-time system (iterate $\phi$) into a continuous-time system (flow on $E_\phi$) with the same qualitative behaviour, and conversely every flow with a global cross-section is the suspension of its return map. In low-dimensional topology, mapping tori of surface diffeomorphisms are exactly the three-manifolds that fibre over the circle, and Thurston's classification of surface diffeomorphisms is read off from the geometry of the corresponding mapping torus. Two mapping tori $E_\phi$ and $E_\psi$ are isomorphic as bundles when $\phi$ and $\psi$ are conjugate by a diffeomorphism, and trivial exactly when $\phi$ is isotopic to the identity — the precise link between the topology of the total space and the isotopy class of the gluing map, made rigorous in the next theorem.

Both constructions are the first appearance in the series of the principle that a fibre bundle is *locally trivial data glued by a symmetry*: for $S(E)$ the symmetry is the orthogonal group $O(r)$ acting on the model fibre, for $E_\phi$ it is the single diffeomorphism $\phi$ generating a $\mathbb Z$ of monodromy. Everything in §3.3 (transition functions and cocycles) is the systematic development of exactly this observation.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Verification that $S(E)$ is a fibre bundle with fibre $S^{r-1}$.** Let $E\to B$ be Euclidean of rank $r\ge1$. We check three things clause by clause: that $S(E)$ is an embedded submanifold of $E$, that its fibres are diffeomorphic to $S^{r-1}$, and that orthonormal frames give local trivialisations.

*Step 1 — $S(E)$ is an embedded submanifold.* Consider the smooth function $N\colon E\to\mathbb R$, $N(v):=\lvert v\rvert^2=\langle v,v\rangle_{\pi(v)}$; it is smooth because in any local trivialisation the fibre metric is a smooth field of symmetric matrices (by the smoothness clause of the [[Def - Euclidean Vector Bundle and Metric Connection|Euclidean structure]]) and $N$ is a smooth quadratic function of the fibre coordinates. Then $S(E)=N^{-1}(1)$. We claim $1$ is a regular value of $N$ (in the sense of [[Def - Regular and Critical Points|regular values]]). Fix $v\in N^{-1}(1)$; since $v\ne0$, restrict $N$ to the single fibre $E_{\pi(v)}$, on which $N(w)=\langle w,w\rangle$ has differential at $v$ given by $w\mapsto2\langle v,w\rangle$ (by the symmetry and bilinearity of the inner product). Taking $w=v$ gives $2\langle v,v\rangle=2\ne0$ (since $\lvert v\rvert=1$), so $dN_v$ is already surjective onto $\mathbb R$ along the fibre directions, hence surjective on all of $T_vE$. Therefore $dN_v\ne0$ at every $v\in N^{-1}(1)$, so $1$ is a regular value, and by the [[Thm - Regular Value Theorem on Manifolds|regular value theorem]] — the preimage of a regular value under a smooth map is an [[Def - Embedded Submanifold|embedded submanifold]] whose codimension equals the dimension of the target — $S(E)=N^{-1}(1)$ is an embedded submanifold of $E$ of codimension $1$, hence of dimension $\dim E-1=(\dim B+r)-1=\dim B+(r-1)$.

*Step 2 — the fibres are round spheres.* For $x\in B$, the fibre of $\pi_S$ is $S(E)_x=\{v\in E_x:\langle v,v\rangle_x=1\}$, the unit sphere of the $r$-dimensional inner-product space $(E_x,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_x)$. Choosing any orthonormal basis identifies $(E_x,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_x)$ isometrically with $(\mathbb R^r,\text{standard})$, carrying $S(E)_x$ diffeomorphically onto $S^{r-1}=\{a\in\mathbb R^r:\sum a_i^2=1\}$. So each fibre is diffeomorphic to the typical fibre $S^{r-1}$, as a fibre bundle requires.

*Step 3 — orthonormal frames trivialise.* Let $U\subset B$ be an open set carrying a local orthonormal frame $e=(e_1,\dots,e_r)$ of $E$ (such $U$ cover $B$, by [[Thm - Gram-Schmidt Procedure|Gram–Schmidt]] applied to any local frame). Every $v\in\pi^{-1}(x)$ with $x\in U$ is uniquely $v=\sum_{i=1}^r a_i(v)\,e_i(x)$ with smooth coordinate functions $a_i$, and by orthonormality $\lvert v\rvert^2=\sum_i a_i(v)^2$. Define
$$\psi_U\colon\pi_S^{-1}(U)\to U\times S^{r-1},\qquad \psi_U(v):=\big(\pi(v),(a_1(v),\dots,a_r(v))\big).$$
This is well-defined into $U\times S^{r-1}$: for $v\in S(E)$ we have $\sum_i a_i(v)^2=\lvert v\rvert^2=1$, so the coordinate vector lies on $S^{r-1}$. It satisfies $\operatorname{pr}_1\circ\psi_U=\pi_S$ by construction. It is the restriction to the submanifold $S(E)$ of the vector-bundle trivialisation $\Psi_U\colon\pi^{-1}(U)\to U\times\mathbb R^r$, $v\mapsto(\pi(v),(a_i(v)))$ associated to the frame $e$; $\Psi_U$ is a diffeomorphism, and it carries $\pi_S^{-1}(U)=\pi^{-1}(U)\cap S(E)$ diffeomorphically onto $\Psi_U(S(E)\cap\pi^{-1}(U))=U\times S^{r-1}$ (because $\Psi_U$ is fibrewise the isometry sending $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ to the standard inner product, so it sends the unit-norm locus to the standard sphere). A diffeomorphism restricted to an embedded submanifold and its image is a diffeomorphism onto that image, so $\psi_U$ is a diffeomorphism over $U$. Thus the $\psi_U$ are local trivialisations, and $(S(E),\pi_S,B)$ is a fibre bundle with typical fibre $S^{r-1}$. $\blacksquare$

**Instance — the unit tangent bundle of $S^2$.** Take $B=S^2$ with its round metric, $E=TS^2$, so $r=2$ and the fibre is $S^{1}$. The unit tangent bundle $S(TS^2)=\{(x,v):x\in S^2,\;v\in T_xS^2,\;\lvert v\rvert=1\}$ is a circle bundle over $S^2$; it is diffeomorphic to the rotation group $SO(3)$ (equivalently to real projective space $\mathbb{RP}^3$), a nontrivial circle bundle whose identification with $SO(3)$ is worked out in [[Ex - The Unit Tangent Bundle of S^2 is Diffeomorphic to SO(3)]]. This is the promised example of a fibre bundle that is genuinely not a product: $S(TS^2)\cong SO(3)$ is not diffeomorphic to $S^2\times S^1$.

**Verification that the $\mathbb Z$-action $\Theta$ is properly discontinuous.** Recall that a smooth action of a discrete group $\Gamma$ on a manifold $M$ is **[[Def - Discrete Group and Properly Discontinuous Action|properly discontinuous]]** if (i) every $p\in M$ has a neighbourhood $U$ with $g\cdot U\cap U\ne\varnothing\Rightarrow g=e$, and (ii) any two points $p,q$ in distinct orbits have neighbourhoods $U\ni p$, $V\ni q$ with $g\cdot U\cap V=\varnothing$ for all $g\in\Gamma$. We verify both for $\Gamma=\mathbb Z$, $M=\mathbb R\times F$, and $\Theta(k,(t,f))=(t+k,\phi^k(f))$. First, $\Theta$ is a smooth action: it is smooth because $\phi$ is a diffeomorphism (so each $\phi^k$ is smooth), and it is an action because $\phi^k\circ\phi^l=\phi^{k+l}$ gives $k\cdot(l\cdot(t,f))=(t+k+l,\phi^{k+l}(f))=(k+l)\cdot(t,f)$ and $0\cdot(t,f)=(t,f)$.

> [!note]- Full verification of properly discontinuous (both clauses)
> **Clause (i): local rigidity.** Fix $p=(t_0,f_0)\in\mathbb R\times F$ and set $U:=(t_0-\tfrac12,t_0+\tfrac12)\times F$, an open neighbourhood of $p$. For $k\in\mathbb Z$,
> $$k\cdot U=(t_0-\tfrac12+k,\;t_0+\tfrac12+k)\times\phi^k(F)\qquad(\text{by the definition of }\Theta\text{ and }\phi^k(F)=F).$$
> Suppose $k\cdot U\cap U\ne\varnothing$. Projecting to the first factor, the intervals $(t_0-\tfrac12+k,t_0+\tfrac12+k)$ and $(t_0-\tfrac12,t_0+\tfrac12)$ must meet; two open intervals of length $1$ with centres $t_0+k$ and $t_0$ meet only if $\lvert k\rvert<1$ (since their centres are at distance $\lvert k\rvert$ and each has radius $\tfrac12$, they are disjoint once $\lvert k\rvert\ge1$). As $k\in\mathbb Z$, this forces $k=0=e$. Hence $k\cdot U\cap U\ne\varnothing\Rightarrow k=e$, which is clause (i).
>
> **Clause (ii): separation of distinct orbits.** Let $p=(t_1,f_1)$ and $q=(t_2,f_2)$ lie in distinct $\mathbb Z$-orbits. Their orbits are $\{(t_1+k,\phi^k(f_1)):k\in\mathbb Z\}$ and $\{(t_2+k,\phi^k(f_2)):k\in\mathbb Z\}$. We split on whether $t_2-t_1$ is an integer, exhausting all cases.
>
> *Case A: $t_2-t_1\notin\mathbb Z$.* Put $\varepsilon:=\min_{k\in\mathbb Z}\lvert t_2-(t_1+k)\rvert$; the minimum is attained and $\varepsilon>0$ because $t_2-t_1\notin\mathbb Z$ (the distance from $t_2$ to the lattice $t_1+\mathbb Z$ is positive). Set $U:=(t_1-\tfrac\varepsilon2,t_1+\tfrac\varepsilon2)\times F$ and $V:=(t_2-\tfrac\varepsilon2,t_2+\tfrac\varepsilon2)\times F$. For any $k\in\mathbb Z$, the first factor of $k\cdot U$ is the open interval of radius $\tfrac\varepsilon2$ about $t_1+k$, and the first factor of $V$ is the open interval of radius $\tfrac\varepsilon2$ about $t_2$; two open intervals of radius $\tfrac\varepsilon2$ whose centres are at distance $\lvert t_2-(t_1+k)\rvert\ge\varepsilon$ (by the definition of $\varepsilon$ as the minimum) are disjoint, since a point in both would lie within $\tfrac\varepsilon2$ of each centre and so put the centres within $\varepsilon$ of each other, contradicting the distance bound. Hence $k\cdot U\cap V=\varnothing$ for all $k$, giving clause (ii).
>
> *Case B: $t_2-t_1=m\in\mathbb Z$ but $q\notin\mathbb Z\cdot p$.* Being in distinct orbits with $t_2=t_1+m$ forces $f_2\ne\phi^m(f_1)$ (otherwise $q=m\cdot p$). Choose the first factors small: let $I_1:=(t_1-\tfrac14,t_1+\tfrac14)$ and $I_2:=(t_2-\tfrac14,t_2+\tfrac14)$. For $k\in\mathbb Z$, the first factor of $k\cdot U$ is $I_1+k$, centred at $t_1+k$; it meets $I_2$ (centred at $t_1+m$) only when $\lvert (t_1+k)-(t_1+m)\rvert=\lvert k-m\rvert<\tfrac12$, i.e. only for $k=m$. So for every $k\ne m$ the first factors are already disjoint and $k\cdot U\cap V=\varnothing$ regardless of the fibre factor. It remains to kill $k=m$. Since $F$ is Hausdorff and $\phi^m(f_1)\ne f_2$, pick disjoint open sets $B\ni\phi^m(f_1)$ and $A_2\ni f_2$ in $F$; set $A_1:=\phi^{-m}(B)$, an open neighbourhood of $f_1$ (as $\phi^{-m}$ is a diffeomorphism and $\phi^{-m}(\phi^m(f_1))=f_1$). Now define $U:=I_1\times A_1$ and $V:=I_2\times A_2$. For $k=m$, the fibre factor of $m\cdot U$ is $\phi^m(A_1)=\phi^m(\phi^{-m}(B))=B$, which is disjoint from $A_2$, so $m\cdot U\cap V=\varnothing$; and we saw $k\cdot U\cap V=\varnothing$ for $k\ne m$. Hence clause (ii) holds in this case too.
>
> The two cases are exhaustive, so $\Theta$ is properly discontinuous. $\blacksquare$

Because $\Theta$ is smooth and properly discontinuous, the restated quotient theorem [[Thm - Quotient by a Properly Discontinuous Action is a Manifold and a Covering Map]] applies and endows $E_\phi=\mathbb Z\backslash(\mathbb R\times F)$ with a unique smooth manifold structure making $\mathbb R\times F\to E_\phi$ a smooth covering map. This is exactly the input the definition needed; that $(E_\phi,\pi,S^1)$ is moreover a fibre bundle with fibre $F$ is proved in [[Thm - The Mapping Torus of a Diffeomorphism is a Fibre Bundle over the Circle]].

**Instance — the Möbius strip.** Take $F=(-1,1)$ and $\phi(x)=-x$, a diffeomorphism with $\phi^2=\operatorname{id}$. The verification above applies verbatim ($(-1,1)$ is a Hausdorff manifold and $\phi$ a diffeomorphism), so $E_\phi$ is a smooth manifold and a fibre bundle over $S^1$ with fibre $(-1,1)$. Concretely $E_\phi=[0,1]\times(-1,1)$ with $(1,x)$ glued to $(0,-x)$: travelling once around the base flips the fibre interval, which is the defining twist of the Möbius band. Its nontriviality — that $E_\phi\not\cong S^1\times(-1,1)$ — is the subject of [[Ex - The Möbius Strip as a Mapping Torus is a Nontrivial Bundle]], where it is deduced from the impossibility of a nowhere-vanishing section, matching the Möbius line bundle of [[Ex - The Möbius Bundle is Nontrivial]].

**Is NOT an instance — the mapping "torus" of a non-injective self-map.** Let $F=S^1$ and let $\phi\colon S^1\to S^1$ be the squaring map $z\mapsto z^2$ (writing $S^1\subset\mathbb C$); it is smooth and surjective but two-to-one, hence not a diffeomorphism. Then there is *no* $\mathbb Z$-action to quotient, because $\Theta$ demands $\phi^{-1}$, which does not exist. If one nonetheless forms the naive gluing $M_\phi:=[0,1]\times S^1/\!\sim$ with $(1,z)\sim(0,z^2)$, the projection $M_\phi\to S^1$ is not a fibre bundle: it is not even a manifold along the seam. Concretely, the point $q$ that is the class of $(0,1)$ equals the classes of both $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$, since $1^2=(-1)^2=1$. A neighbourhood of $q$ in $M_\phi$ is therefore the image of neighbourhoods of the three points $(0,1),(1,1),(1,-1)$ in $[0,1]\times S^1$: one half-collar approaching the seam from the $t\to0^+$ side and *two* half-collars approaching from the $t\to1^-$ side (near $z=1$ and near $z=-1$), all glued along a single boundary arc by the two-to-one map $z\mapsto z^2$. Three half-planes meeting along a common edge is not locally homeomorphic to $\mathbb R^{2}$, so $M_\phi$ fails to be a manifold at $q$; since the total space of a fibre bundle with manifold base and manifold fibre is a manifold, $M_\phi\to S^1$ is not a fibre bundle. The hypothesis that $\phi$ be a diffeomorphism is exactly what prevents this collapse.

**Calibration check.** Three quick verifications the reader can carry out from the page. *First,* $E_{\operatorname{id}}=S^1\times F$: when $\phi=\operatorname{id}_F$ the action is $k\cdot(t,f)=(t+k,f)$, so orbits are $(\mathbb Z\text{-orbit of }t)\times\{f\}$ and the map $\mathbb R\times F\to S^1\times F$, $(t,f)\mapsto([t],f)$ is constant on orbits, smooth, and descends by the universal property to a smooth bijection $E_{\operatorname{id}}\to S^1\times F$ with smooth inverse $([t],f)\mapsto[t,f]$; it commutes with the projections, so $E_{\operatorname{id}}\cong S^1\times F$ as bundles, the trivial bundle. *Second,* the mapping torus of a rotation of the circle is the two-torus: take $F=S^1=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ and $\phi(f)=f+\alpha$ a rotation, so $\phi^k(f)=f+k\alpha$; the diffeomorphism $\mathbb R\times S^1\to\mathbb R\times S^1$, $(t,f)\mapsto(t,f-t\alpha)$ conjugates the action $k\cdot(t,f)=(t+k,f+k\alpha)$ into the action $(t,y)\mapsto(t+k,y)$, whose quotient is $S^1\times S^1$; hence $E_\phi\cong S^1\times S^1=T^2$ (this is the "$\phi$ isotopic to the identity $\Rightarrow$ trivial" phenomenon, since a rotation is isotopic to the identity through rotations). *Third,* the unit sphere bundle of the trivial Euclidean bundle $B\times\mathbb R^r$ is the product $B\times S^{r-1}$: the constant frame is orthonormal and its trivialisation $\psi_B$ is defined over all of $B$, so $S(B\times\mathbb R^r)=B\times S^{r-1}$ globally.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The mapping torus is a fibre bundle over the circle *(from §3.1)*
> The construction here supplies the manifold $E_\phi$; that $(E_\phi,\pi,S^1)$ is a fibre bundle with fibre $F$, and is trivial precisely when $\phi$ is isotopic to the identity, is [[Thm - The Mapping Torus of a Diffeomorphism is a Fibre Bundle over the Circle]]. Applied to the reflection of an interval it produces the Möbius strip, and applied to a reflection of the circle it produces the **Klein bottle** — see [[Ex - The Klein Bottle as a Mapping Torus]].

> [!tip] Sphere bundles and characteristic classes *(from Gauge Theory VI)*
> The unit sphere bundle $S(E)$ is the geometric object whose obstruction to having a section is measured by the **Euler class** of the oriented Euclidean bundle $E$; the Gysin sequence of $S(E)\to B$ relates the cohomology of base and total space through the top class of the fibre $S^{r-1}$, and for $E=TB$ the Euler class evaluated on the fundamental class is the Euler characteristic $\chi(B)$.

> [!tip] Suspension of a dynamical system *(from dynamical systems)*
> Reading $E_\phi$ as the **suspension flow** of $\phi$ turns every theorem about the topology of bundles over $S^1$ into a theorem about first-return maps, and vice versa; this is the bridge by which the isotopy classification of the gluing diffeomorphism becomes the classification of flows admitting a global cross-section.
