# Notation

Throughout, $\pi\colon P \to M$ is a [[Def - Principal G-Bundle|principal $G$-bundle]]: $P$ is the total space, $M$ the base, $G$ a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] with identity $e$ and Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g} = T_eG$, and $G$ acts on $P$ on the **right**, freely and transitively on each fibre $P_m := \pi^{-1}(m)$. We write $R_g\colon P \to P$, $R_g(p) := p \cdot g$ for the action of $g \in G$, so that $(p \cdot g) \cdot h = p \cdot (gh)$ and $p \cdot e = p$; the projection is $G$-invariant, $\pi \circ R_g = \pi$. For a smooth map $\phi$ between manifolds we write $d\phi_p$ (equivalently $\phi_{*,p}$, or $\pi_*$ when $\phi = \pi$) for its differential at $p$.

For $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$ the **fundamental vector field** $\xi_P$ on $P$ is
$$\xi_P(p) \,=\, \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}\, p \cdot \exp(t\xi),$$
the velocity at $p$ of the orbit curve $t \mapsto p \cdot \exp(t\xi)$ of the one-parameter subgroup generated by $\xi$ (see [[Def - Fundamental Vector Field of a Group Action]]). The **vertical subspace** at $p$ is $V_p := \ker d\pi_p \subseteq T_pP$, the tangent directions along the fibre; we prove in [The Definition](#The%20Definition) that $V_p = \{\xi_P(p) : \xi \in \mathfrak{g}\}$ and that $\xi \mapsto \xi_P(p)$ is a linear isomorphism $\mathfrak{g} \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ } V_p$.

A **representation** of $G$ on a finite-dimensional real or complex vector space $V$ is a smooth group homomorphism $\rho\colon G \to GL(V)$ (see [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group]]); $GL(V)$ is the group of invertible linear self-maps of $V$. A **$V$-valued $q$-form** on $P$ is a smooth section $\omega \in \Omega^q(P; V) = \Gamma(\Lambda^q T^*P \otimes V)$; concretely $\omega$ assigns to $p \in P$ an alternating $q$-linear map $\omega_p\colon (T_pP)^q \to V$ depending smoothly on $p$ (see [[Def - Bundle-Valued Differential Forms]]). For a form $\omega \in \Omega^q(P; V)$ and $g \in G$, the **pullback** $R_g^*\omega \in \Omega^q(P; V)$ is defined by
$$(R_g^*\omega)_p(v_1, \dots, v_q) \,=\, \omega_{p \cdot g}\big(d(R_g)_p v_1, \dots, d(R_g)_p v_q\big), \qquad v_1, \dots, v_q \in T_pP,$$
the ordinary pullback of a differential form applied componentwise in the fixed target $V$ (see [[Def - Pullback of a Differential Form on a Manifold]]); note that $\rho(g^{-1})$, being a fixed linear map of $V$, may be applied to a $V$-valued form pointwise, $(\rho(g^{-1})\omega)_p := \rho(g^{-1}) \circ \omega_p$. We write $\operatorname{Ad}\colon G \to GL(\mathfrak{g})$, $\operatorname{Ad}_g = d_e(h \mapsto ghg^{-1})$ for the adjoint representation; for a matrix group $\operatorname{Ad}_g \xi = g\xi g^{-1}$.

This is a compound page: it defines three interlocking notions — the vertical subspace of a principal bundle, an **equivariant** $V$-valued form, and a **basic** $V$-valued form — because the space that matters for gauge theory, $\Omega^q_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; V)^G$, is exactly the forms that are both, and neither half is meaningful without the vertical subspace to test against.

> [!warning] Convention: names for the fundamental field and for equivariance
> Haydys writes $K_\xi$ for the fundamental vector field of $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$ and Bär writes $\bar X$ (or $\widetilde X$) for the fundamental field of $X \in \mathfrak{g}$; both are the object the series calls $\xi_P$, with the identical formula $\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{0}\, p \cdot \exp(t\xi)$ under the series' right-action convention. The equivariance condition $R_g^*\omega = \rho(g^{-1})\,\omega$ is Haydys' Definition 38. Bär states the special case relevant to a connection form (a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form with $R_g^* = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} \circ$) and reads the vertical picture off the figure of Remark 2.3.2; we adopt Haydys' general representation $\rho$ so that the definition covers connections ($V = \mathfrak{g}$, $\rho = \operatorname{Ad}$), curvature, and associated-bundle-valued forms uniformly. Where Haydys writes $\Omega^q_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; V)^G$ we keep his notation verbatim.

# Axiom Motivation

The point of a principal bundle is to compute on the total space $P$, where the group symmetry is visible and the connection lives, and then to translate the answer back down to the base $M$, where geometry actually happens. The two definitions on this page — equivariant and basic — are the exact conditions that make this translation possible and reversible. A $V$-valued form on $P$ that is both equivariant and basic is the same data as a form on $M$ with values in the associated bundle $P \times_\rho V$; this is the content of the theorem [[Thm - Bundle-Valued Forms on the Base are Basic Equivariant Forms on the Total Space|Bundle-Valued Forms on the Base are Basic Equivariant Forms on the Total Space]] (Haydys' Proposition 39), and everything on this page is the vocabulary that theorem needs. So the design question is: **which forms on $P$ descend to the base, and which record base-with-coefficients data faithfully?** The answer has two clauses, one for each of the two things that can go wrong.

The first thing that can go wrong is that a form on $P$ may see the fibre directions. A form on $M$, pulled up to $P$, is blind to any motion along a fibre, because $\pi$ collapses the fibre to a point: if $v \in T_pP$ is vertical, meaning $d\pi_p(v) = 0$, then $(\pi^*\alpha)_p(\dots, v, \dots) = \alpha_{\pi(p)}(\dots, d\pi_p(v), \dots) = 0$. A form that does *not* vanish on vertical vectors is carrying information transverse to the base — it is measuring the group directions, not the base directions — and such a form cannot be the pullback of anything on $M$. The **basic** condition is precisely the demand "vanish whenever one argument is vertical," and it is the exact obstruction to being horizontal-along-the-fibre. Dropping it lets in forms like the Maurer–Cartan-type form that reads off the $\mathfrak{g}$-component of a vertical vector (verified in [Examples](#Examples%20%2F%20Corollaries) below); that form is genuinely a form on the *group*, not on the base, and no amount of the second condition will make it descend.

The second thing that can go wrong is that even a fibre-blind form may fail to be consistently defined across the fibre. To specify a form on $M$ at $m$ we may pick any $p \in P_m$ and read the form there; for this to be well-defined the readings at $p$ and at $p \cdot g$ must agree after we account for how the coefficients in $V$ are meant to transform. If the coefficients are inert (the trivial representation, $\rho \equiv \operatorname{id}$) the readings must literally agree, $R_g^*\omega = \omega$ — this is **invariance**. If the coefficients themselves rotate under the group, as they do for $V = \mathfrak{g}$ with $\rho = \operatorname{Ad}$ (so that the value lands in the associated bundle $P \times_\rho V$, whose points are equivalence classes $[p, v] = [p \cdot g, \rho(g^{-1})v]$), then the reading at $p \cdot g$ must be the reading at $p$ turned by $\rho(g^{-1})$. This is **equivariance**, $R_g^*\omega = \rho(g^{-1})\,\omega$, and it is exactly the compatibility that makes the class $[p, \omega_p(\dots)] \in P \times_\rho V$ independent of the chosen $p$: replacing $p$ by $p \cdot g$ multiplies the representative $\omega_p(\dots)$ by $\rho(g^{-1})$ and simultaneously shifts the basepoint by $g$, and these two changes cancel in the associated bundle by its defining relation.

Now the per-clause failure analysis. **Drop basic.** Then a form need not descend at all: on the trivial bundle $P = M \times G$ the left Maurer–Cartan form $\theta \in \Omega^1(G; \mathfrak{g})$ pulled back to $P$ is $\operatorname{Ad}$-equivariant (a standard identity, $R_g^*\theta = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\theta$, verified below) but has $\theta(\xi_P) = \xi \neq 0$, so it fails to vanish on the vertical field $\xi_P$; it is a perfectly good equivariant form that corresponds to no form on $M$, precisely because it carries fibre data. Without the basic clause the correspondence with $\Omega^q(M; P \times_\rho V)$ breaks: the map "restrict to the base" is not even defined on the vertical part. **Drop equivariance.** Then a form may be basic yet ambiguous down on $M$. Take a $V$-valued $0$-form, that is a function $f\colon P \to V$; a $0$-form is automatically basic (it has no vector arguments to test). If $f$ is not equivariant — for the trivial representation, if $f$ is not $G$-invariant — then $f(p)$ and $f(p \cdot g)$ disagree, and "$f$ at the point $m = \pi(p)$" has no single value; the descended object does not exist. The concrete witness is $df$ for a non-invariant $f$, worked out in the non-example below. **Weaken equivariance to a different representation.** The representation $\rho$ is part of the data: a form equivariant of type $\rho$ descends to $P \times_\rho V$, and one equivariant of type $\rho'$ descends to $P \times_{\rho'} V$, a genuinely different bundle. Using the wrong $\rho$ produces a form that satisfies *some* transformation law but not the one matching the target bundle, and it descends to the wrong place; there is no strengthening that repairs a representation mismatch, only the correct choice. The lesson is that the two clauses are independent and jointly necessary: basic controls the *vertical* behaviour, equivariance controls the *cross-fibre* behaviour, and only their conjunction is the pullback of base-with-coefficients data.

Could a reader invent the definition from this alone? The desiderata are: capture exactly the $V$-valued forms on $P$ that are pullbacks-with-coefficients of forms on $M$ with values in $P \times_\rho V$. Vanishing on vertical vectors (basic) is forced by the pullback's blindness to fibre motion; the transformation law $R_g^*\omega = \rho(g^{-1})\omega$ (equivariance) is forced by the associated bundle's identification $[p, v] = [p \cdot g, \rho(g^{-1})v]$. The definition is the conjunction of these two, and the space $\Omega^q_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; V)^G$ is its name.

# The Definition

We first record the description of the vertical subspace, since the notion "basic" is defined by testing against it. Recall that $G$ acts on $P$ on the right, freely and fibrewise-transitively, and that $\xi_P(p) = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_{0}\, p \cdot \exp(t\xi)$ for $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$.

**The vertical subspace as fundamental fields.** For every $p \in P$,
$$V_p \,:=\, \ker d\pi_p \,=\, \{\xi_P(p) : \xi \in \mathfrak{g}\}, \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathfrak{g} \xrightarrow{\ \xi \,\mapsto\, \xi_P(p)\ } V_p \ \text{ is a linear isomorphism.}$$

> [!note]- Verification of the vertical-subspace description (Haydys, unlabelled statement p. 15)
> We must show three things: (a) each $\xi_P(p)$ lies in $\ker d\pi_p$; (b) $\xi \mapsto \xi_P(p)$ is linear and injective; (c) its image is all of $\ker d\pi_p$. Fix $p \in P$ and set $m := \pi(p)$, $n := \dim M$, $k := \dim G = \dim \mathfrak{g}$; then $\dim P = n + k$ because $P$ is locally $U \times G$ over a chart $U \subseteq M$.
>
> **(a) Fundamental fields are vertical.** By the $G$-invariance of the projection, $\pi(p \cdot \exp(t\xi)) = \pi(p) = m$ for all $t$, so the orbit curve $t \mapsto p \cdot \exp(t\xi)$ is constant after applying $\pi$. Differentiating at $t = 0$,
> $$d\pi_p(\xi_P(p)) \,=\, \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}\, \pi\big(p \cdot \exp(t\xi)\big) \,=\, \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}\, m \,=\, 0 \qquad \text{(chain rule; then } \pi \circ R_{\exp(t\xi)} = \pi\text{).}$$
> Hence $\xi_P(p) \in \ker d\pi_p = V_p$, so $\{\xi_P(p) : \xi \in \mathfrak{g}\} \subseteq V_p$.
>
> **(b) The map $\xi \mapsto \xi_P(p)$ is linear and injective.** Linearity is the statement that $\xi_P(p)$ is the image of $\xi$ under the differential $d(\ell_p)_e$ of the orbit map $\ell_p\colon G \to P$, $\ell_p(g) := p \cdot g$, evaluated on the tangent vector $\xi \in T_eG = \mathfrak{g}$. Indeed $\frac{d}{dt}\big|_0 \exp(t\xi) = \xi$ (the defining property of the Lie-group exponential, [[Def - Exponential Map of a Lie Group|exponential map]]), so by the chain rule
> $$\xi_P(p) \,=\, \frac{d}{dt}\Big|_{0}\, \ell_p(\exp(t\xi)) \,=\, d(\ell_p)_e\Big(\tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_0 \exp(t\xi)\Big) \,=\, d(\ell_p)_e(\xi) \qquad \text{(chain rule; } \tfrac{d}{dt}\big|_0\exp(t\xi) = \xi\text{),}$$
> and $d(\ell_p)_e\colon \mathfrak{g} \to T_pP$ is a linear map, so $\xi \mapsto \xi_P(p)$ is linear. For injectivity, suppose $\xi_P(p) = 0$. Then the orbit curve $c(t) := p \cdot \exp(t\xi)$ has $\dot c(0) = 0$; more is true, since $c$ is the integral curve of the fundamental field and $\xi_P(p) = 0$ forces $\exp(t\xi)$ to fix $p$ for all $t$ (proved on [[Def - Fundamental Vector Field of a Group Action]]: $\xi_P(p) = 0 \iff p \cdot \exp(t\xi) = p$ for all $t$). Because the action is **free**, $p \cdot \exp(t\xi) = p$ forces $\exp(t\xi) = e$ for all $t$, whence $\xi = \frac{d}{dt}\big|_0 \exp(t\xi) = 0$. Thus the map is injective, and $\dim\{\xi_P(p) : \xi \in \mathfrak{g}\} = k$.
>
> **(c) The image is all of $\ker d\pi_p$.** The projection $\pi$ is a submersion (part of the definition of a fibre bundle: in a local trivialisation $\pi$ is $\operatorname{pr}_1\colon U \times G \to U$, which is a submersion), so $d\pi_p\colon T_pP \to T_mM$ is surjective, and by rank–nullity $\dim \ker d\pi_p = \dim P - \dim M = (n + k) - n = k$. By (a) the $k$-dimensional subspace $\{\xi_P(p) : \xi \in \mathfrak{g}\}$ (using (b) for its dimension) is contained in the $k$-dimensional space $\ker d\pi_p$; two subspaces of equal finite dimension with one contained in the other are equal, so $\{\xi_P(p) : \xi \in \mathfrak{g}\} = \ker d\pi_p = V_p$. Combining (a), (b), (c): $\xi \mapsto \xi_P(p)$ is a linear isomorphism $\mathfrak{g} \xrightarrow{\sim} V_p$. $\blacksquare$
>
> This is exactly the fact recorded in Bär's Remark 2.3.2 and its accompanying figure — "all vectors tangent to the fibres are of the form $\bar X$ for a suitable $X \in \mathfrak{g}$" — and it is what makes clause 2 of a connection form (fixing $\omega$ on vertical vectors) a complete prescription. It is proved again, with the full one-parameter-subgroup input, on [[Ex - Vertical Vectors are Exactly the Fundamental Vector Fields]].

With the vertical subspace in hand we state the two conditions. Let $\rho\colon G \to GL(V)$ be a representation and $\omega \in \Omega^q(P; V)$ a $V$-valued $q$-form on $P$.

1. **Equivariance.** $\omega$ is **$G$-equivariant of type $\rho$** if
$$R_g^*\omega \,=\, \rho(g^{-1})\,\omega \qquad \text{for every } g \in G;$$
written out, $\omega_{p \cdot g}\big(d(R_g)_p v_1, \dots, d(R_g)_p v_q\big) = \rho(g^{-1})\,\omega_p(v_1, \dots, v_q)$ for all $p \in P$ and $v_1, \dots, v_q \in T_pP$. When $\rho$ is the **trivial** representation ($\rho(g) = \operatorname{id}_V$ for all $g$) this reads $R_g^*\omega = \omega$, and $\omega$ is called **invariant**.

2. **Basic.** $\omega$ is **basic** if it vanishes whenever one of its arguments is vertical:
$$\omega_p(v_1, \dots, v_q) \,=\, 0 \qquad \text{whenever some } v_j \in V_p \,=\, \ker d\pi_p.$$
By the alternating property of a $q$-form it suffices that $\omega_p(v_1, \dots, v_q) = 0$ when the *first* argument is vertical, or equivalently that the contraction $\iota_{\xi_P}\omega = 0$ for every $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$ (by the vertical-subspace description above, the $\xi_P(p)$ span $V_p$).

The space of $V$-valued $q$-forms on $P$ that are both basic and $G$-equivariant of type $\rho$ is written
$$\Omega^q_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; V)^G \,:=\, \{\omega \in \Omega^q(P; V) : \omega \text{ is basic and } R_g^*\omega = \rho(g^{-1})\omega \ \forall g \in G\}.$$
It is a real (or complex, if $V$ is complex) vector subspace of $\Omega^q(P; V)$: both conditions are linear in $\omega$ — the pullback $R_g^*$ and the pointwise map $\rho(g^{-1})$ are linear, and "vanishes on vertical vectors" is a linear condition — so a linear combination of basic equivariant forms is again basic equivariant.

**Equivalent formulation of equivariance via the fundamental fields (infinitesimal form).** Differentiating $R_{\exp(t\xi)}^*\omega = \rho(\exp(-t\xi))\omega$ at $t = 0$ gives the Lie-derivative identity $\mathcal{L}_{\xi_P}\omega = -\rho_*(\xi)\,\omega$, where $\rho_* = d_e\rho\colon \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{gl}(V)$ is the induced Lie-algebra representation. For a connected group $G$ this infinitesimal condition, together with basic, is equivalent to the global one, because a connected Lie group is generated by any neighbourhood of $e$ and $\exp$ covers such a neighbourhood; we use the global finite form $R_g^*\omega = \rho(g^{-1})\omega$ throughout, and note the infinitesimal form only because it is how equivariance is checked in practice on a connection or curvature form.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

There is a clean structural reading that explains why these two clauses, and no others, are the right ones. The right $G$-action makes the graded algebra $\Omega^\bullet(P; V)$ into a $G$-space (via pullback $R_g^*$, twisted by $\rho(g^{-1})$ on coefficients) and a $\mathfrak{g}$-space (via the interior products $\iota_{\xi_P}$ and Lie derivatives $\mathcal{L}_{\xi_P}$ by the fundamental fields). In the language of the Cartan model of equivariant cohomology, a form is called **horizontal** if $\iota_{\xi_P}\omega = 0$ for all $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$ (this is exactly our "basic" in the sense of vanishing on vertical vectors), **invariant** if $\mathcal{L}_{\xi_P}\omega = -\rho_*(\xi)\omega$ for all $\xi$ (the infinitesimal equivariance above), and **basic** in Cartan's combined sense if it is both horizontal and invariant. Our $\Omega^q_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; V)^G$ is the degree-$q$ part of the basic subcomplex of $(\Omega^\bullet(P; V), d)$ for the twisting representation $\rho$.

The universal-property statement this structure encodes is the descent isomorphism itself: the pullback $\pi^*$ is the initial map out of forms on $M$ into forms on $P$, and its image, after twisting by $\rho$, is exactly the basic equivariant forms. Precisely, the theorem [[Thm - Bundle-Valued Forms on the Base are Basic Equivariant Forms on the Total Space|Bundle-Valued Forms on the Base are Basic Equivariant Forms on the Total Space]] exhibits a linear isomorphism
$$\Omega^q(M; P \times_\rho V) \,\xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\, \Omega^q_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; V)^G,$$
natural in the bundle, so that "basic equivariant form on $P$ of type $\rho$" is not merely one description of the associated-bundle-valued forms on $M$ but the canonical model of them upstairs, where the exterior derivative and a connection are available before any local trivialisation is chosen. We do not use the isomorphism on this page — it is proved on its own page — and we record only that the two clauses of the definition are precisely the two ways a form can fail to be in the image of $\pi^*$: a vertical component (killed by "horizontal/basic") and a cross-fibre inconsistency (killed by "invariant/equivariant").

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** A basic equivariant $V$-valued $q$-form on $P$ of type $\rho$ *is* a $q$-form on the base $M$ with values in the associated bundle $P \times_\rho V$, written upstairs. The official definition is the pair of conditions (basic, equivariant); the operational characterisation is "$\pi^*$ of an $(P \times_\rho V)$-valued form on $M$," and the two are identified by [[Thm - Bundle-Valued Forms on the Base are Basic Equivariant Forms on the Total Space|the descent theorem]]. When you meet a form on $P$ and want to know what it is, check the two conditions; if both hold, it lives on $M$.

This definition is the principal-bundle instance of a pattern that recurs wherever one computes on a covering, resolution, or total space and pushes down to a quotient. In the theory of a group $G$ acting on a manifold $N$, the **$G$-invariant** differential forms $\Omega^\bullet(N)^G$ are the forms that descend to the orbifold or quotient $N/G$ when the action is free and proper — invariance is our equivariance for the trivial representation. In representation theory, an equivariant map between $G$-representations is a map intertwining the actions; a form of type $\rho$ intertwines the geometric $G$-action on $P$ with the linear $G$-action $\rho$ on the coefficients $V$, so equivariance is the differential-form-valued version of an intertwiner. In the [[Def - de Rham Cohomology|de Rham theory]] of a bundle, the basic subcomplex computes the cohomology of the base: for the trivial representation, $\pi^*$ identifies $(\Omega^\bullet(M), d)$ with $(\Omega^\bullet_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; \mathbb{R})^G, d)$, so $H^\bullet_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$ is the cohomology of the basic forms. The single unifying compression is: **to do calculus on a quotient, do the equivariant, transverse-to-the-orbits calculus upstairs.** Basic isolates "transverse to the orbits," equivariant isolates "consistent along the orbits," and the associated bundle is the mechanism that lets the coefficients rotate as we cross an orbit.

A second compression concerns the special role of the adjoint representation. When $V = \mathfrak{g}$ and $\rho = \operatorname{Ad}$, the associated bundle is the **adjoint bundle** $\operatorname{ad}P = P \times_{\operatorname{Ad}} \mathfrak{g}$ (see [[Def - Adjoint Bundles ad P and Ad P]]), and $\Omega^q_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; \mathfrak{g})^G$ with $\rho = \operatorname{Ad}$ is $\Omega^q(M; \operatorname{ad}P)$. This is the home of the curvature, of the difference of two connections, and of every infinitesimal gauge quantity; it is the single most-used instance of the definition on this page, and the reason the definition is stated for a general representation rather than only for the trivial one.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the pullback of a base form is basic and invariant.** Let $\alpha \in \Omega^q(M)$ be an ordinary real-valued $q$-form on the base, and consider $\pi^*\alpha \in \Omega^q(P; \mathbb{R})$, with $V = \mathbb{R}$ and $\rho$ the trivial representation. We verify both clauses.
*Basic:* let $v_1, \dots, v_q \in T_pP$ with some $v_j \in V_p = \ker d\pi_p$, say $d\pi_p(v_1) = 0$. Then
$$(\pi^*\alpha)_p(v_1, \dots, v_q) \,=\, \alpha_{\pi(p)}\big(d\pi_p(v_1), \dots, d\pi_p(v_q)\big) \,=\, \alpha_{\pi(p)}\big(0, d\pi_p(v_2), \dots\big) \,=\, 0 \qquad \text{(definition of pullback; } d\pi_p(v_1) = 0\text{; linearity of } \alpha \text{ in its first slot).}$$
So $\pi^*\alpha$ vanishes when any argument is vertical, hence is basic.
*Invariant:* using $\pi \circ R_g = \pi$ (the projection is $G$-invariant) and functoriality of pullback,
$$R_g^*(\pi^*\alpha) \,=\, (\pi \circ R_g)^*\alpha \,=\, \pi^*\alpha \qquad \text{(} (\phi \circ \psi)^* = \psi^* \circ \phi^* \text{ for pullbacks; then } \pi \circ R_g = \pi\text{),}$$
which is the equivariance law $R_g^*(\pi^*\alpha) = \rho(g^{-1})\,\pi^*\alpha$ for the trivial $\rho$. Hence $\pi^*\alpha \in \Omega^q_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; \mathbb{R})^G$. (This is the easy half of the descent theorem: pullbacks of base forms are always basic and invariant. The content of that theorem is the converse — that *every* basic equivariant form so arises.)

**Is an instance — a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued form built from an equivariant function.** Let $f\colon P \to V$ be smooth and equivariant of type $\rho$, meaning $f(p \cdot g) = \rho(g^{-1})f(p)$, and let $\beta \in \Omega^q(M)$; set $\omega := f \cdot \pi^*\beta \in \Omega^q(P; V)$, that is $\omega_p = f(p)\,(\pi^*\beta)_p$. Then $\omega$ is basic (it is $\pi^*\beta$, already basic, scaled pointwise by $f$, and scaling by a function preserves vanishing on vertical vectors) and equivariant of type $\rho$:
$$R_g^*\omega \,=\, (f \circ R_g)\,R_g^*(\pi^*\beta) \,=\, \rho(g^{-1})f \cdot \pi^*\beta \,=\, \rho(g^{-1})\,\omega \qquad \text{(} R_g^* \text{ is multiplicative; } f \circ R_g = \rho(g^{-1})f \text{ by equivariance of } f\text{; } R_g^*\pi^*\beta = \pi^*\beta\text{).}$$
This is the mechanism by which sections of an associated bundle, which are exactly the equivariant functions $f\colon P \to V$ (see [[Thm - Sections of an Associated Bundle are Equivariant Functions]]), produce basic equivariant forms; it is the $q = 0$ case that shows $\Omega^0_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; V)^G = \Gamma(P \times_\rho V)$.

**Is an instance but not basic — the Maurer–Cartan form on the trivial bundle.** Let $P = M \times G$ with $\pi = \operatorname{pr}_1$ and the right action $R_g(m, h) = (m, hg)$, and let $\theta \in \Omega^1(G; \mathfrak{g})$ be the **left Maurer–Cartan form** $\theta_h = d(L_{h^{-1}})_h\colon T_hG \to \mathfrak{g}$ (for a matrix group $\theta = g^{-1}\,dg$). Take $V = \mathfrak{g}$, $\rho = \operatorname{Ad}$, and $\omega := \operatorname{pr}_G^*\theta \in \Omega^1(P; \mathfrak{g})$ where $\operatorname{pr}_G\colon M \times G \to G$ is the second projection.
*Equivariant of type $\operatorname{Ad}$:* the Maurer–Cartan form satisfies the classical identity $R_g^*\theta = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\theta$ on $G$ (differentiate $L_{(hg)^{-1}} \circ R_g = c_{g^{-1}} \circ L_{h^{-1}}$, where $c_{g^{-1}}(x) = g^{-1}xg$, at $h$: the left side is $\theta_{hg} \circ d(R_g)_h$ and the right side is $\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}} \circ \theta_h$). Pulling back along $\operatorname{pr}_G$ and using $\operatorname{pr}_G \circ R_g = R_g^G \circ \operatorname{pr}_G$ (the fibre action is right translation on the $G$-factor), $R_g^*\omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\omega$; so $\omega$ is equivariant of type $\operatorname{Ad}$.
*Not basic:* the fundamental field of $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$ at $p = (m, h)$ is $\xi_P(p) = (0, \frac{d}{dt}\big|_0 h\exp(t\xi)) = (0, d(L_h)_e\xi)$, which is vertical (it projects to $0$ under $\pi = \operatorname{pr}_1$). Evaluating,
$$\omega_p(\xi_P(p)) \,=\, \theta_h\big(d(L_h)_e\xi\big) \,=\, d(L_{h^{-1}})_h\,d(L_h)_e\,\xi \,=\, d(L_{h^{-1}} \circ L_h)_e\,\xi \,=\, d(\operatorname{id}_G)_e\,\xi \,=\, \xi \qquad \text{(definition of } \theta \text{; chain rule; } L_{h^{-1}} \circ L_h = \operatorname{id}\text{).}$$
Thus $\omega(\xi_P) = \xi \neq 0$ for $\xi \neq 0$, so $\omega$ fails on vertical vectors and is **not basic**. This is the prototype of a connection form: a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form that is $\operatorname{Ad}$-equivariant and *reproduces $\xi$ on $\xi_P$*, hence deliberately non-basic. It is the cleanest witness that equivariance alone does not force descent to $M$: this $\omega$ corresponds to no form on the base, because it measures the fibre direction.

**Is NOT an instance — the differential of a non-invariant function.** Take again $P = M \times U(1)$ with $U(1) = \{e^{i\phi}\}$ acting by $R_{e^{i\psi}}(m, e^{i\phi}) = (m, e^{i(\phi + \psi)})$, and let $f \in C^\infty(P)$ be the angular coordinate function's cosine, $f(m, e^{i\phi}) = \cos\phi$ (globally smooth on $P$). Consider $df \in \Omega^1(P; \mathbb{R})$ with the trivial representation, so equivariance would mean invariance, $R_g^*df = df$.
*Not invariant:* $R_{e^{i\psi}}^*f = f \circ R_{e^{i\psi}}$ sends $(m, e^{i\phi}) \mapsto \cos(\phi + \psi)$, so $R_{e^{i\psi}}^*f = \cos(\cdot + \psi) \neq \cos(\cdot) = f$ whenever $\psi \notin 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$; therefore
$$R_{e^{i\psi}}^*(df) \,=\, d\big(R_{e^{i\psi}}^*f\big) \,=\, d\big(\cos(\phi + \psi)\big) \,=\, -\sin(\phi + \psi)\,d\phi \,\neq\, -\sin(\phi)\,d\phi \,=\, df \qquad \text{(} d \text{ commutes with pullback; then substitute).}$$
So $df$ is not invariant, hence not in $\Omega^1_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; \mathbb{R})^{U(1)}$. (It is also not basic: $df(\partial_\phi) = -\sin\phi \not\equiv 0$ on the vertical field $\partial_\phi = (\xi_P$ for $\xi = i)$, and this quantity even changes sign around the fibre, which is exactly why no single value can be assigned on the base.) The failure is precisely the "cross-fibre inconsistency" the equivariance clause exists to exclude: $f$ has different values at $p$ and $p \cdot g$, so neither $f$ nor $df$ records data on $M$.

**Corollary — a basic $0$-form is a function, and a basic invariant $0$-form is a function on the base.** A $0$-form has no vector arguments, so the "vanishes on vertical vectors" condition is vacuously satisfied: *every* $V$-valued function on $P$ is basic. Among these, the equivariant ones of type $\rho$ are the equivariant functions $f\colon P \to V$, $f(p \cdot g) = \rho(g^{-1})f(p)$, and by [[Thm - Sections of an Associated Bundle are Equivariant Functions]] these are exactly the sections of $P \times_\rho V$; for the trivial representation they are the $G$-invariant functions, that is the functions $f = \pi^*u$ pulled back from $u \in C^\infty(M)$. Hence $\Omega^0_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; V)^G = \Gamma(P \times_\rho V)$, and in the trivial case $\Omega^0_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; \mathbb{R})^G = C^\infty(M)$. This is the base case ($q = 0$) of the descent theorem and confirms the definition's intent: a basic invariant scalar is a function on $M$.

**Corollary — for an abelian structure group, equivariant of type $\operatorname{Ad}$ means invariant.** If $G$ is abelian then $\operatorname{Ad}_g = \operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}}$ for all $g$ (conjugation $h \mapsto ghg^{-1} = h$ is the identity, so its differential is), and the equivariance law $R_g^*\omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\omega$ collapses to $R_g^*\omega = \omega$. Thus for $\mathfrak{g}$-valued forms on an abelian principal bundle — for instance a $U(1)$-bundle — "$\operatorname{Ad}$-equivariant" and "invariant" coincide, which is Haydys' remark that "for abelian groups the notions of equivariant and invariant forms coincide" (used in his Example 42 to see that the metric-orthogonal $1$-form on the Hopf fibration $S^{2n+1} \to \mathbb{CP}^n$ is a connection). More generally this holds for any representation $\rho$ that happens to be trivial, and for the coefficients $\mathfrak{g}$ it holds exactly when $G$ is abelian.

**Calibration check.** First, confirm that a basic $0$-form is automatically basic: with $q = 0$ there is no argument to be vertical, so the defining condition is empty — every function qualifies, and only equivariance is a real constraint at degree $0$. Second, confirm that the pullback $\pi^*\alpha$ of $\alpha \in \Omega^q(M)$ lands in $\Omega^q_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; \mathbb{R})^G$ by the two-line verification above (basic from $d\pi \circ (\text{vertical}) = 0$, invariant from $\pi \circ R_g = \pi$). Third — the payoff, proved on the next page — a form that is both basic and equivariant *of type $\operatorname{Ad}$* descends to an element of $\Omega^q(M; \operatorname{ad}P)$: this is the case $V = \mathfrak{g}$, $\rho = \operatorname{Ad}$ of [[Thm - Bundle-Valued Forms on the Base are Basic Equivariant Forms on the Total Space|the descent theorem]], and it is the fact that lets curvature, defined upstairs as a basic $\operatorname{Ad}$-equivariant $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $2$-form, be read as a $2$-form on $M$ with values in the adjoint bundle.

> [!tip] The Descent Isomorphism *(from Gauge Theory III)*
> The theorem [[Thm - Bundle-Valued Forms on the Base are Basic Equivariant Forms on the Total Space|Bundle-Valued Forms on the Base are Basic Equivariant Forms on the Total Space]] (Haydys' Proposition 39) upgrades the "is an instance" example above to an isomorphism $\Omega^q(M; P \times_\rho V) \cong \Omega^q_{\mathrm{bas}}(P; V)^G$. This page supplies both of its ingredients — the vertical subspace (so "basic" is defined) and the equivariance law (so the associated-bundle class is well-defined).

> [!tip] The Adjoint Bundle and Infinitesimal Gauge Theory *(from Gauge Theory III)*
> With $V = \mathfrak{g}$ and $\rho = \operatorname{Ad}$, basic equivariant forms are $\operatorname{ad}P$-valued forms on the base (see [[Def - Adjoint Bundles ad P and Ad P]]). The difference of two [[Def - Principal G-Bundle|principal]] connections, and the curvature of a connection, are exactly such forms; this is why the space of connections is an affine space modelled on $\Omega^1(M; \operatorname{ad}P)$.

> [!tip] Connection and Curvature Forms *(from Gauge Theory IV)*
> A **connection form** is an $\operatorname{Ad}$-equivariant $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form that is *deliberately not basic* (it reproduces $\xi$ on $\xi_P$), while its **curvature** is $\operatorname{Ad}$-equivariant and *basic*. The two conditions on this page are precisely the bookkeeping that separates the non-descending connection from its descending curvature.
