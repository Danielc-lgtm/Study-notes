# Notation

Throughout, $M$ is a smooth manifold (smooth, Hausdorff, second countable, $C^\infty$), and $E\to M$ is a smooth real [[Def - Vector Bundle|vector bundle]] over $M$, of real rank $\operatorname{rk}E$. We write $E_m$ for the fibre over $m\in M$, $\Gamma(E)$ for the space of smooth sections, and $\operatorname{End}E$ for the bundle whose fibre over $m$ is $\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb R}(E_m)=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb R}(E_m,E_m)$; a section $I\in\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$ is a smooth choice of a real-linear endomorphism $I_m\colon E_m\to E_m$ for each $m$, and $\operatorname{id}=\operatorname{id}_E$ is the identity endomorphism. The letter $i$ always denotes the imaginary unit $\sqrt{-1}\in\mathbb C$, never an index; indices are $j,\ell$. For a complex number $\lambda=a+bi$ with $a,b\in\mathbb R$ we write $\bar\lambda=a-bi$ for its conjugate and $\lvert\lambda\rvert^2=\lambda\bar\lambda$. The standard Hermitian scalar product on $\mathbb C^k$ is $h_0(z,w)=\sum_{j=1}^k z_j\bar w_j$.

We identify $\mathbb R^{2k}$ with $\mathbb C^k$ through $(x_1,y_1,\dots,x_k,y_k)\mapsto(x_1+iy_1,\dots,x_k+iy_k)$. Under this identification, multiplication by $i$ becomes the real-linear map
$$I_{\mathrm{st}}(x_1,y_1,\dots,x_k,y_k):=(-y_1,x_1,\dots,-y_k,x_k),$$
called the **standard complex structure** on $\mathbb R^{2k}$; one checks directly that $I_{\mathrm{st}}^2=-\operatorname{id}$. The subgroup of real matrices commuting with $I_{\mathrm{st}}$,
$$GL_k(\mathbb C)=\{A\in GL_{2k}(\mathbb R)\mid A\circ I_{\mathrm{st}}=I_{\mathrm{st}}\circ A\}\subset GL_{2k}(\mathbb R),$$
is precisely the group of $\mathbb C$-linear automorphisms of $\mathbb C^k$, and $U(k)\subset GL_k(\mathbb C)$ is the subgroup preserving $h_0$.

> [!warning] Convention: sesquilinearity
> A **Hermitian scalar product** on a complex vector space is taken here to be $\mathbb C$-linear in its *first* argument and conjugate-linear in its *second*, so that $h(\lambda v,w)=\lambda\,h(v,w)$ and $h(v,\lambda w)=\bar\lambda\,h(v,w)$, with $h(w,v)=\overline{h(v,w)}$ and $h(v,v)>0$ for $v\neq0$. The physics literature and some texts use the opposite (conjugate-linear in the first slot); every formula below that mentions a slot is stated in the convention just fixed, and converting to the other convention amounts to replacing $h(v,w)$ by $\overline{h(v,w)}=h(w,v)$ throughout. The standard $h_0(z,w)=\sum_j z_j\bar w_j$ obeys this convention.

This is a compound page: it defines several interlocking notions — a complex structure on a real vector space and on a bundle, a complex vector bundle, the equivalent description as a locally trivial family of complex vector spaces, a Hermitian structure, a fibrewise volume form, and the tensor, dual and conjugate operations on complex line bundles — because they are one circle of ideas: each says how much of the linear algebra of $\mathbb C$ can be carried smoothly over the fibres of a real bundle, and each is used together with the others throughout gauge theory (a line bundle carries a Hermitian metric, its curvature lands in the imaginary numbers, and its conjugate is its inverse). The relevant standing series convention is that structure groups act on the **right** and that the [[Def - Frame Bundle of a Vector Bundle|frame bundle]] of a rank-$k$ real bundle is a principal $GL_k(\mathbb R)$-bundle.

# Axiom Motivation

We begin with a real vector bundle $E\to M$ and ask the most economical question one can ask about its fibres: what does it take to multiply the vectors in each fibre by complex scalars, smoothly across $M$? A complex vector space is, after all, nothing more than a real vector space on which one additionally knows how to multiply by $i$; and to multiply by $i$ is to have a real-linear operator $I$ with the single defining property that applying it twice is multiplication by $-1$, since $i\cdot(i\cdot v)=i^2v=-v$. So the whole of "complex structure" is captured by one endomorphism and one equation, $I^2=-\operatorname{id}$. Turning this from pointwise linear algebra into bundle geometry requires only that the operator vary smoothly, that is, that it be a genuine section $I\in\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$. This is the entire content of the definition, and it is worth seeing why each part of it is exactly right and cannot be moved.

The desideratum is that each fibre $E_m$ become a complex vector space in a way that is smooth in $m$ and compatible with the bundle structure. We must capture: (a) that "multiplication by $i$" is defined on every fibre; (b) that it is genuinely complex multiplication, meaning $i^2=-1$; and (c) that the whole family is smooth, so that the resulting complex bundle is again locally trivial. We must exclude: an operator that squares to $+\operatorname{id}$ (that would split $E$ into $\pm1$-eigenbundles, giving a *real* product structure, not a complex one), an operator that is not invertible (multiplication by $i$ must be an isomorphism), and any pointwise-but-discontinuous choice (which would destroy local triviality).

Consider the failure analysis clause by clause. **If we drop the equation $I^2=-\operatorname{id}$ entirely** and merely ask for some $I\in\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$, we have said nothing: every bundle has endomorphisms, and none of them make the fibres complex. **If we weaken it to $I^2=+\operatorname{id}$** with $I\neq\pm\operatorname{id}$, then $E$ splits as the direct sum of the $(+1)$- and $(-1)$-eigenbundles of $I$ (a "paracomplex" or product structure); the fibres do not become complex, because the equation forces real eigenvalues $\pm1$ rather than the imaginary $\pm i$ that complex multiplication demands. The concrete witness is $E=M\times\mathbb R^2$ with $I(x,y)=(x,-y)$: this squares to the identity and produces two real line subbundles, no complex line bundle. **If we keep $I^2=-\operatorname{id}$ but drop smoothness**, choosing $I_m$ freely at each point, then there is no reason for the complex trivialisations we build below to be continuous, and the family of complex vector spaces need not be locally trivial; the corollary that a complex bundle is a bundle with fibre $\mathbb C^k$ (proved in *The Definition*) fails, since its proof produces the complex frames by a continuity argument that needs $I$ smooth. **The invertibility of $I$ is not a separate clause to be dropped**, because it is already forced: from $I^2=-\operatorname{id}$ we get $I\cdot(-I)=\operatorname{id}$, so $I$ is automatically an isomorphism with inverse $-I$. This is a small illustration that the single equation $I^2=-\operatorname{id}$ is doing exactly the right amount of work — no more, no less.

The equation also silently forces a numerical constraint that a reader could discover unaided, and which is the source of the standard non-example. Taking fibrewise determinants of $I^2=-\operatorname{id}$ gives $\det(I_m)^2=\det(-\operatorname{id}_{E_m})=(-1)^{\operatorname{rk}E}$; since $\det(I_m)$ is a real number, its square is nonnegative, so we must have $(-1)^{\operatorname{rk}E}\geq0$, that is, $\operatorname{rk}E$ even. A complex structure is thus possible only on an even-rank real bundle, and a reader who did not already know this could deduce it from the definition alone — the mark of a well-made definition. A theorem downstream that would fail without evenness is the identification of complex structures with reductions of the structure group to $GL_k(\mathbb C)$ (see [[Thm - Euclidean and Hermitian Structures are Reductions of the Structure Group|the reductions theorem]]): $GL_k(\mathbb C)$ lives inside $GL_{2k}(\mathbb R)$, and there is simply no such subgroup of $GL_{2k+1}(\mathbb R)$.

For the second object, the Hermitian structure, the desiderata are different but no less forced. Once each fibre is complex we want to measure lengths and angles of complex vectors, and the measurement must respect the complex scalars: the length of $iv$ should equal the length of $v$, and the inner product should be $\mathbb C$-linear in one slot so that it interacts with complex multiplication at all. A real Euclidean structure $g$ on the underlying real bundle does measure lengths, but on its own it need not satisfy $g(Iv,Iw)=g(v,w)$; without that compatibility, rotating a vector by the complex scalar $i$ could change its length, which is incoherent for a complex geometry. **Dropping positive-definiteness** gives an indefinite Hermitian form, which has no associated norm and no reduction to the compact unitary group — useful in other contexts, but not what "metric on a complex bundle" should mean. **Dropping Hermitian symmetry** $h(w,v)=\overline{h(v,w)}$ destroys the reality of $h(v,v)$, so "the squared length of $v$" is no longer a real number. **Dropping the sesquilinearity/$I$-compatibility** reduces $h$ to a mere real inner product that ignores the complex structure, and then, as shown in *Examples*, the natural formula relating $h$ to $g$ breaks. The Hermitian structure is precisely the complex refinement of a Euclidean structure that is compatible with $I$, and its real part is a genuine [[Def - Euclidean Vector Bundle and Metric Connection|Euclidean structure]] — the calibration check below.

# The Definition

**Complex structure on a real vector space.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional real vector space. A **complex structure** on $V$ is a real-linear endomorphism $I\in\operatorname{End}_{\mathbb R}(V)$ with
$$I^2=-\operatorname{id}_V.$$
Given such an $I$, the space $V$ becomes a complex vector space under the scalar multiplication
$$(a+bi)\cdot v:=av+b\,Iv\qquad(a,b\in\mathbb R,\ v\in V),$$
and conversely every complex vector space $V$, viewed as a real vector space, carries the complex structure $Iv:=iv$. In the smallest case $\dim_{\mathbb R}V=2$: writing $v_0\neq0$ and $Iv_0$, the pair $(v_0,Iv_0)$ is a real basis, and the complex line $V\cong\mathbb C$ is spanned over $\mathbb C$ by $v_0$, with $i\cdot v_0=Iv_0$.

> [!note]- Verification that $(a+bi)\cdot v:=av+b\,Iv$ is a complex scalar multiplication
> We must show that this operation makes $V$ a module over the field $\mathbb C$, i.e. that it is additive in $v$, additive in the scalar, and associative, $(\lambda\mu)\cdot v=\lambda\cdot(\mu\cdot v)$ for $\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb C$, and that $1\cdot v=v$.
>
> **Additivity and unit.** For fixed $\lambda=a+bi$, the map $v\mapsto av+b\,Iv$ is $\mathbb R$-linear (a sum of the $\mathbb R$-linear maps $a\,\operatorname{id}$ and $b\,I$), hence additive in $v$ (definition of $\mathbb R$-linearity). For fixed $v$, $(a+bi)\cdot v=av+bIv$ is $\mathbb R$-linear in $(a,b)$, hence additive in the scalar. Taking $\lambda=1$ (so $a=1,b=0$) gives $1\cdot v=v$ (evaluation).
>
> **Associativity.** Let $\lambda=a+bi$ and $\mu=c+di$ with $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb R$. First,
> $$\mu\cdot v=cv+d\,Iv\qquad\text{(definition of the operation)}.$$
> Then
> $$\lambda\cdot(\mu\cdot v)=a(cv+d\,Iv)+b\,I(cv+d\,Iv)=acv+ad\,Iv+bc\,Iv+bd\,I^2v\qquad\text{(definition, and }I\text{ is }\mathbb R\text{-linear)}.$$
> Using $I^2v=-v$ (the defining equation of $I$),
> $$\lambda\cdot(\mu\cdot v)=(ac-bd)v+(ad+bc)\,Iv\qquad\text{(collecting the }v\text{ and }Iv\text{ terms)}.$$
> On the other hand $\lambda\mu=(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i$ (multiplication in $\mathbb C$), so by the definition of the operation
> $$(\lambda\mu)\cdot v=(ac-bd)v+(ad+bc)\,Iv\qquad\text{(definition of the operation).}$$
> The two right-hand sides agree, so $(\lambda\mu)\cdot v=\lambda\cdot(\mu\cdot v)$. Therefore the operation is a $\mathbb C$-scalar multiplication and $V$ is a complex vector space. $\blacksquare$

If $\dim_{\mathbb C}V=k$ with complex basis $e_1,\dots,e_k$, then $(e_1,Ie_1,\dots,e_k,Ie_k)$ is a real basis of $V$, so $\dim_{\mathbb R}V=2k$ is even; conversely a real vector space admitting a complex structure has even real dimension (take fibrewise determinants of $I^2=-\operatorname{id}$: $\det(I)^2=(-1)^{\dim_{\mathbb R}V}\geq0$).

**Complex vector bundle.** A **complex vector bundle** is a real vector bundle $E\to M$ together with a section
$$I\in\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)\qquad\text{such that}\qquad I^2=-\operatorname{id}_E,$$
called a **complex structure** on $E$. Each fibre $E_m$ is then a complex vector space of complex dimension $k:=\tfrac12\operatorname{rk}E$ under $(a+bi)\cdot v=av+b\,I_m v$, and the fibrewise complex scalar multiplication $\mathbb C\times E\to E$, $(a+bi,v)\mapsto av+b\,Iv$, is smooth because $I$ is smooth. The **complex rank** of $(E,I)$ is $\operatorname{rk}_{\mathbb C}E:=k$. A **complex line bundle** is a complex vector bundle of complex rank $1$ (real rank $2$).

**Equivalent formulation (locally trivial family of complex vector spaces).** The following is an equivalent description, and it is the one that matches the fibre-bundle definition of a vector bundle with fibre $\mathbb C^k$ (the complex case of [[Def - Vector Bundle|the vector-bundle definition]]): a rank-$k$ complex vector bundle is a fibre bundle $\pi\colon E\to M$ with typical fibre $\mathbb C^k$ whose fibres $E_m$ carry $\mathbb C$-vector-space structures for which the local trivialisations $\psi_U\colon E|_U\to U\times\mathbb C^k$ restrict, on each fibre, to $\mathbb C$-linear isomorphisms $E_m\to\{m\}\times\mathbb C^k$. Equivalently, the transition functions take values in $GL_k(\mathbb C)$.

> [!note]- Proof that the two formulations are equivalent (Haydys Exercise 28(i))
> We prove both directions. Fix throughout $k=\tfrac12\operatorname{rk}E$ in the first direction and $k$ the complex rank in the second.
>
> **Direction 1 (a complex structure gives $\mathbb C$-linear trivialisations).** Assume $(E,I)$ is a complex vector bundle in the first sense, so $I\in\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$ with $I^2=-\operatorname{id}$ and $\operatorname{rk}E=2k$. We must produce, near each point, a $\mathbb C$-linear local trivialisation. Fix $m\in M$; we construct sections $v_1,\dots,v_k$ of $E$ near $m$ such that $(v_1,Iv_1,\dots,v_k,Iv_k)$ is a real frame on a neighbourhood of $m$.
>
> **Step 1: choose a complex basis of the fibre $E_m$.** Because $E_m$ with $I_m$ is a complex vector space of complex dimension $k$, pick a complex basis $u_1,\dots,u_k\in E_m$. Then $(u_1,I_m u_1,\dots,u_k,I_m u_k)$ is a real basis of $E_m$: it is the real basis associated to the complex basis, as recorded above (its real linear independence is the standard fact that a complex basis, together with its images under $I_m$, forms a real basis — if $\sum_j(a_j u_j+b_j I_m u_j)=0$ with $a_j,b_j\in\mathbb R$ then $\sum_j(a_j+ib_j)u_j=0$ in the complex structure, forcing all $a_j+ib_j=0$, hence all $a_j=b_j=0$).
>
> **Step 2: extend to smooth sections.** Choose any smooth local trivialisation (as a real bundle) of $E$ near $m$ and let $v_1,\dots,v_k\in\Gamma(E|_W)$ be the smooth sections on a neighbourhood $W$ of $m$ that are constant in that trivialisation and equal $u_1,\dots,u_k$ at $m$ (i.e. $v_j(m)=u_j$). These exist because a constant section in a real trivialisation is smooth.
>
> **Step 3: the extended sections remain a real frame near $m$.** Consider the $2k$ smooth sections $v_1,Iv_1,\dots,v_k,Iv_k$ (each $Iv_j$ is smooth because $I$ and $v_j$ are). Expressing them in the chosen real trivialisation gives a smooth map $W\to\mathrm{Mat}_{2k}(\mathbb R)$ whose value at $m$ is invertible (Step 1), hence whose determinant is nonzero at $m$; by continuity of the determinant there is a neighbourhood $U\subseteq W$ of $m$ on which the determinant is nonzero (continuity of $\det$ and of the sections). On $U$ the family $(v_1,Iv_1,\dots,v_k,Iv_k)$ is therefore a real frame, and consequently $(v_1,\dots,v_k)$ is a *complex* frame: for each $x\in U$ the vectors $v_1(x),\dots,v_k(x)$ are a complex basis of $E_x$, since $\{v_j(x),I_x v_j(x)\}$ is a real basis and complex multiplication by $i$ is $I_x$.
>
> **Step 4: define the $\mathbb C$-linear trivialisation.** Let $\psi_U\colon E|_U\to U\times\mathbb C^k$ send a vector $w\in E_x$, written uniquely as $w=\sum_{j=1}^k\bigl(a_j v_j(x)+b_j\,I_x v_j(x)\bigr)$ with $a_j,b_j\in\mathbb R$, to $\bigl(x,(a_1+ib_1,\dots,a_k+ib_k)\bigr)$. This is a diffeomorphism over $U$ (it is the real trivialisation with respect to the real frame $(v_j,Iv_j)$, followed by the fixed $\mathbb R$-linear identification $\mathbb R^{2k}\cong\mathbb C^k$). It is $\mathbb C$-linear on each fibre: complex multiplication by $i$ sends $v_j\mapsto Iv_j$ and $Iv_j\mapsto I^2v_j=-v_j$, i.e. $(a_j,b_j)\mapsto(-b_j,a_j)$, which is exactly multiplication by $i$ on the coordinate $a_j+ib_j$. Hence $\psi_U$ is fibrewise $\mathbb C$-linear, and $E$ is a locally trivial family of complex vector spaces. The transition function between two such trivialisations is fibrewise $\mathbb C$-linear and invertible, hence takes values in $GL_k(\mathbb C)$.
>
> **Direction 2 (a $\mathbb C^k$-bundle gives a complex structure).** Assume $E\to M$ is a fibre bundle with fibre $\mathbb C^k$ and a family of trivialisations $\psi_\alpha\colon E|_{U_\alpha}\to U_\alpha\times\mathbb C^k$ that are fibrewise $\mathbb C$-linear, with transition functions in $GL_k(\mathbb C)$. Define $I$ fibrewise by transporting multiplication by $i$: for $w\in E_x$ with $x\in U_\alpha$, set $I_x w:=\psi_\alpha^{-1}\bigl(x,\,i\cdot\operatorname{pr}_2\psi_\alpha(w)\bigr)$, where $\operatorname{pr}_2$ is projection to $\mathbb C^k$. **Well-definedness (independence of $\alpha$).** If also $x\in U_\beta$, the transition function $g_{\beta\alpha}(x)\in GL_k(\mathbb C)$ satisfies $\operatorname{pr}_2\psi_\beta=g_{\beta\alpha}(x)\,\operatorname{pr}_2\psi_\alpha$ on $E_x$, and since $g_{\beta\alpha}(x)$ is $\mathbb C$-linear it commutes with multiplication by $i$; hence the vector defined using $\beta$ equals the vector defined using $\alpha$ (the two differ only by conjugating the operation "$\times i$" by the $\mathbb C$-linear $g_{\beta\alpha}(x)$, which leaves it unchanged). **Smoothness.** In each trivialisation $I$ is the constant fibrewise map "multiply the $\mathbb C^k$-coordinate by $i$", conjugated by the smooth $\psi_\alpha$, hence smooth, so $I\in\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$. **The equation.** $I^2$ transports multiplication by $i^2=-1$, so $I^2=-\operatorname{id}$. Thus $(E,I)$ is a complex vector bundle in the first sense.
>
> The two constructions are mutually inverse: starting from $(E,I)$, Direction 1 produces $\mathbb C$-linear trivialisations in which $I$ acts as $\times i$, and Direction 2 recovers exactly this $I$; starting from a $\mathbb C^k$-bundle, Direction 2 produces $I$, and Direction 1's trivialisations are again $\mathbb C$-linear for it. Therefore the two definitions coincide. $\blacksquare$

**Hermitian structure.** A **Hermitian structure** (or Hermitian metric) on a complex vector bundle $(E,I)$ is a smooth family $h=\{h_m\}_{m\in M}$ of Hermitian scalar products $h_m$ on the complex vector spaces $E_m$ — smooth in the sense that $m\mapsto h_m(s(m),t(m))$ is a smooth complex-valued function for all $s,t\in\Gamma(E)$. Explicitly, $h$ is a smooth section of the real bundle of $\mathbb R$-bilinear maps $E\times_M E\to\mathbb C$ such that at each $m$, $h_m$ is $\mathbb C$-linear in the first argument, satisfies $h_m(w,v)=\overline{h_m(v,w)}$, and is positive definite ($h_m(v,v)>0$ for $v\neq0$).

**Fibrewise volume form.** A **fibrewise volume form** on a real vector bundle $E\to M$ of real rank $r$ is a nowhere-vanishing section $\mu\in\Gamma(\Lambda^r E^*)$; equivalently, a smooth choice, for each $m$, of a nonzero alternating $r$-linear form $\mu_m\colon E_m\times\cdots\times E_m\to\mathbb R$. Here $\Lambda^r E^*$ is the top exterior power of the dual bundle, a real line bundle, and a fibrewise volume form is exactly a trivialisation of it.

**Operations on complex line bundles.** For complex line bundles $L,L'\to M$ (each of complex rank $1$):

- the **tensor product** $L\otimes_{\mathbb C}L'$ is the complex line bundle with fibre $(L\otimes_{\mathbb C}L')_m=L_m\otimes_{\mathbb C}L'_m$, of complex rank $1$; its complex structure is $i\cdot(\ell\otimes\ell')=(i\ell)\otimes\ell'=\ell\otimes(i\ell')$;
- the **tensor power** $L^{\otimes k}:=L\otimes_{\mathbb C}\cdots\otimes_{\mathbb C}L$ ($k$ factors), with $L^{\otimes0}:=M\times\mathbb C$ the trivial line bundle;
- the **dual** $L^*$ with fibre $L^*_m=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb C}(L_m,\mathbb C)$;
- the **conjugate** $\bar L$, the same underlying real bundle as $L$ but with complex structure $-I$; equivalently, $\bar L$ is $L$ with the scalar multiplication $\lambda\cdot_{\bar L}v:=\bar\lambda\cdot_L v$.

These are the complex refinements of the corresponding real operations recorded on [[Def - Operations on Vector Bundles and Pull-Back Bundles|the operations page]]; the tensor product and dual are formed over $\mathbb C$ rather than $\mathbb R$, and the conjugate has no real analogue. For a Hermitian line bundle $(L,h)$ there is a canonical isomorphism $\bar L\cong L^*$ (proved in *Examples*), so tensoring with $\bar L$ is the same as tensoring with $L^*$, and $L\otimes_{\mathbb C}\bar L\cong M\times\mathbb C$ is trivial: complex line bundles form a group under $\otimes_{\mathbb C}$ with inverse given by the conjugate.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

There is a clean structural reading of a complex structure as a **reduction of the structure group**. A rank-$2k$ real vector bundle $E$ has [[Def - Frame Bundle of a Vector Bundle|frame bundle]] $\operatorname{Fr}(E)$, a principal $GL_{2k}(\mathbb R)$-bundle whose points over $m$ are the real linear isomorphisms $\mathbb R^{2k}\to E_m$. A complex structure $I$ on $E$ singles out those frames that are $\mathbb C$-linear, i.e. that intertwine $I_{\mathrm{st}}$ with $I$; these form a principal subbundle with structure group $GL_k(\mathbb C)=\{A\in GL_{2k}(\mathbb R):AI_{\mathrm{st}}=I_{\mathrm{st}}A\}$. Conversely a [[Def - Reduction and Extension of the Structure Group|reduction]] of $\operatorname{Fr}(E)$ to $GL_k(\mathbb C)$ picks out a consistent "multiplication by $i$" on every fibre, hence a complex structure. In the same language, a Hermitian structure is a further reduction to the compact group $U(k)\subset GL_k(\mathbb C)$: it is the choice of the frames that are $\mathbb C$-linear *and* orthonormal for $h$. The precise statement and proof of these bijections — Euclidean $\leftrightarrow O(k)$, complex $\leftrightarrow GL_k(\mathbb C)$, Hermitian $\leftrightarrow U(k)$, fibrewise volume form $\leftrightarrow SL_{2k}(\mathbb R)$ — is [[Thm - Euclidean and Hermitian Structures are Reductions of the Structure Group|the reductions theorem]]:

> **Theorem (structures as reductions, complex and Hermitian rows).** For a real vector bundle $E$ of rank $2k$, complex structures on $E$ correspond bijectively to reductions of $\operatorname{Fr}(E)$ to $GL_k(\mathbb C)$; and for a complex bundle $(E,I)$, Hermitian structures correspond bijectively to reductions to $U(k)$.

We use this only as a structural viewpoint and as the source of the $U(1)$-language for line bundles; nothing on the present page is proved by means of it. From this viewpoint a complex line bundle is a principal $GL_1(\mathbb C)=\mathbb C^\times$-bundle's associated line bundle, and a Hermitian line bundle is a $U(1)$-bundle's — the reason line bundles and $U(1)$-bundles carry the same classification.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** Operationally, a complex vector bundle is *a rank-$2k$ real bundle whose transition functions can be chosen in $GL_k(\mathbb C)$*, and the endomorphism $I$ is merely the coordinate-free way to say this. Whenever one meets a real bundle and wants to know whether it is "secretly complex", the test is not to hunt for a magic $I$ but to check whether the frame bundle reduces to $GL_k(\mathbb C)$ — equivalently, whether there is an atlas of $\mathbb C$-linear trivialisations. The two are the same by the equivalence proved above.

Complex structures on bundles are the linear shadow of complex structures on manifolds. A complex manifold $X$ has a holomorphic tangent bundle $TX$ carrying a canonical fibrewise $I$ (multiplication by $i$ in holomorphic coordinates); the requirement that these fit together into an *integrable* complex structure on $X$ is a further condition (the vanishing of the Nijenhuis tensor, the Newlander–Nirenberg theorem) that is invisible at the level of a single bundle. A bare complex structure on a bundle, with no integrability, is what differential geometers call an almost complex structure; on $TM$ it is exactly an almost complex structure on $M$. The two examples below, $\mathcal O(-1)$ over $\mathbb{CP}^n$ and $TS^2$, are in fact both integrable (they come from the complex manifold $\mathbb{CP}^n$), but we use only their bundle-level structure here.

The Hermitian structure is the bundle-level version of a Hermitian metric in complex geometry, and it is the object that makes the compact group $U(k)$ — and hence Chern–Weil theory and Chern classes — available: a unitary connection on a Hermitian bundle has curvature in the Lie algebra $\mathfrak u(k)$ of skew-Hermitian endomorphisms, and for a line bundle in $\mathfrak u(1)=i\mathbb R$, which is why the first Chern class of a Hermitian line bundle is represented by $\tfrac{i}{2\pi}F_A$ with $F_A$ imaginary-valued. The conjugate operation $L\mapsto\bar L$ corresponds, at the level of curvature, to $F\mapsto\overline F=-F$, hence to $c_1\mapsto-c_1$: conjugating a line bundle inverts it in the group of line bundles, exactly as complex conjugation inverts a unit complex number.

Finally, the passage "real bundle with $I$" $\leftrightarrow$ "complex bundle" is the geometric face of the algebraic dictionary between real and complex linear algebra, in which a $\mathbb C$-vector space of dimension $k$ is a $\mathbb R$-vector space of dimension $2k$ with a distinguished square root of $-\operatorname{id}$. The same dictionary, run the other way, is *complexification*: a real bundle $E$ has $E\otimes_{\mathbb R}\mathbb C$, a complex bundle of complex rank $\operatorname{rk}E$, on which conjugation is a real structure; a complex bundle $(E,I)$ arises this way only up to the splitting $E\otimes\mathbb C=E^{1,0}\oplus E^{0,1}$ into the $\pm i$-eigenbundles of $I$, a decomposition that reappears throughout Hodge theory.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the tautological line bundle $\mathcal O(-1)$ over $\mathbb{CP}^n$.** Recall [[Def - Complex Projective Space as a Quotient|complex projective space]] $\mathbb{CP}^n=(\mathbb C^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/\mathbb C^\times$, whose point $[z]=[z_0:\dots:z_n]$ is the complex line $\ell_{[z]}=\mathbb Cz\subset\mathbb C^{n+1}$ through $z$. The tautological line bundle is
$$\mathcal O(-1):=\bigl\{([z],w)\in\mathbb{CP}^n\times\mathbb C^{n+1}\mid w\in\ell_{[z]}\bigr\}=\bigl\{([z],w):w=\lambda z\text{ for some }\lambda\in\mathbb C\bigr\},$$
with projection $\pi([z],w)=[z]$. We verify it is a complex line bundle clause by clause. **Fibre.** Over $[z]$ the fibre is $\{[z]\}\times\ell_{[z]}$, the complex line $\ell_{[z]}$, which is well-defined (independent of the representative $z$, since scaling $z$ leaves the line $\mathbb Cz$ unchanged) and has complex dimension $1$. Its complex structure is multiplication by $i$ inherited from $\mathbb C^{n+1}$, so $I([z],w)=([z],iw)$; this is smooth and squares to $-\operatorname{id}$ because it does so on $\mathbb C^{n+1}$. **Local triviality with $\mathbb C$-linear trivialisations.** On the standard chart $U_j=\{[z]:z_j\neq0\}$ define $\psi_j\colon\mathcal O(-1)|_{U_j}\to U_j\times\mathbb C$ by $\psi_j([z],w)=([z],w_j)$, the $j$-th coordinate of $w$. This is $\mathbb C$-linear on each fibre (taking the $j$-th coordinate is $\mathbb C$-linear) and bijective: its inverse sends $([z],\mu)$ to $([z],\mu\hat z)$, where $\hat z=z/z_j$ is the unique representative with $j$-th coordinate $1$, since then $\mu\hat z\in\ell_{[z]}$ has $j$-th coordinate $\mu$. Both $\psi_j$ and its inverse are smooth. **Transition functions.** On $U_j\cap U_\ell$,
$$\psi_j\circ\psi_\ell^{-1}([z],\mu)=\psi_j\bigl([z],\mu\,z/z_\ell\bigr)=\Bigl([z],\ \mu\,\frac{z_j}{z_\ell}\Bigr),$$
so $g_{j\ell}([z])=z_j/z_\ell\in\mathbb C^\times=GL_1(\mathbb C)$, a smooth nonvanishing complex-valued function. All clauses hold, so $\mathcal O(-1)$ is a complex line bundle. (With the complex orientation conventions of the series, $\int_{\mathbb{CP}^1}c_1(\mathcal O(-1))=-1$; the computation of the Chern class belongs to Chern–Weil theory and is not needed here.) The same bundle also arises as the [[Def - The Hopf Bundle|Hopf bundle]]'s associated line bundle $S^{2n+1}\times_{U(1)}\mathbb C$, a fact placed with associated bundles.

**Is an instance — $TS^2$ with rotation by ninety degrees.** Realise $S^2=\{x\in\mathbb R^3:\lvert x\rvert=1\}$ with tangent space $T_xS^2=x^\perp=\{v\in\mathbb R^3:\langle v,x\rangle=0\}$, where $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is the Euclidean inner product and $\times$ the cross product. Define $I_x\colon T_xS^2\to T_xS^2$ by
$$I_x v:=x\times v.$$
We verify $(TS^2,I)$ is a complex line bundle. **$I$ maps $T_xS^2$ to itself.** For $v\perp x$, $\langle x\times v,x\rangle=0$ (the cross product $x\times v$ is orthogonal to $x$), so $I_xv\in T_xS^2$. **$I$ is real-linear and smooth.** For fixed $x$, $v\mapsto x\times v$ is $\mathbb R$-linear; and $(x,v)\mapsto x\times v$ is a polynomial in the coordinates, hence $I\in\Gamma(\operatorname{End}TS^2)$ is smooth. **$I^2=-\operatorname{id}$.** Using the vector triple product identity $x\times(x\times v)=\langle x,v\rangle x-\langle x,x\rangle v$ together with $\langle x,v\rangle=0$ (as $v\in T_xS^2$) and $\lvert x\rvert^2=1$,
$$I_x^2 v=x\times(x\times v)=\langle x,v\rangle x-\lvert x\rvert^2 v=0-v=-v,$$
so $I_x^2=-\operatorname{id}$. Since $\operatorname{rk}TS^2=2$, this makes each tangent plane a complex line and $S^2$ the [[Thm - CP^1 is Diffeomorphic to S^2 via the Hopf Map|Riemann sphere]] $\mathbb{CP}^1$; geometrically $I_x$ is rotation by ninety degrees in the oriented tangent plane, since for a unit $v\perp x$ the triple $(x,v,x\times v)$ is a positively oriented orthonormal basis of $\mathbb R^3$, so $x\times v$ is $v$ rotated by a quarter turn.

**Is an instance — a Hermitian structure and its relation to a Euclidean one.** Let $(E,I)$ be a complex bundle. Given a Hermitian structure $h$, write $h=g+i\omega$ with $g=\operatorname{Re}h$ and $\omega=\operatorname{Im}h$, two smooth real bilinear forms on the underlying real bundle. We verify that $g$ is a [[Def - Euclidean Vector Bundle and Metric Connection|Euclidean structure]] compatible with $I$, and that $h$ is recovered from $g$. **$g$ is symmetric.** From $h(w,v)=\overline{h(v,w)}$ (Hermitian symmetry), $g(w,v)=\operatorname{Re}h(w,v)=\operatorname{Re}\overline{h(v,w)}=\operatorname{Re}h(v,w)=g(v,w)$. **$g$ is positive definite.** $g(v,v)=\operatorname{Re}h(v,v)=h(v,v)>0$ for $v\neq0$, since $h(v,v)$ is real and positive (positive-definiteness of $h$). So $g$ is a genuine real inner product, smooth in $m$ because $h$ is; this is the sense in which *a Hermitian structure restricts to a Euclidean one*. **$g$ is $I$-invariant.** Using $\mathbb C$-linearity in the first slot and conjugate-linearity in the second (the standing convention),
$$h(Iv,Iw)=h(iv,iw)=i\,h(v,iw)=i\,\bar i\,h(v,w)=h(v,w)\qquad(\text{since }i\bar i=\lvert i\rvert^2=1),$$
hence $g(Iv,Iw)=\operatorname{Re}h(Iv,Iw)=\operatorname{Re}h(v,w)=g(v,w)$. **Recovery of $h$.** Compute $g(v,Iw)=\operatorname{Re}h(v,iw)=\operatorname{Re}(\bar i\,h(v,w))=\operatorname{Re}(-i\,h(v,w))=\operatorname{Im}h(v,w)=\omega(v,w)$, so
$$h(v,w)=g(v,w)+i\,g(v,Iw).$$
Conversely, any $I$-invariant Euclidean structure $g$ (that is, $g(Iv,Iw)=g(v,w)$) defines a Hermitian structure by this last formula, as one checks by reversing the computation. Thus Hermitian structures on $(E,I)$ are the same as $I$-invariant Euclidean structures.

**Is NOT an instance — an odd-rank real bundle.** Any real vector bundle $E$ of odd rank admits no complex structure. Indeed, if $I\in\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$ satisfied $I^2=-\operatorname{id}$, then at any $m$, taking determinants of the fibrewise identity $I_m^2=-\operatorname{id}_{E_m}$ gives
$$\det(I_m)^2=\det(I_m^2)=\det(-\operatorname{id}_{E_m})=(-1)^{\operatorname{rk}E}\qquad(\text{multiplicativity of }\det\text{, and }\det(-\operatorname{id})=(-1)^{\operatorname{rk}}).$$
If $\operatorname{rk}E$ is odd, the right-hand side is $-1$, so $\det(I_m)^2=-1$, which is impossible for the real number $\det(I_m)$ (a real square is nonnegative). The contradiction shows no such $I$ exists. The cleanest concrete witness is a real line bundle (rank $1$), for instance the trivial line bundle $M\times\mathbb R$ or the Möbius line bundle over $S^1$: neither is a complex bundle, because complex line bundles have real rank $2$. The same argument rules out $TS^3$ (rank $3$) and, more generally, the tangent bundle of any odd-dimensional manifold.

**Corollary — every complex vector bundle admits a Hermitian structure.** This is a genuine existence theorem, proved by patching local Hermitian products with a partition of unity; the drill version is [[Ex - Every Complex Vector Bundle Admits a Hermitian Structure|the corresponding exercise]], and we give the complete argument here because it is a corollary this page relies on.

> [!note]- Proof that a Hermitian structure exists (Haydys Exercise 29(i))
> **Goal.** Given a complex vector bundle $(E,I)$ of complex rank $k$ over $M$, construct a Hermitian structure $h$ on $E$.
>
> **Step 1: local Hermitian products.** By the equivalence proved in *The Definition*, choose an open cover $\{U_\alpha\}$ of $M$ together with fibrewise $\mathbb C$-linear trivialisations $\psi_\alpha\colon E|_{U_\alpha}\to U_\alpha\times\mathbb C^k$. Pull back the standard Hermitian product $h_0(z,w)=\sum_{j}z_j\bar w_j$: for $v,v'\in E_x$ with $x\in U_\alpha$ set
> $$h_\alpha(v,v'):=h_0\bigl(\operatorname{pr}_2\psi_\alpha(v),\,\operatorname{pr}_2\psi_\alpha(v')\bigr).$$
> Because $\psi_\alpha$ is fibrewise $\mathbb C$-linear, $h_\alpha$ is $\mathbb C$-linear in $v$, conjugate-linear in $v'$, Hermitian symmetric, and positive definite (these properties are inherited from $h_0$ through the $\mathbb C$-linear isomorphism $\operatorname{pr}_2\psi_\alpha$), and it depends smoothly on $x$ (as $\psi_\alpha$ is smooth). So $h_\alpha$ is a Hermitian structure on $E|_{U_\alpha}$.
>
> **Step 2: a subordinate partition of unity.** By [[Thm - Existence of Smooth Partitions of Unity|the existence of smooth partitions of unity]] on the smooth (hence paracompact) manifold $M$ — for any open cover of a smooth manifold there is a smooth partition of unity $\{\chi_\alpha\}$ with $0\leq\chi_\alpha\leq1$, $\operatorname{supp}\chi_\alpha\subset U_\alpha$, the supports locally finite, and $\sum_\alpha\chi_\alpha\equiv1$ — pick such a $\{\chi_\alpha\}$ subordinate to $\{U_\alpha\}$.
>
> **Step 3: patch.** Define
> $$h:=\sum_\alpha\chi_\alpha\,h_\alpha,$$
> where each summand $\chi_\alpha h_\alpha$ is extended by zero outside $U_\alpha$ (legitimate because $\operatorname{supp}\chi_\alpha\subset U_\alpha$, and smooth because it vanishes near the boundary of $U_\alpha$). The sum is locally finite (local finiteness of the supports), hence a smooth section, and each partial map $h_x$ is a finite real-nonnegative combination of Hermitian products.
>
> **Step 4: verify $h$ is a Hermitian structure.** Sesquilinearity and Hermitian symmetry are preserved under multiplication by the real scalars $\chi_\alpha(x)\geq0$ and under summation, so each $h_x$ is $\mathbb C$-linear in the first slot, conjugate-linear in the second, and Hermitian symmetric. For positive definiteness, fix $x$ and $v\neq0$: then
> $$h_x(v,v)=\sum_\alpha\chi_\alpha(x)\,h_\alpha(v,v)\geq0\qquad(\text{each }\chi_\alpha(x)\geq0\text{ and }h_\alpha(v,v)\geq0),$$
> and because $\sum_\alpha\chi_\alpha(x)=1$ at least one index $\alpha_0$ has $\chi_{\alpha_0}(x)>0$, for which $x\in U_{\alpha_0}$ and $h_{\alpha_0}(v,v)>0$ (positive-definiteness of $h_{\alpha_0}$); hence $h_x(v,v)\geq\chi_{\alpha_0}(x)h_{\alpha_0}(v,v)>0$. Therefore $h$ is a Hermitian structure on $E$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary — the conjugate of a Hermitian line bundle is its dual.** For a Hermitian line bundle $(L,h)$ there is a canonical bundle isomorphism $\bar L\xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }L^*$, and hence $L\otimes_{\mathbb C}\bar L\cong M\times\mathbb C$.

> [!note]- Proof that $\bar L\cong L^*$ for a Hermitian line bundle
> **Goal.** Exhibit a $\mathbb C$-linear bundle isomorphism $\Phi\colon\bar L\to L^*$.
>
> **Construction.** Recall $\bar L$ has the same fibres as $L$ but scalar multiplication $\lambda\cdot_{\bar L}v=\bar\lambda\cdot_L v$, and $L^*_m=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb C}(L_m,\mathbb C)$. For $v\in L_m$ define $\Phi_m(v):=h_m(\cdot\,,v)\colon L_m\to\mathbb C$, the map $u\mapsto h_m(u,v)$.
>
> **$\Phi_m(v)$ is $\mathbb C$-linear on $L_m$.** By $\mathbb C$-linearity of $h_m$ in its first slot, $u\mapsto h_m(u,v)$ is $\mathbb C$-linear, so $\Phi_m(v)\in L^*_m$.
>
> **$\Phi_m$ is $\mathbb C$-linear as a map $\bar L_m\to L^*_m$.** Additivity, $\Phi_m(v+v')=\Phi_m(v)+\Phi_m(v')$, follows from additivity of $h_m$ in its second slot. For a scalar $\lambda$, using conjugate-linearity of $h_m$ in the second slot,
> $$\Phi_m(\lambda\cdot_{\bar L}v)=\Phi_m(\bar\lambda\cdot_L v)=h_m(\cdot\,,\bar\lambda v)=\overline{\bar\lambda}\,h_m(\cdot\,,v)=\lambda\,\Phi_m(v),$$
> so $\Phi_m$ respects the $\bar L$-scalar multiplication and is $\mathbb C$-linear from $\bar L_m$ to $L^*_m$.
>
> **$\Phi_m$ is an isomorphism.** Since $L_m$ is one complex dimension, $L^*_m$ is one complex dimension; $\Phi_m$ is injective because if $\Phi_m(v)=0$ then $h_m(v,v)=0$, forcing $v=0$ (positive-definiteness), so $\Phi_m$ is a $\mathbb C$-linear injection between equal finite dimensions, hence bijective.
>
> **Smoothness.** In a local $\mathbb C$-linear frame $e$ of $L$ with $\lvert e\rvert_h^2=h(e,e)$ a smooth positive function, $\Phi(e)$ is the functional $u\mapsto h(u,e)$, whose value on the dual frame is the smooth $h(e,e)$; hence $\Phi$ is smooth with smooth inverse. Therefore $\Phi\colon\bar L\to L^*$ is a bundle isomorphism. Composing with the evaluation pairing $L\otimes_{\mathbb C}L^*\cong M\times\mathbb C$ gives $L\otimes_{\mathbb C}\bar L\cong M\times\mathbb C$. $\blacksquare$

**Calibration check.** Three quick verifications consolidate the definitions. First, *$(E,I)$ and $(E,-I)$ are conjugate bundles*: $-I$ satisfies $(-I)^2=I^2=-\operatorname{id}$, so $(E,-I)$ is a complex bundle, and its multiplication by $i$ is $-I$, which is by definition the conjugate complex structure; the identity map of the underlying real bundle is the conjugate-linear identification $E\to\bar E$. Second, *a Hermitian structure restricts to a Euclidean one*: its real part $g=\operatorname{Re}h$ is symmetric positive definite and $I$-invariant, as verified above, so it is a Euclidean structure — and it is exactly the structure whose orthonormal frames, further required $\mathbb C$-linear, are the unitary frames. Third, *a fibrewise volume form is a trivialisation of $\Lambda^r E^*$*: on an oriented Euclidean bundle one is produced canonically by taking, in each fibre, the top form $e^1\wedge\dots\wedge e^r$ dual to a positively oriented orthonormal frame $(e_1,\dots,e_r)$; this is independent of the oriented orthonormal frame chosen, because any two are related by an element of $SO(r)$, which has determinant $1$ and so fixes the top form, and it is nowhere vanishing and smooth. If you can also see why the determinant argument forbids a complex structure on an odd-rank bundle, and why the conjugate line bundle inverts the first Chern class, the definitions are in hand.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Hermitian and unitary connections *(from Gauge Theory IV)*
> A Hermitian structure singles out the connections that preserve $h$ — the **unitary connections**, whose local connection matrices are skew-Hermitian, valued in $\mathfrak u(k)$. For a line bundle the curvature is then imaginary-valued, $F_A\in\Omega^2(M;i\mathbb R)$, which is the starting point for the first Chern class $c_1(L)=[\tfrac{i}{2\pi}F_A]$.

> [!tip] The classification of complex line bundles *(from Gauge Theory VI)*
> The tensor product, dual and conjugate make isomorphism classes of Hermitian line bundles into an abelian group, the **Picard group** $\operatorname{Pic}(M)$, with $[L]^{-1}=[\bar L]=[L^*]$; the first Chern class gives a homomorphism $\operatorname{Pic}(M)\to H^2_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)$ that classifies line bundles up to isomorphism.

> [!tip] Almost complex structures and the Newlander–Nirenberg theorem *(from complex geometry)*
> A complex structure on $TM$ is an **almost complex structure**; it comes from an honest complex-manifold atlas exactly when its Nijenhuis tensor vanishes. The examples $TS^2=T\mathbb{CP}^1$ and $\mathcal O(-1)$ are integrable, but the bundle-level notion defined here needs no such condition.
