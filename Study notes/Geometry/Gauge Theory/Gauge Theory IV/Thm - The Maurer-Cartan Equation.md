# Notation

Throughout, $G$ is a Lie group in the sense of the standing conventions of this series: a smooth, Hausdorff, second-countable manifold with smooth multiplication and inversion. Its Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$, the tangent space at the identity $e \in G$, carrying the bracket $[\cdot,\cdot] : \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$ transported from the commutator of left-invariant vector fields; for a matrix group this bracket is the matrix commutator $[\xi,\eta] = \xi\eta - \eta\xi$. We write $L_h : G \to G$, $L_h(g) = hg$, for left translation by $h \in G$, and $d_g L_h : T_g G \to T_{hg} G$ for its differential at $g$.

The object of study is the **left Maurer–Cartan form** $\theta \in \Omega^1(G; \mathfrak{g})$, the $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form on $G$ defined pointwise by
$$\theta_g := d_g L_{g^{-1}} : T_g G \to T_e G = \mathfrak{g},$$
so that $\theta_g$ carries a tangent vector at $g$ back to the identity by left translation. This is the form introduced on **[[Def - The Maurer-Cartan Form]]**, where the two facts we use here are established: for the left-invariant vector field $\tilde\xi$ generated by $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$ (that is, $\tilde\xi_g = d_e L_g\,\xi$), one has $\theta(\tilde\xi) = \xi$ as a constant $\mathfrak{g}$-valued function on $G$; and for a matrix group $G \subseteq GL(n,\mathbb{K})$, viewing $g$ as the matrix-valued coordinate function, $\theta = g^{-1}\,dg$.

For a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $p$-form $\alpha$ and $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $q$-form $\beta$ we write $[\alpha \wedge \beta]$ for the bracket of Lie-algebra-valued forms of **[[Def - Lie-Algebra-Valued Differential Forms and Their Bracket]]**: on decomposable elements $\alpha = a \otimes \xi$, $\beta = b \otimes \eta$ (with $a \in \Omega^p(M)$, $b \in \Omega^q(M)$, $\xi,\eta \in \mathfrak{g}$) it is $[\alpha \wedge \beta] = (a \wedge b) \otimes [\xi,\eta]$, and for two $1$-forms it evaluates as
$$[\alpha \wedge \beta](X,Y) = [\alpha(X),\beta(Y)] - [\alpha(Y),\beta(X)],$$
so in particular $[\theta \wedge \theta](X,Y) = 2\,[\theta(X),\theta(Y)]$. We write $d$ for the exterior derivative, applied to a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued form componentwise in any fixed basis of $\mathfrak{g}$; $X, Y$ denote smooth vector fields on the manifold at hand; $\mathfrak{X}(G)$ is the space of smooth vector fields on $G$; and for a smooth map $g : U \to G$ from an open set $U$ of another manifold, $g^*\theta \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{g})$ is the pull-back of $\theta$ along $g$.

> [!warning] Convention: sign in Bär's intermediate line
> This page follows Bär, *Gauge Theory* (lecture notes), Remark 2.5.9, where the Maurer–Cartan form is written $\phi_g = dL_{g^{-1}}$ (our $\theta$) and the structure equation appears as $d\phi + \tfrac12[\phi,\phi] = 0$. Bär's displayed computation of $d\phi$ on left-invariant fields prints the intermediate value $d\phi(X,Y) = +[X,Y](e)$; this is a sign slip (Appendix B, item 12 of the content map). The correct intermediate value is $d\phi(X,Y) = -[X,Y](e)$, which is exactly what is needed for $d\phi + \tfrac12[\phi,\phi] = 0$ to hold together with $[\phi,\phi](X,Y) = 2[X,Y](e)$; Bär's final conclusion $d\phi + \tfrac12[\phi,\phi] = 0$ is correct, only the printed intermediate sign is wrong. The proof below derives the corrected value in full.

> [!warning] Convention: the bracket carries the factor $\tfrac12$
> The $\tfrac12$ in $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta] = 0$ is a consequence of the convention $[\alpha \wedge \beta](X,Y) = [\alpha(X),\beta(Y)] - [\alpha(Y),\beta(X)]$, which gives $[\theta \wedge \theta](X,Y) = 2[\theta(X),\theta(Y)]$ rather than $[\theta(X),\theta(Y)]$. For a matrix group, where $\tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta] = \theta \wedge \theta$ (see **[[Ex - The Bracket of Matrix-Valued 1-Forms is Twice the Wedge Square]]**), the same equation reads $d\theta + \theta \wedge \theta = 0$, with no visible fraction. Both are the same identity.

# Statement

> **Theorem (Maurer–Cartan equation).** Let $G$ be a Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$, and let $\theta \in \Omega^1(G; \mathfrak{g})$ be its left Maurer–Cartan form, $\theta_g = d_g L_{g^{-1}}$. Then
> $$d\theta + \tfrac12\,[\theta \wedge \theta] = 0 \quad \text{in } \Omega^2(G; \mathfrak{g}).$$

> **Corollary (matrix form).** If $G \subseteq GL(n,\mathbb{K})$ is a matrix Lie group, so that $\theta = g^{-1}\,dg$, then
> $$d(g^{-1}\,dg) = -\,g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg,$$
> where $g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$ is the wedge of matrix-valued $1$-forms formed by matrix multiplication of entries. This is the same identity: for matrix groups $\tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta] = \theta \wedge \theta$.

> **Corollary (pull-back form; the structure equation for a pure-gauge potential).** Let $U$ be a manifold and $g : U \to G$ a smooth map. Then the pulled-back form $A := g^*\theta \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{g})$ satisfies
> $$d(g^*\theta) + \tfrac12\,[g^*\theta \wedge g^*\theta] = 0,$$
> and for a matrix group $d(g^{-1}\,dg) = -\,g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$ with $g : U \to G$ the matrix-valued map. This is the local structure equation obeyed by every **pure-gauge** potential $g^{-1}\,dg$.

# Motivation

A Lie group is the one manifold that carries, for free, a canonical way to identify the tangent space at any point with a single fixed vector space: left translation. The Maurer–Cartan form $\theta$ is the bookkeeping device for that identification, sending each tangent vector back to $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$. Once one has $\theta$, the entire local geometry of $G$ near the identity is encoded in a single $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form, and the question that decides everything is: how non-integrable is this identification? A form on a manifold is closed exactly when the "connection" it defines has no curvature; here $\theta$ is emphatically not closed, and its failure to be closed is measured by the bracket $[\theta \wedge \theta]$. The Maurer–Cartan equation says that this failure is not arbitrary — it is completely determined by the algebraic bracket of $\mathfrak{g}$:
$$d\theta = -\tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta].$$
The equation is therefore the exact statement that *the Lie group knows its Lie algebra locally, and nothing more*: the entire non-flatness of the canonical trivialisation is the structure constants of $\mathfrak{g}$.

Its importance in gauge theory is threefold, and each will be drawn out in the Targets section. First, it is the computation behind the fact that the **product connection** $\operatorname{pr}_2^*\theta$ on a trivial bundle $M \times G$ is **flat**: the curvature of that connection is $\operatorname{pr}_2^*(d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta])$, and the Maurer–Cartan equation makes the parenthesis vanish. This is the mechanism by which trivial bundles carry no characteristic classes (chapter VI). Second, it is the local model of every gauge transformation: under a change of local trivialisation by $g : U \to G$, the connection potential picks up the term $g^*\theta = g^{-1}\,dg$, and the Maurer–Cartan equation is precisely the statement that this "pure-gauge" potential is flat by itself, which is why a gauge transformation of the trivial connection does not create curvature. Third, it is the prototype of the structure equation $F_A = dA + \tfrac12[A \wedge A]$ for a general connection: the Maurer–Cartan form is the connection potential for which the curvature is identically zero.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening).** These are the disguised hypotheses under which one is really invoking the Maurer–Cartan equation.

- *A $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form that is left-invariant and reproduces $\mathfrak{g}$ on left-invariant fields.* One rarely meets $\theta$ as "the differential of left translation"; far more often one meets a $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form $\alpha$ on $G$ that happens to be left-invariant ($L_h^*\alpha = \alpha$) and satisfies $\alpha(\tilde\xi) = \xi$ for every $\xi \in \mathfrak{g}$. Any such $\alpha$ *is* $\theta$: a left-invariant $1$-form is determined by its value at $e$, and the two conditions pin that value to the identity map $T_e G \to \mathfrak{g}$. The bridge $B \Rightarrow A$ is: verify left-invariance and the normalisation on the left-invariant frame, conclude $\alpha = \theta$, and the Maurer–Cartan equation follows. *Example problem:* on $SU(2) \cong S^3$, the dual basis $(\alpha^1, \alpha^2, \alpha^3)$ to a left-invariant frame packages into $\theta = \sum_i \alpha^i \otimes \xi_i$, and the equation $d\alpha^k = -\tfrac12\sum_{i,j} c^k_{ij}\alpha^i \wedge \alpha^j$ (with $c^k_{ij}$ the structure constants) is the Maurer–Cartan equation read component by component.

- *A smooth map into a Lie group whose derivative one must differentiate again.* Whenever an argument produces a term $g^{-1}\,dg$ for a $G$-valued function $g$ — the change-of-frame term in a connection transformation law, the logarithmic derivative of a matrix path, the phase gradient of a wavefunction valued in $U(1)$ — its exterior derivative is not free to be anything: it is forced to be $-g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$. The bridge is the pull-back corollary: $d(g^*\theta) = -\tfrac12[g^*\theta \wedge g^*\theta]$. *Example problem:* to check that the gauge-transformed potential $A' = g^{-1}Ag + g^{-1}\,dg$ has curvature $g^{-1} F_A g$, one must compute $d(g^{-1}\,dg)$, and the only way to do so is the Maurer–Cartan equation.

- *A flat connection on a trivial principal bundle presented as a product.* Any time a bundle is exhibited as $M \times G$ with the connection $\operatorname{pr}_2^*\theta$, one is implicitly asserting flatness, and the assertion reduces to the Maurer–Cartan equation. The bridge is the naturality of $d$ and of the bracket under $\operatorname{pr}_2^*$. *Example problem:* proving that a trivial $SU(2)$-bundle over $S^4$ carries a flat connection, and hence that its second Chern number vanishes.

**Targets (Output Amplification).** These are the results one produces by combining the equation with one extra ingredient.

- *Maurer–Cartan equation $+$ naturality of $\operatorname{pr}_2^*$ $\Rightarrow$ the product connection is flat.* On $M \times G$, the form $\omega = \operatorname{pr}_2^*\theta$ is a principal connection (its two defining properties are checked on **[[Def - Connection on a Principal Bundle]]**), and its curvature is $\Omega = \operatorname{pr}_2^*(d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta])$. The extra ingredient is that $\operatorname{pr}_2^*$ commutes with $d$ and with the bracket; the payoff is $\Omega = 0$, hence every characteristic form of a trivial bundle vanishes (chapter VI), so a bundle with a non-zero characteristic class is non-trivial.

- *Maurer–Cartan equation $+$ the transformation law of a connection potential $\Rightarrow$ gauge-invariance of curvature.* Under a local gauge transformation $g : U \to G$, the potential transforms as $A \mapsto \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}A + g^*\theta$. The extra ingredient is the derivation rule $d[\alpha \wedge \beta] = [d\alpha \wedge \beta] + (-1)^{|\alpha|}[\alpha \wedge d\beta]$; combined with $d(g^*\theta) = -\tfrac12[g^*\theta \wedge g^*\theta]$ it yields $F_{A'} = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}F_A$, so the curvature is gauge-covariant and $|F|^2$ is gauge-invariant.

- *Maurer–Cartan equation $+$ integration over a compact group $\Rightarrow$ the Chern–Simons number of a pure gauge.* On a compact $3$-manifold the Chern–Simons integrand of a pure-gauge potential $g^{-1}\,dg$ reduces, using $d(g^{-1}\,dg) = -g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$, to a multiple of $\operatorname{tr}\big((g^{-1}\,dg)^{\wedge 3}\big)$, whose integral is the winding number of $g$. The extra ingredient is the trace identity and Stokes' theorem; the payoff is the quantisation of the Chern–Simons functional and the instanton number (chapter VI, VII).

# Why Is It True

The single mechanism is that **$\theta$ is left-invariant and normalised so that it turns the left-invariant frame into a constant $\mathfrak{g}$-valued coframe; its exterior derivative on that frame therefore has no "derivative of the coefficients" term and reduces to the bracket alone.** Concretely, the invariant formula for the exterior derivative of a $1$-form reads
$$d\theta(X,Y) = X\big(\theta(Y)\big) - Y\big(\theta(X)\big) - \theta([X,Y]).$$
Feed it a pair of left-invariant fields $\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta$. The first two terms are derivatives of the *constant* functions $\theta(\tilde\eta) = \eta$ and $\theta(\tilde\xi) = \xi$, so they vanish. Only the last term survives, and because the bracket of left-invariant fields is again the left-invariant field of the algebra bracket ($[\tilde\xi,\tilde\eta] = \widetilde{[\xi,\eta]}$), it equals $-\theta(\widetilde{[\xi,\eta]}) = -[\xi,\eta]$. Meanwhile $[\theta \wedge \theta](\tilde\xi,\tilde\eta) = 2[\xi,\eta]$ by the definition of the bracket of forms. The two contributions to $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta]$ are $-[\xi,\eta]$ and $+[\xi,\eta]$; they cancel. Because the left-invariant fields form a global frame, a $2$-form that vanishes on every such pair vanishes everywhere.

The intuition, stripped of formulas: the only way $\theta$ can fail to be closed is by feeling the non-commutativity of $G$, and the invariant $d$-formula extracts exactly that non-commutativity as the bracket term. The Maurer–Cartan equation is the accounting identity that says the failure to be closed ($d\theta$) is minus one-half the failure to commute ($[\theta \wedge \theta]$), with the factor $\tfrac12$ a pure convention of how the bracket of forms is normalised.

For the matrix form the mechanism is even more elementary: $\theta = g^{-1}\,dg$, and differentiating the constraint $g^{-1}g = I$ gives $d(g^{-1}) = -g^{-1}\,dg\,g^{-1}$; substituting into $d(g^{-1}\,dg) = d(g^{-1}) \wedge dg$ produces $-g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$ directly, with no reference to left-invariant fields at all.

# What Makes This Hard

The genuinely non-obvious step is recognising that both terms $\theta(\tilde\xi)$ and $\theta(\tilde\eta)$ are *constant* functions, so their directional derivatives vanish; a reader who computes $X(\theta(Y))$ as though $\theta(Y)$ were a general function will not see the cancellation and will drown in extra terms. The common error is the sign, which is precisely where Bär's printed line slips: the surviving term is $-\theta([X,Y])$, and forgetting that leading minus sign gives $d\theta(\tilde\xi,\tilde\eta) = +[\xi,\eta]$, which then makes $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta] = 2[\xi,\eta] \neq 0$ and contradicts the very equation one is trying to prove. The second subtlety is that one must know the equation holds *everywhere*, not just that $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta]$ vanishes on left-invariant fields; this needs the observation that left-invariant fields furnish a global frame and that both $d\theta$ and $[\theta \wedge \theta]$ are genuine tensorial $2$-forms.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** Evaluate the $2$-form $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta]$ on a pair of left-invariant vector fields, using the invariant formula for $d$ on $1$-forms; three of the four terms are derivatives of constants and drop out; the survivors cancel; then extend from the global left-invariant frame to all of $TG$. The matrix corollary is a one-line differentiation of $g^{-1}g = I$, and the pull-back corollary is naturality of $d$ and of the bracket.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Establish the invariant formula $d\theta(X,Y) = X(\theta(Y)) - Y(\theta(X)) - \theta([X,Y])$.** *Hint:* work in a chart, expand both sides in coordinates using the coordinate formula for $d$, and watch the coefficient-derivative terms cancel against $\theta([X,Y])$. *Why needed:* it is the only tool that converts $d\theta$ into something evaluable on a frame without choosing coordinates on $G$.

2. **Assemble the three evaluation facts on left-invariant fields.** *Hint:* $\theta(\tilde\xi) = \xi$ is constant (from the definition of $\theta$); $[\tilde\xi,\tilde\eta] = \widetilde{[\xi,\eta]}$ (left-invariant fields form a Lie algebra); left-invariant fields form a global frame. *Why needed:* these are the inputs that kill the derivative terms and identify the surviving bracket.

3. **Compute $[\theta \wedge \theta](\tilde\xi,\tilde\eta) = 2[\xi,\eta]$.** *Hint:* apply the two-$1$-form formula $[\alpha \wedge \beta](X,Y) = [\alpha(X),\beta(Y)] - [\alpha(Y),\beta(X)]$ with $\alpha = \beta = \theta$. *Why needed:* it supplies the $+[\xi,\eta]$ that cancels the $-[\xi,\eta]$ from $d\theta$.

4. **Cancel and extend.** *Hint:* $-[\xi,\eta] + \tfrac12 \cdot 2[\xi,\eta] = 0$ on every pair of left-invariant fields; a $2$-form vanishing on a global frame vanishes. *Why needed:* completes the abstract statement.

5. **Matrix corollary by differentiating $g^{-1}g = I$; pull-back corollary by naturality.** *Hint:* $d(g^{-1}) = -g^{-1}\,dg\,g^{-1}$; and $g^*$ commutes with $d$ and with the (pointwise, bilinear) bracket. *Why needed:* delivers the two forms used in practice.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: Invariant formula for the exterior derivative of a $1$-form
> **Statement:** For a smooth $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form $\omega \in \Omega^1(M; \mathfrak{g})$ and smooth vector fields $X, Y$ on $M$,
> $$d\omega(X,Y) = X\big(\omega(Y)\big) - Y\big(\omega(X)\big) - \omega([X,Y]).$$
> **Hint:** Fix a basis of $\mathfrak{g}$ to reduce to a scalar $1$-form; then work in a chart and expand both sides using the coordinate expression $d\omega = \sum_{i<j}(\partial_i\omega_j - \partial_j\omega_i)\,dx^i \wedge dx^j$.
> **Why needed:** It is the coordinate-free tool that lets us evaluate $d\theta$ on the left-invariant frame; without it we could not exploit that $\theta(\tilde\xi)$ is constant.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Reduction to scalar forms.** Fix a basis $(\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_m)$ of $\mathfrak{g}$ and write $\omega = \sum_a \omega^a \otimes \zeta_a$ with each $\omega^a \in \Omega^1(M)$; the exterior derivative, the evaluation $\omega(Y) = \sum_a \omega^a(Y)\,\zeta_a$, and the bracketless operations on the right-hand side are all applied componentwise in this basis, so it suffices to prove the identity for a scalar $1$-form $\omega^a$, that is, we may assume $\mathfrak{g} = \mathbb{R}$. We drop the index and prove it for $\omega \in \Omega^1(M)$.
> >
> > **Both sides are pointwise and can be checked in a chart.** Both sides are $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear over constants and, being the values of tensors ($d\omega$ is a $2$-form; the right-hand side, by the Leibniz cancellation below, is $C^\infty(M)$-bilinear in $(X,Y)$), depend only on the pointwise values $X_p, Y_p$. Hence it suffices to verify the identity in a chart $(U, x^1, \dots, x^n)$ with $\omega = \sum_i \omega_i\,dx^i$ ($\omega_i \in C^\infty(U)$), $X = \sum_i X^i\,\partial_i$, $Y = \sum_j Y^j\,\partial_j$.
> >
> > **Left-hand side.** By the coordinate expression for the exterior derivative — for $\omega = \sum_i \omega_i\,dx^i$ one has $d\omega = \sum_{i<j}(\partial_i\omega_j - \partial_j\omega_i)\,dx^i \wedge dx^j$, which is the antisymmetrised form of the coordinate formula $d\omega = \sum_{i,j}\partial_i\omega_j\,dx^i \wedge dx^j$ (by the [[Thm - Coordinate Expression for the Exterior Derivative|coordinate expression for $d$]], which states $d\big(\sum_i \omega_i\,dx^i\big) = \sum_{i,j}\partial_j\omega_i\,dx^j \wedge dx^i$) — and using $(dx^i \wedge dx^j)(X,Y) = X^i Y^j - X^j Y^i$,
> > $$d\omega(X,Y) = \sum_{i,j}\partial_i\omega_j\,(X^i Y^j - X^j Y^i) \qquad \text{(coordinate expression for } d\text{, and evaluation of } dx^i \wedge dx^j\text{)}.$$
> >
> > **Right-hand side.** Expand each term. First, $\omega(Y) = \sum_j \omega_j Y^j$, so by the Leibniz rule for the directional derivative,
> > $$X\big(\omega(Y)\big) = \sum_{i,j}X^i\,(\partial_i\omega_j)\,Y^j + \sum_j \omega_j\,X(Y^j) \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule, } X = \sum_i X^i\partial_i\text{).}$$
> > Symmetrically,
> > $$Y\big(\omega(X)\big) = \sum_{i,j}Y^i\,(\partial_i\omega_j)\,X^j + \sum_j \omega_j\,Y(X^j) \qquad \text{(same computation with } X \leftrightarrow Y\text{).}$$
> > Third, the coordinate expression for the Lie bracket is $[X,Y]^j = X(Y^j) - Y(X^j)$ (by the [[Def - The Lie Bracket of Vector Fields|definition of the Lie bracket in coordinates]]), so
> > $$\omega([X,Y]) = \sum_j \omega_j\,[X,Y]^j = \sum_j \omega_j\big(X(Y^j) - Y(X^j)\big) \qquad \text{(bracket in coordinates).}$$
> > **Combine.** Subtracting,
> > $$X(\omega(Y)) - Y(\omega(X)) - \omega([X,Y]) = \sum_{i,j}(\partial_i\omega_j)\,(X^i Y^j - Y^i X^j) + \sum_j \omega_j\big(X(Y^j) - Y(X^j)\big) - \sum_j \omega_j\big(X(Y^j) - Y(X^j)\big),$$
> > where the reason is that the first summand collects the two coefficient-derivative terms and the remaining two summands are the $\omega_j X(Y^j)$ and $\omega_j Y(X^j)$ pieces together with $\omega([X,Y])$. The last two summands cancel, leaving
> > $$X(\omega(Y)) - Y(\omega(X)) - \omega([X,Y]) = \sum_{i,j}(\partial_i\omega_j)\,(X^i Y^j - X^j Y^i) \qquad \text{(relabelling } i \leftrightarrow j \text{ in the } Y^i X^j \text{ term).}$$
> > This is exactly the left-hand side computed above. **Conclusion.** The two sides agree in every chart, and both are tensorial, so the identity holds globally. Restoring the $\mathfrak{g}$-index gives the stated formula for $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $\omega$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The evaluation facts on the left-invariant frame
> **Statement:** Let $\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta$ be the left-invariant vector fields on $G$ generated by $\xi, \eta \in \mathfrak{g}$ (so $\tilde\xi_g = d_e L_g\,\xi$). Then: (i) $\theta(\tilde\xi) = \xi$ as a constant function $G \to \mathfrak{g}$, and likewise $\theta(\tilde\eta) = \eta$; (ii) $[\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta] = \widetilde{[\xi,\eta]}$, the left-invariant field of the algebra bracket; (iii) for any basis $(\zeta_1, \dots, \zeta_m)$ of $\mathfrak{g}$, the fields $(\tilde\zeta_1, \dots, \tilde\zeta_m)$ form a global smooth frame of $TG$.
> **Hint:** (i) is unwinding the definition $\theta_g = d_g L_{g^{-1}}$ against $\tilde\xi_g = d_e L_g\,\xi$; (ii) is the theorem that left-invariant fields form a Lie subalgebra; (iii) is that $d_e L_g$ is a linear isomorphism.
> **Why needed:** (i) makes the derivative terms in Lemma 1 vanish; (ii) identifies the surviving term; (iii) upgrades "vanishes on the frame" to "vanishes identically".
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **(i) $\theta(\tilde\xi)$ is the constant $\xi$.** By definition of the left-invariant field, $\tilde\xi_g = d_e L_g\,\xi \in T_g G$. Apply $\theta_g = d_g L_{g^{-1}}$:
> > $$\theta_g(\tilde\xi_g) = d_g L_{g^{-1}}\big(d_e L_g\,\xi\big) = d_e\big(L_{g^{-1}} \circ L_g\big)\,\xi = d_e(L_{g^{-1}g})\,\xi = d_e(L_e)\,\xi = \xi,$$
> > where the second equality is the chain rule (functoriality of the differential), the third is the group law $L_{g^{-1}} \circ L_g = L_{g^{-1}g}$, and the last uses $g^{-1}g = e$ and $L_e = \operatorname{id}_G$. Since this holds for every $g$, the function $g \mapsto \theta_g(\tilde\xi_g)$ is the constant $\xi$. The same computation gives $\theta(\tilde\eta) = \eta$. (This is the normalisation recorded on **[[Def - The Maurer-Cartan Form]]**.)
> >
> > **(ii) The bracket of left-invariant fields.** By the [[Thm - Left-Invariant Vector Fields Form a Lie Algebra|theorem that left-invariant vector fields form a Lie algebra]] — which states that the space of left-invariant vector fields on $G$ is closed under the Lie bracket and that evaluation at $e$ is a Lie-algebra isomorphism onto $(\mathfrak{g}, [\cdot,\cdot])$ — the bracket $[\tilde\xi,\tilde\eta]$ is again left-invariant and its value at $e$ is $[\tilde\xi_e, \tilde\eta_e] = [\xi,\eta]$, the algebra bracket (this is the definition of $[\cdot,\cdot]$ on $\mathfrak{g}$). A left-invariant field is determined by its value at $e$, so $[\tilde\xi,\tilde\eta] = \widetilde{[\xi,\eta]}$.
> >
> > **(iii) Global frame.** For each fixed $g \in G$, the differential $d_e L_g : T_e G \to T_g G$ is a linear isomorphism, because $L_g$ is a diffeomorphism with inverse $L_{g^{-1}}$ (so $d_g L_{g^{-1}} \circ d_e L_g = \operatorname{id}$). Hence $(\tilde\zeta_1{}_g, \dots, \tilde\zeta_m{}_g) = (d_e L_g\,\zeta_1, \dots, d_e L_g\,\zeta_m)$ is the image of the basis $(\zeta_a)$ of $T_e G$ under a linear isomorphism, hence a basis of $T_g G$; the fields are smooth because left multiplication is smooth. Therefore $(\tilde\zeta_a)$ is a global smooth frame. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The bracket of $\theta$ with itself on the left-invariant frame
> **Statement:** For left-invariant fields $\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta$ on $G$, $[\theta \wedge \theta](\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta) = 2[\xi,\eta]$.
> **Hint:** Use the evaluation rule for the bracket of two $1$-forms and part (i) of Lemma 2.
> **Why needed:** It supplies the $+[\xi,\eta]$ term of $\tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta]$ that cancels the $-[\xi,\eta]$ coming from $d\theta$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > By the definition of the bracket of Lie-algebra-valued $1$-forms — for $\alpha, \beta \in \Omega^1(G; \mathfrak{g})$ one has $[\alpha \wedge \beta](X,Y) = [\alpha(X), \beta(Y)] - [\alpha(Y), \beta(X)]$, established on **[[Def - Lie-Algebra-Valued Differential Forms and Their Bracket]]** — take $\alpha = \beta = \theta$ and $X = \tilde\xi$, $Y = \tilde\eta$:
> > $$[\theta \wedge \theta](\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta) = [\theta(\tilde\xi), \theta(\tilde\eta)] - [\theta(\tilde\eta), \theta(\tilde\xi)] \qquad \text{(evaluation rule for the bracket of } 1\text{-forms).}$$
> > By Lemma 2(i), $\theta(\tilde\xi) = \xi$ and $\theta(\tilde\eta) = \eta$, so
> > $$[\theta \wedge \theta](\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta) = [\xi,\eta] - [\eta,\xi] = [\xi,\eta] + [\xi,\eta] = 2[\xi,\eta] \qquad \text{(Lemma 2(i), then antisymmetry } [\eta,\xi] = -[\xi,\eta] \text{ of the Lie bracket).} \; \blacksquare$$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> We prove the theorem and both corollaries. Throughout, $\xi, \eta \in \mathfrak{g}$ are arbitrary and $\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta$ are the left-invariant vector fields they generate.
>
> **Step 0 — the objects are well-posed.** The form $\theta \in \Omega^1(G; \mathfrak{g})$ is smooth (its value $\theta_g = d_g L_{g^{-1}}$ depends smoothly on $g$ because inversion and multiplication are smooth), so $d\theta \in \Omega^2(G; \mathfrak{g})$ and $[\theta \wedge \theta] \in \Omega^2(G; \mathfrak{g})$ are well-defined $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $2$-forms. Both are tensorial: $d\theta$ is a $2$-form by construction, and $[\theta \wedge \theta]$ is $C^\infty(G)$-bilinear and antisymmetric in its two arguments because the bracket of forms is (by **[[Def - Lie-Algebra-Valued Differential Forms and Their Bracket]]**). Hence $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta]$ is a genuine $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $2$-form, and to prove it is zero it suffices to prove it vanishes on every pair of vectors from a global frame.
>
> **Part I — the abstract equation $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta] = 0$.**
>
> **Evaluate $d\theta$ on the left-invariant frame.** Apply the invariant formula of Lemma 1 to $\omega = \theta$ and the fields $\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta$:
> $$d\theta(\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta) = \tilde\xi\big(\theta(\tilde\eta)\big) - \tilde\eta\big(\theta(\tilde\xi)\big) - \theta([\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta]) \qquad \text{(Lemma 1).}$$
> **The two derivative terms vanish.** By Lemma 2(i), $\theta(\tilde\eta) = \eta$ and $\theta(\tilde\xi) = \xi$ are *constant* $\mathfrak{g}$-valued functions on $G$; the directional derivative of a constant function is zero, so $\tilde\xi(\theta(\tilde\eta)) = 0$ and $\tilde\eta(\theta(\tilde\xi)) = 0$ (differentiating componentwise in a basis of $\mathfrak{g}$).
> **Identify the surviving term.** By Lemma 2(ii), $[\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta] = \widetilde{[\xi,\eta]}$, and then by Lemma 2(i) applied to $\widetilde{[\xi,\eta]}$, $\theta(\widetilde{[\xi,\eta]}) = [\xi,\eta]$. Substituting the three facts,
> $$d\theta(\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta) = 0 - 0 - \theta(\widetilde{[\xi,\eta]}) = -[\xi,\eta] \qquad \text{(Lemma 2(i) twice, Lemma 2(ii)).}$$
> This is the corrected value; Bär's printed $+[\xi,\eta]$ omits the leading minus sign carried by $-\theta([X,Y])$ in the invariant formula.
> **Evaluate the bracket term.** By Lemma 3, $[\theta \wedge \theta](\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta) = 2[\xi,\eta]$.
> **Cancel.** Adding,
> $$\Big(d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta]\Big)(\tilde\xi, \tilde\eta) = -[\xi,\eta] + \tfrac12 \cdot 2[\xi,\eta] = -[\xi,\eta] + [\xi,\eta] = 0 \qquad \text{(the two contributions cancel).}$$
> **Extend to all of $TG$.** By Lemma 2(iii), fixing a basis $(\zeta_a)$ of $\mathfrak{g}$ makes $(\tilde\zeta_a)$ a global frame of $TG$; the computation just made, with $\xi = \zeta_a$, $\eta = \zeta_b$, shows $(d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta])(\tilde\zeta_a, \tilde\zeta_b) = 0$ for all $a, b$. A tensorial $2$-form vanishing on all pairs of a global frame vanishes identically (its value at each point $g$ on the basis $(\tilde\zeta_a{}_g)$ of $T_g G$ is zero, and it is multilinear). Therefore $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta] = 0$.
>
> **Part II — the matrix corollary $d(g^{-1}\,dg) = -\,g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$.**
>
> Let $G \subseteq GL(n,\mathbb{K})$ be a matrix Lie group, viewed as an open subset of the vector space $\operatorname{Mat}(n,\mathbb{K})$ near each of its points, and let $g : G \to \operatorname{Mat}(n,\mathbb{K})$ be the identity coordinate (a matrix-valued $0$-form). Then $\theta = g^{-1}\,dg$ (established on **[[Def - The Maurer-Cartan Form]]**), where $dg$ is the matrix of $1$-forms whose $(a,b)$ entry is $d(g_{ab})$, and all products are matrix products of matrix-valued forms.
> **Differentiate the constraint.** Since $g^{-1}g = I$ (the constant identity matrix) as matrix-valued functions, applying $d$ and the Leibniz rule for matrix-valued forms gives
> $$0 = d(g^{-1}g) = d(g^{-1})\,g + g^{-1}\,dg \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule; } dI = 0\text{),}$$
> hence, right-multiplying by $g^{-1}$,
> $$d(g^{-1}) = -\,g^{-1}\,dg\,g^{-1} \qquad \text{(solving the previous line).}$$
> **Differentiate $\theta$.** Using the Leibniz rule again and $d(dg) = 0$ (the exterior derivative of the exact matrix-valued $1$-form $dg$ vanishes entrywise, by [[Thm - d-Squared-is-Zero|$d^2 = 0$]]),
> $$d\theta = d\big(g^{-1}\,dg\big) = d(g^{-1}) \wedge dg + g^{-1}\,d(dg) = d(g^{-1}) \wedge dg \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule; } d(dg) = 0\text{).}$$
> **Substitute.** Insert $d(g^{-1}) = -g^{-1}\,dg\,g^{-1}$:
> $$d\theta = -\,g^{-1}\,dg\,g^{-1} \wedge dg = -\,g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg \qquad \text{(the entries of } g^{-1} \text{ are } 0\text{-forms, so they pass through the wedge inside the matrix product).}$$
> The last equality is the statement that for matrix-valued forms $M\,g^{-1} \wedge dg = M \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$ when $g^{-1}$ is a matrix of functions: entrywise, $\sum_k M_{ik}\,g^{-1}_{k\ell} \wedge (dg)_{\ell j}$ and $\sum_k M_{ik} \wedge g^{-1}_{k\ell}(dg)_{\ell j}$ coincide because a $0$-form commutes with any form under the wedge. Thus $d(g^{-1}\,dg) = -g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$.
> **Consistency with Part I.** For matrix groups the bracket of forms satisfies $[\theta \wedge \theta] = 2\,\theta \wedge \theta$ (**[[Ex - The Bracket of Matrix-Valued 1-Forms is Twice the Wedge Square]]**), so $\tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta] = \theta \wedge \theta = g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$, and Part II says exactly $d\theta = -\tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta]$, in agreement with Part I.
>
> **Part III — the pull-back corollary $d(g^*\theta) + \tfrac12[g^*\theta \wedge g^*\theta] = 0$.**
>
> Let $U$ be a manifold and $g : U \to G$ a smooth map. Write $A := g^*\theta \in \Omega^1(U; \mathfrak{g})$.
> **Pull back the equation.** Apply $g^*$ to the identity of Part I, $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta] = 0$:
> $$0 = g^*\Big(d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta]\Big) = g^*(d\theta) + \tfrac12\,g^*[\theta \wedge \theta] \qquad \text{(} g^* \text{ is } \mathbb{R}\text{-linear).}$$
> **Naturality of $d$.** The pull-back commutes with the exterior derivative, $g^*(d\theta) = d(g^*\theta) = dA$ (by the [[Thm - Pull-Back Commutes with the Exterior Derivative|naturality of the exterior derivative]], applied componentwise in a basis of $\mathfrak{g}$).
> **Naturality of the bracket.** The pull-back commutes with the bracket of Lie-algebra-valued forms, $g^*[\theta \wedge \theta] = [g^*\theta \wedge g^*\theta] = [A \wedge A]$. Indeed, writing $\theta = \sum_a \theta^a \otimes \zeta_a$ in a basis $(\zeta_a)$ of $\mathfrak{g}$, the bracket is $[\theta \wedge \theta] = \sum_{a,b}(\theta^a \wedge \theta^b) \otimes [\zeta_a, \zeta_b]$, and since $g^*$ acts only on the scalar-form factors and is an algebra homomorphism for the wedge ($g^*(\theta^a \wedge \theta^b) = g^*\theta^a \wedge g^*\theta^b$, again by naturality of pull-back on forms), $g^*[\theta \wedge \theta] = \sum_{a,b}(g^*\theta^a \wedge g^*\theta^b) \otimes [\zeta_a, \zeta_b] = [g^*\theta \wedge g^*\theta]$.
> **Combine.** Substituting the last two facts into the pulled-back equation,
> $$0 = dA + \tfrac12[A \wedge A], \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad d(g^*\theta) + \tfrac12[g^*\theta \wedge g^*\theta] = 0 \qquad \text{(naturality of } d \text{ and of the bracket).}$$
> For a matrix group, $A = g^{-1}\,dg$ (now with $g : U \to G$ the matrix-valued map), and the identical differentiation of $g^{-1}g = I$ as in Part II — valid because $g$ is a matrix-valued function on $U$ — gives $d(g^{-1}\,dg) = -g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$ directly.
>
> **Conclusion.** The Maurer–Cartan form satisfies $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta] = 0$; in matrix form this is $d(g^{-1}\,dg) = -g^{-1}\,dg \wedge g^{-1}\,dg$; and for any smooth map $g$ into $G$ the pure-gauge potential $g^*\theta = g^{-1}\,dg$ obeys the local structure equation $d(g^*\theta) + \tfrac12[g^*\theta \wedge g^*\theta] = 0$. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Structure constants of $SU(2)$ and the round metric on $S^3$.** Choose the basis $\xi_i = -\tfrac{i}{2}\sigma_i$ of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ ($\sigma_i$ the Pauli matrices), with $[\xi_i, \xi_j] = \sum_k \varepsilon_{ijk}\xi_k$, and let $(\alpha^1, \alpha^2, \alpha^3)$ be the dual left-invariant coframe, so $\theta = \sum_i \alpha^i \otimes \xi_i$. The theorem applies because $\theta$ is the Maurer–Cartan form; reading it in components gives $d\alpha^k = -\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}\varepsilon_{ijk}\,\alpha^i \wedge \alpha^j = -(\alpha^1 \wedge \alpha^2$, cyclically$)$, the structure equations of the round $3$-sphere. It is non-obvious because the same three $1$-forms, squared in the metric $\sum_i (\alpha^i)^2$, give the bi-invariant round metric, so a single algebraic identity controls both the exterior calculus and the Riemannian geometry of $SU(2)$.

**Winding number of a map $S^3 \to SU(2)$.** For $g : S^3 \to SU(2)$ smooth, the $3$-form $\operatorname{tr}\big((g^{-1}\,dg)^{\wedge 3}\big)$ integrates to $24\pi^2$ times the degree of $g$. The theorem applies through the matrix corollary: computing $d\operatorname{tr}\big((g^{-1}\,dg)^{\wedge 2}\big)$ requires $d(g^{-1}\,dg) = -(g^{-1}\,dg)^{\wedge 2}$, and the trace of a total cube turns out to be closed. It is non-obvious because the integrand looks like it should be exact (it is built from $g^{-1}\,dg$, which is "pure gauge") yet its integral is a non-zero topological invariant — the resolution is exactly that $g^{-1}\,dg$ is flat but not globally trivial.

**Logarithmic derivatives in the theory of linear differential equations.** For a matrix path $g : \mathbb{R} \to GL(n,\mathbb{C})$, the pulled-back Maurer–Cartan form $g^{-1}\,dg = g^{-1}\dot g\,dt$ is the "companion" of the linear system $\dot y = g^{-1}\dot g\,y$; the pull-back corollary $d(g^{-1}\,dg) + (g^{-1}\,dg)^{\wedge 2} = 0$ is, in one dimension, trivial, but for a two-parameter family $g(s,t)$ it is the integrability (zero-curvature) condition that makes the system consistent. The theorem applies because $g^{-1}\,dg$ is always a pulled-back Maurer–Cartan form; the connection to Lax pairs and flat connections in integrable systems is the non-obvious payoff.

# Bridges

**To the structure equation of a general connection.** The Maurer–Cartan equation is the special case, at zero curvature, of the structure equation $F_A = dA + \tfrac12[A \wedge A]$ for the local potential $A$ of a principal connection. Concretely, take the product bundle $M \times G \to M$ with the product connection $\omega = \operatorname{pr}_2^*\theta$; its local potential in the trivialising section $s(m) = (m, e)$ is $A = s^*\omega = 0$ over $M$, but along the fibre direction the connection restricts to $\theta$, and the vanishing of $F$ is the vanishing of $d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta]$. The general structure equation is obtained by adding a base-form correction $\pi^*A$ to $\operatorname{pr}_2^*\theta$ and recomputing $d + \tfrac12[\cdot \wedge \cdot]$; the Maurer–Cartan term is the piece that cancels, leaving $F_A = dA + \tfrac12[A \wedge A]$ as the residue.

**To the gauge transformation law.** When two local trivialisations of a principal bundle overlap, related by a transition map $g$, the local connection potentials satisfy $A' = \operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}A + g^*\theta$. The Maurer–Cartan equation is what guarantees that this transformation is consistent — that applying two successive gauge transformations $g_1$ then $g_2$ gives the same answer as the single transformation $g_1 g_2$ — because the cocycle term $g^*\theta$ obeys the composition rule $(g_1 g_2)^*\theta = \operatorname{Ad}_{g_2^{-1}}(g_1^*\theta) + g_2^*\theta$, whose differentiation is controlled by $d(g^*\theta) = -\tfrac12[g^*\theta \wedge g^*\theta]$. The construction is spelled out on **[[Ex - The Pull-Back of the Maurer-Cartan Form along a Product of Maps]]**.

**To de Rham cohomology of a Lie group.** On a compact Lie group the bi-invariant forms compute the real cohomology (averaging a closed form over the group by left translation lands it in the left-invariant, then also right-invariant, subcomplex). The Maurer–Cartan equation turns the exterior derivative on the left-invariant subcomplex $\Lambda^\bullet \mathfrak{g}^*$ into the purely algebraic Chevalley–Eilenberg differential $d\alpha(\xi_0, \dots, \xi_k) = \sum_{i<j}(-1)^{i+j}\alpha([\xi_i,\xi_j], \dots)$, so that the cohomology of the group is computed by the Lie-algebra cohomology of $\mathfrak{g}$. The bridge is that $d$ of a left-invariant coframe is exactly the structure-constant expression that the Maurer–Cartan equation provides.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Concept: The product connection on $M \times G$ is flat *(from principal bundle theory)*
> With $\Omega = \operatorname{pr}_2^*(d\theta + \tfrac12[\theta \wedge \theta]) = 0$, the trivial bundle carries a canonical flat connection, and all of its Chern–Weil characteristic forms vanish, giving the obstruction criterion "non-zero characteristic class $\Rightarrow$ non-trivial bundle" (chapter VI).

> [!tip] Concept: The Chevalley–Eilenberg differential *(from Lie algebra cohomology)*
> The Maurer–Cartan equation is the identity that promotes the exterior derivative on left-invariant forms to a purely algebraic operator on $\Lambda^\bullet \mathfrak{g}^*$ built from the structure constants, opening the door to computing the cohomology of a compact group from its Lie algebra.

> [!tip] Concept: Cartan's method of moving frames and the flat model *(from differential geometry)*
> A $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-form on a manifold that satisfies the Maurer–Cartan equation and is pointwise an isomorphism is, by Cartan's theorem, locally a pull-back of $\theta$ — that is, the manifold is locally the group $G$. The equation is thus the integrability condition distinguishing the flat model geometry from a curved Cartan geometry.
