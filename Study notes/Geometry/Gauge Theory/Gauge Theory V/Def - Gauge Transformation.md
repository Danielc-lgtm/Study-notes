# Notation

Throughout, $\pi\colon P\to M$ is a [[Def - Principal G-Bundle|principal $G$-bundle]] over a smooth manifold $M$, with structure group a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] $G$ acting on the **right**: $R_g(p)=p\cdot g$ for $p\in P$, $g\in G$, and this action is smooth, free, and transitive on each fibre $P_m=\pi^{-1}(m)$ (the standing conventions of this series). We write $e\in G$ for the identity, $Z(G)=\{z\in G:zg=gz\ \forall g\in G\}$ for the [[Def - Centraliser and Centre|centre]], and $\operatorname{Diff}(N)$ for the group of [[Def - Diffeomorphism|diffeomorphisms]] of a manifold $N$ under composition. The space of principal [[Def - Connection on a Principal Bundle|connections]] on $P$ — the $\mathfrak{g}$-valued $1$-forms $\omega\in\Omega^1(P;\mathfrak{g})$ with $\omega(\xi_P)=\xi$ and $R_g^*\omega=\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\omega$ — is denoted $\mathcal{A}(P)$; here $\mathfrak{g}=T_eG$ is the [[Def - Lie Algebra|Lie algebra]], $\xi_P$ the fundamental vector field of $\xi\in\mathfrak{g}$, and $\operatorname{Ad}_g\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}$ the adjoint representation. For a section $s$ of an [[Def - Associated Bundle|associated bundle]] $P\times_\rho V$ (with $\rho\colon G\to GL(V)$ a [[Def - Representation of a Lie Group|representation]]) we write $[p,v]$ for the equivalence class of $(p,v)\in P\times V$ under $(p,v)\sim(p\cdot g,\rho(g^{-1})v)$. The gauge group of a [[Def - Vector Bundle|vector bundle]] $E$, namely $\mathcal{G}(E)=\{g\in\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E):g(m)\in GL(E_m)\ \forall m\}$, is the object of [[Def - Gauge Group of a Vector Bundle]]; it reappears here in the compression section. All maps are smooth ($C^\infty$) unless stated otherwise. The full symbol registry for the chapter is on **Gauge Theory V — Parallel Transport, Holonomy, Gauge Transformations, and Flat Connections**.

> [!warning] Convention: the names $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ and $\mathcal{G}(P)$ in the two sources
> Bär (source B, Definitions 2.7.1 and 2.7.3) reserves $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ for **all** $G$-equivariant diffeomorphisms of $P$ — including those that move the base — and writes $\mathcal{G}(P)$ only for the subgroup that covers the identity of $M$. Haydys (source A, the unlabelled definition on p. 21) instead writes
> $$\mathcal{G}(P):=\{\psi\colon P\to P\mid \pi\circ\psi=\pi,\ \psi(pg)=\psi(p)g\ \forall p\in P,\ g\in G\}$$
> and calls this "the automorphism group". The clause $\pi\circ\psi=\pi$ already forces the induced base map to be the identity, so **Haydys's $\mathcal{G}(P)$ is exactly Bär's gauge group $\mathcal{G}(P)$, not Bär's larger $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$.** This series follows Bär's naming: $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ is the full equivariant-diffeomorphism group and $\mathcal{G}(P)\subset\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ is the base-fixing subgroup. Whenever we quote Haydys's formulas — the identification with $\Gamma(\operatorname{Ad}P)$, the action $a\cdot\psi=\psi^*a$ of his equation (61) — his "$\mathcal{G}(P)$" is read as our $\mathcal{G}(P)$.

This is a compound page: it introduces four interlocking notions — the automorphism group $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$, the induced base diffeomorphism $\bar f$, the gauge group $\mathcal{G}(P)$, and the reduced gauge group $\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ — together with the right action of these groups on connections, because they are inseparable: the gauge group is defined as a kernel of a homomorphism into $\operatorname{Diff}(M)$ that only exists once $\bar f$ has been constructed, and the whole apparatus earns its keep only through its action on $\mathcal{A}(P)$.

# Axiom Motivation

A [[Def - Principal G-Bundle|principal bundle]] encodes a geometric structure "up to a choice of frame": the fibre $P_m$ over a point is a $G$-torsor, a copy of $G$ with no distinguished identity element, and picking a point $p\in P_m$ is exactly picking a reference frame at $m$. Two questions then present themselves. Which self-maps of $P$ respect this structure — that is, which are the symmetries of the bundle? And, among those, which are invisible from the base $M$ — which relabel the frames fibre by fibre without disturbing the underlying geometry? The first question produces the automorphism group; the second, sharper one produces the gauge group, and it is the gauge group that governs everything downstream: the redundancy in a physical gauge field, the equivalence relation whose quotient is a moduli space, the group whose orbits the Yang–Mills and Seiberg–Witten functionals are constant along.

The desiderata for "symmetry of the bundle" are three, and each is forced. First, the map must be a diffeomorphism $f\colon P\to P$, so that it can be inverted and so that it carries smooth objects to smooth objects. Second, it must **commute with the group action**, $f(p\cdot g)=f(p)\cdot g$ for all $p\in P$ and $g\in G$; this is the condition that $f$ respect the torsor structure of the fibres, for the right action is the only structure a principal bundle carries beyond being a fibre bundle, and a symmetry that ignored it would be a symmetry of the underlying manifold $P$ rather than of $P$-as-a-principal-bundle. Third — for the gauge group specifically — the map must **cover the identity of $M$**, so that it is a pure relabelling of frames and changes nothing a base-observer can see. Dropping any one of these clauses breaks the notion, and it is instructive to see exactly how.

**If we drop equivariance** and ask only for a fibre-preserving diffeomorphism, we admit maps that mix the frames incoherently. Take $P=M\times G$ with $G$ non-abelian and a fixed non-central $g_0\in G$; the map $f(m,h)=(m,h g_0)$ is a fibre-preserving diffeomorphism, but it is right multiplication by $g_0$ and, as the calibration below verifies, $f(p\cdot g)=f(p)\cdot g$ fails whenever $g_0$ does not commute with $g$. Such a map does not act on connections by pull-back to give a connection again — the equivariance $R_g^*\omega=\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}\omega$ that defines a principal connection is precisely what the proof that $f^*\omega\in\mathcal{A}(P)$ consumes (see [[Thm - Gauge Transformations Act on Connections and Curvature]]), and it consumes the equivariance of $f$. So without equivariance the group would not act on $\mathcal{A}(P)$ at all, and the entire point would be lost. The strengthening that repairs matters is exactly equivariance; nothing weaker will do.

**If we drop the diffeomorphism requirement** and allow arbitrary equivariant smooth maps, we lose the group structure: a non-invertible equivariant self-map cannot be undone, so the automorphisms would form a monoid, not a group, and there would be no orbits and no quotient $\mathcal{A}/\mathcal{G}$ to serve as a moduli space. **If we drop the covering-the-identity clause** we get the larger group $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$, which is genuinely useful — it contains the lifts of isometries and other base symmetries — but it is the wrong group to quotient connections by, because an automorphism that moves the base carries a connection to a connection on a *different* geometric situation, not to a gauge-equivalent one. The example to hold in mind is a rotation of $S^2$ lifted to the [[Def - The Hopf Bundle|Hopf bundle]] $S^3\to S^2$: it is a perfectly good element of $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$, but it rotates the base and so is not a gauge transformation. This is why the definition installs the base map $\bar f$ first and then singles out $\mathcal{G}(P)=\{f:\bar f=\operatorname{id}_M\}$: the gauge group is defined *by cutting the automorphism group down to the transformations the base cannot detect*.

One subtlety must be resolved before the definition of $\mathcal{G}(P)$ even makes sense: given $f\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$, is there a base map $\bar f$ to speak of, and is it unique and smooth? An equivariant diffeomorphism sends whole fibres to whole fibres (if $p'=p\cdot g$ then $f(p')=f(p)\cdot g$ lies in the same fibre as $f(p)$), so it descends to a well-defined map of $M$; the content is that this descended map is smooth, which is not automatic because $\pi$ has no smooth inverse. The resolution — a local section produces the smoothness — is written out in full as the first proposition below, and only then is the kernel $\{f:\bar f=\operatorname{id}\}$ available. A reader who follows the failure analysis and this precondition can reconstruct the definition unaided: the symmetries of a torsor bundle that a base observer cannot see, made into a group by insisting they be diffeomorphisms.

# The Definition

We introduce the notions in the order in which each becomes definable.

**Automorphism group.** An **automorphism** of the principal $G$-bundle $\pi\colon P\to M$ is a diffeomorphism $f\colon P\to P$ that is $G$-equivariant:
$$f(p\cdot g)=f(p)\cdot g\qquad\text{for all }p\in P,\ g\in G.$$
The set of automorphisms is written $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$. It is a group under composition (proved as a corollary below).

Before defining the gauge group we establish the base map that the definition needs.

**Proposition (existence, uniqueness, and smoothness of the induced base map).** *Every $f\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ maps fibres to fibres and induces a unique smooth map $\bar f\colon M\to M$ with $\pi\circ f=\bar f\circ\pi$. Moreover $\bar f$ is a diffeomorphism, with $\overline{f^{-1}}=(\bar f)^{-1}$.*

> [!note]- Full proof of the Proposition
> *We must show four things: (i) $f$ carries each fibre into a single fibre; (ii) there is a map $\bar f\colon M\to M$ with $\pi\circ f=\bar f\circ\pi$; (iii) it is unique; (iv) it is smooth and a diffeomorphism.*
>
> **Step 1 — $f$ sends fibres to fibres.** Fix $m\in M$ and two points $p,p'\in P_m$ of the same fibre. By transitivity and freeness of the right $G$-action on the fibre (a defining property of a [[Def - Principal G-Bundle|principal bundle]]), there is a unique $g\in G$ with $p'=p\cdot g$. Applying $f$ and using its equivariance, $f(p')=f(p\cdot g)=f(p)\cdot g$ (by the equivariance hypothesis $f(p\cdot g)=f(p)\cdot g$). Hence $f(p')$ and $f(p)$ differ by the right action of $g$, so they lie in one fibre: $\pi(f(p'))=\pi(f(p))$. Therefore $\pi\circ f$ is constant on each fibre of $\pi$.
>
> **Step 2 — existence and uniqueness of $\bar f$.** Since $\pi$ is surjective and $\pi\circ f$ is constant on fibres, the map $\bar f\colon M\to M$ defined by $\bar f(m):=\pi(f(p))$ for any $p\in P_m$ is well-defined (independent of the chosen $p$ by Step 1) and satisfies $\bar f(\pi(p))=\pi(f(p))$, i.e. $\pi\circ f=\bar f\circ\pi$. If $\bar f'$ also satisfies $\pi\circ f=\bar f'\circ\pi$ then $\bar f'\circ\pi=\bar f\circ\pi$, and since $\pi$ is surjective this forces $\bar f'=\bar f$ (surjectivity of $\pi$). This proves existence and uniqueness.
>
> **Step 3 — smoothness of $\bar f$.** Fix $m_0\in M$. By local triviality of the principal bundle there is an open neighbourhood $U\ni m_0$ carrying a smooth local section $s\colon U\to P$ (a right inverse of $\pi$, $\pi\circ s=\operatorname{id}_U$; local sections exist because the equivariant local trivialisation $\psi_U\colon\pi^{-1}(U)\to U\times G$ of the [[Def - Principal G-Bundle|principal-bundle definition]] gives one, $s(m):=\psi_U^{-1}(m,e)$). For every $m\in U$,
> $$\bar f(m)=\bar f(\pi(s(m)))=\pi(f(s(m)))\qquad(\text{by }\pi\circ f=\bar f\circ\pi\text{ and }\pi\circ s=\operatorname{id}_U),$$
> so $\bar f|_U=\pi\circ f\circ s$. The right-hand side is a composition of the smooth maps $s$, $f$, and $\pi$, hence smooth on $U$ (composition of smooth maps is smooth). As $m_0$ was arbitrary, $\bar f$ is smooth on all of $M$.
>
> **Step 4 — $\bar f$ is a diffeomorphism.** The assignment $f\mapsto\bar f$ satisfies $\overline{f_1\circ f_2}=\bar f_1\circ\bar f_2$: indeed $\pi\circ(f_1\circ f_2)=(\bar f_1\circ\pi)\circ f_2=\bar f_1\circ(\pi\circ f_2)=\bar f_1\circ\bar f_2\circ\pi$, and by the uniqueness of Step 2 the base map of $f_1\circ f_2$ is $\bar f_1\circ\bar f_2$. Also $\overline{\operatorname{id}_P}=\operatorname{id}_M$, since $\pi\circ\operatorname{id}_P=\operatorname{id}_M\circ\pi$. Applying this with $f_1=f$, $f_2=f^{-1}$ (which is again an automorphism, by the corollary below) gives $\bar f\circ\overline{f^{-1}}=\overline{f\circ f^{-1}}=\overline{\operatorname{id}_P}=\operatorname{id}_M$ and likewise $\overline{f^{-1}}\circ\bar f=\operatorname{id}_M$. Thus $\bar f$ has the smooth two-sided inverse $\overline{f^{-1}}$ and is a diffeomorphism.
>
> Therefore every automorphism induces a unique smooth base diffeomorphism $\bar f$, and $f\mapsto\bar f$ respects composition and identities. $\blacksquare$

With $\bar f$ in hand, the remaining notions are immediate.

**Gauge group.** A **gauge transformation** of $P$ is an automorphism $f\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ whose induced base map is the identity, $\bar f=\operatorname{id}_M$. Equivalently, by the defining relation $\pi\circ f=\bar f\circ\pi$, a gauge transformation is an equivariant diffeomorphism $f$ with $\pi\circ f=\pi$ — this is Haydys's form of the definition. The set of gauge transformations,
$$\mathcal{G}(P):=\{f\in\operatorname{Aut}(P):\bar f=\operatorname{id}_M\}=\ker\big(\operatorname{Aut}(P)\xrightarrow{\ f\mapsto\bar f\ }\operatorname{Diff}(M)\big),$$
is the **gauge group** of $P$. That $f\mapsto\bar f$ is a group homomorphism, so that $\mathcal{G}(P)$ is a (normal) subgroup, is the corollary that proves source B's Theorem 2.7.2, written out below.

**Reduced gauge group.** Fix a base point $b\in M$. Restriction to the fibre over $b$ defines a map $\mathcal{G}(P)\to\operatorname{Diff}(P_b)$, $f\mapsto f|_{P_b}$ (this lands in $\operatorname{Diff}(P_b)$ because a gauge transformation covers the identity and so maps $P_b$ to $P_b$). The **reduced gauge group** based at $b$ is its kernel,
$$\mathcal{G}_b(P):=\{f\in\mathcal{G}(P):f|_{P_b}=\operatorname{id}_{P_b}\}=\ker\big(\mathcal{G}(P)\to\operatorname{Diff}(P_b)\big).$$
It consists of the gauge transformations that fix every frame over the chosen point $b$.

**The right action on connections.** For $\omega\in\mathcal{A}(P)$ and $f\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ we set
$$\omega\cdot f:=f^*\omega$$
(Haydys's equation (61)). This is a **right** action of $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ on $\mathcal{A}(P)$: for $f_1,f_2\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$,
$$\omega\cdot(f_1\circ f_2)=(f_1\circ f_2)^*\omega=f_2^*(f_1^*\omega)=(\omega\cdot f_1)\cdot f_2\qquad(\text{since }(f_1\circ f_2)^*=f_2^*\circ f_1^*\text{ for pull-back}),$$
and $\omega\cdot\operatorname{id}_P=\operatorname{id}_P^*\omega=\omega$. Restricting to $\mathcal{G}(P)\subset\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ gives the right action of the gauge group on connections that all later moduli-space constructions use. That $f^*\omega$ is again a principal connection — that the action is well-defined and lands in $\mathcal{A}(P)$ — together with the explicit transformation formulas for the local connection and curvature forms, is proved on the theorem page [[Thm - Gauge Transformations Act on Connections and Curvature]], whose statement we restate here for convenience.

> [!note] Restatement of the well-definedness theorem invoked here
> **Theorem** ([[Thm - Gauge Transformations Act on Connections and Curvature]]). For every $\omega\in\mathcal{A}(P)$ and $f\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$, the pull-back $f^*\omega$ again belongs to $\mathcal{A}(P)$, and $(\omega,f)\mapsto f^*\omega$ is a right action of $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$, hence of $\mathcal{G}(P)$, on $\mathcal{A}(P)$; the curvature transforms by $\Omega_{f^*\omega}=f^*\Omega_\omega$, and for a gauge transformation written $f(p)=p\cdot\hat g(p)$ the local forms transform by $A_{f^*\omega,s}=\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}A_{\omega,s}+g^*\theta$ and $F_{f^*\omega,s}=\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}F_{\omega,s}$.

**The table of groups.** These groups and homomorphisms assemble into the commuting diagram of source B (Definition 2.7.8), which we record as three compatible statements rather than a picture. The horizontal arrows are inclusions of subgroups, from the smallest to the largest,
$$\mathcal{G}_b(P)\ \hookrightarrow\ \mathcal{G}(P)\ \hookrightarrow\ \operatorname{Aut}(P)\ \hookrightarrow\ \operatorname{Diff}(P);$$
the two evaluation-and-descent homomorphisms are $\mathcal{G}(P)\xrightarrow{f\mapsto f|_{P_b}}\operatorname{Diff}(P_b)$ (whose kernel is $\mathcal{G}_b(P)$) and $\operatorname{Aut}(P)\xrightarrow{f\mapsto\bar f}\operatorname{Diff}(M)$ (whose kernel is $\mathcal{G}(P)$); and these are compatible in the sense that $\mathcal{G}(P)$ sits inside $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ exactly as the fibre over $\operatorname{id}_M$ of the second homomorphism, while $\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ sits inside $\mathcal{G}(P)$ exactly as the fibre over $\operatorname{id}_{P_b}$ of the first.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The gauge group has a one-line categorical description that makes its role transparent. Let $\mathbf{Bun}_G(M)$ be the category whose objects are principal $G$-bundles over the fixed base $M$ and whose morphisms $P_1\to P_2$ are the $G$-equivariant smooth maps covering $\operatorname{id}_M$ (that is, $\phi$ with $\phi(p\cdot g)=\phi(p)\cdot g$ and $\pi_2\circ\phi=\pi_1$). Then
$$\mathcal{G}(P)=\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathbf{Bun}_G(M)}(P),$$
the automorphism group of the object $P$ in this category: an element of $\mathcal{G}(P)$ is precisely an invertible endomorphism of $P$ as a principal bundle over $M$. (Every such endomorphism is automatically invertible — a $G$-equivariant map of principal bundles covering $\operatorname{id}_M$ is an isomorphism, because on each fibre it is a $G$-equivariant self-map $\phi$ of a $G$-torsor, and any such $\phi$ is a bijection: fixing any $p_0$ in the fibre there is a unique $a\in G$ with $\phi(p_0)=p_0\cdot a$ (freeness and transitivity of the action), equivariance forces $\phi(p_0\cdot h)=\phi(p_0)\cdot h=p_0\cdot ah$ for every $h\in G$, and $h\mapsto ah$ is a bijection of $G$ with inverse $h\mapsto a^{-1}h$; so $\phi$ is a bijection and the monoid of endomorphisms is already the group of automorphisms.) In the same spirit, $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ is the automorphism group of $P$ in the larger category whose morphisms are allowed to cover arbitrary diffeomorphisms of $M$, and the homomorphism $\operatorname{Aut}(P)\to\operatorname{Diff}(M)$, $f\mapsto\bar f$, is the functor "record what the morphism does to the base".

The torsor viewpoint sharpens this. On each fibre $P_m$, which is a right $G$-torsor, a gauge transformation restricts to a $G$-equivariant self-map $f_m\colon P_m\to P_m$; such a map is completely described by a single "twisting element", since fixing any $p\in P_m$ there is a unique $a\in G$ with $f_m(p)=p\cdot a$ and equivariance then forces $f_m(p\cdot h)=p\cdot ah$ for all $h\in G$ — that is, $f_m$ is left translation by $a$ read through the reference frame $p$. Changing the reference frame $p\mapsto p\cdot h$ conjugates the twisting element, $a\mapsto h^{-1}ah$, so letting $m$ vary this datum is exactly the section of the associated group bundle $\operatorname{Ad}P=P\times_G G$ (conjugation action) that the compression section records. Thus $\mathcal{G}(P)$ is the group of global sections of the bundle of automorphism groups of the fibres, $\underline{\operatorname{Aut}}_G(P_m)\cong G$, twisted by conjugation.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** The official definition presents a gauge transformation as an abstract equivariant diffeomorphism, but its operational content is a **fibrewise change of frame performed smoothly over all of $M$ at once**, and this is captured by a single $G$-valued function twisted by conjugation. Because a gauge transformation preserves fibres, we may write $f(p)=p\cdot\hat f(p)$ for a unique function $\hat f\colon P\to G$ (uniqueness by freeness of the action), and equivariance of $f$ is equivalent to the conjugation-equivariance
$$\hat f(p\cdot g)=g^{-1}\,\hat f(p)\,g\qquad\text{for all }p\in P,\ g\in G.$$
(Source A prints this relation with the typographical error "$\hat f(pg)=g^{-1}pg$"; the corrected form has $\hat f(p)$ in place of the middle $p$, as forced by substituting $f(p)=p\hat f(p)$ into $f(pg)=f(p)g$.) A function $\hat f$ obeying this identity is the same thing as a section of the group bundle $\operatorname{Ad}P=P\times_G G$, and this yields the identification proved on

> [!note] Restatement of the identification theorem
> **Theorem** ([[Thm - The Gauge Group is the Space of Sections of the Adjoint Group Bundle]]). The assignment $f\mapsto\hat f$ is a group isomorphism $\mathcal{G}(P)\cong\Gamma(\operatorname{Ad}P)$, where $\operatorname{Ad}P=P\times_G G$ carries the fibrewise group structure and $\Gamma(\operatorname{Ad}P)$ has pointwise multiplication. In particular, if $G$ is abelian then $\mathcal{G}(P)\cong C^\infty(M;G)$, every smooth $g\colon M\to G$ giving the gauge transformation $f(p)=p\cdot g(\pi(p))$; for non-abelian $G$ this recipe produces a gauge transformation exactly when $g$ takes values in the centre $Z(G)$.

So the "true name" of the gauge group is $\Gamma(\operatorname{Ad}P)$, the smooth sections of the adjoint group bundle. This is the characterisation one actually computes with — winding numbers of maps into $U(1)$, Sobolev completions in the analytic chapters, the infinite-dimensional Lie group structure — even though the geometric definition as base-fixing automorphisms is the one that explains what the object *is*.

**The vector-bundle gauge group.** When $P=\operatorname{Fr}(E)$ is the [[Def - Frame Bundle of a Vector Bundle|frame bundle]] of a rank-$k$ real vector bundle $E$, with structure group $GL_k(\mathbb{R})$, the principal gauge group coincides with the vector-bundle gauge group of [[Def - Gauge Group of a Vector Bundle]].

> [!warning] Convention: $\mathcal{G}(E)\subset\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E)$ versus $\mathcal{G}(\operatorname{Fr}(E))$
> Source A defines the gauge group of a vector bundle (chapter II) as $\mathcal{G}(E)=\{g\in\Gamma(\operatorname{End}E):g(m)\in GL(E_m)\}$ — fibrewise invertible endomorphisms — whereas the present page defines the gauge group of a principal bundle as base-fixing equivariant automorphisms of $\operatorname{Fr}(E)$. These are the same group: a fibrewise-invertible endomorphism of $E$ is the same datum as an equivariant automorphism of its frame bundle, via $g\mapsto(\text{the map on frames }p\mapsto g\circ p)$. The identification $\mathcal{G}(\operatorname{Fr}(E))\cong\mathcal{G}(E)$ is proved on [[Thm - The Gauge Group is the Space of Sections of the Adjoint Group Bundle]]; use whichever description is convenient, and remember that $\Gamma(\operatorname{Ad}\operatorname{Fr}(E))\cong\mathcal{G}(E)$ ties both to sections of the adjoint bundle.

**Physics: local gauge versus global automorphism.** In the physics literature "gauge transformation" most often means a **local** change of gauge — a map $g\colon U\to G$ over a coordinate patch $U$ that acts on the connection potential $A$ by $A\mapsto\operatorname{Ad}_{g^{-1}}A+g^*\theta$ (the matrix formula $g^{-1}Ag+g^{-1}dg$). The global object defined on this page is the coordinate-free counterpart: a genuine automorphism of the whole bundle, which in any local section $s$ *induces* a local $g=\hat f\circ s\colon U\to G$ acting by exactly that formula. The two readings are reconciled — the global automorphism acts on the connection $1$-form by the same formula a change of trivialisation does — on [[Thm - Gauge Transformations Act on Connections and Curvature]]; the mechanism is that a gauge transformation *is* a change of local section performed globally.

**Algebra: kernels and normal subgroups.** The construction is a pure instance of the group-theoretic pattern that a kernel is a normal subgroup. Both $\mathcal{G}(P)=\ker(\operatorname{Aut}(P)\to\operatorname{Diff}(M))$ and $\mathcal{G}_b(P)=\ker(\mathcal{G}(P)\to\operatorname{Diff}(P_b))$ are defined as kernels of group homomorphisms, so each is a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] of its immediate overgroup ([[Def - Kernel and Image|every kernel is normal]]). This is why the quotient $\operatorname{Aut}(P)/\mathcal{G}(P)$ makes sense as a group — it injects into $\operatorname{Diff}(M)$ as the diffeomorphisms of the base that lift to bundle automorphisms — whereas the quotient $\mathcal{A}(P)/\mathcal{G}(P)$ that we actually care about is a quotient of a space by a group action, not a quotient group.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — gauge transformations of the trivial bundle.** Let $P=M\times G$ with the right action $(m,h)\cdot g=(m,hg)$. For any smooth $g\colon M\to G$ — abelian or not — the map
$$f(m,h)=(m,\ g(m)\,h)$$
is a gauge transformation. *Verification, clause by clause.* It is smooth with smooth inverse $f^{-1}(m,h)=(m,g(m)^{-1}h)$, hence a diffeomorphism. It is equivariant: for $k\in G$,
$$f\big((m,h)\cdot k\big)=f(m,hk)=(m,g(m)\,hk)=\big(m,g(m)h\big)\cdot k=f(m,h)\cdot k\qquad(\text{by associativity in }G),$$
so $f\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$. It covers the identity, since $\pi(f(m,h))=m=\pi(m,h)$, so $\bar f=\operatorname{id}_M$ and $f\in\mathcal{G}(P)$. Note that the group element **multiplies on the left**: the recipe $f(m,h)=(m,g(m)h)$ is equivariant precisely because left multiplication commutes with the right principal action. This reconciles with the "true name" form $f(p)=p\cdot\hat f(p)$: writing $p=(m,h)$, one finds $\hat f(m,h)=h^{-1}g(m)h$, which satisfies the conjugation-equivariance $\hat f(pk)=k^{-1}\hat f(p)k$, and the arbitrary $g\colon M\to G$ here is $g(m)=\hat f(m,e)$. On the trivial bundle, then, $\mathcal{G}(M\times G)\cong C^\infty(M;G)$ for every $G$, the left-multiplying maps; the abelian-versus-central subtlety enters only when one insists on writing gauge transformations of a *general* bundle in the fibre-coordinate-free form $p\mapsto p\cdot g(\pi(p))$, which needs $g$ central.

**Is an instance — the identity.** The identity $\operatorname{id}_P$ is equivariant (since $\operatorname{id}_P(p\cdot g)=p\cdot g=\operatorname{id}_P(p)\cdot g$) and covers $\operatorname{id}_M$ (since $\pi\circ\operatorname{id}_P=\pi$), so $\operatorname{id}_P\in\mathcal{G}_b(P)\subset\mathcal{G}(P)$; under the identification with $\Gamma(\operatorname{Ad}P)$ it corresponds to the constant section $\pi(p)\mapsto[p,e]$ (source B, Remark 2.7.7), the everywhere-identity of the group bundle, which is source item B-E2.7.2.

**Is an instance — constant central gauge transformations.** For $z\in Z(G)$, right multiplication $R_z(p)=p\cdot z$ is a gauge transformation. *Verification.* It covers the identity ($\pi(p\cdot z)=\pi(p)$), and it is equivariant because $z$ is central: $R_z(p\cdot g)=p\cdot g\cdot z=p\cdot z\cdot g=R_z(p)\cdot g$ (using $gz=zg$). These are exactly the gauge transformations coming from the constant central sections; on a bundle with connected structure group and irreducible connection they will turn out to be the entire stabiliser (see [[Thm - The Reduced Gauge Group Acts Freely on Connections]]).

**Is NOT an instance — right multiplication by a non-central element.** For $g_0\notin Z(G)$, the map $R_{g_0}(p)=p\cdot g_0$ is a fibre-preserving diffeomorphism but **not** an automorphism, because equivariance fails. *Verification.* Pick $g\in G$ with $g g_0\ne g_0 g$ (such $g$ exists as $g_0\notin Z(G)$). Then
$$R_{g_0}(p\cdot g)=p\cdot g\cdot g_0\qquad\text{but}\qquad R_{g_0}(p)\cdot g=p\cdot g_0\cdot g,$$
and $p\cdot(gg_0)\ne p\cdot(g_0 g)$ because the action is free and $gg_0\ne g_0 g$. Hence $R_{g_0}(p\cdot g)\ne R_{g_0}(p)\cdot g$, so $R_{g_0}\notin\operatorname{Aut}(P)$. This is the concrete failure the axiom motivation warned of: dropping equivariance admits maps that do not act sensibly on connections.

**Is NOT an instance of the gauge group — an automorphism covering a rotation.** Consider the [[Def - The Hopf Bundle|Hopf bundle]] $\pi\colon S^3\to S^2$ with structure group $U(1)$, realised as the unit sphere in $\mathbb{C}^2$ with $\pi$ the quotient by the diagonal $U(1)$-action $u\cdot(z_1,z_2)=(uz_1,uz_2)$ and $S^2=\mathbb{CP}^1$. A unitary $A\in SU(2)$ acts on $S^3\subset\mathbb{C}^2$ by matrix multiplication; this commutes with the diagonal $U(1)$-action (scalar multiplication commutes with any linear map), so $f_A(v)=Av$ is $U(1)$-equivariant, and it is a diffeomorphism of $S^3$. Thus $f_A\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$. Its induced base map $\bar f_A$ is the isometry of $S^2=\mathbb{CP}^1$ induced by $A$, which for $A\ne\pm I$ is a non-trivial rotation, so $\bar f_A\ne\operatorname{id}$. Therefore $f_A\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)\setminus\mathcal{G}(P)$: it is a genuine bundle symmetry, but not a gauge transformation, because a base observer sees $S^2$ rotate. This is the canonical witness that $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ is strictly larger than $\mathcal{G}(P)$.

**Corollary (source B, Theorem 2.7.2) — $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ is a group and $\mathcal{G}(P)$ is a normal subgroup.** *The composition of two automorphisms is an automorphism, $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ is a subgroup of $\operatorname{Diff}(P)$, the map $\Phi\colon\operatorname{Aut}(P)\to\operatorname{Diff}(M)$, $f\mapsto\bar f$, is a group homomorphism, and $\mathcal{G}(P)=\ker\Phi$ is a normal subgroup of $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$.*

> [!note]- Full proof of the Corollary
> *We show closure, inverses, the homomorphism property, and conclude normality of the kernel.*
>
> **Closure and inverses.** Let $f_1,f_2\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$. The composite $f_1\circ f_2$ is a diffeomorphism (composition of diffeomorphisms) and is equivariant: $(f_1\circ f_2)(p\cdot g)=f_1(f_2(p)\cdot g)=f_1(f_2(p))\cdot g=(f_1\circ f_2)(p)\cdot g$ (applying the equivariance of $f_2$, then of $f_1$). The identity $\operatorname{id}_P$ is equivariant. For $f\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$, the inverse $f^{-1}$ is a diffeomorphism, and it is equivariant: given $q\in P$ and $g\in G$, write $q=f(p)$ with $p=f^{-1}(q)$; then $f(p\cdot g)=f(p)\cdot g=q\cdot g$ (equivariance of $f$), so applying $f^{-1}$ gives $p\cdot g=f^{-1}(q\cdot g)$, that is $f^{-1}(q)\cdot g=f^{-1}(q\cdot g)$. Hence $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ is closed under composition, contains the identity, and is closed under inverses, so it is a subgroup of $\operatorname{Diff}(P)$.
>
> **$\Phi$ is a homomorphism.** By Step 4 of the Proposition, $\overline{f_1\circ f_2}=\bar f_1\circ\bar f_2$ and $\overline{\operatorname{id}_P}=\operatorname{id}_M$; that is, $\Phi(f_1\circ f_2)=\Phi(f_1)\circ\Phi(f_2)$ and $\Phi(\operatorname{id}_P)=\operatorname{id}_M$. So $\Phi\colon\operatorname{Aut}(P)\to\operatorname{Diff}(M)$ is a group homomorphism.
>
> **$\mathcal{G}(P)$ is a normal subgroup.** By definition $\mathcal{G}(P)=\{f:\bar f=\operatorname{id}_M\}=\ker\Phi$. The kernel of a group homomorphism is a [[Def - Normal Subgroup|normal subgroup]] of the domain (for $n\in\ker\Phi$ and $h$ in the domain, $\Phi(hnh^{-1})=\Phi(h)\Phi(n)\Phi(h)^{-1}=\Phi(h)\,\operatorname{id}\,\Phi(h)^{-1}=\operatorname{id}$, so $hnh^{-1}\in\ker\Phi$). Therefore $\mathcal{G}(P)\trianglelefteq\operatorname{Aut}(P)$.
>
> Therefore $\operatorname{Aut}(P)\le\operatorname{Diff}(P)$ and $\mathcal{G}(P)\trianglelefteq\operatorname{Aut}(P)$. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary — normality of the reduced gauge group.** *The reduced gauge group $\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ is a normal subgroup of $\mathcal{G}(P)$, but it is not in general normal in $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$; conjugation by $h\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ satisfies $h\,\mathcal{G}_b(P)\,h^{-1}=\mathcal{G}_{\bar h(b)}(P)$.*

> [!note]- Full proof of the Corollary
> *We prove normality in $\mathcal{G}(P)$ directly, then compute the effect of conjugation by a general automorphism.*
>
> **Normal in $\mathcal{G}(P)$.** The restriction map $\rho\colon\mathcal{G}(P)\to\operatorname{Diff}(P_b)$, $f\mapsto f|_{P_b}$, is a group homomorphism: for $f_1,f_2\in\mathcal{G}(P)$ and $p\in P_b$, we have $f_2(p)\in P_b$ (a gauge transformation covers the identity, so preserves each fibre), hence $(f_1\circ f_2)|_{P_b}(p)=f_1(f_2(p))=f_1|_{P_b}\big(f_2|_{P_b}(p)\big)$, i.e. $\rho(f_1\circ f_2)=\rho(f_1)\circ\rho(f_2)$. By definition $\mathcal{G}_b(P)=\ker\rho$, and the kernel of a group homomorphism is normal ([[Def - Normal Subgroup|as above]]). Therefore $\mathcal{G}_b(P)\trianglelefteq\mathcal{G}(P)$.
>
> **Effect of conjugation by $h\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$.** Let $f\in\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ and $h\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$, and consider $hfh^{-1}$, which lies in $\mathcal{G}(P)$ (a conjugate of a gauge transformation is a gauge transformation, since $\mathcal{G}(P)\trianglelefteq\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ by the previous corollary). We compute which fibre it fixes. Set $b':=\bar h(b)$ and take any $q\in P_{b'}$. Then $\pi(h^{-1}(q))=\bar h^{-1}(\pi(q))=\bar h^{-1}(b')=b$ (using $\pi\circ h^{-1}=\overline{h^{-1}}\circ\pi=(\bar h)^{-1}\circ\pi$), so $h^{-1}(q)\in P_b$. Since $f$ fixes $P_b$ pointwise, $f(h^{-1}(q))=h^{-1}(q)$, and therefore
> $$(hfh^{-1})(q)=h\big(f(h^{-1}(q))\big)=h\big(h^{-1}(q)\big)=q\qquad(\text{by }f|_{P_b}=\operatorname{id}_{P_b}).$$
> Thus $hfh^{-1}$ fixes $P_{b'}$ pointwise, i.e. $hfh^{-1}\in\mathcal{G}_{b'}(P)=\mathcal{G}_{\bar h(b)}(P)$; the same argument applied to $h^{-1}$ gives the reverse inclusion, so $h\,\mathcal{G}_b(P)\,h^{-1}=\mathcal{G}_{\bar h(b)}(P)$.
>
> **When conjugation escapes $\mathcal{G}_b(P)$.** If $h\in\mathcal{G}(P)$ then $\bar h=\operatorname{id}_M$, so $\bar h(b)=b$ and the identity above reads $h\mathcal{G}_b(P)h^{-1}=\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ — consistent with normality in $\mathcal{G}(P)$. But if $h\in\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ has $\bar h(b)\ne b$ (for instance the Hopf-bundle automorphism $f_A$ above with $\bar f_A(b)\ne b$), then $h\mathcal{G}_b(P)h^{-1}=\mathcal{G}_{\bar h(b)}(P)\ne\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ whenever the two based reduced gauge groups differ. Hence $\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ is not normal in $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ in general.
>
> Therefore $\mathcal{G}_b(P)\trianglelefteq\mathcal{G}(P)$, whereas conjugation by base-moving automorphisms sends $\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ to $\mathcal{G}_{\bar h(b)}(P)$, so normality fails in $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$. $\blacksquare$

**Calibration check.** Three verifications the reader can carry out from the definitions on this page. First, confirm that $\mathcal{G}(P)$ really is a subgroup of $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ directly, without invoking the kernel argument: $\operatorname{id}_P$ covers $\operatorname{id}_M$; if $f_1,f_2$ cover $\operatorname{id}_M$ then $\overline{f_1\circ f_2}=\bar f_1\circ\bar f_2=\operatorname{id}_M$, and $\overline{f^{-1}}=(\bar f)^{-1}=\operatorname{id}_M$, so $\mathcal{G}(P)$ is closed under composition and inverses. Second, verify that $\mathcal{G}(P)$ acts on sections of an [[Def - Associated Bundle|associated bundle]] $E=P\times_\rho V$ by $f\cdot[p,v]:=[f(p),v]$ and that this is well-defined: if $[p,v]=[p',v']$, then $p'=p\cdot g$ and $v'=\rho(g^{-1})v$ for a unique $g\in G$, whence
$$[f(p'),v']=[f(p\cdot g),\rho(g^{-1})v]=[f(p)\cdot g,\rho(g^{-1})v]=[f(p),v]\qquad(\text{by equivariance }f(p\cdot g)=f(p)\cdot g\text{, then the associated-bundle relation}),$$
so the value is independent of the representative; because $f$ covers the identity, $\pi_E(f\cdot[p,v])=\pi(f(p))=\pi(p)=\pi_E([p,v])$, so the induced map fixes the base and is the element $\gamma(f)\in\mathcal{G}(E)$ used later. Third, decide whether $\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ is normal in $\mathcal{G}(P)$ — it is, being a kernel, and the reader who has understood the two corollaries above will also see why the same is false in $\operatorname{Aut}(P)$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Action of the gauge group on connections and curvature *(from this chapter, §5.3)*
> With $\mathcal{G}(P)$ defined, the right action $\omega\cdot f=f^*\omega$ becomes the central object: [[Thm - Gauge Transformations Act on Connections and Curvature]] proves that it preserves $\mathcal{A}(P)$ and computes the potential and field-strength transformation laws. Gauge-invariant quantities — the pointwise norm of the curvature, the Yang–Mills functional, the Chern–Weil forms — are the functions on $\mathcal{A}(P)$ constant along these orbits.

> [!tip] The gauge group as sections of the adjoint bundle *(from this chapter, §5.3)*
> [[Thm - The Gauge Group is the Space of Sections of the Adjoint Group Bundle]] identifies $\mathcal{G}(P)\cong\Gamma(\operatorname{Ad}P)$, turning the group of automorphisms into a space of sections, hence into an object that can be given a topology, a Sobolev completion, and (for matrix groups) a Banach–Lie group structure.

> [!tip] Free action of the reduced gauge group *(from this chapter, §5.3)*
> [[Thm - The Reduced Gauge Group Acts Freely on Connections]] shows that for connected $M$ the reduced gauge group $\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ acts freely on $\mathcal{A}(P)$, which is why $\mathcal{G}_b(P)$ — rather than $\mathcal{G}(P)$, whose stabilisers are the centralisers of the holonomy — is the group that produces smooth framed moduli spaces.

> [!tip] Moduli spaces of connections *(from Gauge Theory VI, VII, XI)*
> The quotient $\mathcal{A}(P)/\mathcal{G}(P)$, and its subquotients cut out by curvature conditions — flat connections in **Def - Flat Connection**, (anti-)self-dual connections in Yang–Mills theory, Seiberg–Witten solutions — are the moduli spaces whose geometry carries the four-manifold invariants. The gauge group defined here is the group whose orbits are the points of every one of these spaces.
