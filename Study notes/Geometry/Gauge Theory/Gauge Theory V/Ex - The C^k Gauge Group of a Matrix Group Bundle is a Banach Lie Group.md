# Problem Statement

Let $G \subset GL(N; \mathbb{K})$ be a closed subgroup, where $\mathbb{K} = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, and let $\mathfrak{g} \subset \mathfrak{gl}(N; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{Mat}(N \times N; \mathbb{K})$ be its Lie algebra. Let $M$ be a **compact** smooth manifold and $P \to M$ a principal $G$-bundle. Write $\operatorname{ad} P = P \times_{\operatorname{Ad}} \mathfrak{g}$ and $\operatorname{Ad} P = P \times_{\mathrm{conj}} G$ for the adjoint bundles, and let $E := P \times_G \mathbb{K}^N$ be the vector bundle associated to the defining representation $G \hookrightarrow GL(N; \mathbb{K})$ on $\mathbb{K}^N$. Fix a non-negative integer $k$.

Prove that the group $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ of $C^k$ sections of the group bundle $\operatorname{Ad} P$, under pointwise multiplication, is a **Banach Lie group** — a group that is at the same time a Banach manifold on which multiplication and inversion are smooth (indeed real-analytic) — and that its Lie algebra is the Banach space $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ of $C^k$ sections of $\operatorname{ad} P$, with the pointwise commutator bracket.

This is Haydys, Exercise 59 (item A-X2.2.9). We treat only the **matrix-group case** $G \subset GL(N; \mathbb{K})$; this is the case in which the group and Lie algebra exponentials are the universal power series $\exp$ and $\log$, so that the entire manifold structure can be built from Banach-algebra operations and no smoothness theory for the map "compose with a fixed fibrewise-smooth function" (the so-called $\omega$-lemma) is required. The general Lie group case is genuinely harder and is not treated here; see the closing remark.

**Recall.** The construction rests on: the identification of the gauge group with sections of $\operatorname{Ad} P$; the adjoint bundles as sub-bundles of $\operatorname{End} E$; the matrix exponential as the group exponential of a matrix group; the exponential as a local diffeomorphism at the origin; and the closed-subgroup theorem, which characterises $\mathfrak{g}$ inside $\operatorname{Mat}(N \times N; \mathbb{K})$ and pins the image of a neighbourhood of $0$ under $\exp$.

![[Thm - The Gauge Group is the Space of Sections of the Adjoint Group Bundle#Statement]]

By this identification the gauge group $\mathcal{G}(P)$ is isomorphic to $\Gamma(\operatorname{Ad} P)$ with pointwise multiplication; the present exercise gives the Banach-manifold refinement, replacing smooth sections $\Gamma$ by $C^k$ sections and producing an honest infinite-dimensional Lie group.

![[Def - Adjoint Bundles ad P and Ad P#The Definition]]

For a matrix group $G \subset GL(N; \mathbb{K})$, the definition page proves that $\operatorname{ad} P$ is a sub-**vector**-bundle of $\operatorname{End} E$ (fibrewise, $(\operatorname{ad} P)_m \cong \mathfrak{g} \subset \mathfrak{gl}(N; \mathbb{K}) = \operatorname{End}(\mathbb{K}^N)$) and that $\operatorname{Ad} P$ is a sub-**group**-bundle of the bundle of automorphisms $GL(E) \subset \operatorname{End} E$ (fibrewise, $(\operatorname{Ad} P)_m \cong G \subset GL(N; \mathbb{K})$), with pointwise multiplication $[p, g] \cdot [p, g'] = [p, gg']$ well defined; moreover the fibre $(\operatorname{ad} P)_m$ is the Lie algebra of the fibre group $(\operatorname{Ad} P)_m$.

![[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential#Statement]]

![[Thm - The Exponential Map is a Local Diffeomorphism at the Origin#Statement]]

![[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem#Statement]]

We use one precise consequence of the last three: there are open neighbourhoods $\widetilde{U} \ni 0$ in $\operatorname{Mat}(N \times N; \mathbb{K})$ and $\widetilde{V} \ni \mathbf{1}$ in $GL(N; \mathbb{K})$ such that $\exp|_{\widetilde{U}} \colon \widetilde{U} \to \widetilde{V}$ is a diffeomorphism with inverse $\log$, and
$$\exp\big(\widetilde{U} \cap \mathfrak{g}\big) \;=\; \widetilde{V} \cap G, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \log\big(\widetilde{V} \cap G\big) \subset \mathfrak{g}.$$
The left-to-right inclusion is the matrix-exponential theorem ($\exp(\mathfrak{g}) \subset G$); the reverse inclusion, that $\log$ carries group elements near $\mathbf{1}$ back into $\mathfrak{g}$, is the substance of the closed-subgroup theorem, whose characterisation of $\mathfrak{g}$ is $\mathfrak{g} = \{X \in \operatorname{Mat} : \exp(tX) \in G \text{ for all } t \in \mathbb{R}\}$ and whose proof shows that near $e$ the group $G$ is precisely the $\exp$-image of a neighbourhood of $0$ in $\mathfrak{g}$.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a *promote-a-set-of-sections-to-a-Lie-group* problem, the infinite-dimensional analogue of putting a manifold structure on a matrix group. The obstruction that makes it a three-star problem is that the naive route fails: $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ is a closed subgroup of the group of units of a Banach algebra, but **Cartan's closed-subgroup theorem is false in infinite dimensions**, so being a closed subgroup does *not* by itself confer a Lie-group structure. One must exhibit the charts by hand. The whole strategy is to borrow the manifold structure from a Banach algebra, in which everything is a convergent power series, and to check by hand that the power-series charts respect the subgroup.

**Assumption pattern.** Two hypotheses do the heavy lifting. *Compactness of $M$* is used everywhere continuity must be made uniform: it makes the $C^k$-norm finite and complete, makes different choices of metric and connection give equivalent norms, and turns "each $s(m)$ is close to the identity" into "$s$ is close to $\mathbf{1}$ in $C^0$", which is what lets a single small neighbourhood of $\mathbf{1}$ work for the chart. *$G$ a closed matrix subgroup* is used twice: closedness makes $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ a closed subset (so pointwise limits stay in the group bundle), and the matrix structure makes the group and algebra exponentials equal to the universal series $\exp$ and $\log$, which are analytic maps of the ambient Banach algebra and restrict, fibre by fibre, between $\mathfrak{g}$ and $G$ by the closed-subgroup theorem.

**Theorem routing.** The route has six moves. (1) Turn $C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ into a Banach algebra $\mathcal{A}$ (completeness plus submultiplicativity). (2) Realise $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ as a closed subgroup of the open group of units $\mathcal{A}^\times$, using that inversion in a Banach algebra is given by the Neumann series. (3) Build the analytic exponential $\operatorname{Exp}$ and logarithm $\operatorname{Log}$ of $\mathcal{A}$ and prove they are mutually inverse near $\mathbf{1}$, reducing the identity $\log \circ \exp = \operatorname{id}$ to its scalar form because all powers of one element commute. (4) Show, via [[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential]] and [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]], that $\operatorname{Exp}$ and $\operatorname{Log}$ restrict to mutually inverse bijections between neighbourhoods of $0$ in $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ and of $\mathbf{1}$ in $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ — this is the chart at the identity. (5) Translate the chart around the group by left multiplication and check the transition maps are analytic. (6) Read off that multiplication and inversion are analytic and that the tangent space at $\mathbf{1}$, with the commutator bracket, is $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.

**Key decision point.** The decisive move is (3)–(4): *do not invoke an abstract inverse function theorem in Banach space*; instead exhibit the chart concretely as $\operatorname{Exp}$ with inverse $\operatorname{Log}$, and prove the two are mutually inverse by the elementary fact that, for a single fixed element, all of its powers commute, so the composite $\operatorname{Exp} \circ \operatorname{Log}$ and $\operatorname{Log} \circ \operatorname{Exp}$ are governed coefficient-by-coefficient by the classical scalar identities $\exp(\log(1 + y)) = 1 + y$ and $\log(\exp x) = x$. This sidesteps both the Banach inverse function theorem and the $\omega$-lemma, and it is exactly the simplification the matrix hypothesis buys.

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations, in the sense of the chapter's topic-page Legal Operations (to be reconciled by number when that page is written):

1. **Complete a space of sections to a Banach space by a fixed metric-and-connection norm.** Fix a fibre metric and a connection and set $\|s\|_{C^k} = \max_{0 \le j \le k} \sup_M |\nabla^j s|$; over a compact base this is a complete norm, and different choices give equivalent norms.

2. **Turn a bundle of associative algebras into a Banach algebra.** Pointwise composition of endomorphisms makes $C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ an associative unital algebra; submultiplicativity of the (renormed) $C^k$-norm makes it a Banach algebra.

3. **Realise the group as a closed subgroup of the units of the algebra.** Use that a $C^k$ section is invertible in the algebra exactly when it is fibrewise invertible (compactness plus the Neumann series makes the pointwise inverse $C^k$), and that closedness of $G$ makes the subgroup closed.

4. **Borrow charts from the Banach-algebra exponential and logarithm.** The universal power series $\operatorname{Exp}$, $\operatorname{Log}$ are analytic and mutually inverse near $\mathbf{1}$; this is the manifold structure of $\mathcal{A}^\times$.

5. **Restrict the charts to the subgroup fibre by fibre.** The matrix-exponential theorem and the closed-subgroup theorem make $\operatorname{Exp}$, $\operatorname{Log}$ carry $\operatorname{ad} P$-sections to $\operatorname{Ad} P$-sections and back, giving a submanifold chart at $\mathbf{1}$ modelled on $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.

6. **Translate a single chart around the group by left multiplication.** Left multiplication by a fixed element is a bounded linear (hence analytic) automorphism of the algebra preserving the subgroup, so one chart at $\mathbf{1}$ generates a compatible atlas with analytic transitions.

7. **Read the Lie algebra off the tangent space at the identity.** The model space $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ is the tangent space at $\mathbf{1}$; the commutator bracket of the ambient unit group restricts to it because $\mathfrak{g}$ is a Lie subalgebra.

Instead, embed everything into the Banach algebra $\mathcal{A} = C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E)$, where $\operatorname{ad} P$ and $\operatorname{Ad} P$ sit fibrewise inside $\operatorname{End} E$ (this uses the matrix hypothesis $G \subset GL(N; \mathbb{K})$). First convince yourself $\mathcal{A}$ is a Banach algebra: complete (compactness of $M$) and submultiplicative under pointwise composition.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> The group of units $\mathcal{A}^\times$ of any Banach algebra is open and is a Banach Lie group: inversion is given locally by the Neumann series $s^{-1} = \sum_{n \ge 0} (\mathbf{1} - s)^n$ when $\|\mathbf{1} - s\| < 1$. Show $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ is a *closed subgroup* of $\mathcal{A}^\times$. Warn yourself: in infinite dimensions "closed subgroup" is not enough to be a Lie subgroup — you still owe explicit charts.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> Build charts from the exponential $\operatorname{Exp}(a) = \sum a^n/n!$ and logarithm $\operatorname{Log}(\mathbf{1} + b) = \sum (-1)^{n+1} b^n / n$ of $\mathcal{A}$. They are analytic and mutually inverse near $\mathbf{1}$. The only reason the abstract identity $\operatorname{Log}(\operatorname{Exp}(a)) = a$ holds is that all powers of a *single* element $a$ commute, so the double series collapses by the classical scalar identity $\log(\exp x) = x$ applied coefficient by coefficient.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> Now restrict. For $\eta \in C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$, $\operatorname{Exp}(\eta)(m) = \exp(\eta(m)) \in G$ by [[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential]], so $\operatorname{Exp}(\eta) \in C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$. Conversely, for $s \in C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ close to $\mathbf{1}$, $\operatorname{Log}(s)(m) = \log(s(m)) \in \mathfrak{g}$ by [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]] (near $e$, $G = \exp(\text{nbhd of }0 \text{ in } \mathfrak{g})$), so $\operatorname{Log}(s) \in C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$. Compactness turns "each $s(m)$ near $e$" into "$s$ near $\mathbf{1}$ in $C^0$". This is your chart at $\mathbf{1}$; translate by left multiplication to cover the group.

# Solution

The plan is to manufacture the group as a piece of a Banach algebra. First we make $\mathcal{A} = C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ a Banach algebra (completeness from compactness, submultiplicativity from the Leibniz rule). Then $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ becomes a closed subgroup of the open unit group $\mathcal{A}^\times$, whose manifold structure comes from the analytic exponential and logarithm; the crux is that these two power series restrict, fibre by fibre, to mutually inverse maps between $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ near $0$ and $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ near $\mathbf{1}$, because the matrix exponential carries $\mathfrak{g}$ into $G$ and the closed-subgroup theorem carries $G$ near $e$ back into $\mathfrak{g}$. Left translation spreads this one chart into an atlas with analytic transitions, and analyticity of multiplication and inversion (algebra product and Neumann series) finishes the Lie-group structure; the tangent space at $\mathbf{1}$, with the commutator bracket, is $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.

Throughout, fix once and for all a fibre metric $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ on $E$ and a connection $\nabla$ on $E$; these induce a fibre metric and a connection (still written $\nabla$) on $\operatorname{End} E$ and on all tensor bundles $T^*M^{\otimes j} \otimes \operatorname{End} E$, with the identity endomorphism parallel, $\nabla \mathbf{1} = 0$. On each fibre we use the operator norm $|\phi| := \sup_{|v| \le 1} |\phi v|$ on $\operatorname{End}(E_m)$, which is submultiplicative, $|\phi \psi| \le |\phi| |\psi|$, and satisfies $|\mathbf{1}| = 1$. Set
$$\|s\|_{C^k} \;:=\; \max_{0 \le j \le k} \sup_{m \in M} |(\nabla^j s)(m)|, \qquad s \in C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E),$$
finite because $M$ is compact and $s$ is $C^k$.

**Step 0: The candidate model space $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ is a closed linear subspace.**

$C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P) = \{\eta \in C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E) : \eta(m) \in (\operatorname{ad} P)_m \ \forall m\}$ is a closed vector subspace, hence a Banach space (once completeness is proved in Step 1).

> [!note]- Derivation
> By [[Def - Adjoint Bundles ad P and Ad P]], $\operatorname{ad} P$ is a smooth sub-vector-bundle of $\operatorname{End} E$; in particular each fibre $(\operatorname{ad} P)_m$ is a linear subspace of $\operatorname{End}(E_m)$, so pointwise linear combinations of sections of $\operatorname{ad} P$ are again sections of $\operatorname{ad} P$, and $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ is a linear subspace of $\mathcal{A}$. **Closedness.** Convergence in $\|\cdot\|_{C^k}$ implies, in particular, uniform (hence pointwise) convergence of the sections themselves ($j = 0$ term of the norm). If $\eta_n \to \eta$ in $\mathcal{A}$ with $\eta_n(m) \in (\operatorname{ad} P)_m$ for all $m, n$, then for each fixed $m$, $\eta(m) = \lim_n \eta_n(m)$ lies in $(\operatorname{ad} P)_m$ because $(\operatorname{ad} P)_m$ is a finite-dimensional, hence closed, subspace of $\operatorname{End}(E_m)$. Thus $\eta \in C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$, and the subspace is closed. A closed subspace of a Banach space is a Banach space. Moreover it is *complemented*: the fibrewise $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$-orthogonal projection $\Pi_m \colon \operatorname{End}(E_m) \to (\operatorname{ad} P)_m$ assembles into a smooth bundle endomorphism $\Pi$ of $\operatorname{End} E$, and composition with $\Pi$ is a bounded projection of $\mathcal{A}$ onto $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$, giving the topological direct sum $\mathcal{A} = C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P) \oplus C^k(M; \ker \Pi)$. (Complementedness is needed only for the submanifold interpretation in Step 4.)

**Step 1: $\mathcal{A} = C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ is a Banach algebra.**

Under pointwise composition $\mathcal{A}$ is an associative unital algebra, complete in $\|\cdot\|_{C^k}$, with an equivalent submultiplicative norm; hence a Banach algebra.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **(a) Completeness (Lemma A).** We claim $(\mathcal{A}, \|\cdot\|_{C^k})$ is complete.
>
> *Local model.* Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and bounded, $\overline{\Omega}$ its compact closure, and $C^k(\overline{\Omega}; \mathbb{R}^r)$ carry $\|f\| = \max_{|\alpha| \le k} \sup_{\overline{\Omega}} |\partial^\alpha f|$. If $(f_j)$ is Cauchy, then for each multi-index $\alpha$ with $|\alpha| \le k$ the sequence $(\partial^\alpha f_j)$ is uniformly Cauchy, hence converges uniformly to some continuous $g_\alpha$; write $f := g_0$. We show $f \in C^k$ with $\partial^\alpha f = g_\alpha$, by induction on $|\alpha|$. The inductive step is the single-derivative statement: *if $h_j \to h$ uniformly and $\partial_i h_j \to k_i$ uniformly on a convex neighbourhood, then $\partial_i h = k_i$.* Fix $x$ and a segment $[x, x + t e_i] \subset \Omega$. By the fundamental theorem of calculus, $h_j(x + t e_i) - h_j(x) = \int_0^t (\partial_i h_j)(x + s e_i)\, ds$. Since $\partial_i h_j \to k_i$ uniformly on the compact segment, we may pass the limit through the integral (uniform convergence permits interchange of limit and integral on a compact interval), giving $h(x + t e_i) - h(x) = \int_0^t k_i(x + s e_i)\, ds$. The right side is differentiable in $t$ with derivative $k_i(x + t e_i)$ (fundamental theorem of calculus, $k_i$ continuous), so at $t = 0$, $\partial_i h(x) = k_i(x)$. Applying this to $h = g_\alpha$, $\partial_i h_j = \partial_i \partial^\alpha f_j = \partial^{\alpha + e_i} f_j \to g_{\alpha + e_i}$, the induction climbs from order $|\alpha|$ to order $|\alpha| + 1$ and shows $\partial^\alpha f = g_\alpha$ for all $|\alpha| \le k$; hence $f_j \to f$ in $C^k(\overline{\Omega}; \mathbb{R}^r)$, which is therefore complete.
>
> *Globalisation.* Choose a finite atlas $\{(U_\alpha, \varphi_\alpha)\}_{\alpha = 1}^L$ trivialising $\operatorname{End} E$, with local frames, and compact sets $K_\alpha \subset U_\alpha$ with $\bigcup_\alpha \operatorname{int} K_\alpha = M$ (possible since $M$ is compact). In the frame and coordinates on $K_\alpha$, a section $s$ has component functions $s_\alpha \in C^k(\varphi_\alpha(K_\alpha); \mathbb{K}^{N^2})$. On the compact $K_\alpha$ the metric, the frame, and the Christoffel-type symbols of $\nabla$ are bounded together with all their derivatives up to order $k$; consequently $\nabla^j s$ and the coordinate partials $\partial^\beta s_\alpha$ ($|\beta| \le k$) are related by an invertible triangular linear system with bounded, boundedly-differentiable coefficients. Taking the maximum over the finite atlas yields two-sided bounds
> $$c\, \|s\|_{C^k} \;\le\; \max_\alpha \|s_\alpha\|_{C^k(\varphi_\alpha(K_\alpha))} \;\le\; C\, \|s\|_{C^k}$$
> for constants $0 < c \le C$ independent of $s$. Hence the global norm is equivalent to $N(s) := \max_\alpha \|s_\alpha\|_{C^k(\varphi_\alpha(K_\alpha))}$. A $\|\cdot\|_{C^k}$-Cauchy sequence is $N$-Cauchy, so each component sequence $(s_\alpha)$ is Cauchy in the complete local model $C^k(\varphi_\alpha(K_\alpha); \mathbb{K}^{N^2})$ and converges; the limits agree on overlaps (uniform limits are consistent where the sections overlap) and patch to a global $C^k$ section $s$ with $s_j \to s$ in $N$, hence in $\|\cdot\|_{C^k}$. Therefore $\mathcal{A}$ is complete. $\square$ (Lemma A)
>
> **(b) Algebra structure.** Pointwise composition $(s \cdot t)(m) := s(m) \circ t(m)$ is associative, bilinear, and has unit the identity section $\mathbf{1}$ (with $s \cdot t \in C^k$ because $\nabla$ obeys the Leibniz rule for composition, so all derivatives up to order $k$ exist and are continuous). Composition is a parallel bundle map $\operatorname{End} E \otimes \operatorname{End} E \to \operatorname{End} E$, whence the Leibniz rule
> $$\nabla^j(s \cdot t) \;=\; \sum_{i = 0}^{j} \binom{j}{i} (\nabla^i s) \cdot (\nabla^{j - i} t) \qquad (\text{Leibniz rule, iterated}).$$
> **(c) Submultiplicativity.** Using the fibrewise operator-norm submultiplicativity $|\phi \psi| \le |\phi||\psi|$,
> $$|\nabla^j(s \cdot t)| \;\le\; \sum_{i = 0}^{j} \binom{j}{i} |\nabla^i s|\, |\nabla^{j - i} t| \;\le\; \Big(\sum_{i = 0}^{j} \binom{j}{i}\Big) \|s\|_{C^k} \|t\|_{C^k} \;=\; 2^j \|s\|_{C^k} \|t\|_{C^k} \;\le\; 2^k \|s\|_{C^k} \|t\|_{C^k},$$
> so $\|s \cdot t\|_{C^k} \le 2^k \|s\|_{C^k} \|t\|_{C^k}$. Define the equivalent norm $\|s\| := 2^k \|s\|_{C^k}$; then $\|s \cdot t\| = 2^k \|s \cdot t\|_{C^k} \le 2^k \cdot 2^k \|s\|_{C^k} \|t\|_{C^k} = \|s\| \|t\|$, submultiplicative. With this norm $\mathcal{A}$ is a complete normed associative unital algebra, that is, a Banach algebra. We use $\|\cdot\|$ from now on; it defines the same topology as $\|\cdot\|_{C^k}$.

**Step 2: $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ is a closed subgroup of the open unit group $\mathcal{A}^\times$.**

The units of $\mathcal{A}$ are the fibrewise-invertible $C^k$ sections; they form an open set, inversion is analytic, and $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ is a subgroup of $\mathcal{A}^\times$ that is closed in $\mathcal{A}^\times$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **Units are the fibrewise invertibles.** If $s \in \mathcal{A}^\times$ with inverse $t \in \mathcal{A}$, then $s(m) t(m) = t(m) s(m) = \mathbf{1}$ for all $m$, so each $s(m) \in GL(E_m)$. Conversely suppose $s \in \mathcal{A}$ with $s(m) \in GL(E_m)$ for every $m$. Then $m \mapsto s(m)^{-1}$ is continuous (Cramer's rule) and, $M$ being compact, $\inf_m |\det s(m)| > 0$; the inverse is $C^k$ because on each $K_\alpha$ it is a rational (hence $C^\infty$) function of the $C^k$ entries of $s$, so $s^{-1} := (m \mapsto s(m)^{-1}) \in \mathcal{A}$ and $s \cdot s^{-1} = s^{-1} \cdot s = \mathbf{1}$. Hence $\mathcal{A}^\times = \{s \in \mathcal{A} : s(m) \in GL(E_m)\ \forall m\}$.
>
> **Openness and analyticity of inversion (Neumann series).** In any Banach algebra, if $\|\mathbf{1} - s\| < 1$ then $s$ is invertible with $s^{-1} = \sum_{n \ge 0} (\mathbf{1} - s)^n$, the series converging absolutely because $\|(\mathbf{1} - s)^n\| \le \|\mathbf{1} - s\|^n$ and $\|\mathbf{1} - s\| < 1$ (geometric majorant). Thus the ball $\{s : \|\mathbf{1} - s\| < 1\}$ lies in $\mathcal{A}^\times$, and on it inversion is given by a norm-convergent power series in $(\mathbf{1} - s)$, hence is analytic. For arbitrary $s_0 \in \mathcal{A}^\times$, writing $s = s_0(\mathbf{1} - s_0^{-1}(s_0 - s))$ shows $s \in \mathcal{A}^\times$ whenever $\|s_0 - s\| < \|s_0^{-1}\|^{-1}$, so $\mathcal{A}^\times$ is open, and $s^{-1} = (\mathbf{1} - s_0^{-1}(s_0 - s))^{-1} s_0^{-1}$ is analytic near $s_0$ by the same series. Therefore $\mathcal{A}^\times$ is an open subset of $\mathcal{A}$ on which multiplication (the restriction of the bounded bilinear algebra product) and inversion are analytic — the group of units is a Banach Lie group modelled on $\mathcal{A}$.
>
> **The subgroup.** Set $\mathcal{H} := C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P) = \{s \in \mathcal{A} : s(m) \in (\operatorname{Ad} P)_m\ \forall m\}$. Each $(\operatorname{Ad} P)_m \cong G$ is a subgroup of $GL(E_m)$, so $\mathcal{H} \subset \mathcal{A}^\times$; it contains $\mathbf{1}$ (as $e \in G$), is closed under the pointwise product ($G$ closed under multiplication) and under inversion (with $s^{-1}(m) = s(m)^{-1} \in (\operatorname{Ad} P)_m$ since $G$ is closed under inversion, and $s^{-1} \in \mathcal{A}$ by the paragraph above). Hence $\mathcal{H}$ is a subgroup of $\mathcal{A}^\times$.
>
> **Closedness.** Suppose $s_n \to s$ in $\mathcal{A}^\times$ with $s_n \in \mathcal{H}$. Convergence in $\mathcal{A}$ gives uniform, hence pointwise, convergence, so for each $m$, $s(m) = \lim_n s_n(m)$ lies in the closure of $(\operatorname{Ad} P)_m$ inside $GL(E_m)$. But $(\operatorname{Ad} P)_m \cong G$ is **closed** in $GL(N; \mathbb{K}) \cong GL(E_m)$ by hypothesis, and $s(m) \in GL(E_m)$ because $s \in \mathcal{A}^\times$; therefore $s(m) \in (\operatorname{Ad} P)_m$ for every $m$, i.e. $s \in \mathcal{H}$. So $\mathcal{H}$ is closed in $\mathcal{A}^\times$. (As warned, closedness is not yet a Lie-subgroup structure — Steps 3–4 supply the charts.)

**Step 3: The analytic exponential and logarithm of $\mathcal{A}$ are mutually inverse near $\mathbf{1}$.**

The Banach-algebra series $\operatorname{Exp}$ and $\operatorname{Log}$ are analytic and invert each other on suitable neighbourhoods of $0$ and $\mathbf{1}$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **Definition and convergence.** For $a \in \mathcal{A}$ set $\operatorname{Exp}(a) := \sum_{n \ge 0} a^n / n!$, and for $\|b\| < 1$ set $\operatorname{Log}(\mathbf{1} + b) := \sum_{n \ge 1} (-1)^{n + 1} b^n / n$. Both converge absolutely in the Banach algebra: $\sum \|a^n\|/n! \le \sum \|a\|^n / n! = e^{\|a\|} < \infty$ (submultiplicativity), and $\sum \|b^n\|/n \le \sum \|b\|^n / n = -\log(1 - \|b\|) < \infty$ for $\|b\| < 1$. Each is a norm-convergent series of the bounded homogeneous polynomials $a \mapsto a^n/n!$, respectively $b \mapsto (-1)^{n+1} b^n/n$, hence defines an analytic map ($\operatorname{Exp} \colon \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{A}$; $\operatorname{Log} \colon \{\mathbf{1} + b : \|b\| < 1\} \to \mathcal{A}$).
>
> **Mutual inverses.** Fix $a \in \mathcal{A}$. All powers $a^n$ commute with one another, so $\operatorname{Exp}(a)$, $\operatorname{Log}(\mathbf{1} + b)$ with $b$ a series in $a$, and their composites lie in the commutative closed subalgebra generated by $a$ and $\mathbf{1}$. Within a commutative Banach algebra, an absolutely convergent double series may be rearranged (Cauchy product / Fubini for absolutely convergent series), so the coefficients of the composite power series in $a$ are exactly the coefficients of the classical scalar composites. The scalar identities $\log(\exp x) = x$ (for $|x|$ small) and $\exp(\log(1 + y)) = 1 + y$ (for $|y| < 1$) hold as convergent real/complex power series, so the corresponding coefficient identities give
> $$\operatorname{Log}(\operatorname{Exp}(a)) = a \quad (\|a\| \text{ small}), \qquad \operatorname{Exp}(\operatorname{Log}(\mathbf{1} + b)) = \mathbf{1} + b \quad (\|b\| < 1).$$
> Concretely, choose $r \in (0, \log 2)$ so that $\|a\| < r$ forces $\|\operatorname{Exp}(a) - \mathbf{1}\| = \|\sum_{n \ge 1} a^n/n!\| \le e^{\|a\|} - 1 < 1$, keeping $\operatorname{Exp}(a)$ in the domain of $\operatorname{Log}$. Then $\operatorname{Exp}$ restricts to an analytic bijection from the ball $U_0 := \{a : \|a\| < r\}$ onto an open neighbourhood $V_{\mathbf{1}} := \operatorname{Exp}(U_0)$ of $\mathbf{1}$, with analytic inverse $\operatorname{Log}$. This is a chart of $\mathcal{A}^\times$ at $\mathbf{1}$, confirming that $\mathcal{A}^\times$ is a Banach Lie group with Lie algebra $\mathcal{A}$ under the commutator bracket $[a, b] = ab - ba$.

**Step 4: The charts restrict to $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$; the chart at the identity.**

$\operatorname{Exp}$ and $\operatorname{Log}$ carry $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$-neighbourhoods of $0$ to $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$-neighbourhoods of $\mathbf{1}$ and back, giving a chart at $\mathbf{1}$ modelled on the Banach space $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **Evaluation intertwines the two exponentials.** For each $m$, evaluation $\operatorname{ev}_m \colon \mathcal{A} \to \operatorname{End}(E_m)$, $s \mapsto s(m)$, is a continuous unital algebra homomorphism, so it commutes with the (absolutely convergent) power series: $\operatorname{Exp}(a)(m) = \exp(a(m))$ and $\operatorname{Log}(\mathbf{1} + b)(m) = \log(\mathbf{1} + b(m))$, where $\exp, \log$ are the matrix series on $\operatorname{End}(E_m) \cong \operatorname{Mat}(N \times N; \mathbb{K})$.
>
> **$\operatorname{Exp}$ maps $\operatorname{ad} P$-sections to $\operatorname{Ad} P$-sections.** Let $\eta \in C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$. For each $m$, $\eta(m) \in (\operatorname{ad} P)_m \cong \mathfrak{g}$, and by [[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential]] (the group exponential of the matrix group $G$ is the matrix series, and $\exp(\mathfrak{g}) \subset G$), $\operatorname{Exp}(\eta)(m) = \exp(\eta(m)) \in G \cong (\operatorname{Ad} P)_m$. Since also $\operatorname{Exp}(\eta) \in \mathcal{A} = C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ (completeness, Step 1), we conclude $\operatorname{Exp}(\eta) \in C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$. Thus $\operatorname{Exp}\big(C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)\big) \subset C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$.
>
> **$\operatorname{Log}$ maps $\operatorname{Ad} P$-sections near $\mathbf{1}$ to $\operatorname{ad} P$-sections.** Let $\widetilde{U} \ni 0$, $\widetilde{V} \ni e$ be the neighbourhoods in $\operatorname{Mat}(N \times N; \mathbb{K})$, $GL(N; \mathbb{K})$ from the Recall, with $\exp|_{\widetilde{U}}$ a diffeomorphism onto $\widetilde{V}$, $\log = (\exp|_{\widetilde{U}})^{-1}$, and $\exp(\widetilde{U} \cap \mathfrak{g}) = \widetilde{V} \cap G$ (this is [[Thm - The Exponential Map is a Local Diffeomorphism at the Origin]] together with the closed-subgroup characterisation of $\mathfrak{g}$ in [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]]). In particular $\log(\widetilde{V} \cap G) \subset \mathfrak{g}$. The set $\mathcal{W} := \{s \in \mathcal{H} : s(m) \in \widetilde{V} \cap G\ \forall m\}$ is, by compactness of $M$, a $C^0$-neighbourhood of $\mathbf{1}$ in $\mathcal{H}$ (if $\sup_m |s(m) - \mathbf{1}|$ is small enough, each $s(m)$ lies in the fixed neighbourhood $\widetilde{V}$; here we use the finiteness of a trivialising atlas to compare the fibre norms uniformly). Shrinking, assume also $\mathcal{W} \subset V_{\mathbf{1}}$ so $\operatorname{Log}$ is defined on it. For $s \in \mathcal{W}$, $\operatorname{Log}(s)(m) = \log(s(m)) \in \mathfrak{g} \cong (\operatorname{ad} P)_m$ for every $m$, and $\operatorname{Log}(s) \in \mathcal{A}$, so $\operatorname{Log}(s) \in C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ (using closedness of that subspace, Step 0). Thus $\operatorname{Log}(\mathcal{W}) \subset C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.
>
> **The chart.** Combining, and shrinking $U_0$ to $U_0' := U_0 \cap C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ and $V_{\mathbf{1}}$ to $\mathcal{W}$ as needed, the restriction
> $$\operatorname{Exp} \colon U_0' \;\longrightarrow\; \mathcal{W}, \qquad \operatorname{Log} \colon \mathcal{W} \;\longrightarrow\; U_0'$$
> are mutually inverse bijections: $\operatorname{Exp}(U_0') \subset \mathcal{H}$ and lands in $\mathcal{W}$ after shrinking; $\operatorname{Log}(\mathcal{W}) \subset U_0'$; and $\operatorname{Log} \circ \operatorname{Exp} = \operatorname{id}$, $\operatorname{Exp} \circ \operatorname{Log} = \operatorname{id}$ by Step 3. They are homeomorphisms (restrictions of the ambient analytic diffeomorphisms $\operatorname{Exp}, \operatorname{Log}$ to the subspace $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$, which is closed and, by Step 0, complemented). This is a chart of $\mathcal{H}$ at $\mathbf{1}$ modelled on the Banach space $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$; the tangent space at $\mathbf{1}$ is $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.

**Step 5: Translate the chart around the group; the atlas has analytic transitions.**

Left multiplication by a fixed element spreads the identity chart to a chart at every point, and the transition maps are analytic, so $\mathcal{H}$ is a Banach manifold modelled on $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> For $s_0 \in \mathcal{H}$, left multiplication $L_{s_0} \colon \mathcal{A} \to \mathcal{A}$, $s \mapsto s_0 \cdot s$, is a bounded linear map (indeed $\|s_0 \cdot s\| \le \|s_0\| \|s\|$), hence analytic, with analytic inverse $L_{s_0^{-1}}$; it restricts to a bijection $\mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}$ sending $\mathbf{1}$ to $s_0$. Define the chart at $s_0$ by
> $$\psi_{s_0} \colon \mathcal{O}_{s_0} := L_{s_0}(\mathcal{W}) \longrightarrow U_0', \qquad \psi_{s_0}(s) := \operatorname{Log}(s_0^{-1} \cdot s),$$
> with inverse $\eta \mapsto s_0 \cdot \operatorname{Exp}(\eta)$. Each $\mathcal{O}_{s_0}$ is open in $\mathcal{H}$ (preimage of the open $\mathcal{W}$ under the homeomorphism $L_{s_0^{-1}}$), and the $\mathcal{O}_{s_0}$ cover $\mathcal{H}$ (as $s_0$ ranges over $\mathcal{H}$, since $s \in \mathcal{O}_s$). **Transition maps.** For $s_0, s_1 \in \mathcal{H}$ with $\mathcal{O}_{s_0} \cap \mathcal{O}_{s_1} \ne \varnothing$, on the overlap
> $$(\psi_{s_1} \circ \psi_{s_0}^{-1})(\eta) \;=\; \operatorname{Log}\big(s_1^{-1} s_0 \cdot \operatorname{Exp}(\eta)\big) \qquad (\text{definition of } \psi_{s_1}, \psi_{s_0}^{-1}),$$
> a composition of the analytic maps $\operatorname{Exp}$, left multiplication by the fixed element $s_1^{-1} s_0$ (bounded linear), and $\operatorname{Log}$ (on its domain), hence analytic; and it lands in $U_0' \subset C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ because its argument $s_1^{-1} s_0 \operatorname{Exp}(\eta)$ lies in $\mathcal{H}$ near $\mathbf{1}$, so its $\operatorname{Log}$ is an $\operatorname{ad} P$-section (Step 4). Therefore the transition maps are analytic diffeomorphisms between open subsets of the Banach space $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$, and $\{(\mathcal{O}_{s_0}, \psi_{s_0})\}$ is a Banach-manifold atlas for $\mathcal{H}$.

**Step 6: Multiplication and inversion are analytic; the Lie algebra is $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.**

The group operations are analytic in these charts, so $\mathcal{H}$ is a Banach Lie group, and its Lie algebra — the tangent space at $\mathbf{1}$ with the commutator bracket — is $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> **Analyticity of multiplication.** Read in charts near a pair $(s_0, s_1)$ and their product $s_0 s_1$, multiplication becomes
> $$(\eta, \zeta) \;\longmapsto\; \operatorname{Log}\Big((s_0 s_1)^{-1}\, (s_0 \operatorname{Exp}(\eta))\, (s_1 \operatorname{Exp}(\zeta))\Big) \;=\; \operatorname{Log}\Big(s_1^{-1}\, \operatorname{Exp}(\eta)\, s_1\, \operatorname{Exp}(\zeta)\Big),$$
> a composition of: $\operatorname{Exp}$ (analytic); conjugation $x \mapsto s_1^{-1} x s_1$ by the fixed element $s_1$ (bounded linear, hence analytic); the algebra product (bounded bilinear, hence analytic); and $\operatorname{Log}$ (analytic on its domain). Hence multiplication is analytic. **Analyticity of inversion.** By the same reading in charts, $s \mapsto s^{-1}$ reads, near $s_0$, as $\eta \mapsto \operatorname{Log}\big(s_0 (s_0 \operatorname{Exp}(\eta))^{-1}\big) = \operatorname{Log}\big(\operatorname{Exp}(\eta)^{-1}\big) = \operatorname{Log}(\operatorname{Exp}(-\eta)) = -\eta$ near $\mathbf{1}$, and in general is the composite of $\operatorname{Exp}$, the analytic Banach-algebra inversion (Neumann series, Step 2), left/right translation, and $\operatorname{Log}$ — all analytic. Therefore $\mathcal{H} = C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ is a Banach Lie group.
>
> **The Lie algebra.** The tangent space at $\mathbf{1}$ is, by the chart $\operatorname{Log}$, the model Banach space $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$. The Lie bracket of the Lie algebra of $\mathcal{H}$ is inherited from that of the ambient unit group $\mathcal{A}^\times$, whose Lie algebra is $(\mathcal{A}, [\cdot, \cdot])$ with the commutator $[a, b] = a b - b a$ (Step 3): indeed the group commutator of one-parameter subgroups differentiates to the commutator, $\frac{\partial^2}{\partial s\, \partial t}\big|_0 \operatorname{Exp}(sa)\operatorname{Exp}(tb)\operatorname{Exp}(-sa)\operatorname{Exp}(-tb) = [a, b]$. It remains to check that $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ is closed under this bracket, so that it is a Lie subalgebra. For $\eta, \zeta \in C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ and each $m$,
> $$[\eta, \zeta](m) \;=\; \eta(m)\zeta(m) - \zeta(m)\eta(m) \;=\; [\eta(m), \zeta(m)] \;\in\; (\operatorname{ad} P)_m,$$
> because $(\operatorname{ad} P)_m \cong \mathfrak{g}$ is a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}(N; \mathbb{K})$, closed under the commutator (this is precisely the statement that $\mathfrak{g}$ is a Lie algebra under the matrix commutator, from the classification of matrix Lie algebras). And $[\eta, \zeta] \in \mathcal{A}$ (product and difference of $C^k$ sections). Hence $[\eta, \zeta] \in C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$: the model space is a Lie subalgebra, and the Lie algebra of $\mathcal{H}$ is $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ with the pointwise commutator bracket. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> **Claim.** For $G \subset GL(N; \mathbb{K})$ closed, $M$ compact, and $P \to M$ a principal $G$-bundle, $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ is a Banach Lie group with Lie algebra $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.
>
> *Proof.* Fix a fibre metric and connection on $E = P \times_G \mathbb{K}^N$; on $\operatorname{End} E$ use the induced connection and fibrewise operator norm, and set $\|s\|_{C^k} = \max_{j \le k} \sup_M |\nabla^j s|$.
>
> *(1) Banach algebra.* $C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E)$ is complete (reduce to $C^k(\overline\Omega; \mathbb{K}^r)$ by a finite trivialising atlas and norm-equivalence over the compact base; local completeness is the classical statement that uniform convergence of all derivatives up to order $k$ implies $C^k$ convergence, proved by the fundamental theorem of calculus and induction on the order). Pointwise composition is associative unital, and the Leibniz rule gives $\|s t\|_{C^k} \le 2^k \|s\|_{C^k}\|t\|_{C^k}$; the equivalent norm $\|s\| := 2^k\|s\|_{C^k}$ is submultiplicative, so $\mathcal{A} := (C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E), \|\cdot\|)$ is a Banach algebra.
>
> *(2) Unit group and subgroup.* $\mathcal{A}^\times = \{s : s(m) \in GL(E_m)\ \forall m\}$ is open (Neumann series $s^{-1} = \sum(\mathbf{1} - s)^n$ for $\|\mathbf{1} - s\| < 1$, then translate), with analytic inversion; it is a Banach Lie group modelled on $\mathcal{A}$ with commutator bracket. $\mathcal{H} := C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ is a subgroup (each $(\operatorname{Ad} P)_m \cong G$ a subgroup of $GL(E_m)$) and is closed in $\mathcal{A}^\times$ (pointwise limits of $G$-valued sections that are units stay $G$-valued, since $G$ is closed).
>
> *(3) Exp/Log charts.* $\operatorname{Exp}(a) = \sum a^n/n!$ and $\operatorname{Log}(\mathbf{1} + b) = \sum(-1)^{n+1}b^n/n$ ($\|b\| < 1$) converge absolutely, are analytic, and are mutually inverse near $\mathbf{1}$ (the identities reduce, for a single element, to the scalar identities $\log\exp = \operatorname{id}$, $\exp\log = \operatorname{id}$, since all its powers commute).
>
> *(4) Restriction.* Since evaluation $\operatorname{ev}_m$ is an algebra homomorphism, $\operatorname{Exp}(a)(m) = \exp(a(m))$ and $\operatorname{Log}(s)(m) = \log(s(m))$. By [[Thm - The Exponential Map of a Matrix Group is the Matrix Exponential]], $\exp(\mathfrak{g}) \subset G$, so $\operatorname{Exp}$ maps $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ into $\mathcal{H}$; by [[Thm - The Exponential Map is a Local Diffeomorphism at the Origin]] and [[Thm - The Closed Subgroup Theorem]], $\log(G \cap \widetilde V) \subset \mathfrak{g}$, so $\operatorname{Log}$ maps a $C^0$-neighbourhood $\mathcal{W}$ of $\mathbf{1}$ in $\mathcal{H}$ into $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ (uniformity by compactness of $M$). Hence $\operatorname{Exp} \colon U_0' \to \mathcal{W}$, $\operatorname{Log} \colon \mathcal{W} \to U_0'$ are mutually inverse homeomorphisms — a chart at $\mathbf{1}$ modelled on the Banach space $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.
>
> *(5) Atlas.* Charts $\psi_{s_0}(s) = \operatorname{Log}(s_0^{-1} s)$ obtained by left translation cover $\mathcal{H}$; transitions $\eta \mapsto \operatorname{Log}(s_1^{-1}s_0 \operatorname{Exp}(\eta))$ are analytic (compositions of $\operatorname{Exp}$, bounded-linear translation, $\operatorname{Log}$). So $\mathcal{H}$ is a Banach manifold modelled on $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$.
>
> *(6) Group operations and Lie algebra.* In charts, multiplication is $(\eta, \zeta) \mapsto \operatorname{Log}(s_1^{-1}\operatorname{Exp}(\eta) s_1 \operatorname{Exp}(\zeta))$ and inversion is (near $\mathbf{1}$) $\eta \mapsto -\eta$; both are analytic (algebra product bounded bilinear, inversion by Neumann, $\operatorname{Exp}, \operatorname{Log}$ analytic). Hence $\mathcal{H}$ is a Banach Lie group. Its Lie algebra is $T_{\mathbf{1}}\mathcal{H} = C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ with the commutator bracket inherited from $\mathcal{A}$; this is closed on $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ because $[\eta, \zeta](m) = [\eta(m), \zeta(m)] \in (\operatorname{ad} P)_m$, $\mathfrak{g}$ being a Lie subalgebra of $\mathfrak{gl}(N; \mathbb{K})$. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Illegal but tempting: "closed subgroup, therefore Lie subgroup"
> The single most tempting shortcut is to observe (correctly) that $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ is a *closed subgroup* of the Banach Lie group $\mathcal{A}^\times$ and to conclude that it is a Banach Lie subgroup by "the closed-subgroup theorem". This is a false transplant. Cartan's closed-subgroup theorem — every closed subgroup of a finite-dimensional Lie group is an embedded Lie subgroup — **fails in infinite dimensions**: there are closed subgroups of Banach Lie groups with no compatible manifold structure. The theorem's proof leans on local compactness, which Banach spaces lack. The honest replacement is exactly Steps 3–4: one must *produce* the submanifold chart, and here it exists only because the matrix hypothesis makes the chart the universal power series $\operatorname{Exp}$, which restricts to the subgroup fibrewise by the matrix-exponential and closed-subgroup theorems. Whenever you meet "closed subgroup" in an infinite-dimensional setting, treat it as topological data only and build the charts by hand.

**Remark (only the matrix case).** The proof used $G \subset GL(N; \mathbb{K})$ twice essentially: to embed $\operatorname{ad} P, \operatorname{Ad} P$ into $\operatorname{End} E$ and to make the group and algebra exponentials the *same universal power series* $\exp, \log$, which are analytic operations of the Banach algebra $\mathcal{A}$ and restrict fibrewise between $\mathfrak{g}$ and $G$. For a general (non-matrix) Lie group $G$ the exponential is not a power series in a linear ambient space, and the chart map $\eta \mapsto (m \mapsto \exp_G(\eta(m)))$ can no longer be recognised as an operation on a Banach algebra; proving that "post-compose with the fixed smooth map $\exp_G$" sends $C^k$ sections to $C^k$ sections smoothly requires the $\omega$-lemma (the smoothness of the pushforward map on section spaces induced by a fibrewise smooth bundle map). The matrix case treated here deliberately avoids that machinery.

# Key Takeaways

**To put a Lie-group structure on an infinite-dimensional space of sections, borrow the manifold structure from a Banach algebra and check that a single power-series chart respects the subgroup.** The reusable architecture is: (i) find a Banach algebra $\mathcal{A}$ in which the group sits as a closed subgroup of the units — here $\mathcal{A} = C^k(M; \operatorname{End} E)$, with units open and inversion analytic by the Neumann series; (ii) build the analytic $\operatorname{Exp}, \operatorname{Log}$ of $\mathcal{A}$; (iii) prove they restrict to a chart of the subgroup at the identity; (iv) translate to an atlas. The trigger for this method is any request to make "the group of $C^k$/Sobolev/smooth maps or sections into a Lie group $G$" into a topological or Lie group: gauge groups, loop groups, current groups, diffeomorphism groups all yield to a version of it. The transferable diagnostic is that the model space of the resulting manifold is always the corresponding space of *Lie-algebra-valued* sections or maps — here $C^k(M; \operatorname{ad} P)$ — because that is the tangent space at the identity, obtained by linearising the exponential chart.

**Compactness of the base is what converts pointwise smallness into uniform smallness, and it is used at every quantitative step.** The role of "$M$ compact" is easy to underrate. It makes the $C^k$-norm finite; it makes the space complete via a *finite* trivialising atlas and the resulting two-sided norm equivalence; it makes different metrics and connections give equivalent norms, so the construction does not depend on the auxiliary choices; and — most delicately — it is what lets a single small neighbourhood of the identity serve as the chart domain, by turning "each $s(m)$ lies in the fixed neighbourhood $\widetilde{V}$ of $e$" into "$s$ lies in a $C^0$-neighbourhood of $\mathbf{1}$". On a non-compact base one must switch to weighted or Sobolev norms and be far more careful about uniformity; the clean statement here is a compact-base statement. When adapting the argument, audit every "uniformly" and "for all $m$ at once" and ask which of them is silently using compactness.

**The closed-subgroup theorem is the bridge that makes the logarithm land in the Lie algebra, and its failure in infinite dimensions is why the whole exercise is non-trivial.** Two facts about $G$ inside $GL(N; \mathbb{K})$ carry the restriction step: $\exp(\mathfrak{g}) \subset G$ (the easy inclusion, from the matrix-exponential theorem) and $\log(G \cap \widetilde V) \subset \mathfrak{g}$ (the hard inclusion, from the closed-subgroup theorem's structural description of $G$ near $e$ as $\exp$ of a neighbourhood in $\mathfrak{g}$). Together they say the finite-dimensional chart $\exp \colon \mathfrak{g} \to G$ is a genuine submanifold chart, and this is what gets promoted, fibre by fibre, to the infinite-dimensional chart. The cautionary half is that one may *not* run the same logic abstractly: the infinite-dimensional group $C^k(M; \operatorname{Ad} P)$ is a closed subgroup of $\mathcal{A}^\times$, yet closedness alone gives it no charts, because Cartan's theorem needs local compactness. The lesson to store for spaced practice: in finite dimensions "closed subgroup" is a licence to differentiate; in infinite dimensions it is only topology, and the analytic structure must be exhibited by an explicit exponential chart. This exercise is the companion, at the level of manifolds, to [[Thm - The Gauge Group is the Space of Sections of the Adjoint Group Bundle]], which is the corresponding statement at the level of abstract groups.
