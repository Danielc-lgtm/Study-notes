# Notation

Throughout, $M$ is a connected [[Def - Smooth Manifold|smooth manifold]] (hence, by our standing convention, Hausdorff and second countable) with a chosen base point $m\in M$, and $\Gamma:=\pi_1(M,m)$ is its [[Def - Path-Product and the Fundamental Group|fundamental group]] at $m$. We write $G$ for a compact [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]], $e\in G$ for its identity, and — since a compact Lie group is a compact smooth manifold, and a smooth manifold is metrisable (it is regular and second countable, so Urysohn's metrisation theorem applies) — we fix once and for all a metric $d_G$ inducing the topology of $G$; replacing $d_G$ by $\min(d_G,1)$ if necessary, we assume $d_G\le 1$. A **Hausdorff** space is one in which any two distinct points have disjoint open neighbourhoods; a compact Lie group, being a manifold, is Hausdorff.

For any group $\Gamma$, $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ denotes the set of group homomorphisms $\rho\colon\Gamma\to G$. We regard it as a subset of the product $G^{\Gamma}=\prod_{\gamma\in\Gamma}G$ of one copy of $G$ for each element $\gamma\in\Gamma$, and give it the subspace topology inherited from the [[Def - Product Topology|product topology]] on $G^{\Gamma}$. Concretely this is the **topology of pointwise convergence**: a net (or, once $\Gamma$ is countable, a sequence) $\rho_k\to\rho$ in $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ if and only if $\rho_k(\gamma)\to\rho(\gamma)$ in $G$ for every $\gamma\in\Gamma$. For $g\in G$ the projection $\pi_\gamma\colon G^{\Gamma}\to G$, $\pi_\gamma(\rho)=\rho(\gamma)$, is by definition of the product topology continuous.

The group $G$ acts on $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ by **conjugation**: for $g\in G$ and $\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$,
$$(g\cdot\rho)(\gamma):=g\,\rho(\gamma)\,g^{-1}\qquad(\gamma\in\Gamma),$$
which is again a homomorphism because $g\,\rho(\gamma\delta)\,g^{-1}=g\,\rho(\gamma)\rho(\delta)\,g^{-1}=(g\rho(\gamma)g^{-1})(g\rho(\delta)g^{-1})$. The orbit space is the **representation variety**
$$\mathcal R(M;G):=\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)/G,$$
given the [[Def - Quotient Topology and Identification Map|quotient topology]] for the [[Def - Continuous Map|continuous]] quotient map $\pi\colon\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)\to\mathcal R(M;G)$. Two representations are identified precisely when they are conjugate. When $\Gamma$ is generated by finitely many elements $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_N$, we write $G^N=\underbrace{G\times\cdots\times G}_{N}$ and let $G$ act on $G^N$ by **diagonal conjugation**, $g\cdot(g_1,\dots,g_N)=(gg_1g^{-1},\dots,gg_Ng^{-1})$, with orbit space $G^N/G$.

We write $\mathcal M^\flat_G(M)$ for the [[Def - Flat Connection|moduli space of flat connections]]: the set of pairs $(P,\omega)$ with $P$ a principal $G$-bundle over $M$ and $\omega$ a flat connection on $P$, taken up to isomorphism of bundles-with-connection; for a fixed bundle $P$ it specialises to $\mathcal M^\flat(P)=\mathcal A^\flat(P)/\mathcal G(P)$, the space of flat connections modulo the [[Def - Gauge Transformation|gauge group]]. The previous theorem, [[Thm - Flat Connections and Monodromy Representations of the Fundamental Group]], furnishes a canonical bijection $\mathcal M^\flat_G(M)\to\mathcal R(M;G)$, sending a flat connection to the conjugacy class of its monodromy representation; we topologise $\mathcal M^\flat_G(M)$ so that this bijection is a homeomorphism, which is the sense in which the results below are statements about the moduli space of flat connections.

> [!warning] Convention: the topology on the representation variety
> Haydys topologises $\mathcal R(M;G)$ by choosing a finite presentation of $\pi_1(M)$ and viewing $\mathcal R(M;G)$ as a subspace of $G^N/G$ (his Remark following Proposition 106). We instead take the intrinsic topology of pointwise convergence on $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$, which needs no presentation and is defined for every connected manifold. The two topologies agree whenever $\Gamma$ is finitely generated: Part II below proves that the evaluation map $\rho\mapsto(\rho(\gamma_1),\dots,\rho(\gamma_N))$ is a homeomorphism of $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ (pointwise topology) onto a closed subspace of $G^N$ (subspace topology). So no generality is lost, and none is silently assumed.

> [!note] Scope remark
> It is a standard fact that the fundamental group of a compact manifold is **finitely presented**; we neither prove nor use this here. Part I (compactness and Hausdorffness of $\mathcal R(M;G)$) requires only that $\Gamma$ be **countable**, which we prove for every connected manifold from the universal-cover theorem. Part II takes **finite generation** of $\Gamma$ as an explicit hypothesis and never assumes finitely many relations.

# Statement

> **Theorem (compactness of the moduli space of flat connections; Haydys, Proposition 106).** Let $M$ be a connected manifold with base point $m$, let $\Gamma=\pi_1(M,m)$, and let $G$ be a compact Lie group. Give $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ the topology of pointwise convergence as a subspace of $G^{\Gamma}$, and $\mathcal R(M;G)=\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)/G$ the quotient topology for the conjugation action.
>
> **Part I.** $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ is a compact Hausdorff space, and $\mathcal R(M;G)$ is a compact Hausdorff space.
>
> **Part II.** If moreover $\Gamma$ is generated by finitely many elements $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_N$, then the evaluation map
> $$\operatorname{ev}\colon\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)\longrightarrow G^N,\qquad \operatorname{ev}(\rho)=(\rho(\gamma_1),\dots,\rho(\gamma_N)),$$
> is a homeomorphism onto a closed subset of $G^N$; it is equivariant for the conjugation action on the source and the diagonal conjugation action on $G^N$, and hence descends to a homeomorphism of $\mathcal R(M;G)$ onto a closed subset of the compact Hausdorff space $G^N/G$.

The two parts are tied together as follows. Part I already gives everything the title claims; Part II is the sharper, coordinate description that recovers Haydys's presentation-based picture and reproves compactness by an elementary route when $\Gamma$ is finitely generated. Transporting the conclusion along the monodromy correspondence of the previous theorem, we obtain the companion statement about connections themselves.

> **Corollary (the moduli space of flat connections is compact).** For every connected manifold $M$ and compact Lie group $G$, the moduli space $\mathcal M^\flat_G(M)$ of flat $G$-connections over $M$, topologised through the monodromy bijection $\mathcal M^\flat_G(M)\cong\mathcal R(M;G)$ of [[Thm - Flat Connections and Monodromy Representations of the Fundamental Group]], is compact and Hausdorff. In particular, for any fixed principal $G$-bundle $P$ the moduli space $\mathcal M^\flat(P)=\mathcal A^\flat(P)/\mathcal G(P)$, being (again by that theorem) the subset of $\mathcal R(M;G)$ of conjugacy classes whose associated flat bundle is isomorphic to $P$, is contained in a compact Hausdorff space.

# Motivation

This is the first genuine **compactness theorem** of the series, and it is worth pausing on what kind of statement it is. A moduli space in gauge theory is the set of solutions of a nonlinear partial differential equation — here the flatness equation $F_\omega=0$ — divided by the enormous symmetry group of gauge transformations. Written that way it is an infinite-dimensional quotient of an infinite-dimensional space, and there is no reason on its face for it to be a well-behaved topological object at all. Haydys poses exactly the two questions one wants answered about such a space (his Remark preceding Proposition 106): *is it compact?* and *is it a manifold?* The present theorem settles the first, and it does so decisively and cheaply, without any of the analytic machinery — Sobolev completions, elliptic regularity, Uhlenbeck compactness — that the corresponding question for the Yang–Mills or Seiberg–Witten equations will demand in later chapters.

The reason it is cheap is the content of the previous two theorems. A flat connection is rigid: its parallel transport depends only on the homotopy class of the loop traversed, so a flat connection is *nothing more than* a representation of $\pi_1(M)$ into the structure group, and gauge equivalence is *nothing more than* conjugation of representations. The entire infinite-dimensional analytic problem collapses onto a finite piece of algebra, the representation variety $\mathcal R(M;G)=\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_1(M),G)/G$. Once that collapse has been made, compactness is a statement about a space of tuples in the compact group $G$ subject to closed conditions, and it follows from point-set topology alone.

There are two ways to see why compactness should hold, and the tension between them is instructive. If one already knows that $\pi_1(M)$ is finitely generated by $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_N$ — true for compact manifolds — then a representation is determined by the $N$ elements $\rho(\gamma_i)\in G$, so $\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_1(M),G)$ sits inside the compact space $G^N$ as the locus where the group relations hold, and that locus is closed, hence compact. This is Haydys's argument. It is completely correct but it leans on finite generation, a fact the series has chosen not to prove. The alternative, which we take as the primary route, uses only that $\pi_1(M)$ is **countable** — which we do prove, for every connected manifold, from the construction of the universal cover — together with the observation that a countable product of the compact metrisable space $G$ is again compact metrisable, by a diagonal-subsequence argument that sidesteps the general Tychonoff theorem. The finitely generated case then reappears as a corollary, sharper because it embeds the variety in a finite-dimensional model.

Why care whether such a moduli space is compact? Compactness is the topological precondition for every counting and integration argument that makes moduli spaces useful invariants: a compact moduli space has a well-defined fundamental class, its characteristic numbers are finite, and continuous functions on it attain their extrema. When later chapters deform the flatness equation to the anti-self-duality or Seiberg–Witten equations, the moduli space is no longer compact for free, and recovering compactness — Uhlenbeck's and the Seiberg–Witten compactness theorems — becomes the central analytic labour of the theory. Seeing here, in the easiest case, exactly which point-set facts compactness rests on is the right preparation for appreciating what those later theorems have to supply by hand.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening).** The theorem's literal hypotheses are mild — a connected manifold and a compact structure group — so the useful question is which less obvious situations secretly present these hypotheses.

The first disguised source is **a space presented as a homogeneous space or a quotient of a Lie group**, whose fundamental group one wishes to feed in. If $X=G'/H$ is a coset space of Lie groups, or more generally a manifold built by a quotient construction, then $X$ is a connected manifold and its fundamental group is available; the bridge $B\Rightarrow A$ is simply that "manifold" is the only hypothesis Part I places on the base, so any construction that outputs a connected manifold outputs a legitimate input. The non-obvious part is recognising that one does *not* need $X$ compact for Part I: countability of $\pi_1$, which is all the argument uses, holds for every connected manifold, compact or not. *Example problem:* show that the moduli space of flat $U(1)$-connections over an open surface of finite type is still a compact torus, even though the surface is noncompact, because its fundamental group is a finitely generated free group.

The second disguised source is **a compact group appearing as a symmetry, holonomy, or gauge group rather than as a structure group by name**. Any closed subgroup of a compact Lie group is a compact Lie group; any finite group is a compact ($0$-dimensional) Lie group; the unitary, orthogonal, symplectic, and special variants $U(k),O(k),SU(k),SO(k),Sp(k)$ are all compact. The bridge is that "$G$ compact Lie group" is preserved under passing to closed subgroups and under the standard classical constructions, so a problem phrased with any of these groups is an instance of the theorem. The non-obvious step is to notice compactness where the problem only names, say, "$SU(2)$ holonomy" or "a finite gauge group". *Example problem:* the moduli space of flat connections on a bundle with finite structure group $\Gamma_0$ is the finite set $\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_1(M),\Gamma_0)/\Gamma_0$, which is compact because a finite discrete space is compact.

The third disguised source is **a system of matrix equations of the form "commuting or relation-satisfying tuples in a compact group, up to simultaneous conjugation"**, arising with no manifold in sight. Such a locus $\{(g_1,\dots,g_N)\in G^N: w_j(g)=e\}/G$ is exactly $\mathcal R(M;G)$ for the manifold $M$ whose fundamental group has presentation $\langle\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_N\mid w_j\rangle$ (and one exists, for any finitely presented group, as a compact manifold). The bridge is Part II read backwards: the compactness of any such algebraic quotient is an instance of this theorem. The non-obvious point is that a purely algebraic compactness question — is the set of commuting pairs in $SU(2)$ up to conjugation compact? — is answered geometrically. *Example problem:* deduce that the space of commuting pairs $(A,B)\in SU(2)^2$ modulo simultaneous conjugation is compact, by realising it as $\mathcal R(T^2;SU(2))$.

**Targets (Output Amplification).** The bare output is a compact Hausdorff space. Combined with further ingredients it does more.

Combine the conclusion with **a continuous invariant of representations**, such as a character $\rho\mapsto\operatorname{tr}\rho(\gamma)$ or a Chern–Simons value. On a compact space every continuous real function attains its maximum and minimum and has bounded range. The amplified result $E$ is that any such invariant is bounded and attains its extreme values on the moduli space; this is the mechanism by which Chern–Simons theory selects distinguished flat connections as critical points on a compact configuration space, and it is non-obvious because it converts "the equation $F=0$ has solutions" into "the best solution, by any continuous criterion, exists".

Combine the conclusion with **a free or almost-free group action producing a manifold structure**. If the conjugation action is free on an open dense set and the quotient there is a manifold, then compactness of the total quotient plus local manifold structure yields a compact space that is a manifold away from a small singular set — the standard shape of a moduli space with orbifold or stratified structure. The extra ingredient is the transversality or slice theorem that gives local charts; the payoff is a compact stratified space carrying a fundamental class, the object one integrates characteristic forms over. This is non-obvious because compactness and smoothness come from completely different arguments and only their combination gives an invariant.

Combine the conclusion with **a family of manifolds or groups depending on a parameter**. Because each fibre $\mathcal R(M_t;G)$ is compact, a total space fibring over the parameter with compact fibres is proper over the parameter, so limits of flat connections as $t$ varies stay flat and stay within the moduli space. The amplified result is a compactness statement for families — the total moduli space is proper over the base of parameters — which underlies wall-crossing and gluing arguments. It is non-obvious because it upgrades fibrewise compactness to a statement about the whole family, using that compactness is exactly properness over a point.

# Why Is It True

Strip away the geometry and look at what object we are really compactifying. After the monodromy correspondence, a flat connection is a homomorphism $\rho\colon\Gamma\to G$, and two of them are the same point of the moduli space exactly when they are conjugate. So the moduli space is a set of $G$-valued "labellings" of the group $\Gamma$, cut down by the requirement that the labelling respect multiplication, and then folded up by conjugation. Every one of these three operations — labelling, respecting multiplication, folding by conjugation — is topologically tame *because $G$ is compact*, and compactness of the moduli space is the sum of those three tamenesses.

The first tameness is that the space of *all* labellings $\rho\colon\Gamma\to G$, ignoring multiplicativity, is compact. A labelling is just a point of the product $G^{\Gamma}$, one coordinate of $G$ for each element of $\Gamma$. If $\Gamma$ were finite this would be a finite product of compact spaces and therefore compact. The only new feature is that $\Gamma$ is infinite — but it is **countable**, and a countable product of copies of a compact metric space is still compact: given any sequence of labellings, one can extract a subsequence converging in the first coordinate, then a sub-subsequence converging also in the second, and so on, and the diagonal of these choices converges in every coordinate at once. This is the whole use of compactness of $G$ and countability of $\Gamma$.

The second tameness is that imposing multiplicativity does not spoil compactness, because multiplicativity is a **closed** condition. For fixed group elements $a,b$, the equation $\rho(ab)=\rho(a)\rho(b)$ says two continuous functions of $\rho$ agree, and — since $G$ is Hausdorff — the locus where two continuous $G$-valued functions agree is closed. The homomorphisms are the intersection of all these closed loci, hence a closed subset of the compact product, hence compact.

The third tameness is that folding by conjugation preserves both compactness and the Hausdorff property. Compactness survives any continuous surjection, and the quotient map is one. The Hausdorff property is the delicate half, and it is exactly here that compactness of $G$ is used a second time: the set of pairs $(\rho,\rho')$ that are conjugate is the image of the compact space $G\times\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ under the continuous map $(g,\rho)\mapsto(\rho,g\cdot\rho)$, hence compact, hence closed; and an orbit space by a group action with closed orbit relation and open quotient map is Hausdorff.

> **The mechanism in one sentence: compactness of the structure group makes the space of all $G$-labellings of the countable group $\pi_1(M)$ compact, makes the homomorphism condition carve out a closed hence compact subspace, and makes the conjugation orbits close up into a compact Hausdorff quotient.**

The failure mode ruled out by compactness of $G$ is escape to infinity. If $G$ were noncompact — say $G=GL_k(\mathbb R)$ — a sequence of representations could have $\rho_k(\gamma)$ marching off to infinity, or degenerating so that conjugating classes collide, and neither the product nor the quotient would be compact; the representation variety $\mathcal R(M;GL_k(\mathbb R))$ is genuinely noncompact and is one reason that theory is harder. Compactness of $G$ is precisely the hypothesis that forbids escape.

# What Makes This Hard

The single subtle point is the **Hausdorff property of the quotient**, and it is the one place the argument can quietly go wrong. Quotients by group actions are notorious for being non-Hausdorff — the orbit space of an irrational flow on the torus is the standard warning — and the naive hope that "a nice space modulo a nice action is nice" is false in general. What saves us is a specific mechanism: because $G$ is *compact*, the conjugation orbit relation is a closed subset of the square, and a group quotient with closed orbit relation and (automatically) open quotient map is Hausdorff. Omitting the verification that the orbit relation is closed, or assuming Hausdorffness of the quotient because the source is Hausdorff, is the common error. The second, lesser trap is reaching for the Tychonoff theorem to make the infinite product $G^{\Gamma}$ compact; that theorem is true but its full proof rests on the axiom of choice and on machinery this series has chosen not to develop, and it is unnecessary here — countability of $\pi_1(M)$ reduces the product to a countable one, for which a bare-hands diagonal argument suffices.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:** After the monodromy correspondence has reduced flat connections to $\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_1(M),G)$ modulo conjugation, prove compactness in three moves and then, separately, the finitely generated refinement. First make the ambient product $G^{\Gamma}$ compact using only countability of $\Gamma$ (diagonal argument, no Tychonoff). Second, show the homomorphisms form a closed, hence compact, subset. Third, pass to the conjugation quotient, checking compactness (continuous image) and Hausdorffness (closed orbit relation plus open quotient map). Finally, if $\Gamma$ is finitely generated, embed everything into the finite product $G^N$ by evaluation on generators.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **$\Gamma$ is countable.**
   - *Hint:* This is part (d) of the universal-cover theorem; do not reprove it here, invoke it.
   - *Why needed:* It reduces the product $G^{\Gamma}$ to a countable product, which is metrisable and admits a diagonal argument.

2. **A countable product of copies of a compact metric space is compact.**
   - *Hint:* Put an explicit weighted-sum metric on the product, check it induces the product topology, run a diagonal subsequence extraction to get sequential compactness, then invoke the three-equivalents theorem for metric spaces.
   - *Why needed:* It makes $G^{\Gamma}$ compact without Tychonoff.

3. **The homomorphisms are closed in $G^{\Gamma}$.**
   - *Hint:* For each pair $(a,b)$ the condition $\rho(ab)=\rho(a)\rho(b)$ is the preimage of the diagonal of $G\times G$ under a continuous map; intersect over all pairs. Recover $\rho(e)=e$ and $\rho(a^{-1})=\rho(a)^{-1}$ from multiplicativity.
   - *Why needed:* A closed subset of a compact space is compact, so $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ is compact.

4. **The conjugation quotient of a compact Hausdorff space by a compact group is compact Hausdorff.**
   - *Hint:* Openness of the quotient map is automatic for group actions; closedness of the orbit relation comes from writing it as the image of $G\times X$ under a continuous map and using compactness of $G$.
   - *Why needed:* It delivers Part I's conclusion for $\mathcal R(M;G)$.

5. **The evaluation-on-generators map is a closed equivariant embedding.**
   - *Hint:* Continuity and injectivity are immediate from finite generation; the inverse is continuous because every element is a word in the generators; the image is an intersection of relation loci, hence closed.
   - *Why needed:* It is Part II, and it reconciles the pointwise topology with Haydys's presentation topology.

6. **The product formula for fundamental groups.**
   - *Hint:* Loops into a product are pairs of loops; homotopies are pairs of homotopies.
   - *Why needed:* It computes $\pi_1(T^n)=\mathbb Z^n$ for the worked torus example.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: A countable product of copies of a compact metric space is compact metrisable
> **Statement:** Let $(K,d_K)$ be a compact metric space with $d_K\le 1$, and let $I$ be a countable index set. Then the product $K^{I}=\prod_{i\in I}K$, with the product topology, is metrisable and compact.
>
> **Hint:** Enumerate $I$, put the weighted metric $D(x,y)=\sum_n 2^{-n}d_K(x_{i_n},y_{i_n})$, verify it induces the product topology, extract a diagonal convergent subsequence from any sequence, and invoke the three-equivalents theorem.
>
> **Why needed:** With $K=G$ and $I=\Gamma$ it makes the ambient product $G^{\Gamma}$ compact, using only countability of $\Gamma$ and compactness of $G$, and avoids the Tychonoff theorem.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > If $I$ is finite the claim reduces to the finite-factor case handled inside this proof by the same sequential argument with a finite sum, so assume $I$ is countably infinite and fix an enumeration $I=\{i_1,i_2,i_3,\dots\}$ without repetitions. Define
> > $$D\colon K^{I}\times K^{I}\to[0,\infty),\qquad D(x,y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\,d_K(x_{i_n},y_{i_n}).$$
> > **The series converges and $D$ is a metric.** Each summand satisfies $2^{-n}d_K(x_{i_n},y_{i_n})\le 2^{-n}$ (since $d_K\le 1$), so $D(x,y)\le\sum_{n\ge 1}2^{-n}=1<\infty$ (by comparison with a convergent geometric series). Symmetry $D(x,y)=D(y,x)$ holds termwise (symmetry of $d_K$). The triangle inequality holds termwise, $d_K(x_{i_n},z_{i_n})\le d_K(x_{i_n},y_{i_n})+d_K(y_{i_n},z_{i_n})$, and summing the inequality with the nonnegative weights $2^{-n}$ preserves it (a convergent sum of valid inequalities). If $D(x,y)=0$ then every summand vanishes (a sum of nonnegative terms is zero only if each is zero), so $d_K(x_{i_n},y_{i_n})=0$ for all $n$, hence $x_{i_n}=y_{i_n}$ for every $n$; as the $i_n$ exhaust $I$, $x=y$. Thus $D$ is a metric.
> >
> > **$D$ induces the product topology.** We show the two topologies have the same open sets by comparing basic neighbourhoods.
> >
> > *Product-open sets are $D$-open.* A subbasic product-open set is $\pi_{i_m}^{-1}(U)$ with $U\subseteq K$ open, where $\pi_{i_m}(x)=x_{i_m}$. Let $x\in\pi_{i_m}^{-1}(U)$, so $x_{i_m}\in U$; choose $r>0$ with the $d_K$-ball $B_{d_K}(x_{i_m},r)\subseteq U$ (possible, $U$ open). If $D(x,y)<2^{-m}r$ then $2^{-m}d_K(x_{i_m},y_{i_m})\le D(x,y)<2^{-m}r$ (the single $m$-th term is bounded by the whole nonnegative sum), so $d_K(x_{i_m},y_{i_m})<r$ and $y_{i_m}\in U$, i.e. $y\in\pi_{i_m}^{-1}(U)$. Hence the $D$-ball $B_D(x,2^{-m}r)\subseteq\pi_{i_m}^{-1}(U)$, so $\pi_{i_m}^{-1}(U)$ is $D$-open. Every product-open set is a union of finite intersections of such subbasic sets, and finite intersections and arbitrary unions of $D$-open sets are $D$-open, so every product-open set is $D$-open.
> >
> > *$D$-balls contain product-open neighbourhoods.* Fix $x$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $N$ with $\sum_{n>N}2^{-n}<\varepsilon/2$ (tail of a convergent series). Put $\delta=\varepsilon/2$ and let
> > $$W=\{y: d_K(x_{i_n},y_{i_n})<\delta\text{ for }n=1,\dots,N\}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{N}\pi_{i_n}^{-1}\!\big(B_{d_K}(x_{i_n},\delta)\big),$$
> > a finite intersection of subbasic product-open sets, hence product-open, and containing $x$. For $y\in W$,
> > $$D(x,y)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}2^{-n}d_K(x_{i_n},y_{i_n})+\sum_{n>N}2^{-n}d_K(x_{i_n},y_{i_n})<\delta\sum_{n=1}^{N}2^{-n}+\sum_{n>N}2^{-n}<\delta\cdot 1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\le\varepsilon,$$
> > using $d_K(x_{i_n},y_{i_n})<\delta$ on the first block (definition of $W$), $\sum_{n=1}^N 2^{-n}<1$, $d_K\le 1$ on the tail, and the choice of $N$. Thus $W\subseteq B_D(x,\varepsilon)$, so every $D$-ball contains a product-open neighbourhood of its centre, and every $D$-open set is product-open. The two topologies coincide.
> >
> > **$K^{I}$ is sequentially compact.** Since $K$ is a compact metric space it is sequentially compact by [[Thm - Compactness in Metric Spaces (Three Equivalents)|the three-equivalents theorem]] — which states that for a metric space, being compact, being sequentially compact, and being complete and totally bounded are equivalent. Let $(x^{(k)})_{k\ge 1}$ be a sequence in $K^{I}$. Construct nested infinite index sets $\mathbb N\supseteq S_1\supseteq S_2\supseteq\cdots$ as follows. The sequence $(x^{(k)}_{i_1})_{k}$ lies in the sequentially compact $K$, so it has a convergent subsequence along an infinite $S_1\subseteq\mathbb N$: $x^{(k)}_{i_1}\to a_1$ as $k\to\infty$ within $S_1$. Inductively, given the infinite $S_{n-1}$, the sequence $(x^{(k)}_{i_n})_{k\in S_{n-1}}$ has a convergent subsequence along an infinite $S_n\subseteq S_{n-1}$: $x^{(k)}_{i_n}\to a_n$ as $k\to\infty$ within $S_n$. Now define the diagonal indices $k_1<k_2<\cdots$ by letting $k_j$ be the smallest element of $S_j$ exceeding $k_{j-1}$ (with $k_0=0$); this is possible because each $S_j$ is infinite, and it gives a strictly increasing sequence with $k_j\in S_j$ for every $j$. For each fixed $n$, the tail $(k_j)_{j\ge n}$ lies in $S_n$ (since $S_j\subseteq S_n$ for $j\ge n$), so $x^{(k_j)}_{i_n}\to a_n$ as $j\to\infty$. Thus the subsequence $(x^{(k_j)})_j$ converges coordinatewise to the point $a=(a_n)_n\in K^{I}$; coordinatewise convergence is convergence in the product topology (a net converges in a product iff every coordinate does, by definition of the product topology), equivalently $D$-convergence. Hence every sequence in $K^{I}$ has a convergent subsequence: $K^{I}$ is sequentially compact.
> >
> > **Conclusion.** $K^{I}$ is metrisable, and a metrisable sequentially compact space is compact by [[Thm - Compactness in Metric Spaces (Three Equivalents)|the three-equivalents theorem]] (the equivalence of sequential compactness and compactness in a metric space). Therefore $K^{I}$ is compact and metrisable.

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The elementary compactness toolkit
> **Statement:** In arbitrary topological spaces the following hold. (a) A continuous image of a compact space is compact. (b) A closed subset of a compact space is compact. (c) A compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed. (d) A continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism.
>
> **Hint:** (a) pull an open cover back through the map; (b) adjoin the complement to an open cover; (c) separate a fixed exterior point from the compact set by Hausdorffness and take a finite subcover; (d) combine (a),(b),(c) to show the map is closed.
>
> **Why needed:** Every compactness bookkeeping step in Part I and Part II — compactness of the quotient, closedness of the orbit relation, closedness of the evaluation image, the homeomorphism onto the image — is one of these four facts. They are stated and proved here because the corresponding vault pages carry only partial proofs, and this page must be self-contained.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **(a) Continuous image of compact is compact.** Let $f\colon X\to Y$ be continuous with $X$ compact, and let $\{V_\alpha\}_{\alpha}$ be a family of open subsets of $Y$ covering $f(X)$. Then $\{f^{-1}(V_\alpha)\}_\alpha$ is a family of open subsets of $X$ (preimages of open sets are open, by continuity of $f$) covering $X$: for $x\in X$ we have $f(x)\in V_\alpha$ for some $\alpha$, so $x\in f^{-1}(V_\alpha)$. By compactness of $X$ there is a finite subcover $X=f^{-1}(V_{\alpha_1})\cup\cdots\cup f^{-1}(V_{\alpha_r})$. Then $f(X)\subseteq V_{\alpha_1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{\alpha_r}$: if $y=f(x)\in f(X)$ then $x\in f^{-1}(V_{\alpha_j})$ for some $j$, so $y\in V_{\alpha_j}$. Hence every open cover of $f(X)$ has a finite subcover, so $f(X)$ is compact.
> >
> > **(b) Closed subset of compact is compact.** Let $C\subseteq X$ be closed with $X$ compact, and let $\{U_\alpha\}$ be open subsets of $X$ covering $C$. Adjoin the open set $X\setminus C$ (open, since $C$ is closed); then $\{U_\alpha\}\cup\{X\setminus C\}$ covers $X$: any point of $X$ is either in $C$, hence in some $U_\alpha$, or in $X\setminus C$. By compactness of $X$ there is a finite subcover; discarding $X\setminus C$ from it (it contains no point of $C$) leaves finitely many $U_\alpha$ still covering $C$. Hence $C$ is compact.
> >
> > **(c) Compact subset of Hausdorff is closed.** Let $C\subseteq X$ be compact with $X$ Hausdorff. We show $X\setminus C$ is open. Fix $y\in X\setminus C$. For each $x\in C$, Hausdorffness gives disjoint open sets $U_x\ni x$ and $V_x\ni y$. The family $\{U_x\}_{x\in C}$ covers $C$, so by compactness of $C$ finitely many suffice: $C\subseteq U_{x_1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{x_n}$. Put $V=V_{x_1}\cap\cdots\cap V_{x_n}$, a finite intersection of open sets containing $y$, hence an open neighbourhood of $y$. For each $j$, $V\subseteq V_{x_j}$ is disjoint from $U_{x_j}$, so $V$ is disjoint from $U_{x_1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{x_n}\supseteq C$; thus $V\subseteq X\setminus C$. Every point of $X\setminus C$ has an open neighbourhood inside $X\setminus C$, so $X\setminus C$ is open and $C$ is closed.
> >
> > **(d) Continuous bijection from compact to Hausdorff is a homeomorphism.** Let $f\colon X\to Y$ be a continuous bijection with $X$ compact and $Y$ Hausdorff. It remains to show $f^{-1}$ is continuous, equivalently that $f$ is a closed map. Let $C\subseteq X$ be closed. By (b), $C$ is compact; by (a), $f(C)$ is compact; by (c), $f(C)$ is closed in the Hausdorff space $Y$. Thus $f$ carries closed sets to closed sets, so $(f^{-1})^{-1}(C)=f(C)$ is closed for every closed $C$, i.e. $f^{-1}$ is continuous. Hence $f$ is a homeomorphism.

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The set of homomorphisms is closed in the product
> **Statement:** For any group $\Gamma$ and any Hausdorff topological group $G$, the set $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ is a closed subset of $G^{\Gamma}$ (product topology).
>
> **Hint:** Show that pointwise multiplicativity alone characterises homomorphisms, then write $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ as an intersection of preimages of the diagonal of $G\times G$.
>
> **Why needed:** Combined with compactness of $G^{\Gamma}$ (Lemma 1) and Lemma 2(b), it makes $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ compact.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > **Multiplicativity characterises homomorphisms.** A map $\rho\colon\Gamma\to G$ of sets is a group homomorphism if and only if $\rho(ab)=\rho(a)\rho(b)$ for all $a,b\in\Gamma$. The forward implication is the definition. For the converse, assume $\rho(ab)=\rho(a)\rho(b)$ for all $a,b$. Taking $a=b=e_\Gamma$ gives $\rho(e_\Gamma)=\rho(e_\Gamma)\rho(e_\Gamma)$, and multiplying on the left by $\rho(e_\Gamma)^{-1}$ in $G$ gives $\rho(e_\Gamma)=e$ (identity is preserved). Taking $b=a^{-1}$ gives $e=\rho(e_\Gamma)=\rho(a)\rho(a^{-1})$, so $\rho(a^{-1})=\rho(a)^{-1}$ (inverses are preserved). Hence multiplicativity alone yields a homomorphism, so no further conditions are needed.
> >
> > **Each multiplicativity condition is closed.** Fix $a,b\in\Gamma$ and define
> > $$\Psi_{a,b}\colon G^{\Gamma}\to G\times G,\qquad \Psi_{a,b}(\rho)=\big(\rho(ab),\,\rho(a)\rho(b)\big).$$
> > The first component is the projection $\pi_{ab}$, continuous by definition of the product topology. The second component is $\rho\mapsto(\pi_a(\rho),\pi_b(\rho))\mapsto\pi_a(\rho)\pi_b(\rho)$, the composition of the continuous map $(\pi_a,\pi_b)\colon G^{\Gamma}\to G\times G$ with the multiplication $G\times G\to G$, which is continuous because $G$ is a topological group. A map into a product is continuous iff its components are, so $\Psi_{a,b}$ is continuous. The diagonal $\Delta_G=\{(g,g):g\in G\}$ is closed in $G\times G$ because $G$ is Hausdorff (Hausdorffness is equivalent to closedness of the diagonal). Therefore
> > $$C_{a,b}:=\{\rho: \rho(ab)=\rho(a)\rho(b)\}=\Psi_{a,b}^{-1}(\Delta_G)$$
> > is closed, as the preimage of a closed set under a continuous map.
> >
> > **Intersect.** By the first paragraph,
> > $$\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)=\bigcap_{(a,b)\in\Gamma\times\Gamma}C_{a,b},$$
> > an intersection of closed sets, which is closed. Hence $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ is a closed subset of $G^{\Gamma}$.

> [!note]- Lemma 4: A compact group acting on a compact Hausdorff space has a compact Hausdorff orbit space
> **Statement:** Let $G$ be a compact Hausdorff topological group acting continuously on a compact Hausdorff space $X$ (with a continuous action map $G\times X\to X$, $(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x$, each $x\mapsto g\cdot x$ a homeomorphism). Then, when in addition $X$ and $G$ are metrisable, the orbit space $X/G$ with the quotient topology is compact and Hausdorff.
>
> **Hint:** The quotient map is open for any group action; the orbit relation is the image of $G\times X$ under a continuous map, hence compact hence closed; separate two distinct orbits using closedness of the relation and openness of the quotient map.
>
> **Why needed:** Applied to conjugation on $X=\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ it gives Part I's conclusion for $\mathcal R(M;G)$; applied to diagonal conjugation on $X=G^N$ it gives the Hausdorffness of $G^N/G$ used in Part II.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Write $\pi\colon X\to X/G$ for the quotient map and $[x]=\pi(x)$ for the orbit of $x$.
> >
> > **The quotient map $\pi$ is open.** Let $U\subseteq X$ be open. By definition of the quotient topology, $\pi(U)$ is open in $X/G$ if and only if $\pi^{-1}(\pi(U))$ is open in $X$. Now $\pi^{-1}(\pi(U))=\{x: G\cdot x\cap U\neq\varnothing\}=\bigcup_{g\in G}g\cdot U$, since $x$ lies in it iff $g\cdot x\in U$ for some $g$, iff $x\in g^{-1}\cdot U$ for some $g$. Each $g\cdot U$ is open, because $x\mapsto g^{-1}\cdot x$ is continuous (a coordinate of the action) with $g\cdot U=(x\mapsto g^{-1}\cdot x)^{-1}(U)$; equivalently $x\mapsto g\cdot x$ is a homeomorphism carrying the open $U$ to the open $g\cdot U$. A union of open sets is open, so $\pi^{-1}(\pi(U))$ is open and $\pi(U)$ is open. Hence $\pi$ is an open map.
> >
> > **$X/G$ is compact.** The map $\pi$ is continuous and surjective and $X$ is compact, so $X/G=\pi(X)$ is compact by Lemma 2(a).
> >
> > **The orbit relation is closed.** Let $R=\{(x,y)\in X\times X: y\in G\cdot x\}$ be the set of pairs lying in a common orbit. Consider
> > $$\Phi\colon G\times X\to X\times X,\qquad \Phi(g,x)=(x,\,g\cdot x).$$
> > Its first component is the projection to $X$ and its second is the action map $G\times X\to X$; both are continuous, so $\Phi$ is continuous. Its image is exactly $R$: a pair $(x,y)$ is of the form $(x,g\cdot x)$ for some $g$ iff $y\in G\cdot x$. Now $G\times X$ is a product of two compact metric spaces, hence compact — indeed metrisable (with the metric $d((g,x),(g',x'))=\max(d_G(g,g'),d_X(x,x'))$, which induces the product topology) and sequentially compact by the two-factor version of the diagonal argument of Lemma 1 (extract a subsequence converging in $G$, then a further one converging in $X$), so compact by [[Thm - Compactness in Metric Spaces (Three Equivalents)|the three-equivalents theorem]]. Therefore $R=\Phi(G\times X)$ is compact by Lemma 2(a). The square $X\times X$ is Hausdorff (a product of Hausdorff spaces is Hausdorff), so $R$ is closed in $X\times X$ by Lemma 2(c).
> >
> > **$X/G$ is Hausdorff.** Let $[x]\neq[y]$ be two distinct points of $X/G$. Then $y\notin G\cdot x$, so $(x,y)\notin R$. Since $R$ is closed, its complement is open, and it contains $(x,y)$; by definition of the product topology on $X\times X$ there are open sets $U\ni x$ and $V\ni y$ in $X$ with $(U\times V)\cap R=\varnothing$. Because $\pi$ is open, $\pi(U)$ and $\pi(V)$ are open neighbourhoods of $[x]$ and $[y]$ respectively. They are disjoint: if some $[z]\in\pi(U)\cap\pi(V)$, then $z'\in U$ and $z''\in V$ exist with $[z']=[z]=[z'']$, so $z''\in G\cdot z'$, i.e. $(z',z'')\in R\cap(U\times V)$, contradicting $(U\times V)\cap R=\varnothing$. Hence any two distinct points of $X/G$ have disjoint open neighbourhoods, so $X/G$ is Hausdorff.

> [!note]- Lemma 5: Evaluation on a finite generating set is a closed equivariant embedding
> **Statement:** Let $\Gamma$ be generated by $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_N$ and let $G$ be a compact Lie group. The map $\operatorname{ev}\colon\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)\to G^N$, $\operatorname{ev}(\rho)=(\rho(\gamma_1),\dots,\rho(\gamma_N))$, is a homeomorphism onto a closed subset of $G^N$; it satisfies $\operatorname{ev}(g\cdot\rho)=g\cdot_{\mathrm{diag}}\operatorname{ev}(\rho)$ for the conjugation action on the source and the diagonal conjugation on $G^N$.
>
> **Hint:** Continuity is coordinatewise; injectivity uses that generators generate; the image is the common zero locus of the relation words, an intersection of closed sets; then invoke Lemma 2(d) or exhibit the continuous inverse directly.
>
> **Why needed:** It is Part II, and it proves that the pointwise topology agrees with Haydys's presentation topology when $\Gamma$ is finitely generated.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $F$ be the free group on symbols $x_1,\dots,x_N$ and $q\colon F\to\Gamma$ the surjective homomorphism with $q(x_i)=\gamma_i$ (surjective because $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_N$ generate $\Gamma$). For a word $w\in F$, written $w=x_{j_1}^{\epsilon_1}\cdots x_{j_r}^{\epsilon_r}$ with $\epsilon_l\in\{\pm 1\}$, and a tuple $g=(g_1,\dots,g_N)\in G^N$, write $w(g):=g_{j_1}^{\epsilon_1}\cdots g_{j_r}^{\epsilon_r}\in G$; the map $g\mapsto w(g)$ is continuous, being a finite product of the continuous coordinate maps $g\mapsto g_i$ and inversion $g\mapsto g^{-1}$ (continuous in a topological group).
> >
> > **$\operatorname{ev}$ is continuous.** Its $i$-th component is $\rho\mapsto\rho(\gamma_i)=\pi_{\gamma_i}(\rho)$, a projection, continuous in the pointwise topology; a map into $G^N$ is continuous iff its components are.
> >
> > **$\operatorname{ev}$ is injective.** Suppose $\operatorname{ev}(\rho)=\operatorname{ev}(\rho')$, i.e. $\rho(\gamma_i)=\rho'(\gamma_i)$ for all $i$. Any $\gamma\in\Gamma$ equals $q(w)$ for some word $w=x_{j_1}^{\epsilon_1}\cdots x_{j_r}^{\epsilon_r}$, so $\gamma=\gamma_{j_1}^{\epsilon_1}\cdots\gamma_{j_r}^{\epsilon_r}$, and then $\rho(\gamma)=\rho(\gamma_{j_1})^{\epsilon_1}\cdots\rho(\gamma_{j_r})^{\epsilon_r}=\rho'(\gamma_{j_1})^{\epsilon_1}\cdots\rho'(\gamma_{j_r})^{\epsilon_r}=\rho'(\gamma)$ (both are homomorphisms, and they agree on the generators). Hence $\rho=\rho'$.
> >
> > **The image is $\{g: w(g)=e\text{ for all }w\in\ker q\}$, and is closed.** If $\rho\in\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ and $g=\operatorname{ev}(\rho)$, then for $w\in\ker q$ we have $w(g)=\rho(q(w))=\rho(e_\Gamma)=e$ (the word map evaluated on generator images equals $\rho$ applied to the corresponding element $q(w)$). Conversely, given $g\in G^N$ with $w(g)=e$ for all $w\in\ker q$, the assignment $q(w)\mapsto w(g)$ is well defined (if $q(w)=q(w')$ then $w'w^{-1}\in\ker q$, so $w'(g)=w(g)$) and defines a homomorphism $\rho\colon\Gamma\to G$ with $\rho(\gamma_i)=g_i$, whence $g=\operatorname{ev}(\rho)$. Therefore
> > $$\operatorname{ev}\big(\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)\big)=\bigcap_{w\in\ker q}\{g\in G^N: w(g)=e\}.$$
> > Each set $\{g: w(g)=e\}$ is the preimage of the closed set $\{e\}$ (a point in the Hausdorff $G$ is closed) under the continuous word map $g\mapsto w(g)$, hence closed; the image is an intersection of closed sets, hence closed. This uses only that $\Gamma$ is finitely generated; the index set $\ker q$ may be infinite (finitely many relations are not assumed).
> >
> > **$\operatorname{ev}$ is a homeomorphism onto its image.** The image, being a closed subset of the compact space $G^N$, is compact (Lemma 2(b) with $X=G^N$ compact — a finite product of the compact space $G$), and it is Hausdorff as a subspace of the Hausdorff $G^N$. The map $\operatorname{ev}$ is a continuous bijection from $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$, which is compact (established in Part I of the Formal Proof), onto this Hausdorff image; by Lemma 2(d) it is a homeomorphism.
> >
> > **Equivariance and descent.** For $g\in G$, $\operatorname{ev}(g\cdot\rho)=\big(g\rho(\gamma_1)g^{-1},\dots,g\rho(\gamma_N)g^{-1}\big)=g\cdot_{\mathrm{diag}}\operatorname{ev}(\rho)$ (definition of the two actions). Thus $\operatorname{ev}$ intertwines the actions and carries conjugation orbits onto diagonal-conjugation orbits, so it descends to a continuous injection $\overline{\operatorname{ev}}\colon\mathcal R(M;G)\to G^N/G$ making $\pi_{G^N}\circ\operatorname{ev}=\overline{\operatorname{ev}}\circ\pi$ (continuity of $\overline{\operatorname{ev}}$ is the universal property of the quotient topology, since $\pi_{G^N}\circ\operatorname{ev}$ is continuous and constant on conjugation orbits). Here $G^N/G$ is compact Hausdorff by Lemma 4 (the compact metric group $G$ acting by diagonal conjugation on the compact metric $G^N$). As $\mathcal R(M;G)$ is compact (Part I) and $G^N/G$ is Hausdorff, $\overline{\operatorname{ev}}$ is a continuous injection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space, hence a homeomorphism onto its image (Lemma 2(d) applied to the corestriction), and its image is compact hence closed in $G^N/G$ (Lemma 2(a),(c)).

> [!note]- Lemma 6: The fundamental group of a product is the product of the fundamental groups
> **Statement:** For based topological spaces $(X,x_0)$ and $(Y,y_0)$, the map $\pi_1(X\times Y,(x_0,y_0))\to\pi_1(X,x_0)\times\pi_1(Y,y_0)$ induced by the two projections is a group isomorphism.
>
> **Hint:** A map into a product is a pair of maps; apply this to loops and to homotopies.
>
> **Why needed:** With $\pi_1(S^1)\cong\mathbb Z$ it gives $\pi_1(T^n)\cong\mathbb Z^n$ for the worked torus example.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Let $p\colon X\times Y\to X$ and $r\colon X\times Y\to Y$ be the projections, and define
> > $$\Theta\colon\pi_1(X\times Y,(x_0,y_0))\to\pi_1(X,x_0)\times\pi_1(Y,y_0),\qquad \Theta([\lambda])=\big([p\circ\lambda],[r\circ\lambda]\big).$$
> > **$\Theta$ is well defined and a homomorphism.** By the universal property of the product topology, a continuous map $\lambda\colon[0,1]\to X\times Y$ is the same datum as the pair of continuous maps $(p\circ\lambda,r\circ\lambda)$; $\lambda$ is a loop at $(x_0,y_0)$ iff $p\circ\lambda$ is a loop at $x_0$ and $r\circ\lambda$ is a loop at $y_0$. A based homotopy $H\colon[0,1]^2\to X\times Y$ between loops is likewise the pair $(p\circ H,r\circ H)$ of based homotopies, so homotopic loops have componentwise homotopic projections; thus $\Theta$ is well defined on homotopy classes. Concatenation is computed componentwise, $p\circ(\lambda\ast\mu)=(p\circ\lambda)\ast(p\circ\mu)$ and $r\circ(\lambda\ast\mu)=(r\circ\lambda)\ast(r\circ\mu)$ (in each factor both sides run the first half then the second, because the projection of the concatenated path is the concatenation of the projections), so $\Theta([\lambda][\mu])=\Theta([\lambda])\Theta([\mu])$: $\Theta$ is a homomorphism.
> >
> > **$\Theta$ is surjective.** Given classes $[\alpha]\in\pi_1(X,x_0)$ and $[\beta]\in\pi_1(Y,y_0)$, the map $\lambda:=(\alpha,\beta)\colon[0,1]\to X\times Y$, $t\mapsto(\alpha(t),\beta(t))$, is continuous (its components are) and is a loop at $(x_0,y_0)$, with $p\circ\lambda=\alpha$ and $r\circ\lambda=\beta$; thus $\Theta([\lambda])=([\alpha],[\beta])$.
> >
> > **$\Theta$ is injective.** Suppose $\Theta([\lambda])$ is trivial, i.e. $p\circ\lambda$ is null-homotopic in $X$ by a based homotopy $H_X$ and $r\circ\lambda$ is null-homotopic in $Y$ by a based homotopy $H_Y$. Then $H:=(H_X,H_Y)\colon[0,1]^2\to X\times Y$ is a based homotopy from $\lambda=(p\circ\lambda,r\circ\lambda)$ to the constant loop at $(x_0,y_0)$, so $[\lambda]$ is trivial. Hence $\ker\Theta$ is trivial.
> >
> > Being a bijective homomorphism, $\Theta$ is an isomorphism.

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> Let $M$ be a connected manifold with base point $m$, $\Gamma=\pi_1(M,m)$, and $G$ a compact Lie group with fixed metric $d_G\le 1$.
>
> **Step 0 — the objects are well posed.** By the standing convention, $G$ is a compact metrisable Hausdorff topological group, and $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)\subseteq G^{\Gamma}$ carries the subspace of the product topology (pointwise convergence). The conjugation action $g\cdot\rho=g\rho(\cdot)g^{-1}$ maps $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ to itself and is continuous: for each fixed $\gamma$, the map $(g,\rho)\mapsto(g\cdot\rho)(\gamma)=g\,\pi_\gamma(\rho)\,g^{-1}$ is continuous (composition of the continuous projection $\pi_\gamma$ with the continuous group operations of $G$), and a map into the product $G^{\Gamma}$ is continuous iff each coordinate is; the action lands in $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ by the computation in the Notation. Thus $\mathcal R(M;G)=\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)/G$ and its quotient topology are defined.
>
> ---
> **Part I — $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ and $\mathcal R(M;G)$ are compact Hausdorff.**
>
> **Step 1 — $\Gamma$ is countable.** By [[Thm - Existence of the Universal Cover and the Deck Action of the Fundamental Group|the universal-cover theorem]], part (d) — which states that the fundamental group $\pi_1(M,m)$ of any connected manifold $M$ is countable — the group $\Gamma$ is countable.
>
> **Step 2 — $G^{\Gamma}$ is compact.** The index set $\Gamma$ is countable (Step 1) and $G$ is a compact metric space with $d_G\le 1$. By **Lemma 1**, the product $G^{\Gamma}$ is compact and metrisable. (No appeal to the Tychonoff theorem is made; countability of $\Gamma$ is exactly what reduces the product to the range of Lemma 1's diagonal argument.)
>
> **Step 3 — $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ is compact and Hausdorff.** By **Lemma 3**, $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ is a closed subset of $G^{\Gamma}$. A closed subset of the compact space $G^{\Gamma}$ (Step 2) is compact by **Lemma 2(b)**. As a subspace of the Hausdorff space $G^{\Gamma}$ (a metric space is Hausdorff), $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ is Hausdorff; it is moreover metrisable, being a subspace of the metrisable $G^{\Gamma}$.
>
> **Step 4 — $\mathcal R(M;G)$ is compact and Hausdorff.** The compact group $G$ acts continuously (Step 0) on the compact Hausdorff metrisable space $X:=\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ (Step 3), with each $\rho\mapsto g\cdot\rho$ a homeomorphism (its inverse is $\rho\mapsto g^{-1}\cdot\rho$). By **Lemma 4**, the orbit space $X/G=\mathcal R(M;G)$ is compact and Hausdorff. This proves Part I.
>
> ---
> **Part II — the finitely generated refinement.**
>
> **Step 5 — assume finite generation and apply the embedding lemma.** Suppose in addition that $\Gamma$ is generated by $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_N$. By **Lemma 5**, the evaluation map $\operatorname{ev}\colon\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)\to G^N$, $\operatorname{ev}(\rho)=(\rho(\gamma_1),\dots,\rho(\gamma_N))$, is a homeomorphism onto a closed subset of $G^N$ (its proof of the homeomorphism property uses the compactness of $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ already established in Step 3). It is equivariant for the conjugation action on the source and the diagonal conjugation on $G^N$, and by Lemma 5 it descends to a homeomorphism $\overline{\operatorname{ev}}$ of $\mathcal R(M;G)$ onto a closed subset of $G^N/G$, which is compact Hausdorff by Lemma 4 applied to the diagonal conjugation of the compact group $G$ on the compact space $G^N$. This is exactly the statement of Part II, and it identifies the pointwise-convergence topology on $\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)$ with the subspace topology from $G^N$, reconciling our convention with Haydys's presentation topology.
>
> ---
> **Step 6 — the moduli-space corollary.** By [[Thm - Flat Connections and Monodromy Representations of the Fundamental Group|the monodromy correspondence]] — which states that $\omega\mapsto[\rho_\omega]$ is a bijection from the moduli space $\mathcal M^\flat_G(M)$ of flat $G$-connections over $M$ (up to isomorphism of bundles-with-connection) onto $\mathcal R(M;G)=\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)/G$ — the moduli space $\mathcal M^\flat_G(M)$, given the topology that makes this bijection a homeomorphism, is compact and Hausdorff by Part I. For a fixed bundle $P$, that theorem identifies $\mathcal M^\flat(P)=\mathcal A^\flat(P)/\mathcal G(P)$ with the subset of $\mathcal R(M;G)$ of classes whose associated flat bundle is isomorphic to $P$, so $\mathcal M^\flat(P)$ sits inside the compact Hausdorff space $\mathcal R(M;G)$.
>
> Therefore $\mathcal R(M;G)$ — and with it the moduli space of flat connections — is a compact Hausdorff space, finitely embedded in $G^N/G$ whenever $\pi_1(M)$ is finitely generated. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**A worked instance: flat $U(1)$-connections on the $n$-torus.** Take $M=T^n=(S^1)^n$ and $G=U(1)$. First, $\pi_1(T^n)\cong\mathbb Z^n$: by **Lemma 6** and induction, $\pi_1(T^n)=\pi_1(S^1\times T^{n-1})\cong\pi_1(S^1)\times\pi_1(T^{n-1})$, and $\pi_1(S^1)\cong\mathbb Z$ by [[Thm - Pi_1 of S^1 is Z|the computation of the fundamental group of the circle]] (the base case $n=2$ is also [[Ex - Pi_1 of the Torus is Z Squared|the torus exercise]], giving $\mathbb Z^2$). Next, a homomorphism $\rho\colon\mathbb Z^n\to U(1)$ is freely determined by the images $\rho(e_i)\in U(1)$ of the standard generators $e_1,\dots,e_n$: the only relations in $\mathbb Z^n$ are the commutators $[e_i,e_j]$, and these are automatically satisfied in the abelian group $U(1)$. Hence $\operatorname{ev}\colon\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb Z^n,U(1))\to U(1)^n$ is a bijection, and by Part II a homeomorphism; because $U(1)$ is abelian the conjugation action is trivial, so
$$\mathcal R(T^n;U(1))=\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_1(T^n),U(1))=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb Z^n,U(1))\cong U(1)^n\cong T^n,$$
which is compact, in agreement with Part I. This is Haydys's Example 107.

> [!warning] Convention: a typo in the source
> Haydys's Example 107 writes $\mathcal R(T^n;U(1))=\operatorname{Hom}(T^n,U(1))=U(1)^n$. The middle term should read $\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_1(T^n),U(1))$: the representation variety is built from homomorphisms out of the *fundamental group* $\pi_1(T^n)=\mathbb Z^n$, not out of the manifold $T^n$ itself. The corrected identity is $\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_1(T^n),U(1))=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb Z^n,U(1))=U(1)^n$, used above.

**Commuting tuples in a compact group, read algebraically.** Consider the purely algebraic question: is the set of commuting pairs $(A,B)\in SU(2)^2$, taken up to simultaneous conjugation, a compact space? Realise it as $\mathcal R(T^2;SU(2))$: since $\pi_1(T^2)=\mathbb Z^2=\langle a,b\mid aba^{-1}b^{-1}\rangle$, a representation is a pair $(A,B)=(\rho(a),\rho(b))$ with $[A,B]=1$, and conjugation of representations is simultaneous conjugation of the pair. The theorem (Part II) then says this space is a closed subset of the compact $SU(2)^2/SU(2)$, hence compact — a topological answer to an algebra question. The exercise is non-obvious because compactness of an algebraically defined quotient is not visible from the equations $[A,B]=1$ alone; it comes from realising the equations as the relation locus of a fundamental group inside a compact Lie group.

**Finite gauge groups and covering spaces.** Let $G=\Gamma_0$ be a finite group, viewed as a $0$-dimensional compact Lie group, and let $M$ be a connected manifold. Then $\mathcal R(M;\Gamma_0)=\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_1(M),\Gamma_0)/\Gamma_0$ is a finite set (a finite quotient of the finite set $\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_1(M),\Gamma_0)\subseteq\Gamma_0^N$), hence compact and discrete. The exercise is to interpret each conjugacy class of homomorphisms as a connected covering space of $M$ with deck group inside $\Gamma_0$, so that the compact moduli space of flat $\Gamma_0$-connections is the finite set of such coverings. It illustrates that the theorem specialises, at the opposite extreme from Lie groups of positive dimension, to a finite classification, and that "flat connection with finite structure group" is a synonym for "covering space".

# Bridges

- **The monodromy correspondence.** The entire theorem is a rider on [[Thm - Flat Connections and Monodromy Representations of the Fundamental Group]]: that theorem builds the homeomorphism $\mathcal M^\flat_G(M)\cong\mathcal R(M;G)$ by sending a flat connection to the conjugacy class of the parallel-transport representation of $\pi_1(M)$, using that flat parallel transport is homotopy-invariant. Only after this identification is compactness a finite algebraic question. The construction is the developing map: a flat connection is trivialised over the universal cover, where it becomes the product connection, and the deck action of $\pi_1(M)$ becomes the representation $\rho$.

- **Countability from the universal cover.** The reduction to a countable product rests on [[Thm - Existence of the Universal Cover and the Deck Action of the Fundamental Group]], whose part (d) shows $\pi_1(M)$ is countable for every connected manifold. The construction there covers $M$ by countably many coordinate balls, notes that the intersections have countably many components, and shows every loop is homotopic to a concatenation of finitely many "junction" arcs drawn from a countable set — so $\pi_1(M)$ is a countable set of words. This is the exact input Lemma 1's diagonal argument needs, and it is why the theorem holds for noncompact manifolds too.

- **Compactness in metric spaces.** Lemma 1 and the two-factor compactness inside Lemma 4 both funnel through [[Thm - Compactness in Metric Spaces (Three Equivalents)|the three-equivalents theorem]]: in a metric space, compactness, sequential compactness, and completeness-plus-total-boundedness coincide. The diagonal argument only ever produces a *sequentially* compact conclusion; it is this theorem that upgrades it to genuine (open-cover) compactness, which is what the quotient argument then consumes through "continuous image of compact is compact".

- **Toward the analytic compactness theorems.** This is the flat model of a phenomenon that later chapters must recover by hard analysis. When the flatness equation $F=0$ is replaced by the anti-self-duality equation $F^+=0$ or the Seiberg–Witten equations, the moduli space is no longer a finite algebraic quotient and its compactness fails without an a-priori bound; Uhlenbeck's compactness theorem and the Seiberg–Witten compactness theorem supply, by elliptic estimates and the Weitzenböck formula, exactly the "no escape to infinity" that compactness of $G$ gives here for free. Reading this proof first isolates which point-set facts survive into the analytic setting and which must be re-earned.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Character varieties *(from Geometry and Representation Theory)*
> For $G$ compact, $\mathcal R(M;G)=\operatorname{Hom}(\pi_1(M),G)/G$ is the **character variety** of $M$ with values in $G$. Compactness makes it a legitimate space on which to do topology: for a surface $M=\Sigma_g$ it carries a symplectic structure (Goldman) and its volume and cohomology are computable invariants. The compactness proved here is the entry ticket to that theory. See **Ex - Representation Varieties of Surface Groups**.

> [!tip] Flat connections as critical points *(from Chern–Simons Theory)*
> The Chern–Simons functional on the space of connections has the flat connections as its critical points. Because the flat moduli space is compact, the critical values form a bounded set and the functional attains extrema on each component — the finite-dimensional shadow of the quantum Chern–Simons partition function's localisation onto flat connections, developed in **Gauge Theory VI — Chern–Weil Theory, Characteristic Classes, and the Chern–Simons Functional**.
