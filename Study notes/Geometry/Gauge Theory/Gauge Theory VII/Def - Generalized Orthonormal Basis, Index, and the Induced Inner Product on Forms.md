# Notation

Throughout this page $V$ is a real vector space of finite dimension $\dim V = n$, and $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is a **non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form** on $V$ — a [[Def - Symmetric and Alternating Bilinear Form|symmetric]] [[Def - Bilinear Form|bilinear form]] with the property that the only $v\in V$ satisfying $\langle v,w\rangle = 0$ for all $w\in V$ is $v = 0$. The form is **not** assumed positive definite: $\langle v,v\rangle$ may be positive, negative, or zero for a nonzero $v$. We call such a pair $(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ an inner-product space in the generalized (indefinite) sense; when we need the positive-definite notion we say so and link [[Def - Inner Product Space|inner product space]].

We write $V^*$ for the dual space of $V$ and $\Lambda^k V^*$ for the space of [[Def - Alternating Tensor and Lambda k V Dual|alternating $k$-tensors]] on $V$, that is, $k$-multilinear maps $\omega:V^k\to\mathbb{R}$ that change sign under any transposition of two arguments. We use the **determinant convention** for the dual-basis wedges, exactly as on [[Def - Alternating Tensor and Lambda k V Dual|the alternating-tensor page]]: if $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ is a basis of $V$ and $(e_1^*,\dots,e_n^*)$ its dual basis, with $e_i^*(e_j) = \delta_{ij}$, then for an increasing multi-index $I = (i_1<\dots<i_k)$,
$$(e_{i_1}^*\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_k}^*)(v_1,\dots,v_k) = \det\big(e_{i_a}^*(v_b)\big)_{a,b=1}^k,$$
with no $1/k!$ factor. In particular, evaluated on basis vectors $(e_{j_1},\dots,e_{j_k})$ with $J = (j_1<\dots<j_k)$ increasing, this determinant equals $1$ if $J = I$ and $0$ otherwise. The $\binom{n}{k}$ wedges $e_I^* := e_{i_1}^*\wedge\dots\wedge e_{i_k}^*$ with $I$ increasing form a basis of $\Lambda^k V^*$, and $\dim\Lambda^n V^* = 1$.

The symbols $\epsilon_j\in\{+1,-1\}$ denote the diagonal signs of the form in a generalized orthonormal basis, defined below; $\epsilon_I := \epsilon_{i_1}\cdots\epsilon_{i_k}$ for a multi-index $I = (i_1,\dots,i_k)$; and $p$ denotes the **index** of the form, the number of $\epsilon_j$ equal to $-1$. We write $\delta_{ij}$ for the Kronecker delta and $\operatorname{sign}(\lambda) = \lambda/|\lambda|$ for the sign of a nonzero real number $\lambda$.

This is a compound page: it defines three interlocking notions — a *generalized orthonormal basis* of $(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$, its *index* $p$, and the *induced inner product* on $\Lambda^k V^*$ together with the *volume form* on $V$ — because they are inseparable in practice. One cannot state the induced inner product without a generalized orthonormal basis to sum over; one cannot claim that inner product is a genuine invariant of $V$ (rather than an artefact of the basis) without knowing the index is basis-independent; and the volume form is exactly the top-degree instance whose self-pairing is $(-1)^p$. All three feed the [[Def - Hodge Star in Arbitrary Signature|Hodge star in arbitrary signature]], the object this chapter is built on.

> [!warning] Convention: the word "index"
> Following Bär (Lemma 3.1.1 and the surrounding text of §3.1), the **index** of a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form is the number $p$ of negative signs $\epsilon_j = -1$ in a generalized orthonormal basis. This clashes with two other conventions already in the vault, and the reader must keep the dictionary straight.
> First, [[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia|Sylvester's law of inertia]] writes the invariant of a symmetric form as a triple $(p_{+},q_{-},r_0)$ = (number of positive, negative, zero diagonal entries); in that notation **our index is $q_{-}$, not the $p_{+}$ of that page**. For a non-degenerate form $r_0 = 0$, so $p + q_{-} = n$ where here $p = q_{-}$ is our index.
> Second, [[Def - Semi-Riemannian Metric and Signature|the vault's semi-Riemannian-signature page]] writes the signature as $(n-s,\,s)$ with $s$ the number of negative directions, so $s = p$ is precisely our index.
> Third, the special-relativity chapters (SR XXI onward) adopt the *opposite* metric sign convention $(+,-,-,-)$ on Minkowski space, for which the same physical spacetime has three negative directions, hence index $p = 3$; Bär's $(-,+,+,+)$ gives $p = 1$. The two describe the same geometry; every page that crosses this boundary states which convention it is in and converts by flipping every $\epsilon_j$, which sends $p\mapsto n-p$.

# Axiom Motivation

The problem this page solves is a purely linear-algebraic one, but it is the linear algebra without which no Hodge star, no Yang–Mills action, and no self-duality equation can even be written down. We are handed a vector space $V$ with a non-degenerate symmetric pairing $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ — on a Lorentzian manifold this is the metric $g_x$ on a tangent space $T_xM$, indefinite by physical necessity — and we must manufacture, canonically and without arbitrary choices, an inner product on the exterior powers $\Lambda^k V^*$. The Hodge star is defined by an identity relating the wedge product to that induced inner product; the Yang–Mills Lagrangian is the induced norm-squared of the curvature $2$-form; so the induced inner product is the primitive from which everything downstream is assembled. The whole difficulty is that the natural formula for it is written in terms of a *basis*, and we must be sure the object it produces does not secretly depend on the basis.

Let us see what desiderata force. In the positive-definite (Euclidean) case one already knows the answer: choose an [[Def - Inner Product Space|orthonormal basis]], declare the induced dual-basis wedges orthonormal, and check the result is basis-independent because any two orthonormal bases differ by an orthogonal matrix. The indefinite case cannot copy this verbatim, because when $\langle v,v\rangle$ can be negative there is no orthonormal basis in the usual sense — a vector $e_j$ with $\langle e_j,e_j\rangle = -1$ can never be rescaled to have self-pairing $+1$ over the reals, since rescaling by $t$ multiplies the self-pairing by $t^2 > 0$. The sign $\langle e_j,e_j\rangle$ is a genuine, unremovable invariant of the direction. So the first move is to *keep* the signs rather than try to normalise them away: we ask only that distinct basis vectors be orthogonal and that each self-pairing be $\pm 1$. This is the **generalized orthonormal basis**, and the pattern of signs it carries is the first thing we must understand.

The signs cannot be assigned freely, and here is where the index enters. Suppose we allowed a "basis" in which we simply chose the signs $\epsilon_j$ to our liking. Then the object $\sum_{i_1<\dots<i_k}\epsilon_{i_1}\cdots\epsilon_{i_k}\,\omega(e_{i_1},\dots)\,\eta(e_{i_1},\dots)$ would depend on those choices, and there would be no induced inner product at all, only a basis-dependent bilinear expression. What saves the construction is that the *number* $p$ of negative signs is not a choice: it is an invariant of the form $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ itself. This is exactly the content of [[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia|Sylvester's law of inertia]] — any two diagonalisations of a real symmetric form have the same number of negative diagonal entries — and it is the reason the index is a well-defined attribute of $(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ and not of the basis. Without Sylvester the word "index" would be meaningless and the sign $(-1)^p$ that pervades the whole chapter (in $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)+p}$, in $\langle\star\omega,\star\eta\rangle = (-1)^p\langle\omega,\eta\rangle$, in $\langle\mathrm{vol},\mathrm{vol}\rangle = (-1)^p$) would be ill-defined.

Now examine the defining formula for the induced inner product clause by clause, asking what each ingredient does and what breaks without it. The formula weights the term for the increasing multi-index $I = (i_1<\dots<i_k)$ by the product of signs $\epsilon_{i_1}\cdots\epsilon_{i_k}$. **If we dropped the sign weights** and summed $\sum_{I}\omega(e_I)\eta(e_I)$ with no $\epsilon$'s, we would get the *positive-definite* pairing that treats every basis direction as spacelike; on Minkowski space this would give $\langle\mathrm{vol},\mathrm{vol}\rangle = +1$, the Hodge star would satisfy $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)}$ with no room for the physical minus sign, and the self-dual/anti-self-dual splitting of $2$-forms in Lorentzian signature — which requires $\star^2 = -1$ on $\Lambda^2$ in dimension four — would collapse. The sign weights are precisely what remembers the indefiniteness. **If we summed over all ordered tuples** $(i_1,\dots,i_k)$ rather than increasing ones, without dividing by $k!$, we would overcount each subset by $k!$ and misnormalise the pairing; the increasing-tuple restriction is the honest count of the $\binom{n}{k}$ basis wedges, and it is exactly the sum over the basis $\{e_I^*\}$ of $\Lambda^k V^*$. **If the form were degenerate** — if some nonzero $v$ paired to zero with everything — then, as the Examples show, no generalized orthonormal basis exists at all: a generalized orthonormal basis forces the Gram matrix to be $\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_j)$ with determinant $\prod\epsilon_j = \pm1\neq 0$, so the form it comes from is automatically non-degenerate. Non-degeneracy is therefore not a convenience but the precondition for the entire construction, and it is also exactly what makes the Hodge star exist as a bijection ([[Thm - The Induced Inner Product and Volume Form are Well-Defined|the well-definedness theorem]] shows the induced form is non-degenerate, and non-degeneracy is what the [[Def - Hodge Star in Arbitrary Signature|Hodge star]] needs to invert the wedge pairing).

The volume form is the last clause, and its motivation is orientation. The space $\Lambda^n V^*$ is one-dimensional, so a nonzero top form is determined up to a scalar; the generalized orthonormal condition pins the scalar down to a sign, because the self-pairing of $e_1^*\wedge\dots\wedge e_n^*$ is forced to be $\epsilon_1\cdots\epsilon_n = (-1)^p$ and hence is the same for every generalized orthonormal basis. Only the sign is free, and an [[Def - Orientation of a Vector Space|orientation]] of $V$ is exactly a consistent choice of that sign. A reader could reconstruct all of this from the desiderata alone: keep the signs because you cannot remove them, weight by them so the pairing remembers the metric, restrict to increasing tuples so you count the basis honestly, demand non-degeneracy so a generalized orthonormal basis exists and the star can invert, and use orientation to fix the one remaining sign in top degree.

# The Definition

Let $V$ be a real vector space of dimension $n$ with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$.

**(1) Generalized orthonormal basis.** A basis $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ of $V$ is a **generalized orthonormal basis** of $(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ if
$$\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = 0 \quad\text{for } i\neq j, \qquad \langle e_j,e_j\rangle = \epsilon_j \in \{+1,-1\} \quad\text{for each } j.$$
Equivalently, the Gram matrix $\big(\langle e_i,e_j\rangle\big)_{i,j}$ is the diagonal matrix $\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_1,\dots,\epsilon_n)$ with entries $\pm 1$. The existence of such a basis for every non-degenerate symmetric form is established in the Examples.

**(2) Index.** The **index** of $(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ is the number
$$p := \#\{\,j : \epsilon_j = -1\,\},$$
the count of negative signs in a generalized orthonormal basis. This number is the same for every generalized orthonormal basis, by [[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia|Sylvester's law of inertia]]: *if a real symmetric bilinear form is diagonal in two bases, the number of negative diagonal entries is the same in both.* Since a generalized orthonormal basis diagonalises $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ with entries $\pm1$, and the number of negative entries is a basis-independent invariant, $p$ depends only on the form. (In the triple notation $(p_+,q_-,r_0)$ of the Sylvester page, our index is $q_-$; for a non-degenerate form $r_0 = 0$ and $p_+ + q_- = n$.)

**(3) Induced inner product on $\Lambda^k V^*$.** Fix a generalized orthonormal basis $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ with signs $\epsilon_j$. For $\omega,\eta\in\Lambda^k V^*$ define
$$\langle\omega,\eta\rangle \;:=\; \sum_{i_1<\dots<i_k}\epsilon_{i_1}\cdots\epsilon_{i_k}\,\omega(e_{i_1},\dots,e_{i_k})\,\eta(e_{i_1},\dots,e_{i_k}),$$
the sum ranging over all increasing multi-indices $I = (i_1<\dots<i_k)$ in $\{1,\dots,n\}$. This is a symmetric bilinear form on $\Lambda^k V^*$, denoted by the same symbol $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$. Its value does **not** depend on the generalized orthonormal basis used to compute it, and it is itself non-degenerate; both facts are the content of [[Thm - The Induced Inner Product and Volume Form are Well-Defined|the well-definedness theorem]], restated here for use: *the bilinear form on $\Lambda^k V^*$ defined by the displayed sum is independent of the generalized orthonormal basis, the wedges $\{e_I^*\}_{I\text{ increasing}}$ form a generalized orthonormal basis of $\Lambda^k V^*$ with $\langle e_I^*,e_I^*\rangle = \epsilon_{i_1}\cdots\epsilon_{i_k}$, and the induced form is non-degenerate.* For $k = 1$ the formula returns the original form: identifying $V^*\cong V$ through $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, $\langle e_i^*,e_j^*\rangle = \epsilon_i\delta_{ij}$, so degree one reproduces the metric with its signs.

**(4) Volume form.** Suppose in addition that $V$ is [[Def - Orientation of a Vector Space|oriented]], and let $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ be a **positively oriented** generalized orthonormal basis. The **volume form** of $(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ associated with the orientation is
$$\mathrm{vol} := e_1^*\wedge\dots\wedge e_n^* \in \Lambda^n V^*.$$
It is well-defined — the same element for every positively oriented generalized orthonormal basis — because $\Lambda^n V^*$ is one-dimensional and the self-pairing $\langle e_1^*\wedge\dots\wedge e_n^*,\,e_1^*\wedge\dots\wedge e_n^*\rangle = \epsilon_1\cdots\epsilon_n = (-1)^p$ is forced, pinning the top form down to a sign, which the orientation then fixes ([[Thm - The Induced Inner Product and Volume Form are Well-Defined|well-definedness theorem]], part on $\Lambda^n$). Consequently
$$\langle\mathrm{vol},\mathrm{vol}\rangle = (-1)^p,$$
and reversing the orientation sends $\mathrm{vol}\mapsto -\mathrm{vol}$, since a positively oriented generalized orthonormal basis for the opposite orientation is obtained from a given one by swapping two vectors, which negates the wedge $e_1^*\wedge\dots\wedge e_n^*$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The construction is the indefinite generalisation of a single familiar operation: **turning a metric on a space into a metric on its exterior algebra**. In the Euclidean case this is the statement that if $(e_i)$ is an orthonormal basis of an [[Def - Inner Product Space|inner product space]] then the wedges $e_I^*$ form an orthonormal basis of $\Lambda^k V^*$, the classical fact underlying the Hodge theory of Riemannian manifolds. Everything on this page is that fact with the single modification that the diagonal entries of the metric are allowed to be $-1$ as well as $+1$, and the price is one bookkeeping invariant, the index $p$, together with the sign factors $\epsilon_I$ that trail it through every formula.

**True name.** Operationally, the induced inner product is not really a new definition to memorise; it is *the unique bilinear form on $\Lambda^k V^*$ that makes the dual-basis wedges $e_I^*$ generalized orthonormal with self-pairings $\epsilon_I$.* That characterisation is basis-free in spirit even though it is stated through a basis: any two generalized orthonormal bases are related by a pseudo-orthogonal transformation (a matrix $A$ with $A^t\operatorname{diag}(\epsilon)A = \operatorname{diag}(\epsilon')$), and the induced form is exactly what is invariant under all such transformations. In the language of physics this is the statement that the inner product on forms is Lorentz-invariant: it does not care which inertial frame (which generalized orthonormal basis) an observer uses.

The index is the same invariant that appears throughout geometry and physics under several names. In the theory of [[Def - Semi-Riemannian Metric and Signature|semi-Riemannian metrics]] it is the number of negative directions in the signature; on a [[Def - Lorentzian Manifold|Lorentzian manifold]] it is $1$ (with Bär's convention $(-,+,+,+)$) or $3$ (with the particle-physics convention $(+,-,-,-)$), and this single integer controls the causal structure and the sign of $\star^2$. In quadratic-form theory over $\mathbb{R}$ it is half the difference between $n$ and the *signature* $n - 2p$ that classifies real symmetric forms up to congruence, again by [[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia|Sylvester]]. The reason all these are the same number is that they are all $q_-$ of a single symmetric form: the metric on $T_xM$, read on tangent vectors, on cotangent vectors, or as a quadratic form on coordinates.

A second compression worth stating: the sign $(-1)^p$ that is the self-pairing of the volume form is the reason the *physical* pairing $\int_M \omega\wedge\star\eta$ on a Lorentzian manifold is *not* positive definite, and this is not a defect of the construction but a feature the physics requires. The action of a field is $\int |F|^2\,\mathrm{vol}$ with the indefinite $|F|^2$, and it is exactly the interplay of the wedge product with the sign-carrying induced inner product, mediated by the Hodge star, that produces the correct Maxwell and Yang–Mills equations rather than their Euclidean caricatures.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Existence of a generalized orthonormal basis (the construction that makes clauses (1)–(4) non-vacuous).** We prove that every non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form admits a generalized orthonormal basis, by diagonalising and rescaling.

*What is assumed and what is shown.* We assume $(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ is a real $n$-dimensional space with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form. We must exhibit a basis $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ with $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = 0$ for $i\neq j$ and $\langle e_j,e_j\rangle = \pm1$.

**Step 1: diagonalise.** By [[Thm - Diagonalization of a Symmetric Bilinear Form|the diagonalisation theorem]] — *a symmetric bilinear form on a finite-dimensional vector space over a field of characteristic $\neq 2$ admits a basis in which its matrix is diagonal* — there is a basis $(v_1,\dots,v_n)$ of $V$ with
$$\langle v_i,v_j\rangle = \lambda_i\,\delta_{ij},\qquad \lambda_i\in\mathbb{R}\quad\text{(by the diagonalisation theorem, applied to }\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\text{, which is symmetric and }\operatorname{char}\mathbb{R} = 0\neq 2\text{)}.$$

**Step 2: no diagonal entry vanishes.** We claim $\lambda_i\neq 0$ for every $i$. Suppose, toward a contradiction, that $\lambda_{i_0} = 0$ for some $i_0$. Then for every basis vector $v_j$,
$$\langle v_{i_0},v_j\rangle = \lambda_{i_0}\delta_{i_0 j} = 0 \qquad\text{(by Step 1, since either } j\neq i_0 \text{ gives } \delta_{i_0 j} = 0 \text{, or } j = i_0 \text{ gives the factor } \lambda_{i_0} = 0\text{),}$$
and hence $\langle v_{i_0},w\rangle = 0$ for every $w\in V$ (by bilinearity, expanding $w = \sum_j c_j v_j$). This says $v_{i_0}$ lies in the radical of the form, contradicting the hypothesis that $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ is **non-degenerate** (whose radical is $\{0\}$, and $v_{i_0}\neq 0$ as a basis vector). Therefore $\lambda_i\neq 0$ for all $i$.

**Step 3: rescale.** Define $e_i := v_i/\sqrt{|\lambda_i|}$, which is legitimate because $|\lambda_i| > 0$ by Step 2. Then $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ is again a basis (each $v_i$ scaled by a nonzero real), and
$$\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|\lambda_i|}\,\sqrt{|\lambda_j|}}\,\langle v_i,v_j\rangle = \frac{\lambda_i\,\delta_{ij}}{\sqrt{|\lambda_i|}\,\sqrt{|\lambda_j|}} \qquad\text{(by bilinearity and Step 1).}$$
For $i\neq j$ this is $0$. For $i = j$ it is $\lambda_i/|\lambda_i| = \operatorname{sign}(\lambda_i) = \pm 1 =: \epsilon_i$. **Conclusion.** $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ is a generalized orthonormal basis, with $\epsilon_i = \operatorname{sign}(\lambda_i)$. The index $p = \#\{i:\lambda_i < 0\}$ equals the number of negative eigenvalues in the diagonalisation, which by [[Thm - Sylvester's Law of Inertia|Sylvester's law]] is independent of the diagonalising basis; this is precisely the statement that clause (2) is well-defined. $\blacksquare$

**Is an instance — Euclidean $\mathbb{R}^n$ (index $p = 0$).** Take $V = \mathbb{R}^n$ with the standard dot product $\langle x,y\rangle = \sum_j x_j y_j$ and the standard basis $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$. Then $\langle e_i,e_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$, so this is a generalized orthonormal basis with every $\epsilon_j = +1$ and index $p = 0$. The induced inner product on $\Lambda^k V^*$ has all sign weights $\epsilon_I = +1$ and reduces to the ordinary Euclidean inner product on $k$-forms, for which the wedges $e_I^*$ are genuinely orthonormal, $\langle e_I^*,e_I^*\rangle = 1$. The volume form is $\mathrm{vol} = e_1^*\wedge\dots\wedge e_n^*$ with $\langle\mathrm{vol},\mathrm{vol}\rangle = (+1)^n = 1 = (-1)^0$, matching $(-1)^p$. This is the case that recovers the Riemannian [[Def - The Hodge Star Operator|Hodge star]] of the vault (see the calibration note below on the sign relating the two conventions).

**Is an instance — Minkowski space $\mathbb{R}^{1,3}$ (index $p = 1$).** Take $V = \mathbb{R}^4$ with coordinates $(t,x,y,z)$ and the Minkowski form in Bär's convention $(-,+,+,+)$:
$$\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle = \operatorname{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$$
in the standard basis $(e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3) = (\partial_t,\partial_x,\partial_y,\partial_z)$. Then $\langle e_0,e_0\rangle = -1$ and $\langle e_j,e_j\rangle = +1$ for $j = 1,2,3$, so this is a generalized orthonormal basis with signs $(\epsilon_0,\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2,\epsilon_3) = (-1,+1,+1,+1)$ and index $p = 1$. Writing the dual basis as $(dt,dx,dy,dz) = (e_0^*,e_1^*,e_2^*,e_3^*)$, we verify the induced inner product on $\Lambda^2 V^*$ directly from the defining sum.

*A mixed (time–space) $2$-form.* Take $\omega = \eta = dt\wedge dx = e_0^*\wedge e_1^*$. Here $k = 2$, $n = 4$, and the sum runs over increasing pairs $(i_1<i_2)$ from $\{0,1,2,3\}$:
$$\langle dt\wedge dx,\,dt\wedge dx\rangle = \sum_{i_1<i_2}\epsilon_{i_1}\epsilon_{i_2}\,\big[(e_0^*\wedge e_1^*)(e_{i_1},e_{i_2})\big]^2 \qquad\text{(by the defining sum with }\omega = \eta\text{).}$$
By the determinant convention, $(e_0^*\wedge e_1^*)(e_{i_1},e_{i_2}) = \det\begin{pmatrix}\delta_{0i_1}&\delta_{0i_2}\\\delta_{1i_1}&\delta_{1i_2}\end{pmatrix}$, which for an increasing pair $(i_1<i_2)$ equals $1$ if $(i_1,i_2) = (0,1)$ and $0$ otherwise. Only the term $(i_1,i_2) = (0,1)$ survives, so
$$\langle dt\wedge dx,\,dt\wedge dx\rangle = \epsilon_0\epsilon_1 = (-1)(+1) = -1 \qquad\text{(the single surviving term).}$$
A time–space $2$-form has *negative* self-pairing, exactly as the spec records.

*A spacelike $2$-form.* Take $\omega = \eta = dx\wedge dy = e_1^*\wedge e_2^*$. By the same computation only the increasing pair $(1,2)$ survives, and
$$\langle dx\wedge dy,\,dx\wedge dy\rangle = \epsilon_1\epsilon_2 = (+1)(+1) = +1 \qquad\text{(the single surviving term).}$$
A purely spacelike $2$-form has *positive* self-pairing. The induced form on $\Lambda^2\mathbb{R}^4$ is therefore indefinite: the three time–space wedges $dt\wedge dx,\,dt\wedge dy,\,dt\wedge dz$ have self-pairing $-1$ and the three space–space wedges $dx\wedge dy,\,dy\wedge dz,\,dz\wedge dx$ have self-pairing $+1$, so $\Lambda^2$ has index $3$ and signature $(3,3)$ — a fact that drives the entire theory of self-dual and anti-self-dual $2$-forms in Lorentzian signature.

*The volume form.* With $\mathrm{vol} = dt\wedge dx\wedge dy\wedge dz = e_0^*\wedge e_1^*\wedge e_2^*\wedge e_3^*$, the defining sum in top degree $k = n = 4$ has the single increasing multi-index $(0,1,2,3)$, and $(e_0^*\wedge e_1^*\wedge e_2^*\wedge e_3^*)(e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3) = \det(I_4) = 1$, so
$$\langle\mathrm{vol},\mathrm{vol}\rangle = \epsilon_0\epsilon_1\epsilon_2\epsilon_3 = (-1)(+1)(+1)(+1) = -1 = (-1)^1 = (-1)^p \qquad\text{(single term; } p = 1\text{).}$$
This confirms $\langle\mathrm{vol},\mathrm{vol}\rangle = (-1)^p$ on $\mathbb{R}^{1,3}$.

**Is NOT an instance — a degenerate form (no generalized orthonormal basis).** Take $V = \mathbb{R}^2$ with the form $\langle(x_1,x_2),(y_1,y_2)\rangle = x_1 y_1$, whose Gram matrix in the standard basis is $\operatorname{diag}(1,0)$. This is symmetric but degenerate: the vector $u = (0,1)$ satisfies $\langle u,w\rangle = 0$ for every $w = (y_1,y_2)$, since $\langle u,w\rangle = 0\cdot y_1 = 0$, so the radical is nonzero. We verify that *no* generalized orthonormal basis can exist. Suppose $(e_1,e_2)$ were one; then its Gram matrix would be $G = \operatorname{diag}(\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2)$ with $\epsilon_i = \pm1$, whence
$$\det G = \epsilon_1\epsilon_2 = \pm 1 \neq 0.$$
But the Gram matrix in one basis is related to that in another by $G = C^t G_0 C$ with $C$ the (invertible) change-of-basis matrix and $G_0 = \operatorname{diag}(1,0)$, so by [[Thm - Determinant is Multiplicative|multiplicativity of the determinant]] — *$\det(AB) = \det A\,\det B$* — we would have $\det G = (\det C)^2\det G_0 = (\det C)^2\cdot 0 = 0$, contradicting $\det G = \pm1\neq0$. Therefore a degenerate form has no generalized orthonormal basis, and the construction of clauses (3)–(4) genuinely requires the non-degeneracy hypothesis; there is no "index" and no induced inner product to speak of. This is the precise sense in which non-degeneracy is not decorative.

**Calibration check.** First, re-derive the two Minkowski self-pairings above without looking: in $\mathbb{R}^{1,3}$ with $(-,+,+,+)$, a $2$-form built from one time index and one space index has self-pairing $\epsilon_0\epsilon_j = -1$, while one built from two space indices has self-pairing $\epsilon_i\epsilon_j = +1$ — the sign of a wedge's self-pairing is the product of the signs of the directions it uses. Second, verify $\langle\mathrm{vol},\mathrm{vol}\rangle = (-1)^p$ by counting: the top form uses every direction once, so its self-pairing is $\prod_j\epsilon_j$, and this is $(-1)^p$ because exactly $p$ of the factors are $-1$; on $\mathbb{R}^{1,3}$ this is $(-1)^1 = -1$, and on Euclidean $\mathbb{R}^n$ it is $(-1)^0 = +1$. Third, a consistency check across conventions: switching to the particle-physics signature $(+,-,-,-)$ flips every $\epsilon_j$, sending the four signs to $(+1,-1,-1,-1)$ and the index to $p = 3$; then $\langle\mathrm{vol},\mathrm{vol}\rangle = (+1)(-1)^3 = -1 = (-1)^3$, still $(-1)^p$, and the *mixed* $2$-form now has self-pairing $\epsilon_0\epsilon_1 = (+1)(-1) = -1$ — the same physical statement (a time–space plane is "negative") read in the other bookkeeping. If you can produce all three of these from the definition alone, you have understood how the index and the sign weights transmit the geometry of the metric to the exterior algebra.

> With the induced inner product and the volume form in hand, the [[Def - Hodge Star in Arbitrary Signature|Hodge star]] is the unique linear map $\star:\Lambda^k V^*\to\Lambda^{n-k}V^*$ satisfying $\omega\wedge\eta = \langle\star\omega,\eta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}$ for all $\eta\in\Lambda^{n-k}V^*$. Its existence and uniqueness rest on the non-degeneracy of the induced inner product proved via this page, and the identity $\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)+p}$ carries the index $p$ defined here.

> [!tip] Well-definedness of the whole construction *(from this chapter, §7.1)*
> That the induced inner product, its non-degeneracy, the wedge-basis orthonormality $\langle e_I^*,e_I^*\rangle = \epsilon_I$, and the volume form's value $\langle\mathrm{vol},\mathrm{vol}\rangle = (-1)^p$ are all independent of the chosen generalized orthonormal basis is proved in [[Thm - The Induced Inner Product and Volume Form are Well-Defined|the well-definedness theorem]], whose mechanism is that any two generalized orthonormal bases differ by a pseudo-orthogonal matrix of determinant $\pm1$.

> [!tip] The Yang–Mills and Maxwell actions *(from this chapter, §7.2 and §7.4)*
> The energy functional of a gauge field is $\tfrac12\int_M|F|^2\,\mathrm{vol}$, where $|F|^2 = \langle F,F\rangle$ is the induced inner product of the curvature $2$-form (extended to Lie-algebra-valued forms). On a Lorentzian manifold the index $p = 1$ makes this pairing indefinite, and it is this indefiniteness — traced back to the sign weights $\epsilon_I$ defined on this page — that turns the Euclidean minimisation problem into the Lorentzian field equations.
