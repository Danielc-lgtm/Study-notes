# Notation

Throughout, $M$ is an oriented [[Def - Lorentzian Manifold|Lorentzian]] four-manifold: a smooth four-dimensional manifold carrying a semi-Riemannian metric $g = \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ of signature $(-,+,+,+)$, so that in the notation of the Hodge-star convention its index (number of negative directions) is $p = 1$. This is the mathematical model of spacetime; the flat case, $M = \mathbb{R}^4$ with $g = -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$, is Minkowski space. We work in units with the speed of light $c = 1$, so that time and length are measured in the same unit and $g$ has the displayed form. All local coordinates $(t,x,y,z)$ used below are adapted to the signature, meaning $\langle\partial_t,\partial_t\rangle < 0$ and $\langle\partial_x,\partial_x\rangle,\langle\partial_y,\partial_y\rangle,\langle\partial_z,\partial_z\rangle > 0$; on Minkowski space these are the standard inertial coordinates.

The structure group of electromagnetism is the circle group $U(1) = \{z\in\mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$, an abelian [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] whose Lie algebra is $\mathfrak{u}(1) = i\mathbb{R} = \{i r : r\in\mathbb{R}\}$, the purely imaginary numbers, with the trivial bracket $[i r, i s] = 0$. We write $\pi\colon P\to M$ for a principal $U(1)$-bundle ([[Def - Principal G-Bundle]]), $\mathcal{C}(P)$ for its space of connections ([[Def - Connection on a Principal Bundle]]; Bär's notation, written $\mathcal{A}(P)$ on some series pages), and for a connection $\omega\in\mathcal{C}(P)$ we write $\Omega\in\Omega^2(P;\mathfrak{u}(1))$ for its [[Def - Curvature of a Principal Connection|curvature two-form on the total space]]. For a local section (local gauge) $s\colon U\to P$ over an open set $U\subseteq M$ we write $s^*\omega\in\Omega^1(U;\mathfrak{u}(1))$ and $s^*\Omega\in\Omega^2(U;\mathfrak{u}(1))$ for the pulled-back [[Def - Local Connection Form and Gauge Potential|local connection and curvature forms]]. Given a smooth transition map $g_{\alpha\beta}\colon U_{\alpha\beta}\to U(1)$ between two gauges, $\theta$ denotes the left Maurer–Cartan form of $U(1)$, so $g_{\alpha\beta}^*\theta = g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}\,dg_{\alpha\beta}$; writing $g_{\alpha\beta} = e^{i\chi}$ locally with $\chi\in C^\infty(U_{\alpha\beta};\mathbb{R})$, this reads $g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}\,dg_{\alpha\beta} = i\,d\chi\in\Omega^1(U_{\alpha\beta};i\mathbb{R})$. For matrix groups, $\operatorname{Ad}_h X = h X h^{-1}$; since $U(1)$ is abelian, $\operatorname{Ad}_h = \mathrm{id}$ on $\mathfrak{u}(1)$ for every $h\in U(1)$, a fact used repeatedly below.

We use $\bar\Omega\in\Omega^2(M;i\mathbb{R})$ for the globally defined descended curvature, $F\in\Omega^2(M;\mathbb{R})$ for the electromagnetic field strength with $\bar\Omega = iF$, the six real coordinate functions $E_x,E_y,E_z,B_x,B_y,B_z$ for its components, and the coordinate-dependent vector fields $\mathbf{E} = (E_x,E_y,E_z)$ and $\mathbf{B} = (B_x,B_y,B_z)$. A background (reference) connection is $\omega_0\in\mathcal{C}(P)$, and $A\in\Omega^1(M;\mathbb{R})$ is the potential relative to it, defined by $iA = s^*(\omega - \omega_0)$. The gradient, divergence, and curl of ordinary three-dimensional vector calculus on the spatial slices are written $\nabla\phi$, $\operatorname{div}\mathbf{B} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}$, and $\operatorname{rot}\mathbf{E} = \nabla\times\mathbf{E}$.

This is a compound page: it defines four interlocking notions — the **electromagnetic field strength** $F$, the **electric and magnetic fields** $\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B}$, the **potential relative to a background connection** $A$, and, implicitly, the **$U(1)$ gauge field** itself as the connection $\omega$ — because in the gauge-theoretic formulation these are not independent objects but successive readings of one datum, a connection on a principal $U(1)$-bundle: $F$ is its curvature, $\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B}$ are the coordinate components of $F$, and $A$ is the difference between it and a chosen reference, so they must be introduced together.

> [!warning] Convention: signature, units, and the special-relativity pages
> The special-relativity pages define the same physics with the opposite metric signature and with physical units. [[Def - The Electromagnetic Field Tensor|Special Relativity XXI's electromagnetic field tensor]] uses signature $(+,-,-,-)$ and SI units with the speed of light $c$ and the vacuum permeability $\mu_0$ made explicit, and [[Def - The Four-Potential|Special Relativity XXII's four-potential]] $A$ is defined there by $F = dA$. The translation is exact and requires no recomputation of the fields: the two-form $F$ is *the same* two-form, and the vector fields $\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B}$ are *the same* fields, because $F$ and its components are metric-independent objects (an exterior two-form and its coordinate coefficients). Only metric-dependent operations change sign: with $g_{\mathrm{SR}} = -g_B$ (Bär's metric is the negative of the special-relativity metric), the Hodge star on Minkowski two-forms flips, $\star^{\mathrm{SR}}_V = -\star_B$; this affects the pages that use $\star F$ (the [[Def - Maxwell Equations in Form Language|Maxwell equations]] and the [[Def - Electromagnetic Lagrangian and Action|Lagrangian]]), not the present page, which uses no star. Bär's normalisations of unit mass, unit charge, and $c = 1$ are what remove $\mu_0$ and $c$ from the formulae. The four-potential $A$ of [[Def - The Four-Potential]] is exactly the potential defined below for the trivial background $\omega_0$ equal to the flat connection on the trivial bundle.

# Axiom Motivation

The starting point is a fact we already possess and a question it forces. On a principal $U(1)$-bundle a connection $\omega$ has a curvature $\Omega\in\Omega^2(P;\mathfrak{u}(1))$ living on the total space $P$, and on the base $M$ we have, for each local section $s$, the pulled-back curvature $s^*\Omega\in\Omega^2(U;\mathfrak{u}(1))$. In the general theory these local curvatures do *not* agree on overlaps; they transform by $s_\beta^*\Omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}}(s_\alpha^*\Omega)$ ([[Thm - Transformation of Local Connection and Curvature Forms|the transformation law]]), and only the collection $[s_\alpha, s_\alpha^*\Omega]$ glues into a global two-form valued in the associated adjoint bundle. The question that opens electromagnetism is: what happens to this apparatus when the group is $U(1)$, the smallest and most degenerate case? The answer is that the entire adjoint-bundle machinery collapses, because $U(1)$ is abelian, and out of the collapse falls, unbidden, the whole coordinate-free language of the electromagnetic field.

Here is the desideratum, stated as a physicist would want it. Electromagnetism should be a theory in which the fundamental field is a single, globally defined, coordinate-independent object on spacetime — the field strength $F$ — from which Maxwell's equations, the Lorentz force, and the energy density all read off. The classical vector fields $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are convenient but frame-dependent shadows of it; a moving observer sees a different split of $F$ into electric and magnetic parts, and any honest formulation must have $F$ as primary and $\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B}$ as its components in a chosen coordinate frame. The definition below must therefore (i) produce a global two-form $F$ on $M$ from the connection data, with no dependence on the arbitrary choice of local gauge; (ii) recover the classical fields as its coordinate coefficients; (iii) explain the classical potentials $\phi,\mathbf{A}$ as the components of a one-form $A$; and (iv) reproduce, as identities rather than as separately postulated laws, the two homogeneous Maxwell equations $\operatorname{div}\mathbf{B} = 0$ and $\partial_t\mathbf{B} + \operatorname{rot}\mathbf{E} = 0$.

That the group be $U(1)$, and in particular abelian, is not decoration; it is what makes the definition possible, and it is worth seeing precisely which clause of the general theory each part of the definition leans on. Consider first what would break if we tried to define a global field strength for a non-abelian group. The local curvatures transform by $s_\beta^*\Omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}}(s_\alpha^*\Omega)$, and for a non-abelian $G$ this conjugation is a genuine, non-trivial action: the two local forms are related but not equal, so there is no single $\mathfrak{g}$-valued two-form on $M$ that restricts to all of them at once. The best one can do is glue them into a section of $\Lambda^2 T^*M\otimes\operatorname{ad}P$, an *adjoint-bundle-valued* form, which is not a form with values in a fixed vector space and cannot be split into scalar coordinate components $\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B}$ in any gauge-invariant way. It is precisely because $\operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}} = \mathrm{id}$ for $U(1)$ that $s_\beta^*\Omega = s_\alpha^*\Omega$ on the nose, so the local curvatures agree and descend to one honest $i\mathbb{R}$-valued two-form. Drop the abelian hypothesis and clause (i) of the desideratum fails outright: there is no global field strength of the required kind. The forward reference is to [[Def - The Yang-Mills Field Strength|the Yang–Mills field strength]], where exactly this obstruction is confronted and $F$ must be kept as an adjoint-bundle-valued form.

Consider next the potential. One is tempted to define a global potential $A$ directly as $s^*\omega$, the pulled-back connection form itself. This fails even for $U(1)$, and the failure is instructive. The connection form transforms with an inhomogeneous term, $s_\beta^*\omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}}(s_\alpha^*\omega) + g_{\alpha\beta}^*\theta = s_\alpha^*\omega + g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}\,dg_{\alpha\beta}$ (abelian, so $\operatorname{Ad} = \mathrm{id}$), and the Maurer–Cartan term $g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}\,dg_{\alpha\beta}$ is not zero: it is the reason a connection is not a tensor. So $s^*\omega$ is gauge-dependent, and there is no global one-form deserving the name "the potential of $\omega$". The repair is the one clause that the definition below adds: measure the connection *relative to a fixed reference connection* $\omega_0$. The difference $\omega - \omega_0$ of two connections is tensorial — this is the content of [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space|the affine-space theorem]], that the space of connections is affine over $\Omega^1(M;\operatorname{ad}P)$ — so its local pullbacks transform without the inhomogeneous term and glue to a global one-form. Drop the reference connection and clause (iii) fails: there is no gauge-invariant potential at all, only the transformation-law-carrying $s^*\omega$. What one buys with a background is a global $A$; what one pays is that $A$ depends on the choice of $\omega_0$, and only differences $A - A'$ and the exterior derivative $dA = F - F_0$ are intrinsic.

Finally, why insist on the abelian group *and* not merely on any bundle that happens to be trivial? On a trivial bundle one could use the global section to define $A = s^*\omega$ once and for all, and this is exactly what happens in the flat-space physics of the special-relativity pages. But electromagnetism on a topologically non-trivial spacetime — a magnetic monopole background, the field around a solenoid, a bundle over a compact manifold with non-zero first Chern class — has no global section, and there the field strength $F$ is a closed two-form that is *not* exact: no global $A$ with $F = dA$ exists, and the potential lives only relative to a background or only in local patches. The definition is engineered to survive this: $F$ is always global (clause (i) holds on any $U(1)$-bundle), while the existence of a global background-free potential is a topological luxury available only when $F$ is exact. A reader given the desiderata — one global coordinate-free field from a $U(1)$-connection, its classical components in a frame, a relative potential when a reference is fixed, the homogeneous Maxwell equations for free — could reconstruct every clause of what follows.

# The Definition

Let $M$ be an oriented Lorentzian four-manifold and $\pi\colon P\to M$ a principal $U(1)$-bundle. Fix a connection $\omega\in\mathcal{C}(P)$ with curvature $\Omega\in\Omega^2(P;\mathfrak{u}(1))$.

**The descended curvature and the field strength.** For any local section $s\colon U\to P$ the two-form $s^*\Omega\in\Omega^2(U;\mathfrak{u}(1)) = \Omega^2(U;i\mathbb{R})$ is independent of the choice of $s$, and the collection of these local forms is the restriction to the charts of a single globally defined two-form
$$\bar\Omega\in\Omega^2(M;i\mathbb{R}).$$
We record why the local forms agree, since it is the whole point of the abelian hypothesis. If $s_\alpha,s_\beta$ are two local gauges over $U_{\alpha\beta}$ with $s_\beta = s_\alpha\cdot g_{\alpha\beta}$, then by [[Thm - Transformation of Local Connection and Curvature Forms|the transformation law for the local curvature form]], which states that under a change of gauge $s_\beta = s_\alpha\cdot g_{\alpha\beta}$ the pulled-back curvatures satisfy $s_\beta^*\Omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}}(s_\alpha^*\Omega)$ with no inhomogeneous term,
$$s_\beta^*\Omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}}(s_\alpha^*\Omega) = s_\alpha^*\Omega \qquad \text{(since } U(1) \text{ is abelian, so } \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}} = \mathrm{id} \text{ on } \mathfrak{u}(1)\text{).}$$
Thus the local forms coincide on overlaps and define $\bar\Omega\in\Omega^2(M;i\mathbb{R})$ with $\bar\Omega|_U = s^*\Omega$ for every local section $s$ of $P$ over $U$. Because $\bar\Omega$ takes values in the one-dimensional imaginary line $i\mathbb{R}$, we may extract a real two-form: the **electromagnetic field strength** is the unique
$$F\in\Omega^2(M;\mathbb{R}) \qquad\text{such that}\qquad \bar\Omega = iF.$$
The one-form $\omega$ (equivalently the connection it is) is called the **$U(1)$ gauge field** or **electromagnetic connection**, and $F$ is its curvature written in real form.

**The electric and magnetic fields.** Introduce local coordinates $(t,x,y,z)$ on an open set of $M$ adapted to the signature, so $\langle\partial_t,\partial_t\rangle < 0$ and $\langle\partial_x,\partial_x\rangle,\langle\partial_y,\partial_y\rangle,\langle\partial_z,\partial_z\rangle > 0$. The six coordinate coefficients of $F$ in the basis of two-forms are named as follows: $F$ is written
$$F = E_x\,dx\wedge dt + E_y\,dy\wedge dt + E_z\,dz\wedge dt + B_x\,dy\wedge dz + B_y\,dz\wedge dx + B_z\,dx\wedge dy,$$
and the **electric field** is the time-dependent vector field $\mathbf{E} = (E_x,E_y,E_z)$ and the **magnetic field** is $\mathbf{B} = (B_x,B_y,B_z)$, each a triple of smooth real functions on the coordinate domain. Equivalently, in terms of the coordinate components $F_{\mu\nu} = F(\partial_\mu,\partial_\nu)$ (with $\mu,\nu$ ranging over $0,1,2,3$ for $t,x,y,z$), the electric field is $F_{i0} = E_i$ for $i = 1,2,3$ and the magnetic field is $B_x = F_{23}$, $B_y = F_{31}$, $B_z = F_{12}$. We stress, with Bär, that this split into $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ depends on the coordinate system: it is a decomposition of the invariant object $F$ that a choice of time direction induces, and a different adapted frame — a boosted observer — produces different $\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B}$ from the same $F$.

**The potential relative to a background connection.** Fix a second connection $\omega_0\in\mathcal{C}(P)$, called a **background** or **reference connection**, with descended curvature $\bar\Omega_0 = iF_0$. For any local section $s\colon U\to P$ the one-form $s^*(\omega - \omega_0)\in\Omega^1(U;i\mathbb{R})$ is independent of the choice of $s$ and defines a global one-form; the **potential of $\omega$ relative to $\omega_0$** is the unique real one-form $A = A(\omega,\omega_0)\in\Omega^1(M;\mathbb{R})$ with
$$iA = s^*(\omega - \omega_0) \qquad \text{for every local section } s.$$
Two verifications complete the definition, and both are short because the abelian hypothesis has already done the work.

*Well-definedness of $A$ (independence of the section).* Under a change of gauge $s_\beta = s_\alpha\cdot g_{\alpha\beta}$, [[Thm - Transformation of Local Connection and Curvature Forms|the transformation law for the connection form]] gives, for each of $\omega$ and $\omega_0$,
$$s_\beta^*\omega = \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}}(s_\alpha^*\omega) + g_{\alpha\beta}^*\theta, \qquad s_\beta^*\omega_0 = \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}}(s_\alpha^*\omega_0) + g_{\alpha\beta}^*\theta,$$
where $g_{\alpha\beta}^*\theta = g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}\,dg_{\alpha\beta}$ is the pulled-back Maurer–Cartan form, the *same* inhomogeneous term for both connections because it depends only on the gauge change, not on the connection. Subtracting,
$$s_\beta^*(\omega - \omega_0) = \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}}\big(s_\alpha^*(\omega - \omega_0)\big) = s_\alpha^*(\omega - \omega_0) \qquad \text{(the two } g_{\alpha\beta}^*\theta \text{ terms cancel; then } \operatorname{Ad}_{g_{\alpha\beta}^{-1}} = \mathrm{id} \text{ since } U(1) \text{ is abelian).}$$
So the local one-forms agree on overlaps and glue to the global $iA$. This is the concrete face of [[Thm - The Space of Connections is an Affine Space|the affine-space theorem]]: the space of connections $\mathcal{C}(P)$ is an affine space modelled on $\Omega^1(M;\operatorname{ad}P)$, which for $U(1)$ is $\Omega^1(M;i\mathbb{R})$ because the adjoint bundle $\operatorname{ad}P = P\times_{\operatorname{Ad}}\mathfrak{u}(1)$ is the trivial bundle $M\times i\mathbb{R}$ (the $\operatorname{Ad}$-action being trivial), and the *difference* $\omega - \omega_0$ of two connections is exactly the tensorial element of that model space, which is why it descends where $\omega$ alone does not.

*The identity $dA = F - F_0$.* Because $U(1)$ is abelian, the [[Def - Curvature of a Principal Connection|structure equation]] $\Omega = d\omega + \tfrac12[\omega\wedge\omega]$ loses its bracket term, $[\omega\wedge\omega] = 0$, and reduces to $\Omega = d\omega$; the same holds for $\omega_0$. Hence, using that pullback commutes with the exterior derivative,
$$i\,dA = d(iA) = d\,s^*(\omega - \omega_0) = s^*\big(d\omega - d\omega_0\big) = s^*\Omega - s^*\Omega_0 = \bar\Omega - \bar\Omega_0 = i(F - F_0),$$
with justifications, in order: definition of $iA$; $d$ commutes with $s^*$; the abelian structure equation $d\omega = \Omega$, $d\omega_0 = \Omega_0$; the definitions of $\bar\Omega,\bar\Omega_0$; and $\bar\Omega = iF$, $\bar\Omega_0 = iF_0$. Dividing by $i$, $dA = F - F_0$.

**The closedness of $F$.** By [[Thm - Bianchi Identity for a Principal Connection|the Bianchi identity]] — which in the abelian case states that $\Omega = d\omega$, so $d\Omega = d\,d\omega = 0$, and the descended forms satisfy $d\bar\Omega = 0$ — the field strength is closed:
$$dF = 0.$$
This is Bär's Remark 3.2.1: the field strength of any $U(1)$-connection is automatically a closed real two-form.

> [!warning] Convention: Haydys's local form on a trivialisation
> On a *trivialisation* of the bundle, that is, over an open set $U$ admitting a global section $s\colon U\to P|_U$, one may take the background $\omega_0$ to be the flat connection pulled back from the product bundle, so that $s^*\omega_0 = 0$ and $iA = s^*\omega$ directly. In this local picture, which is the one Haydys and most physics texts write, the local gauge potential is $A_{\mathrm{loc}}$ with $iA_{\mathrm{loc}} = s^*\omega$ and the field strength is $F = dA_{\mathrm{loc}}$, an honest exact form on $U$. The two descriptions agree on their common domain; the difference is only that the relative potential $A(\omega,\omega_0)$ is defined globally on all of $M$ (once a global $\omega_0$ is fixed), whereas $A_{\mathrm{loc}}$ is defined only where a section exists. On a non-trivial bundle no global section exists, so $A_{\mathrm{loc}}$ has no global meaning, and the relative potential is the correct object.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The gauge-theoretic content is cleanest stated structurally, and the structure is that of a torsor. A principal $U(1)$-bundle $\pi\colon P\to M$ is a **bundle of $U(1)$-torsors**: each fibre $P_x = \pi^{-1}(x)$ is a space on which $U(1)$ acts freely and transitively on the right, so $P_x$ is a copy of the circle that has *forgotten which point is the identity* — one can compare two points of a fibre by the unique group element carrying one to the other, but there is no distinguished basepoint. A **$U(1)$ gauge field** is a connection $\omega$ on this torsor bundle: equivalently ([[Def - Connection on a Principal Bundle]]), a $U(1)$-invariant choice of horizontal complement $H = \ker\omega$ to the vertical tangent spaces, giving a notion of parallel transport of phases along paths in $M$.

In this language the objects of the present page have crisp structural readings. The **field strength** $F$ is the curvature of this connection, the obstruction to the horizontal distribution being integrable — the failure of phase transport around an infinitesimal loop to return to the starting phase — and its being a global two-form on $M$ (rather than a bundle-valued form) is the categorical statement that for the abelian group $U(1)$ the adjoint bundle $\operatorname{ad}P = P\times_{\operatorname{Ad}}\mathfrak{u}(1)$ is canonically trivial: the $\operatorname{Ad}$-action being trivial, the associated bundle is $M\times i\mathbb{R}$, so $\operatorname{ad}P$-valued forms *are* $i\mathbb{R}$-valued forms. The **gauge potentials** are the local trivialised forms of the connection: a local section $s$ is a local trivialisation, and $s^*\omega$ is the connection read in that trivialisation. Change of trivialisation acts on potentials by the affine transformation $A\mapsto A + d\chi$ (the abelian gauge transformation), and the invariants of this affine action — the curvature $F = dA$ and its de Rham class $[F]$ — are exactly the data that descend to the base independently of any trivialisation. The **potential relative to a background** is the choice of an origin in the affine space of connections: the torsor of connections $\mathcal{C}(P)$ over the vector space $\Omega^1(M;i\mathbb{R})$ has no canonical origin, and fixing $\omega_0$ trivialises it, identifying $\omega\leftrightarrow iA(\omega,\omega_0)$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

**True name.** Operationally, a $U(1)$ gauge field *is a closed real two-form $F$ together with the integrality datum of which $U(1)$-bundle it is the curvature of.* Everything measurable about the electromagnetic field — the Lorentz force on a test charge, the electromagnetic energy, the two homogeneous Maxwell equations — is a function of $F$ alone; the connection $\omega$ and the potential $A$ are gauges for computing with $F$, physically redundant up to the abelian gauge transformation $A\mapsto A + d\chi$. What $F$ alone does *not* remember, and what the bundle $P$ supplies, is the topological quantisation: on a non-trivial bundle $F$ is closed but not exact, and its periods $\int_\Sigma \tfrac{i}{2\pi}\bar\Omega = \int_\Sigma \tfrac{-1}{2\pi}F$ over closed surfaces $\Sigma$ are integers (the first Chern numbers, by [[Thm - First Chern Class of a Line Bundle from Curvature|the first-Chern-class formula]]). So the true name has two parts: the field $F$ is a de Rham cohomology class refined to an integral one, and the potential $A$ is a local trivialisation of the connection whose curvature is $F$.

This construction is the one place where several theories the reader already knows are literally the same object. In **special relativity** (the [[Def - The Electromagnetic Field Tensor|Special Relativity XXI]] pages) the electromagnetic field tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$ is precisely the coordinate-component array of the two-form $F$ here, and the four-potential $A_\mu$ of [[Def - The Four-Potential|Special Relativity XXII]] is the potential $A$ for the trivial background on flat Minkowski space; the gauge-theoretic account adds only the global, topological layer that flat spacetime hides. In **complex line-bundle geometry** a $U(1)$-connection is the same datum as a Hermitian connection on the associated complex line bundle $L = P\times_{U(1)}\mathbb{C}$, and $F$ is (up to the factor $i$) the curvature of that line bundle, whose cohomology class is the first Chern class; this is the bridge to the [[Thm - First Chern Class of a Line Bundle from Curvature|Chern–Weil theory]] of chapter VI and to the classification of line bundles. In **quantum mechanics** the same $S^3\to S^2$ Hopf bundle whose connection appears below is the bundle of normalised state vectors of a two-level system over the space of physical states, and the parallel transport of $\omega$ is the Berry phase; the fibre $U(1)$ is the unobservable global phase. The compression is that "a field with an unremovable local phase ambiguity" and "a connection on a $U(1)$-bundle" are the same notion, and electromagnetism is its oldest instance.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance — the trivial bundle over Minkowski space, recovering the classical potentials.** Take $M = \mathbb{R}^4$ with the Minkowski metric $g = -dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$, and $P = M\times U(1)$ the trivial bundle with global section $s(m) = (m,1)$. Choose the flat connection as background, $s^*\omega_0 = 0$, so the potential is $iA = s^*\omega$ with $A\in\Omega^1(\mathbb{R}^4;\mathbb{R})$ arbitrary. Write the classical decomposition of a one-form into a scalar potential $\phi\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}^4;\mathbb{R})$ and a vector potential $\mathbf{A} = (A_x,A_y,A_z)$,
$$A = -\phi\,dt + A_x\,dx + A_y\,dy + A_z\,dz = -\phi\,dt + \mathbf{A}\cdot d\mathbf{x}.$$
We verify, clause by clause, that $F = dA$ produces the classical fields $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla\phi - \partial_t\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$. Compute the exterior derivative term by term, writing $\partial_t = \partial/\partial t$ and so on:
$$dA = -d\phi\wedge dt + dA_x\wedge dx + dA_y\wedge dy + dA_z\wedge dz \qquad \text{(Leibniz rule for } d \text{ on products } f\,dx^\mu\text{, and } d(dt)=0\text{).}$$
Expanding each differential — $d\phi = \partial_t\phi\,dt + \partial_x\phi\,dx + \partial_y\phi\,dy + \partial_z\phi\,dz$, and $dA_j = \partial_t A_j\,dt + \partial_x A_j\,dx + \partial_y A_j\,dy + \partial_z A_j\,dz$ for each $j\in\{x,y,z\}$ by the same formula — and using $dt\wedge dt = 0$ and the antisymmetry $du\wedge dv = -dv\wedge du$:
$$-d\phi\wedge dt = -\partial_x\phi\,dx\wedge dt - \partial_y\phi\,dy\wedge dt - \partial_z\phi\,dz\wedge dt \qquad \text{(the } dt\wedge dt \text{ term drops).}$$
$$dA_x\wedge dx = \partial_t A_x\,dt\wedge dx + \partial_y A_x\,dy\wedge dx + \partial_z A_x\,dz\wedge dx = -\partial_t A_x\,dx\wedge dt - \partial_y A_x\,dx\wedge dy + \partial_z A_x\,dz\wedge dx,$$
using $dt\wedge dx = -dx\wedge dt$ and $dy\wedge dx = -dx\wedge dy$. Likewise
$$dA_y\wedge dy = -\partial_t A_y\,dy\wedge dt + \partial_x A_y\,dx\wedge dy - \partial_z A_y\,dy\wedge dz,$$
$$dA_z\wedge dz = -\partial_t A_z\,dz\wedge dt - \partial_x A_z\,dz\wedge dx + \partial_y A_z\,dy\wedge dz.$$
Now collect coefficients and read them against the defining expansion $F = E_x\,dx\wedge dt + \dots + B_x\,dy\wedge dz + \dots$.

> [!note]- Derivation — reading off $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ coefficient by coefficient
> **Electric coefficients (the $\cdot\wedge dt$ terms).**
> - Coefficient of $dx\wedge dt$: from $-d\phi\wedge dt$ we get $-\partial_x\phi$, from $dA_x\wedge dx$ we get $-\partial_t A_x$; total $E_x = -\partial_x\phi - \partial_t A_x$.
> - Coefficient of $dy\wedge dt$: $-\partial_y\phi$ (from $-d\phi\wedge dt$) and $-\partial_t A_y$ (from $dA_y\wedge dy$); total $E_y = -\partial_y\phi - \partial_t A_y$.
> - Coefficient of $dz\wedge dt$: $-\partial_z\phi$ and $-\partial_t A_z$; total $E_z = -\partial_z\phi - \partial_t A_z$.
>
> Hence $\mathbf{E} = (E_x,E_y,E_z) = -\nabla\phi - \partial_t\mathbf{A}$, the classical relation between the electric field and the potentials.
>
> **Magnetic coefficients (the spatial terms).**
> - Coefficient of $dx\wedge dy$: from $dA_x\wedge dx$ we get $-\partial_y A_x$, from $dA_y\wedge dy$ we get $+\partial_x A_y$; total $B_z = \partial_x A_y - \partial_y A_x = (\nabla\times\mathbf{A})_z$.
> - Coefficient of $dy\wedge dz$: from $dA_y\wedge dy$ we get $-\partial_z A_y$, from $dA_z\wedge dz$ we get $+\partial_y A_z$; total $B_x = \partial_y A_z - \partial_z A_y = (\nabla\times\mathbf{A})_x$.
> - Coefficient of $dz\wedge dx$: from $dA_z\wedge dz$ we get $-\partial_x A_z$, from $dA_x\wedge dx$ we get $+\partial_z A_x$; total $B_y = \partial_z A_x - \partial_x A_z = (\nabla\times\mathbf{A})_y$.
>
> Hence $\mathbf{B} = (B_x,B_y,B_z) = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$, the classical relation between the magnetic field and the vector potential. Every coefficient of $F$ has been matched, so the identification is complete.

This confirms clauses (ii) and (iii) of the desiderata on the flat model: $\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B}$ are the coordinate components of $F = dA$, and the classical potentials $\phi,\mathbf{A}$ are the components of the relative potential $A$.

**Calibration check — $dF = 0$ directly in the Minkowski example.** We verify the closedness $dF = 0$ by computing $dF$ in coordinates and reading off the two homogeneous Maxwell equations, following Bär's calculation (his p. 84). Differentiating $F = E_x\,dx\wedge dt + E_y\,dy\wedge dt + E_z\,dz\wedge dt + B_x\,dy\wedge dz + B_y\,dz\wedge dx + B_z\,dx\wedge dy$ and collecting the four independent three-form components gives
$$dF = \big(\partial_x B_x + \partial_y B_y + \partial_z B_z\big)\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz + \big(\partial_y E_z - \partial_z E_y + \partial_t B_x\big)\,dt\wedge dy\wedge dz + \big(\partial_z E_x - \partial_x E_z + \partial_t B_y\big)\,dt\wedge dz\wedge dx + \big(\partial_x E_y - \partial_y E_x + \partial_t B_z\big)\,dt\wedge dx\wedge dy.$$

> [!warning] Source typo (Bär p. 84, Appendix B item 15)
> Bär's printed expansion gives the coefficient of $dt\wedge dy\wedge dz$ as "$-\partial_y E_z + \partial_y E_z + \partial_t B_x$", which is a typographical slip (the first two terms would cancel). The correct coefficient, computed below, is $\partial_y E_z - \partial_z E_y + \partial_t B_x$; we use the corrected form.

> [!note]- Derivation — the coefficient of $dt\wedge dy\wedge dz$ in $dF$
> The three-form $dt\wedge dy\wedge dz$ receives contributions from exactly the terms of $F$ built from $dt,dy,dz$: namely $E_y\,dy\wedge dt$, $E_z\,dz\wedge dt$, and $B_x\,dy\wedge dz$.
> - From $E_y\,dy\wedge dt$: $d(E_y)\wedge dy\wedge dt$ contributes its $\partial_z E_y\,dz\wedge dy\wedge dt$ part. Reordering to the standard basis, $dz\wedge dy\wedge dt = -dt\wedge dy\wedge dz$ (moving $dt$ two places to the front is an even permutation, then swapping $dy,dz$ is one transposition), so this is $-\partial_z E_y\,dt\wedge dy\wedge dz$.
> - From $E_z\,dz\wedge dt$: the $\partial_y E_z\,dy\wedge dz\wedge dt$ part, and $dy\wedge dz\wedge dt = dt\wedge dy\wedge dz$ (moving $dt$ two places forward, an even permutation), gives $+\partial_y E_z\,dt\wedge dy\wedge dz$.
> - From $B_x\,dy\wedge dz$: the $\partial_t B_x\,dt\wedge dy\wedge dz$ part gives $+\partial_t B_x\,dt\wedge dy\wedge dz$.
>
> Summing, the coefficient is $\partial_y E_z - \partial_z E_y + \partial_t B_x = (\operatorname{rot}\mathbf{E})_x + (\partial_t\mathbf{B})_x$, the $x$-component of $\partial_t\mathbf{B} + \operatorname{rot}\mathbf{E}$. The other two mixed coefficients are the $y$- and $z$-components by the same computation with the coordinate labels cycled.

Setting $dF = 0$ and requiring each of the four independent three-form coefficients to vanish gives exactly the two homogeneous Maxwell equations:
$$dF = 0 \iff \operatorname{div}\mathbf{B} = 0 \quad\text{(Gauß's law for magnetism)} \quad\text{and}\quad \partial_t\mathbf{B} + \operatorname{rot}\mathbf{E} = 0 \quad\text{(Faraday's law).}$$
Finally we confirm these hold identically for $F = dA$: with $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$ we have $\operatorname{div}\mathbf{B} = \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{A}) = 0$ (the divergence of a curl vanishes), and with $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla\phi - \partial_t\mathbf{A}$,
$$\partial_t\mathbf{B} + \operatorname{rot}\mathbf{E} = \partial_t(\nabla\times\mathbf{A}) + \nabla\times(-\nabla\phi - \partial_t\mathbf{A}) = \nabla\times\partial_t\mathbf{A} - \nabla\times\nabla\phi - \nabla\times\partial_t\mathbf{A} = -\nabla\times\nabla\phi = 0,$$
with justifications: linearity and the commutation of $\partial_t$ with $\nabla\times$; the curl of a gradient vanishes, $\nabla\times\nabla\phi = 0$. So $dF = 0$ holds directly, in agreement with the abstract Bianchi identity, and the homogeneous Maxwell equations are consequences of $F$ being closed, not separate postulates. This is the promised clause (iv) of the desiderata.

**Is NOT an instance of "$F = dA$ globally" — the Hopf bundle over $S^2$.** The requirement that a $U(1)$ gauge field admit a *global* background-free potential $A$ with $F = dA$ is a topological condition that fails on non-trivial bundles, and the smallest witness is the [[Def - The Hopf Bundle|Hopf bundle]] $\pi\colon S^3\to S^2$. (Here the base is the compact Riemannian two-manifold $S^2$ rather than a Lorentzian four-manifold; the point being made is purely about the descent of the curvature and the exactness of $F$, which is a statement about $U(1)$-connections on any manifold.) Equip the Hopf bundle with any connection $\omega$; its curvature descends, as proved above, to a closed real two-form $F\in\Omega^2(S^2;\mathbb{R})$, and by symmetry considerations one may take $F$ proportional to the area form of the round metric. We verify that $F$ is closed but not exact.

*Closed.* $dF = 0$ by the Bianchi identity, exactly as for any $U(1)$-connection; this is one instance of the general corollary.

*Not exact.* By [[Thm - First Chern Class of a Line Bundle from Curvature|the first-Chern-class formula]], for the Hermitian line bundle $L = P\times_{U(1)}\mathbb{C}$ associated to $P$ the first Chern class is represented by $\tfrac{i}{2\pi}\bar\Omega = \tfrac{i}{2\pi}(iF) = -\tfrac{1}{2\pi}F$, and its integral over the closed surface $S^2$ is the integer degree of the bundle,
$$\int_{S^2} \Big(-\tfrac{1}{2\pi}F\Big) = \deg L \in\mathbb{Z}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \int_{S^2} F = -2\pi\deg L.$$
For the Hopf bundle $\deg L = \pm 1$ (its transition function winds once around $U(1)$, as recorded on [[Def - The Hopf Bundle|its page]]), so $\int_{S^2}F = \mp 2\pi \neq 0$. Now suppose, for contradiction, that $F = dA$ for a *global* one-form $A\in\Omega^1(S^2;\mathbb{R})$. Then by [[Thm - Stokes' Theorem on Manifolds|Stokes' theorem]], which states $\int_M d\alpha = \int_{\partial M}\alpha$ for a compactly supported form $\alpha$ on an oriented manifold with boundary $M$, applied to the closed manifold $S^2$ (whose boundary $\partial S^2 = \varnothing$),
$$\int_{S^2} F = \int_{S^2} dA = \int_{\partial S^2} A = 0,$$
contradicting $\int_{S^2}F \neq 0$. The named contradiction is between the computed non-zero period and the vanishing Stokes integral. Therefore no global potential exists: on the Hopf bundle the field strength is closed but not exact, and $A$ is available only locally on each hemisphere or, globally, only *relative to a background connection* $\omega_0$ (where $dA = F - F_0$ measures the difference of two curvatures, not $F$ itself). This is the non-instance that the definition of the *relative* potential is designed to accommodate, and it is the mathematical content of Dirac's magnetic monopole: the monopole charge is the non-zero period $\int_{S^2}F$, quantised by the integrality of $\deg L$.

**Corollary — the gauge freedom of the potential is exactly $A\mapsto A + d\chi$.** Two potentials for the same connection relative to the same background differ by an exact form: if $iA = s^*(\omega - \omega_0)$ and $iA' = s'^*(\omega-\omega_0)$ for a second global section $s' = s\cdot e^{i\chi}$ with $\chi\in C^\infty(M;\mathbb{R})$ (available when the bundle is trivial), then by the computation of well-definedness above the sections give the *same* $A$; the classical "gauge freedom" instead arises from changing the trivialisation used to write $A_{\mathrm{loc}} = s^*\omega$, under which $A_{\mathrm{loc}}\mapsto A_{\mathrm{loc}} + d\chi$ because $s'^*\omega = s^*\omega + (e^{i\chi})^{-1}d(e^{i\chi}) = s^*\omega + i\,d\chi$. In every case the field strength $F = dA_{\mathrm{loc}}$ is unchanged, since $d(d\chi) = 0$, which is the coordinate statement of the gauge invariance of the electromagnetic field.

**Calibration check.** Confirm the following three quickly from the page. First, that the sign in $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla\phi - \partial_t\mathbf{A}$ comes precisely from the two contributions $-\partial_x\phi$ (from $-\phi\,dt$) and $-\partial_t A_x$ (from the reordering $dt\wedge dx = -dx\wedge dt$), so that flipping the coordinate expansion $F = E_x\,dx\wedge dt$ to the opposite orientation $E_x\,dt\wedge dx$ would flip the sign of $\mathbf{E}$. Second, that the closedness $dF = 0$ is a genuine identity — it follows either abstractly from the Bianchi identity or concretely from $\operatorname{div}\operatorname{curl} = 0$ and $\operatorname{curl}\operatorname{grad} = 0$ — and is not one of the dynamical Maxwell equations (those, $d\star F + J = 0$, involve the metric through the Hodge star and are laws, not identities). Third, that on a trivial bundle the relative potential with flat background *is* a global potential ($F = dA$), while on the Hopf bundle no global potential exists because $\int_{S^2}F\neq 0$ — so the distinction between "field strength" and "potential" is topological, invisible on $\mathbb{R}^4$ and unavoidable on a non-trivial base.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Charge-Current 3-Form and the Maxwell Equations *(from this chapter)*
> With the field strength $F$ in hand, the sources of the field are packaged into a **[[Def - Charge-Current 3-Form|charge-current three-form]]** $J\in\Omega^3(M;\mathbb{R})$, and the two inhomogeneous Maxwell equations become the single form equation $d\star F + J = 0$, the Euler–Lagrange equation of the electromagnetic action. The homogeneous pair is the identity $dF = 0$ proved here; the inhomogeneous pair is a law involving the metric through the Hodge star.

> [!tip] Electromagnetic Lagrangian and Action *(from this chapter)*
> The field strength feeds the **[[Def - Electromagnetic Lagrangian and Action|electromagnetic Lagrangian]]** $L(\omega) = \tfrac12 F\wedge\star F + A\wedge J$, whose critical points are the physical fields. The relative potential $A$ enters through the coupling term $A\wedge J$, and the background-independence of the resulting equations of motion is the first payoff of having defined $A$ relative to a reference.

> [!tip] The Yang–Mills Field Strength *(from §7.4)*
> Replacing $U(1)$ by a non-abelian compact group such as $SU(N)$ gives the **[[Def - The Yang-Mills Field Strength|Yang–Mills field strength]]**, where the abelian collapse used here fails: the curvature no longer descends to a scalar-valued form but only to an adjoint-bundle-valued form, and the gauge transformation acts non-trivially by $\operatorname{Ad}$. Electromagnetism is the abelian shadow of this theory, and comparing the two shows exactly which features are artifacts of $U(1)$ being one-dimensional and commutative.

> [!tip] Dirac Quantisation and the Monopole *(from §7.2 exercises)*
> The non-exactness of $F$ on a non-trivial bundle, illustrated above by the Hopf bundle, is the content of the **Dirac monopole**: a static radial magnetic field on $\mathbb{R}^3\setminus\{0\}$ has $\int_{S^2}F = 4\pi g \neq 0$, forcing the monopole charge $g$ to be quantised so that the underlying $U(1)$-bundle exists. This is worked out in the exercise on the Dirac monopole as a non-trivial $U(1)$-bundle.
