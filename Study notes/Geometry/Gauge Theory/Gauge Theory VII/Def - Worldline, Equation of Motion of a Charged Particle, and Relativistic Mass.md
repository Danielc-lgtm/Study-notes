# Notation

Throughout, $M$ is an oriented **Lorentzian** $4$-manifold — the mathematical model for spacetime — carrying a semi-Riemannian metric $g = \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ of signature $(-,+,+,+)$. In these notes the sign convention is Bär's: one timelike direction, three spacelike, and the speed of light is normalised to $c = 1$, so that "velocity less than the speed of light" reads $|\vec v| < 1$. A tangent vector $X \in T_pM$ is [[Def - Causal Classification of Tangent Vectors|**timelike**]] if $\langle X, X\rangle < 0$, **null** (lightlike) if $\langle X, X\rangle = 0$ and $X \neq 0$, and **spacelike** if $\langle X, X\rangle > 0$ or $X = 0$.

We work in **standard coordinates** $(t, x, y, z)$ on Minkowski space, meaning coordinates in which $\langle \partial_t, \partial_t\rangle = -1$ and $\langle \partial_x, \partial_x\rangle = \langle \partial_y, \partial_y\rangle = \langle \partial_z, \partial_z\rangle = +1$, with all off-diagonal inner products of the coordinate vector fields vanishing. A smooth curve is $c : I \to M$ on an open interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, with parameter written $\tau$; its coordinate presentation is split into a time part and a space part,
$$c(\tau) = \big(t(\tau), x(\tau), y(\tau), z(\tau)\big) = \big(t(\tau), \vec c(\tau)\big), \qquad \vec c := (x, y, z),$$
and $c'(\tau) = \big(t'(\tau), \vec c\,'(\tau)\big) \in T_{c(\tau)}M$ is its velocity, where a prime denotes $d/d\tau$. The Euclidean length of the spatial part is $|\vec c\,'| = \big((x')^2 + (y')^2 + (z')^2\big)^{1/2}$.

The electromagnetic field is encoded in the field strength $F \in \Omega^2(M; \mathbb{R})$, the real $2$-form obtained from the curvature of a [[Def - U(1) Gauge Field and Electromagnetic Connection|$U(1)$ gauge field]] $\omega$ by $\bar\Omega = iF$. In standard coordinates
$$F = E_x\,dx\wedge dt + E_y\,dy\wedge dt + E_z\,dz\wedge dt + B_x\,dy\wedge dz + B_y\,dz\wedge dx + B_z\,dx\wedge dy,$$
so that $\vec E = (E_x, E_y, E_z)$ is the **electric field** and $\vec B = (B_x, B_y, B_z)$ the **magnetic field** relative to these coordinates; both are (coordinate-dependent) time-dependent vector fields on the spatial slices. The symbol $\times$ is the Euclidean cross product on $\mathbb{R}^3$ and $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ used between two spatial vectors is the Euclidean dot product; context distinguishes it from the Lorentzian $g$.

The velocity is differentiated along $c$ by the [[Def - Covariant Derivative along a Curve|covariant derivative along the curve]] $\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}$, the operator induced on vector fields along $c$ by the [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita connection]] $\nabla$ of $g$; in coordinates $\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}X^k = \frac{dX^k}{d\tau} + \Gamma^k_{ij}\,c'^{\,i} X^j$ with $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ the Christoffel symbols of $g$ (all zero in Minkowski standard coordinates). We raise an index on a covector by the [[Def - Musical Isomorphism (Flat and Sharp)|musical isomorphism (sharp)]]: for $\eta \in T^*_pM$, the vector $\eta^\sharp \in T_pM$ is characterised by
$$\eta(Y) = \langle \eta^\sharp, Y\rangle \qquad \text{for all } Y \in T_pM$$
(this is exactly the characterisation in Bär's footnote to equation (3.12), and it coincides with the vault's sharp for the metric $g$). When $F$ is contracted in its first slot with the velocity, $F(c', \cdot)$ denotes the $1$-form $Y \mapsto F(c', Y)$ along $c$, and $F(c', \cdot)^\sharp$ its sharp — a vector field along $c$.

> [!warning] Convention: signature and the sign of the Lorentz four-force
> The vault's special-relativity development, **[[Def - The Lorentz Four-Force]]** (Special Relativity XXI), works in the opposite signature $(+,-,-,-)$ and writes the equation of motion as $m\,dU/d\tau = q\,F(\cdot, U)^\sharp$. The two descriptions define the *same* physical worldlines. The dictionary is: the field strength is literally the same $2$-form $F$ and the electric and magnetic fields $\vec E, \vec B$ have the same physical meaning, while the metrics are related by $g_{\mathrm{SR}} = -g_B$. Passing $g_B \mapsto -g_B$ negates the sharp (an index is raised with the inverse metric), and the antisymmetry $F(c', \cdot) = -F(\cdot, c')$ supplies a second sign, so that with $q = 1$ Bär's $\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}c' + F(c',\cdot)^\sharp = 0$, i.e. $\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}c' = F(\cdot, c')^\sharp$, matches the Special Relativity XXI form. The net effect on the observable trajectory $t \mapsto \vec c(t)$ is nil; only the bookkeeping signs differ.

This is a **compound page**: it fixes three interlocking notions at once — the *worldline* of a test particle together with its *observed velocity*; the *equation of motion* that a charged particle obeys in the field $F$; and the *eigentime parametrisation* together with the *relativistic mass* — because they are inseparable as a modelling package. The observed velocity is only meaningful for a timelike worldline; the equation of motion is stated for such a worldline; and the eigentime normalisation is the choice of parameter in which the mass formula takes its cleanest form. Following Bär's §3.2, we record Newton's law and the velocity-dependence of mass as *definitions* — the physical postulates that turn geometry into dynamics — not as theorems; what *is* provable from them (constancy of speed, existence and uniqueness of solutions, the Lorentz force law) lives on the theorem pages that this page feeds.

# Axiom Motivation

We have built, on the geometric side, a $U(1)$ gauge field on spacetime and distilled it into a closed $2$-form $F$ whose components in a coordinate frame we named $\vec E$ and $\vec B$. Nothing so far tells us how a particle *responds* to $F$. The question this page answers is the physicist's question: given the field, what path does a charged particle trace through spacetime, and how do we read a velocity off that path? The answer is not derivable from the geometry — it is the empirical content of electrodynamics — so it enters as a set of modelling choices. What we can and must do is show that these choices are internally coherent and that each ingredient is forced once we accept the previous ones.

**Why the worldline is timelike.** A "test particle" is an idealised massive point object whose own field is negligible, so that it merely samples $F$. Its history is a curve $c$ in spacetime. The demand that the particle be *massive* and *causal* — that it travels slower than light and has a well-defined rest frame — is precisely the demand that its velocity be timelike, $\langle c', c'\rangle < 0$ everywhere. Drop this clause and two distinct pathologies appear. If we allow $c'$ to be *null* ($\langle c', c'\rangle = 0$), the particle moves at the speed of light: it is a photon, it has no rest frame, and, as we show below, the observed-velocity formula gives $|\vec v| = 1$, so the very quantity we are trying to define sits on the boundary of its allowed range and no proper-time parametrisation exists (the arclength is degenerate). If we allow $c'$ to be *spacelike* ($\langle c', c'\rangle > 0$), the particle is a tachyon: the observed velocity, when it exists at all, satisfies $|\vec v| > 1$, which is acausal, and for a purely spatial tangent ($t' = 0$) the observed velocity is undefined outright. Timelikeness is thus the exact clause that makes "observed velocity below light speed" a theorem rather than an accident; the non-example at the end of the page exhibits the spacelike failure explicitly.

**Why the observed velocity divides by $t'$.** A worldline may be reparametrised freely; the parameter $\tau$ carries no physical meaning by itself. The raw spatial velocity $\vec c\,' = d\vec c/d\tau$ therefore cannot be *the* velocity, because rescaling $\tau$ rescales it. What an observer using coordinate time $t$ actually measures is the rate of change of position with respect to *that observer's clock*, $\vec v = d\vec c/dt$. The chain rule turns this into a reparametrisation-invariant combination,
$$\vec v = \frac{d\vec c}{dt} = \frac{d\vec c}{d\tau}\cdot\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \frac{\vec c\,'}{t'},$$
which is well-defined precisely because timelikeness guarantees $t' \neq 0$ (shown below). Had we not divided by $t'$, the definition would depend on the arbitrary parameter and would fail to be a physical observable. The price of invariance is that $\vec v$, unlike $c'$, is not a tangent vector to $M$ but a coordinate-frame velocity living on the spatial slice; this is the correct object, since velocity in the everyday sense is inherently frame-dependent.

**Why the force is $F(c', \cdot)^\sharp$, and why it is linear in the velocity.** Newton's law in a curved geometry equates the covariant acceleration $\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}c'$ with a force. Electromagnetism supplies a very specific force: one *linear in the velocity* and built from $F$ alone, namely $-F(c', \cdot)^\sharp$. This is the only natural vector one can manufacture from $F$ (a $2$-form) and $c'$ (a vector) using the metric: contract $F$ once with $c'$ to get a covector, then raise the index. The linearity is not an approximation — it is what distinguishes the Lorentz force from every other force law. The most consequential feature of this choice is the *antisymmetry* of $F$: because $F(c', c') = 0$, the force is always orthogonal (in the Lorentzian sense) to the velocity, so it does no work on the particle. Were the force not built from an antisymmetric tensor, the covariant square $\langle c', c'\rangle$ would not be preserved, the particle's speed would drift, and — through the mass formula below — its mass would change under a magnetic field, contradicting experiment. This is the mechanism behind [[Thm - Solutions of the Lorentz Force Equation Have Constant Speed and Exist Uniquely|the constant-speed theorem]]: *the skewness of $F$ is the reason a charged particle keeps its rest mass.* Replace $F(c', \cdot)^\sharp$ by the gradient of a scalar potential and one gets a *conservative* force whose worldlines are entirely different (and whose speed is not preserved); replace it by a generic velocity-dependent vector and one leaves electromagnetism altogether.

**Why eigentime, and why the constant is $-1$.** The equation of motion is stated for an arbitrary timelike parametrisation, but the constant-speed theorem tells us every solution is automatically parametrised proportionally to arclength. It is then a convenience — not a further postulate — to fix the proportionality constant by demanding $\langle c', c'\rangle = -1$. This choice identifies the parameter $\tau$ with the *proper time* (eigentime) measured by a clock carried along the worldline: over a stretch of the curve, $\int \sqrt{-\langle c', c'\rangle}\, d\tau = \int d\tau$ is exactly the elapsed parameter. Any other negative constant would merely rescale $\tau$ linearly; the value $-1$ is the one that makes $\tau$ a genuine clock reading. The supplementary condition $t' > 0$ selects the *future-directed* branch: since a timelike curve has $t' \neq 0$, the sign of $t'$ is constant on a connected worldline, and replacing $\tau$ by $-\tau$ if necessary makes the particle move forward in coordinate time. Both normalisations are achievable for any timelike solution and neither adds physical content; they only pin down the description.

**Why the relativistic mass formula belongs here as a postulate.** The final ingredient, $m = m_0/\sqrt{1 - |\vec v|^2}$, is the special-relativistic statement that a body's inertia grows without bound as its observed speed approaches that of light; $m_0 > 0$ is the *rest mass*, the inertia measured in the body's instantaneous rest frame. This is an experimental law of relativistic dynamics, imported into the model, not something the differential geometry produces on its own. What the geometry *does* give us — and what we prove on this page — is that under the eigentime normalisation the awkward factor $1/\sqrt{1 - |\vec v|^2}$ equals the geometric quantity $t'$, so that $m = m_0\,t'$. This identity is the bridge that lets the coordinate expression of Newton's law be rewritten as the familiar Lorentz force law $\frac{d}{dt}(m\vec v) = \vec E + \vec v\times\vec B$ on [[Thm - The Lorentz Force Law from the Equation of Motion|the Lorentz-force-law page]]. A reader who accepts the timelike worldline, the invariant observed velocity, and the skew force could reconstruct the eigentime normalisation unaided; the mass formula is the one piece that must be handed over from physics.

# The Definition

Fix an oriented Lorentzian $4$-manifold $(M, g)$ and a $U(1)$ gauge field on $M$ with field strength $F \in \Omega^2(M; \mathbb{R})$. A **test particle** is idealised to have rest mass $m_0 = 1$ and charge $1$ unless stated otherwise.

**Worldline and observed velocity.** A **worldline** of a test particle is a timelike smooth curve $c : I \to M$, that is, a smooth curve with
$$\langle c'(\tau), c'(\tau)\rangle < 0 \qquad \text{for all } \tau \in I.$$
In standard coordinates on Minkowski space, writing $c(\tau) = \big(t(\tau), \vec c(\tau)\big)$, the **observed velocity** of the worldline is the spatial vector
$$\vec v := \frac{d\vec c}{dt} = \frac{\vec c\,'}{t'} \in \mathbb{R}^3.$$
It is well-defined (because $t' \neq 0$) and satisfies $|\vec v| < 1$; both facts are proved in the Examples / Corollaries section below.

**Equation of motion.** The particle's response to the field is the **equation of motion** (Bär's equation (3.12)): the worldline $c$ obeys Newton's law with the electromagnetic (Lorentz) force $-F(c', \cdot)^\sharp$,
$$\boxed{\;\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}c' + F\big(c', \cdot\big)^\sharp = 0\;.}$$
Here $\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}c'$ is the covariant derivative of the velocity field along $c$ for the Levi-Civita connection of $g$, $F(c', \cdot)$ is the $1$-form $Y \mapsto F(c', Y)$ along $c$, and $(\,\cdot\,)^\sharp$ raises its index by $\eta(Y) = \langle\eta^\sharp, Y\rangle$. Equivalently, by the antisymmetry of $F$,
$$\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}c' = -F(c', \cdot)^\sharp = F(\cdot, c')^\sharp.$$
This equation is a **modelling postulate**: it defines what it means for the worldline to be that of a charged particle in the field $F$, and it is not derived from anything more primitive.

**Eigentime parametrisation and relativistic mass.** A timelike worldline is said to be **parametrised by eigentime** (proper time) if
$$\langle c'(\tau), c'(\tau)\rangle = -1 \qquad \text{and} \qquad t'(\tau) > 0 \quad\text{for all }\tau.$$
Any timelike curve can be brought to this form by an affine reparametrisation together, if necessary, with the replacement $\tau \mapsto -\tau$; and by [[Thm - Solutions of the Lorentz Force Equation Have Constant Speed and Exist Uniquely|the constant-speed theorem]] — *every solution $c$ of the equation of motion satisfies $\frac{d}{d\tau}\langle c', c'\rangle = 0$, hence is parametrised proportionally to eigentime* — a solution of the equation of motion always admits such a parametrisation. The **relativistic mass** of a test particle of rest mass $m_0 > 0$ moving with observed velocity $\vec v$ is postulated (from special relativity) to be
$$m := \frac{m_0}{\sqrt{1 - |\vec v|^2}}.$$
Under the eigentime normalisation one has the geometric identity $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - |\vec v|^2}} = t'$, so that
$$m = m_0\,t',$$
proved in the Examples / Corollaries section. For a test particle $m_0 = 1$, so $m = t'$.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

The equation of motion is the electromagnetic member of a family that runs through all of geometry: *acceleration equals force*, with the acceleration measured covariantly. Setting $F = 0$ collapses it to the [[Def - Levi-Civita Connection|geodesic equation]] $\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}c' = 0$, so a free relativistic particle traces a timelike geodesic — the Lorentzian analogue of "a free body moves in a straight line." Turning on $F$ bends the geodesic by exactly the amount a skew, velocity-linear force prescribes. In this light the Lorentz force is the simplest possible perturbation of geodesic motion: linear in the velocity, and built from a $2$-form so that it conserves speed. The same template, with a different force term, gives the equation for a particle in a Yang–Mills field (the force is then valued in an associated bundle) and, with the force replaced by a Riemann-curvature term, the Jacobi equation for geodesic deviation.

**True name.** Operationally, the equation of motion is best understood not as a second-order equation for $c$ but as a *first-order flow on the tangent bundle* $TM$. A pair $(c, c')$ evolving by the equation of motion is an integral curve of the smooth vector field $Z$ on $TM$ whose value at $v \in T_pM$ is the geodesic spray at $v$ corrected by the vertical lift of $-F(v, \cdot)^\sharp$; in coordinates this reads $\ddot c^{\,k} + \Gamma^k_{ij}\dot c^{\,i}\dot c^{\,j} + g^{kl}F_{il}\dot c^{\,i} = 0$. This is the operational content that makes existence and uniqueness a one-line consequence of the theory of flows, and it is the form used on [[Thm - Solutions of the Lorentz Force Equation Have Constant Speed and Exist Uniquely|the existence-and-uniqueness page]]. The observed velocity, by contrast, is already operational as defined: it is what a coordinate observer's rods and clocks report.

The relativistic mass is the compression of a dynamical fact into a kinematic factor. In the eigentime picture it is nothing but the time-dilation factor $t' = dt/d\tau$, the rate at which coordinate time runs relative to the particle's own clock. That the inertial mass and the time-dilation factor coincide (up to $m_0$) is the content of the identity $m = m_0 t'$: relativistic dynamics does not need a separate "variable mass" beyond the geometry of proper time. This is why many modern treatments avoid the term "relativistic mass" altogether and speak only of the four-momentum $m_0 c'$; the factor $t'$ then sits visibly in its time component. We keep Bär's terminology because it is the one his §3.2 uses to reach the Lorentz force law.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Corollary — timelikeness forces $t' \neq 0$ and $|\vec v| < 1$.** Let $c$ be a worldline, so $\langle c', c'\rangle < 0$. In standard coordinates,
$$\langle c', c'\rangle = -(t')^2 + |\vec c\,'|^2 \qquad \text{(expanding } c' = t'\partial_t + \vec c\,'\text{ against } \langle\partial_t,\partial_t\rangle=-1,\ \langle\partial_{x^i},\partial_{x^j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}).$$
The timelikeness condition $-(t')^2 + |\vec c\,'|^2 < 0$ gives
$$(t')^2 > |\vec c\,'|^2 \geq 0 \qquad \text{(rearranging the inequality; a square is nonnegative)},$$
so $(t')^2 > 0$, hence $t' \neq 0$ and the observed velocity $\vec v = \vec c\,'/t'$ is well-defined. Dividing the same inequality by $(t')^2 > 0$,
$$|\vec v|^2 = \frac{|\vec c\,'|^2}{(t')^2} < 1 \qquad \text{(divide } (t')^2 > |\vec c\,'|^2 \text{ by } (t')^2 > 0),$$
and therefore $|\vec v| < 1$: the observed velocity of a test particle is strictly less than the speed of light. This verifies the two claims left open in The Definition. $\blacksquare$

**Corollary — under eigentime, $1/\sqrt{1 - |\vec v|^2} = t'$ and $m = m_0 t'$.** Assume $c$ is parametrised by eigentime, so $\langle c', c'\rangle = -1$ and $t' > 0$. Then
$$-(t')^2 + |\vec c\,'|^2 = -1 \qquad \text{(the eigentime condition in coordinates)},$$
hence
$$|\vec c\,'|^2 = (t')^2 - 1 \qquad \text{(adding } (t')^2 \text{ to both sides).}$$
Substituting into the observed speed,
$$1 - |\vec v|^2 = 1 - \frac{|\vec c\,'|^2}{(t')^2} = 1 - \frac{(t')^2 - 1}{(t')^2} = \frac{1}{(t')^2} \qquad \text{(using } |\vec c\,'|^2 = (t')^2 - 1\text{, then combining fractions).}$$
Since $t' > 0$, taking positive square roots gives $\sqrt{1 - |\vec v|^2} = 1/t'$, and therefore
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - |\vec v|^2}} = t' \qquad \text{(reciprocal of the previous line, } t' > 0 \text{ so no sign ambiguity).}$$
Multiplying by the rest mass, $m = m_0 / \sqrt{1 - |\vec v|^2} = m_0 t'$. This is exactly the computation Bär records on p. 88, and it is the identity that lets the equation of motion be rewritten as the Lorentz force law. $\blacksquare$

**Is an instance — the free particle moves on a timelike geodesic.** Suppose $F = 0$ (no field, or a region where the field vanishes). The equation of motion becomes
$$\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}c' = 0 \qquad \text{(the force term } F(c',\cdot)^\sharp = 0 \text{ drops out)},$$
which is the geodesic equation. By [[Thm - Existence and Uniqueness of Geodesics|the existence and uniqueness theorem for geodesics]] — *for every $p \in M$ and $X \in T_pM$ there is a unique maximal geodesic $c$ with $c(\tau_0) = p$ and $c'(\tau_0) = X$* — through each event with each initial velocity there is exactly one free worldline. In standard Minkowski coordinates all Christoffel symbols vanish, so the geodesic equation reads $\ddot c = 0$ and the worldline is the straight line $c(\tau) = c(\tau_0) + (\tau - \tau_0)\,c'(\tau_0)$. Taking $c'(\tau_0)$ timelike keeps $c$ timelike (the inner product $\langle c', c'\rangle$ is constant along a geodesic by metric compatibility, so it stays negative), and its observed velocity $\vec v = \vec c\,'/t'$ is a constant vector of length $< 1$ by the first corollary — uniform motion below light speed, as Newton's first law demands. This is a worldline satisfying every clause of the definition.

**Is an instance — a charge in a constant magnetic field undergoes cyclotron motion.** Take Minkowski space with the static field $\vec E = 0$ and $\vec B = (0, 0, B)$ constant, $B > 0$; this is a genuine electromagnetic field, since the corresponding $F = B\,dx\wedge dy$ is closed ($dF = 0$, as $B$ is constant), so it is the field strength of a $U(1)$ gauge field. For a test particle ($m_0 = 1$) parametrised by eigentime, [[Thm - The Lorentz Force Law from the Equation of Motion|the Lorentz force law]] — *on Minkowski space the equation of motion is equivalent to $\frac{d}{dt}(m\vec v) = \vec E + \vec v\times\vec B$ with $m = m_0 t'$* — reduces here, using $\vec E = 0$, to $\frac{d}{dt}(m\vec v) = \vec v\times\vec B$. By [[Thm - Solutions of the Lorentz Force Equation Have Constant Speed and Exist Uniquely|the constant-speed theorem]] the eigentime factor $t'$ (hence $m = t'$) is constant along the motion, so this is $m\,\dot{\vec v} = \vec v\times\vec B$ with $m$ a positive constant. We verify a solution clause by clause: with cyclotron frequency $\omega := B/m$, set
$$\vec v(t) = (v_\perp\cos\omega t,\ -v_\perp\sin\omega t,\ v_z), \qquad v_\perp, v_z \in \mathbb{R} \text{ constant}.$$
Then $\dot{\vec v} = (-v_\perp\omega\sin\omega t,\ -v_\perp\omega\cos\omega t,\ 0)$, and
$$\vec v\times\vec B = \big(v_y B_z - v_z B_y,\ v_z B_x - v_x B_z,\ v_x B_y - v_y B_x\big) = \big(-v_\perp\sin\omega t\cdot B,\ -v_\perp\cos\omega t\cdot B,\ 0\big) \quad \text{(with } \vec B = (0,0,B)).$$
Comparing components, $m\,\dot{\vec v} = (-m v_\perp\omega\sin\omega t,\ -m v_\perp\omega\cos\omega t,\ 0)$ equals $\vec v\times\vec B$ exactly when $m\omega = B$, which is our choice $\omega = B/m$; so the equation holds identically in $t$. The observed speed $|\vec v| = \sqrt{v_\perp^2 + v_z^2}$ is constant (consistent with constant $m$), the motion is a circle of radius $v_\perp/\omega$ in the plane perpendicular to $\vec B$ superposed with uniform drift along $\vec B$ — a helix. The full timelike worldline, obtained by integrating $t' = m$ and $\vec c\,' = m\vec v$, and its explicit integration, are worked out in [[Thm - Motion of a Charge in a Uniform Field]] (Special Relativity XXI, in the $(+,-,-,-)$ signature; the trajectory is identical after the sign dictionary of the Notation callout). Every clause of the definition is met: the worldline is timelike (speed $< 1$), it solves the equation of motion, and it is eigentime-parametrised.

**Is NOT an instance — a spacelike curve has no admissible observed velocity.** Let $c$ be a smooth curve that is spacelike, $\langle c', c'\rangle > 0$, so in standard coordinates $-(t')^2 + |\vec c\,'|^2 > 0$, i.e. $|\vec c\,'|^2 > (t')^2$. Two ways the definition fails. First, if the tangent is purely spatial at some instant, $t' = 0$ (allowed for a spacelike vector, since then $\langle c', c'\rangle = |\vec c\,'|^2 > 0$ needs only $\vec c\,' \neq 0$), the observed velocity $\vec v = \vec c\,'/t'$ is *undefined* — division by zero. Second, wherever $t' \neq 0$, dividing $|\vec c\,'|^2 > (t')^2$ by $(t')^2 > 0$ gives
$$|\vec v|^2 = \frac{|\vec c\,'|^2}{(t')^2} > 1 \qquad \text{(divide the spacelike inequality by } (t')^2),$$
so $|\vec v| > 1$: the "observed velocity," when it exists, exceeds the speed of light. A spacelike curve is therefore not the worldline of any test particle, and the requirement $\langle c', c'\rangle < 0$ in the definition is exactly what excludes it. (The intermediate null case $\langle c', c'\rangle = 0$ with $c' \neq 0$ gives $|\vec c\,'| = |t'|$ and hence $|\vec v| = 1$ — light speed, again outside the strict inequality $|\vec v| < 1$.)

**Calibration check.** Verify these three from what is on the page. (i) A worldline that is timelike at a single instant of a geodesic ($F = 0$) is timelike for all $\tau$: $\langle c', c'\rangle$ is constant along a geodesic because $\frac{d}{d\tau}\langle c', c'\rangle = 2\langle\frac{\nabla}{d\tau}c', c'\rangle = 0$, so a negative value persists. (ii) The eigentime factor is dimensionless-consistent with the drift: in the cyclotron example, integrating $t' = m$ (constant) gives $t = m(\tau - \tau_0) + t_0$, so coordinate time runs $m = 1/\sqrt{1 - |\vec v|^2}$ times faster than proper time, the expected time dilation. (iii) Reversing orientation of the parameter, $\tau \mapsto -\tau$, flips the sign of $t'$ but leaves $\vec v = \vec c\,'/t'$ unchanged and leaves $\langle c', c'\rangle$ unchanged; this is why the future-directed condition $t' > 0$ is a free normalisation that does not touch the observed velocity.

> [!tip] The Lorentz force law *(from Gauge Theory VII)*
> Written out in standard Minkowski coordinates with $m = m_0 t'$, the equation of motion becomes the classical **[[Thm - The Lorentz Force Law from the Equation of Motion|Lorentz force law]]** $\frac{d}{dt}(m\vec v) = \vec E + \vec v\times\vec B$. The identity $m = m_0 t'$ proved here is exactly the substitution that converts the covariant Newton's law into this three-dimensional form.

> [!tip] Constant speed and well-posedness *(from Gauge Theory VII)*
> The antisymmetry of $F$ makes the force do no work, so **[[Thm - Solutions of the Lorentz Force Equation Have Constant Speed and Exist Uniquely|every solution keeps its speed and exists uniquely]]**: a timelike solution stays timelike, is proportionally eigentime-parametrised, and is determined by its initial position and velocity. This is what justifies imposing the eigentime normalisation on solutions after the fact.

> [!tip] Dirac quantisation *(from Gauge Theory VII)*
> When the field cannot be described by a single global potential — as for a magnetic monopole — the worldline still obeys this equation of motion locally, but the underlying $U(1)$ bundle is nontrivial, and the charge is quantised. This is the content of **[[Ex - The Dirac Monopole as a Nontrivial U(1) Bundle]]**.
