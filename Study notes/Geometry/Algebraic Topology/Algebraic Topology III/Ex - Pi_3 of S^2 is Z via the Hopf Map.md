# Problem Statement

Show that $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$, generated by the **Hopf map** $\eta : S^3 \to S^2$. This is the spectacular computation: a 3-sphere maps non-trivially onto a 2-sphere, despite the "naive" expectation that higher-dimensional spheres should have no homotopy classes to lower-dimensional ones.

**Recall:**

The objects in play are the Hopf map, the Hopf fibration, the long exact sequence of a fibration, and higher homotopy groups.

![[Def - The Hopf Map#The Definition]]

The **Hopf fibration** is the principal $U(1)$-bundle $S^1 \hookrightarrow S^3 \xrightarrow{\eta} S^2$.

![[Thm - Long Exact Sequence of a Fibration#Statement]]

Recall the basic homotopy facts we will use:
- $\pi_k(S^1) = 0$ for $k \geq 2$ (since $S^1 \cong \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ has universal cover $\mathbb{R}$, contractible).
- $\pi_2(S^1) = 0$ and $\pi_3(S^1) = 0$.
- $\pi_2(S^3) = 0$ and $\pi_3(S^3) = \mathbb{Z}$ (the latter by [[Ex - Pi_n of S^n is Z]]).

---

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** This is a **compute a homotopy group via a fibration** problem — the standard application of the [[Thm - Long Exact Sequence of a Fibration|long exact sequence of a fibration]] when the relevant fibration is known. As the [[Algebraic Topology III — Higher Homotopy and Chern Forms#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic-page strategy]] urges, when faced with $\pi_k(X)$ for a space appearing in a fibration, the first move is to write down the long exact sequence and read off what the data forces.

**Assumption pattern.** The hypothesis is the existence of the Hopf fibration $S^1 \to S^3 \to S^2$, plus the values of $\pi_k$ of the spheres $S^1$ and $S^3$ in low degrees. The fibre being $S^1$ is what produces an isomorphism (since $\pi_3(S^1) = \pi_2(S^1) = 0$); the total space being $S^3$ provides the input $\pi_3(S^3) = \mathbb{Z}$. The miracle is that these two combine via exactness to give $\pi_3(S^2) = \pi_3(S^3) = \mathbb{Z}$.

**Theorem routing.** The route is the long exact sequence of the Hopf fibration:
$$\pi_3(S^1) \to \pi_3(S^3) \to \pi_3(S^2) \xrightarrow{\partial} \pi_2(S^1).$$
The two outer terms are zero ($\pi_3(S^1) = \pi_2(S^1) = 0$), so by exactness $\pi_3(S^3) \to \pi_3(S^2)$ is an isomorphism. Since $\pi_3(S^3) = \mathbb{Z}$, we get $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$. The generator of $\pi_3(S^3)$ is the identity map; its image under $\eta_*$ (the map induced by the Hopf projection $\eta$) is the homotopy class of $\eta$ itself, so the Hopf map generates.

**Key decision point.** The crucial recognition is that the relevant fibration is the **Hopf fibration**. There are other fibrations involving $S^3$ and $S^2$ (e.g., the trivial $S^2 \times S^1 \to S^2$), but only the Hopf fibration has fibre $S^1$ with $\pi_3(S^1) = \pi_2(S^1) = 0$ in the right places. The non-obvious move is identifying the Hopf map *as a fibration*, exploiting the special algebraic structure of $\mathbb{C}^2$ (the scalar action of $U(1)$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ gives a free action on $S^3$).

# Legal Operations Used

This solution deploys the following legal operations from [[Algebraic Topology III — Higher Homotopy and Chern Forms#Legal Operations|the topic page's Legal Operations]]:

1. **Compute $\pi_k$ via the long exact sequence of a fibration** (operation 1). The Hopf fibration provides the long exact sequence, and exactness pins down $\pi_3(S^2)$.

2. **Use trivial-group sandwich**: $0 \to A \to B \to 0$ implies $A \cong B$. Here applied to $\pi_3(S^3) \to \pi_3(S^2) \to 0$.

3. **Recognise the Hopf map as the fibration projection** (operation 7, via HLP). The generator of $\pi_3(S^2)$ from the exact sequence is the image of the identity in $\pi_3(S^3)$, which traces back through the fibration to be the Hopf map itself.

# Solution

The proof is in three steps. Step 1 writes down the long exact sequence of the Hopf fibration around degree 3. Step 2 uses the vanishing of $\pi_3(S^1)$ and $\pi_2(S^1)$ to conclude $\pi_3(S^3) \cong \pi_3(S^2)$. Step 3 identifies the generator as the Hopf map.

**Step 1: Write down the long exact sequence around degree 3.**

For the Hopf fibration $S^1 \hookrightarrow S^3 \xrightarrow{\eta} S^2$, the [[Thm - Long Exact Sequence of a Fibration|long exact sequence]] gives, around degree 3:

$$\cdots \to \pi_3(S^1) \to \pi_3(S^3) \xrightarrow{\eta_*} \pi_3(S^2) \xrightarrow{\partial} \pi_2(S^1) \to \pi_2(S^3) \to \cdots$$

> [!note]- Derivation
> The long exact sequence of a fibration $F \hookrightarrow E \xrightarrow{\pi} B$ is
> $$\cdots \to \pi_k(F) \to \pi_k(E) \to \pi_k(B) \to \pi_{k-1}(F) \to \cdots$$
> Specialising to the Hopf fibration with $F = S^1$, $E = S^3$, $B = S^2$, and reading at $k = 3$:
> $$\pi_3(S^1) \to \pi_3(S^3) \to \pi_3(S^2) \to \pi_2(S^1) \to \pi_2(S^3).$$
>
> The map $\pi_3(S^3) \to \pi_3(S^2)$ is induced by the projection $\eta : S^3 \to S^2$ (this is $\eta_*$, the pushforward on $\pi_3$).
>
> The map $\partial : \pi_3(S^2) \to \pi_2(S^1)$ is the connecting boundary homomorphism, constructed via the homotopy lifting property.

**Step 2: $\pi_3(S^1) = \pi_2(S^1) = 0$, so $\eta_*$ is an isomorphism.**

We need $\pi_3(S^1) = 0$ and $\pi_2(S^1) = 0$, both consequences of the contractibility of the universal cover $\mathbb{R}$ of $S^1$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> The universal cover of $S^1$ is $\mathbb{R}$ (the exponential map $\mathbb{R} \to S^1$, $t \mapsto e^{2\pi i t}$, is a covering with discrete fibre $\mathbb{Z}$). By the long exact sequence of the covering $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{R} \to S^1$, we have $\pi_k(S^1) = \pi_k(\mathbb{R}) = 0$ for $k \geq 2$ (since $\mathbb{R}$ is contractible and the discrete fibre $\mathbb{Z}$ contributes only to $\pi_0$ via the deck-group action).
>
> So $\pi_2(S^1) = 0$ and $\pi_3(S^1) = 0$.
>
> Substituting into the long exact sequence:
> $$0 \to \pi_3(S^3) \xrightarrow{\eta_*} \pi_3(S^2) \to 0.$$
> By exactness, this gives $\eta_* : \pi_3(S^3) \to \pi_3(S^2)$ both injective ($\ker \eta_* = \mathrm{im}(\pi_3(S^1) \to \pi_3(S^3)) = 0$) and surjective ($\mathrm{im}\,\eta_* = \ker(\pi_3(S^2) \to \pi_2(S^1)) = \pi_3(S^2)$ since the target $\pi_2(S^1) = 0$). So $\eta_*$ is an isomorphism.

**Step 3: $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$, generated by the Hopf map.**

By the previous step, $\pi_3(S^3) \cong \pi_3(S^2)$ via $\eta_*$. By [[Ex - Pi_n of S^n is Z]], $\pi_3(S^3) = \mathbb{Z}$, generated by the identity map $[\mathrm{id}_{S^3}]$. So $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$, generated by $\eta_*([\mathrm{id}_{S^3}])$.

> [!note]- Derivation
> $\eta_*([\mathrm{id}_{S^3}]) = [\eta \circ \mathrm{id}_{S^3}] = [\eta]$, the homotopy class of the Hopf map itself.
>
> So the generator of $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$ is the homotopy class $[\eta]$ of the Hopf map. Any class $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ is represented by $k \cdot \eta = \eta + \eta + \cdots + \eta$ ($k$ times in the group $\pi_3(S^2)$), or geometrically by a map of "winding number" $k$ relative to the Hopf class.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> Let $\eta : S^3 \to S^2$ be the Hopf map, the principal $U(1)$-bundle projection of the Hopf fibration $S^1 \hookrightarrow S^3 \xrightarrow{\eta} S^2$.
>
> By the [[Thm - Long Exact Sequence of a Fibration|long exact sequence]] of the Hopf fibration, we have at degree 3:
> $$\pi_3(S^1) \to \pi_3(S^3) \xrightarrow{\eta_*} \pi_3(S^2) \xrightarrow{\partial} \pi_2(S^1) \to \pi_2(S^3).$$
>
> The universal cover of $S^1$ is the contractible $\mathbb{R}$, so by the long exact sequence of the covering $\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{R} \to S^1$, $\pi_k(S^1) = 0$ for $k \geq 2$. In particular, $\pi_2(S^1) = \pi_3(S^1) = 0$.
>
> Substituting:
> $$0 \to \pi_3(S^3) \xrightarrow{\eta_*} \pi_3(S^2) \to 0,$$
> so $\eta_*$ is an isomorphism by exactness.
>
> By the computation in [[Ex - Pi_n of S^n is Z]], $\pi_3(S^3) = \mathbb{Z}$ with generator $[\mathrm{id}_{S^3}]$. The isomorphism $\eta_*$ sends $[\mathrm{id}_{S^3}]$ to $[\eta]$, so $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$ with generator the homotopy class of the Hopf map. $\blacksquare$

> [!warning] Illegal but tempting: assume $\pi_k(X \times Y) = \pi_k(X) \oplus \pi_k(Y)$ applies to all fibrations
> A naive computation would write $S^3 \to S^2$ as part of "a product structure", giving $\pi_3(S^2) = \pi_3(S^3) \oplus \pi_3(S^1) = \mathbb{Z} \oplus 0 = \mathbb{Z}$. By coincidence this is correct here, but the *reasoning* is wrong: $S^3$ is *not* the product $S^2 \times S^1$ (different homology and homotopy: $H_2(S^2 \times S^1) = \mathbb{Z}$ but $H_2(S^3) = 0$). The Hopf fibration is a *twisted* bundle, not a product. The long exact sequence (with the boundary map $\partial$) is needed to handle the twist correctly. In other dimensions the same naive reasoning fails: $\pi_2(S^3) = 0$ correctly, but $\pi_2(S^1) \oplus \pi_2(S^2) = 0 \oplus \mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}$ would be wrong if we naively applied the product formula to the Hopf fibration. The correct computation via the long exact sequence: $\pi_2(S^1) \to \pi_2(S^3) \to \pi_2(S^2) \to \pi_1(S^1)$ becomes $0 \to 0 \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$, so the boundary $\partial : \pi_2(S^2) \to \pi_1(S^1)$ is an isomorphism — which is what tells us $\pi_2(S^3) = 0$. The boundary map encodes the topological twist; ignoring it gives wrong answers.

# Key Takeaways

**The long exact sequence is the workhorse of higher-homotopy computations.** When you face a problem of computing $\pi_k$ of a Lie group, a homogeneous space, a sphere, or a bundle total space, look for an associated fibration and apply the long exact sequence. The Hopf fibration is the prototypical example, but the same pattern recurs throughout: $SU(n-1) \to SU(n) \to S^{2n-1}$ gives a recursive computation of $\pi_*(SU(n))$; the path-loop fibration $\Omega X \to PX \to X$ gives $\pi_k(X) = \pi_{k-1}(\Omega X)$; covering spaces give relations between $\pi_1$'s. The trigger to recognise the pattern: "a $\pi_k$ I want is part of a chain of three groups in a fibration, two of which I know".

**The Hopf invariant is one of the great surprises of algebraic topology.** Before Hopf's discovery, there was no reason to believe $\pi_k(S^n)$ could be non-trivial for $k > n$. The Hopf map showed this is false in a spectacular way: there is a continuous, non-contractible map $S^3 \to S^2$. The geometric reason — that $S^3$ can be partitioned into a circle's worth of great circles, with the partition itself parameterised by $S^2$ — is unexpected and beautiful. The algebraic-topological reason — that the long exact sequence of the Hopf fibration gives $\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}$ — is direct and computational. This is the founding example of higher homotopy theory and the reason the subject exists.

**Higher homotopy of spheres is hard, but specific fibrations give important pieces.** Beyond $\pi_3(S^2)$, the higher homotopy of spheres $\pi_k(S^n)$ for $k > n$ becomes very intricate. There are fibration tricks for specific cases — the iterated Hopf fibrations $S^7 \to S^4$ (quaternionic) and $S^{15} \to S^8$ (octonionic) compute generators of $\pi_7(S^4)$ and $\pi_{15}(S^8)$ — but the general structure (stable homotopy, $\pi_k^s$ as $n \to \infty$) requires the **Serre spectral sequence**, **Adams spectral sequence**, and **chromatic homotopy theory**. The lesson is that the long-exact-sequence machinery handles *low-rank* computations beautifully, but breaks down for *systematic* understanding of high stems.
