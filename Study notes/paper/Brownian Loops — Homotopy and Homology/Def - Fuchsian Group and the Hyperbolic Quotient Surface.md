# Notation

$\mathbb{H}^2$ the [[Def - Hyperbolic Plane|hyperbolic plane]]; $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ its orientation-preserving isometry group. $\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ a subgroup. $\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^2$ the set of orbits $\{\Gamma z:z\in\mathbb{H}^2\}$; $\pi:\mathbb{H}^2\to\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^2$ the quotient map $z\mapsto\Gamma z$.

---

# Axiom Motivation

Every hyperbolic surface (a surface with a metric of constant curvature $-1$) is, up to isometry, obtained in one way: take the single model space $\mathbb{H}^2$ and *fold it up* by a group $\Gamma$ of its symmetries. The surface is then $\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^2$, and $\mathbb{H}^2$ is its universal cover. This is the hyperbolic analogue of building a flat torus as $\mathbb{Z}^2\backslash\mathbb{R}^2$. The whole strategy of the paper — do computations upstairs on $\mathbb{H}^2$, where there is a nice invariant heat kernel, and read the answer downstairs on $X$ — depends on this picture.

For the folding to produce a genuine *smooth surface* (not a space with cone points or where the identification is pathological), $\Gamma$ must act nicely: **properly discontinuously** (orbits do not accumulate, so the quotient is Hausdorff and locally like $\mathbb{H}^2$) and **freely** (no nonidentity element fixes a point, so there are no cone points / orbifold singularities). A subgroup meeting the first condition is a *Fuchsian group*; adding the second (torsion-freeness) makes the quotient a manifold. Restricting further to **geometrically finite** groups keeps the surface's topology finite (finite genus, finitely many cusps and funnels) so that sums over the group and its geodesics converge — exactly what the paper's heat-kernel periodisations need.

# The Definition

> **Definition (Fuchsian group; hyperbolic quotient surface).** A **Fuchsian group** is a discrete subgroup $\Gamma\subseteq\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ — discrete meaning it has no sequence of distinct elements converging to the identity. Equivalently, $\Gamma$ acts **properly discontinuously** on $\mathbb{H}^2$: for every compact $K\subset\mathbb{H}^2$, only finitely many $h\in\Gamma$ satisfy $hK\cap K\ne\varnothing$. It is **torsion-free** if it has no nonidentity element of finite order; then it acts **freely** (no $h\ne\mathrm{id}$ fixes any point). For a torsion-free Fuchsian $\Gamma$, the quotient
> $$X := \Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^2$$
> is a smooth **hyperbolic surface**: it inherits from $\mathbb{H}^2$ a metric of constant curvature $-1$, and $\pi:\mathbb{H}^2\to X$ is a covering map exhibiting $\mathbb{H}^2$ as its [[Def - Universal Cover|universal cover]] with **deck transformation group** $\Gamma$ (the isometries $h$ of $\mathbb{H}^2$ with $\pi\circ h=\pi$). It is **geometrically finite** if $\Gamma$ is finitely generated; then $X$ has finite topological type — finite genus with finitely many **cusps** (infinitely long spikes of finite area) and **funnels** (flaring infinite-area ends).

> **Definition (fundamental region).** A **fundamental region** $F\subset\mathbb{H}^2$ for $\Gamma$ is a region containing exactly one point of (almost) every orbit, so that $\mathbb{H}^2=\bigsqcup_{h\in\Gamma}hF$ up to boundary. Integration downstairs is integration over $F$: for any $\Gamma$-invariant integrand, $\int_X(\cdots)\,d\rho_X=\int_F(\cdots)\,d\rho_{\mathbb{H}^2}$.

**Concrete unpacking.** For the flat analogy, $\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^2$ acts on $\mathbb{R}^2$ by integer translations, freely and properly discontinuously; $F=[0,1)^2$ is a fundamental region and $\mathbb{Z}^2\backslash\mathbb{R}^2$ is the torus. Hyperbolically, the simplest example is $\Gamma=\langle z\mapsto e^{\ell}z\rangle$, an infinite cyclic group; its quotient is a **hyperbolic cylinder** (a funnel), and a fundamental region is the strip $1\le\operatorname{Im}z<e^\ell$ — precisely the fundamental strip $\mathcal F_\tau$ of §3. A closed genus-$g$ surface ($g\ge2$) comes from a cocompact $\Gamma$ generated by $2g$ hyperbolic elements with one relation.

**Standard names.** **Fuchsian group** (a discrete subgroup of $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})=\operatorname{Isom}^+(\mathbb{H}^2)$); **deck / covering transformation group**; **geometrically finite**; **cusp** and **funnel** (the two kinds of infinite-volume end). References: Katok, *Fuchsian Groups*; Borthwick, *Spectral Theory of Infinite-Area Hyperbolic Surfaces*.

# Examples and Non-Examples

**Is an instance.** $\langle z\mapsto 2z\rangle$ (cyclic; quotient a funnel). The deck group $\Gamma$ of a closed genus-2 surface (a specific 4-generator subgroup of $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$). The modular group $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ is Fuchsian but **not** torsion-free — it has *elliptic elements* (rotations about an interior point of $\mathbb{H}^2$) of finite order $2$ and $3$; because they fix a point, the quotient is not a smooth surface (it has **cone points** where the point-stabiliser rotation forces angles less than $2\pi$) — an **orbifold**, not a manifold, so excluded here.

**Is NOT an instance.** A **dense** subgroup of $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$ (e.g. generated by two "generic" elements) is not discrete, so not Fuchsian: its orbits accumulate and the quotient is not a surface. Discreteness is exactly what rules this out.

**Calibration check.** (1) Check that $\mathbb{Z}^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ is free and properly discontinuous, and that a nonzero fixed point would force a group element to fix it. (2) For $\Gamma=\langle z\mapsto e^\ell z\rangle$, verify each orbit meets the strip $1\le\operatorname{Im}z<e^\ell$ exactly once. (3) Explain why torsion (a finite-order elliptic element, which fixes an interior point) would create a cone point downstairs (**cone point** = an interior point whose neighbourhood looks like a cone of angle $2\pi/n$ instead of a flat disc).

# Where the paper uses this

From §3 on, every surface is $X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^2$ for a torsion-free geometrically finite Fuchsian $\Gamma$. The deck group $\Gamma$ is what records a loop's homotopy type (§3's lifting picture); the fundamental region $F$ and the strip $\mathcal F_\tau$ are where Theorem 3.2's integral is computed; geometric finiteness makes the heat-kernel periodisation $p^E_X=\sum_{h\in\Gamma}p^E_{\mathbb{H}^2}(t,\tilde z,h\tilde w)$ converge. **[[Paper - Brownian Loops — Homotopy and Homology — §3 Decomposition over Homotopy Classes|§3]]**.
