# Brownian Loops — Homotopy and Homology — the whole paper as one connected story

Here is the whole paper as a single connected story. I will keep it in ordinary language and pull in symbols only where they are carrying weight, so that each of the paper's seven pieces flows into the next — because they really are one chain, not seven facts. The chain has a very clean shape: **loops on a hyperbolic surface carry infinite total mass; sorting them by topological type makes each class finite; summing those class-masses is a Selberg zeta value; the still-divergent total is renormalised to a spectral determinant; dividing by that total gives an honest probability measure on topological types; twisting by characters coarsens it to homology; and the whole thing lifts almost verbatim from surfaces to hyperbolic three-manifolds.** Everything else is machinery in service of that arc.

## 1. What the paper is trying to do

There is a natural family of measures — the **Brownian loop measures** of Lawler and Werner — that assigns a weight to closed random paths on a Riemannian surface. Concretely, take a Brownian motion on a surface $X$, condition it to be a bridge from $z$ back to $z$ in time $t$, integrate over the starting point $z$ and the duration $t$ with the scale-invariant weight $dt/t$, and then forget both the root and the parametrisation. What is left is a measure on the space of unrooted loops in $X$, and it is the fundamental object of two-dimensional statistical mechanics — the loops of a Gaussian free field, of critical percolation clusters, of conformal loop ensembles, are governed by it.

The catch is that this measure has **infinite total mass**. Short loops proliferate without bound near every point: the heat kernel on the diagonal behaves like $p(t,x,x)\sim 1/(4\pi t)$ as $t\downarrow 0$, so the integrand $p(t,x,x)/t\sim 1/(4\pi t^2)$ blows up too fast at $t=0$ for the integral $\int_0^\infty dt/t\int_X p(t,x,x)\,d\mathrm{vol}$ to be finite. You cannot normalise a measure that assigns infinite mass to the whole space — you cannot even ask "what is a typical loop" of this measure — until you find a way to sort the loops into a countable collection of finite-mass classes.

The paper's idea is that **topology is the right sorting**. A closed loop on a surface with holes has a *topological type* — its free homotopy class — recording how it winds around the handles and the cusps. On a hyperbolic surface every non-trivial such class contains a **unique closed geodesic** representative $\gamma^m$ with length $m\ell_\gamma$; the topological types are in one-to-one correspondence with (conjugacy classes of) elements of the fundamental group. When you split the loop measure by class and ask "what is the mass of loops in this topological type", the divergence disappears — each class has finite mass — and the mass takes a beautiful closed form governed by the geodesic's length.

The payoff of the paper is what you can do with those finite class-masses. First, when you sum them over all classes, you recover a **Selberg zeta value** — a classical spectral invariant of the surface built from its geodesic length spectrum. Second, when the naive total still diverges (on finite-area surfaces it does), the right renormalisation is another spectral invariant, the **zeta-regularised determinant of the Laplacian**. Third, dividing each class-mass by the total gives an honest **probability measure on topological types**, whose moments are derivatives of the Selberg zeta function. Fourth, coarsening from topology to homology (which forgets everything except net winding around each handle) turns the Selberg zeta into a **Selberg $L$-function** twisted by a character, and Fourier inversion on the character torus extracts the mass in each homology class. And fifth, all of this lifts almost verbatim from hyperbolic surfaces to hyperbolic three-manifolds, with a single new feature — the closed geodesics now translate *and rotate*, so length becomes a complex length.

That is the whole paper in one paragraph. The rest of this story walks the pieces in order.

## 2. Setting the stage: Brownian bridges, the multiplicative weight, and how to swap the process

Fix a Riemannian surface $(X,g)$ — a two-dimensional manifold with a metric, possibly with boundary, possibly non-compact — and let $\Delta_X$ be its **Laplace–Beltrami operator**, the natural second-order operator that generalises $-\partial_x^2-\partial_y^2$ from the plane. **Brownian motion on $X$** is the diffusion process generated by $-\Delta_X$: an $X$-valued random continuous path started at a chosen point, whose transition probability at time $t$ has a density $p_X(t,x,y)$ called the **heat kernel** — because $p_X$ is what you get by running the heat equation from a point mass. The mental picture is: put a hot spot at $x$ at time $0$, let it diffuse for time $t$, and $p_X(t,x,y)$ is the temperature at $y$.

The heat kernel is the smallest object we need. Everything else is built on top of it. If you condition Brownian motion started at $x$ to reach $y$ at time $t$, you get the **Brownian bridge** from $x$ to $y$ in time $t$ — a random continuous path pinned at both ends. Its law, without the normalisation, is the **unnormalised bridge measure** $\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}$ on the path space $C([0,t],X)$, and it has total mass $p_X(t,x,y)$ (that is the definition — you disintegrate the path law by the endpoint and the density of endpoints is the heat kernel).

Now to build a measure on *loops*, take the bridge measure from $x$ back to $x$, so both ends are pinned at the same point, integrate over the starting point $x$ against the volume measure on $X$, and integrate over the duration $t$ against the multiplicative weight $dt/t$. The result is the **rooted Brownian loop measure**

$$\mu^*_X = \int_0^\infty \frac{dt}{t}\int_X \mathbb{W}^t_{x\to x}\, d\mathrm{vol}(x).$$

Two of these three ingredients deserve mental pictures. The bridge measure is a *density of Brownian arcs that start and end together* — think of it as a smear of pinned random paths. The weight $dt/t$ is the **multiplicative Haar measure** on $(0,\infty)$: the unique measure invariant under rescaling $t\mapsto \lambda t$. It is what you use whenever you want a scale-invariant aggregation across all possible durations, refusing to prefer short loops to long ones or vice versa; and it is the piece that fixes the loop measure's characteristic conformal-invariance property downstream. From now on we quotient out the root and the parametrisation of the loop — glue the endpoints of the interval $[0,t]$ into a circle and forget where "time zero" was on that circle — and what remains is the **unrooted Brownian loop measure** $\mu_X$ on the space of oriented unrooted loops.

Two immediate observations set the stage for the rest of the paper. First, the mass of $\mu_X$ is infinite for the reason already sketched: $\int_X p(t,x,x)\,d\mathrm{vol}(x)$ is bounded below by $\mathrm{Area}(X)/(4\pi t)$ as $t\downarrow 0$, so $\int_0^\infty dt/t \int_X p(t,x,x)\,d\mathrm{vol}$ blows up at zero. Any finite quantity we want from this measure will require either restricting to a manageable subclass or subtracting off a divergent piece by hand. Second, the loop measure has two properties that will keep coming back — **restriction** (its restriction to any open subsurface is the loop measure of that subsurface) and **conformal invariance** (rescaling the metric $g\mapsto e^{2\sigma}g$ leaves $\mu_X$ unchanged, so it depends only on the conformal class of $g$). Both are two-dimensional miracles. The first survives generalisation; the second, we will see, does not.

The natural generalisation the paper wants is: replace Brownian motion by a *related* process — Brownian motion killed at a constant rate $\kappa$, or an $\alpha$-stable process (a pure-jump analogue that moves in bursts and can jump anywhere), or a mixture — and see what the corresponding loop measure looks like. The cleanest way to do this is not through the process at all but through its **Dirichlet form** — a positive quadratic form $\mathcal E(u,u)$ on functions, from which the process, its generator, and its heat kernel can all be reconstructed. The two are equivalent data, and once you have $\mathcal E$ you get the loop measure $\mu^{\mathcal E}_X$ by the same recipe as before, replacing the Brownian heat kernel by whatever $\mathcal E$'s heat kernel is.

The reason to add this layer of abstraction is that Dirichlet forms come with a beautiful operation called **subordination**. Take a Bernstein function $\phi$ — a function $(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ with alternating-sign derivatives, i.e. one whose Lévy–Khintchine representation says it is $\phi(\lambda)=a+b\lambda+\int_0^\infty(1-e^{-\lambda s})\,\nu(ds)$ for some killing rate $a$, drift $b$, and Lévy measure $\nu$. To each such $\phi$ there is an increasing Lévy process $S_t$ on $[0,\infty)$, the **subordinator**, whose Laplace transform is $\mathbb E[e^{-\lambda S_t}]=e^{-t\phi(\lambda)}$. The subordinator is a random clock — a strictly increasing (possibly with jumps) way to reparametrise time. If you run your original process $B_t$ on this random clock, you get a new process $B_{S_t}$: the **subordinate process**. Its Dirichlet form is obtained from the original by replacing the generator $A$ by $\phi(A)$, and its heat kernel is a mixture of the original one against the subordinator's law,

$$p^\phi(t,x,y)=\int_0^\infty p(s,x,y)\,\psi^\phi_t(ds),\qquad \psi^\phi_t(ds)=\mathrm{Law}(S_t).$$

The three examples the paper cares about are the ones you would guess. $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda$ is trivial subordination and gives back Brownian motion. $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda+\kappa$ with $\kappa>0$ gives **Brownian motion killed at constant rate $\kappa$**: the paths are Brownian but each one has an independent exponential death time, so the heat kernel picks up a factor $e^{-\kappa t}$. And $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha/2}$ with $\alpha\in(0,2)$ gives the **$\alpha$-stable process**: a pure-jump process obtained by clocking Brownian motion with an $\alpha/2$-stable subordinator, whose sample paths are càdlàg (right-continuous with left limits) rather than continuous, and which can jump anywhere in $X$ in a single instant. A composite $\phi(\lambda)=(\lambda+\kappa)^{\alpha/2}$ gives the **shifted $\alpha$-stable**, i.e. an $\alpha$-stable process with an exponential killing on top.

Every one of the four cases produces a loop measure by the same recipe. The final wrinkle that makes the paper's calculations clean is the **weighted potential measure** $V^\phi$: a $\sigma$-finite measure on $(0,\infty)$ that packages up all the $\phi$-dependence of the loop mass into a single object,

$$\int_{(0,\infty)}h(s)\,V^\phi(ds)=\int_0^\infty \frac{dt}{t}\int_{(0,\infty)}h(s)\,\psi^\phi_t(ds).$$

The identity that makes it useful (Lemma 2.11) is that the double integral $\int_0^\infty (dt/t)\,p^\phi(t,x,y)$ *collapses* to the single integral $\int_{(0,\infty)}p(s,x,y)\,V^\phi(ds)$: the multiplicative-Haar aggregation of the subordinate kernel is the same as the Brownian kernel aggregated against $V^\phi$. So every $\phi$-dependent loop mass becomes: Brownian heat-kernel integral over the strip $\times$ single measure $V^\phi$ against it. And the four $V^\phi$'s are all explicit: Brownian gives $V^\phi(ds)=ds/s$; killing gives $e^{-\kappa s}\,ds/s$; the $\alpha$-stable gives $(\alpha/2)\,ds/s$; the shifted $\alpha$-stable gives $(\alpha/2)e^{-\kappa s}\,ds/s$. One-line version to hold onto: **the whole subordinate-process apparatus reduces, for the loop measure, to swapping which measure $V^\phi$ on $(0,\infty)$ you integrate the Brownian heat kernel against.**

## 3. Sorting loops by topological type

The surface we work on from now on is $X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb H^2$, the quotient of the hyperbolic plane by a torsion-free Fuchsian group $\Gamma\subset\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb R)$ acting freely and properly discontinuously by isometries. Concretely: $\mathbb H^2$ is the upper half-plane with the hyperbolic metric (the *universal cover*), $\Gamma$ is a discrete group of hyperbolic isometries that pair up the sides of some fundamental polygon, and $X$ is the closed surface (or non-compact surface with cusps and funnels) built by gluing those sides. Every hyperbolic surface arises this way, and the geometry of the surface — its closed geodesics, its lengths, its topology — is encoded in the group $\Gamma$.

Because this is exactly the setting the exemplar page walks in detail, I will keep the four story-steps that section makes and then continue from where it stops.

### 3a. One group element → two boundary points → one geodesic

Start with a single hyperbolic element $\tau$ of $\Gamma$. "Hyperbolic" is the trace condition $|\mathrm{tr}\,\tau|>2$, and the picture attached to it is this: $\tau$ pins two distinct points on the *boundary circle* $\partial\mathbb H^2$ and moves everything else. Why two boundary points: the fixed points of the Möbius map solve a quadratic whose discriminant is $(\mathrm{tr}\,\tau)^2-4$, and "hyperbolic" says this is positive, so the two roots $\xi_\pm$ are distinct real numbers on $\partial\mathbb H^2$ — neither in the interior. That is the whole difference from an elliptic element (rotation), which would have a fixed point *inside* $\mathbb H^2$; torsion-freeness has banished those.

Now the key geometric reflex: **in $\mathbb H^2$, any two distinct boundary points are the two ends of exactly one geodesic.** So $\tau$'s two fixed points $\xi_\pm$ single out one specific geodesic line — its **axis**. And because $\tau$ is an isometry fixing both ends of this line, it must map the line to itself: it can only slide it along itself. So $\tau$ *translates along its axis* by some distance $\ell$, the **translation length**. Picture a conveyor belt running along one particular geodesic, pushing every point on it a fixed distance $\ell$ toward $\xi_+$, and dragging the rest of the plane along coherently. The mental image for $\tau$ is *not* a matrix but **an oriented geodesic line with a marked step length** — a directed track through $\mathbb H^2$ plus a step size $\ell$.

### 3b. From upstairs to the surface: the axis becomes a closed geodesic

Now quotient. The surface is $X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb H^2$, and the projection $\pi\colon\mathbb H^2\to X$ is the universal cover, with $\Gamma$ playing the role of **deck transformations** — the symmetries of the cover invisible from downstairs. Watch what happens to $\tau$'s axis under $\pi$. The axis is an infinite line, but $\tau$ slides it into itself by $\ell$ each step, and downstairs points that differ by $\tau$ get glued. So the infinite axis, glued up by its own $\ell$-translation, closes into a **circle of circumference $\ell$** sitting inside $X$ — and because it is the image of a geodesic under a local isometry, it is a **closed geodesic** on $X$. This is the primitive closed geodesic $\gamma$ with $\ell_\gamma=\ell$. Powers of $\tau$ correspond to winding: $\tau^m$ has the same axis but translates by $m\ell$, so it projects to the same circle traversed $m$ times — the $m$-fold iterate $\gamma^m$, of length $m\ell_\gamma$. One-line version: **group element = oriented closed geodesic, and taking powers = winding around it.**

### 3c. Free homotopy classes = conjugacy classes

Two levels of "which element does a loop record," and the gap between them *is* conjugation. **Based loops record an actual element.** Fix a basepoint $x\in X$ and a specific lift $\tilde x$ upstairs; take a loop $\omega$ based at $x$ and lift it starting at $\tilde x$. The lifted arc need not close up — its endpoint lands at some $h_\omega\tilde x$ for a unique $h_\omega\in\Gamma$, and that $h_\omega$ is the deck transformation the loop accumulated. This is the isomorphism $\pi_1(X,x)\cong\Gamma$. **But free loops have no basepoint**, and that is exactly a conjugation ambiguity: changing the lift $\tilde x\rightsquigarrow q\tilde x$ carries the whole arc to its $q$-translate and changes the recorded element to $qh_\omega q^{-1}$. So the *element* is basepoint-dependent, but the *conjugacy class* is not. Forgetting the basepoint is precisely forgetting the choice of lift, which is precisely quotienting $\Gamma$ by conjugation. Hence

$$\{\text{free homotopy classes of oriented closed curves on }X\}\;\longleftrightarrow\;\{\text{conjugacy classes in }\Gamma\}.$$

The geometric gloss makes this concrete: conjugating $\tau$ by $q$ moves its axis to $q\cdot\mathrm{ax}(\tau)$ but keeps the translation length identical (conjugation is an isometry), and downstairs the two axes project to the *same* closed geodesic. So conjugate elements and same-free-loop-lifted-at-different-basepoints are the same statement, and the invariant that survives — the thing conjugation cannot touch — is the translation length, i.e. the geodesic's length. One-line version: **based loop = element; free loop = conjugacy class; the difference between them = the freedom to move the basepoint = conjugation; the invariant that survives = the geodesic length.**

### 3d. Descending the heat kernel, and folding to one strip

Now the measure. Upstairs on $\mathbb H^2$ we have a heat kernel $p_{\mathbb H^2}(t,z,w)$, and it is **$\Gamma$-invariant** — it cannot tell a pair of points from its $\Gamma$-translate. Downstairs a path on $X$ from $z$ to $w$ can wrap around the surface any number of ways, each corresponding to landing on a *different lift* upstairs, so the kernel on $X$ is the sum over lifts:

$$p_X(t,z,w)=\sum_{h\in\Gamma}p_{\mathbb H^2}(t,\tilde z,h\tilde w).$$

This is the **periodisation**, and the crucial point is that the term indexed by $h$ is exactly the contribution of *loops (when $z=w$) that accumulate deck transformation $h$*, i.e. of loops in the based homotopy class $h$. So the sum is *pre-sorted by homotopy*: to isolate the mass of loops freely homotopic to $\gamma^m$, keep only the terms with $h$ in the conjugacy class $[\tau^m]$. See [[Remark - Descent of the Heat Kernel by Periodisation]].

The starting expression for the class-mass is then

$$\mu_X(C_X(\gamma^m))=\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int_X\sum_{h\in[\tau^m]}p_{\mathbb H^2}(t,z,hz)\,d\rho_X(z).$$

The double structure "sum over conjugacy class $\times$ integrate over all of $X$" is redundant, and the redundancy is exactly the **centraliser** — the elements of $\Gamma$ that commute with $\tau^m$. Because commuting with $\tau^m$ forces you to preserve $\tau$'s axis, and because $\Gamma$ is discrete, the centraliser is the cyclic group $\langle\tau\rangle$ of axis-translations. Distinct conjugates are therefore indexed not by all of $\Gamma$ but by the cosets $\Gamma/\langle\tau\rangle$; unfolding those cosets against a fundamental region for $\Gamma$ tiles a fundamental region for $\langle\tau\rangle$; and since the integrand $p_{\mathbb H^2}(t,z,\tau^m z)$ is $\langle\tau\rangle$-invariant, we can replace that tiled region by any convenient one. The convenient one is a **strip** $\mathcal F_\tau$ that runs "one period" along the axis. Result: [[Thm - Mass of a Free Homotopy Class|Theorem 3.2]] gives

$$\mu^{\mathcal E}_X(C_X(\gamma^m))=\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int_{\mathcal F_\tau}p^{\mathcal E}_{\mathbb H^2}(t,z,\tau^m z)\,d\rho_{\mathbb H^2}(z).$$

Every factor traces to a geometric fact: the strip $\mathcal F_\tau$ from the axis, the $\tau^m z$ inside the kernel from "$\tau$ translates by $m\ell$ along that axis", the $dt/t$ from scale-invariant aggregation. See [[Remark - Collapsing the Conjugacy Sum via the Centraliser]] for the coset-unfolding argument.

### 3e. Standard form as coordinates

Everything above referred to "the axis of $\tau$" abstractly. **Standard form is just choosing coordinates in which that axis is the simplest possible line.** Conjugating $\tau$ inside $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb R)$ (allowed freely — an isometry changes nothing geometric, only the viewpoint) sends $\xi_\pm$ to $0$ and $\infty$, so the axis becomes the vertical imaginary half-line and $\tau$ becomes pure scaling $\tau\colon z\mapsto e^{\ell_\gamma}z$. The single fact this buys is that $\mathrm{Im}(\tau^m z)=e^{m\ell_\gamma}\mathrm{Im}(z)$: $\tau^m$ acts on *height* by multiplication by $e^{m\ell_\gamma}$. That gives the explicit strip $\mathcal F_\tau=\{1\le\mathrm{Im}(z)<e^{\ell_\gamma}\}$ as a fundamental region for $\langle\tau\rangle$, and it makes the distance $d(z,\tau^m z)$ inside the kernel explicitly computable. Standard form is the coordinate system in which "translate along the axis" becomes "scale the height", turning geometry into a clean substitution. See [[Remark - Standard Form as Coordinates on the Axis]].

### 3f. Closed forms for the four Bernstein cases — the first payoff

With the strip in standard form, the spatial integral becomes a one-variable calculation, and thanks to a beautiful identity of Wang and Xue ([[Lemma - Wang-Xue Strip Integral]]) it is completely explicit:

$$\int_{\mathcal F_\tau}p_{\mathbb H^2}(s,z,e^L z)\,d\rho_{\mathbb H^2}(z)=\frac{\ell_\gamma}{2\sinh(L/2)}\cdot\frac{e^{-s/4}e^{-L^2/(4s)}}{2\sqrt{\pi s}},\qquad L=m\ell_\gamma.$$

Plugging this into the general strip formula and collapsing the $dt/t$-integral against the weighted potential measure $V^\phi$ (Lemma 2.11) gives the class-mass **as a single one-variable integral against $V^\phi$**:

$$\mu^\phi_X(C_X(\gamma^m))=\frac{\ell_\gamma}{2\sinh(L/2)}\cdot I^\phi(L),\qquad I^\phi(L):=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-s/4}e^{-L^2/(4s)}}{2\sqrt{\pi s}}\,V^\phi(ds).$$

This is [[Thm - Mass of a Subordinate Brownian Loop Class|Theorem 3.5]], and the four Bernstein functions all evaluate the integral in closed form via a Gauss-type identity $\int_0^\infty s^{-3/2}e^{-as-b/s}ds=\sqrt{\pi/b}\,e^{-2\sqrt{ab}}$:

- **Brownian** ($\phi(\lambda)=\lambda$, $V^\phi=ds/s$): $\ \displaystyle \mu_X(C_X(\gamma^m))=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{m\ell_\gamma}-1}.$
- **Killing** ($\phi(\lambda)=\lambda+\kappa$, $V^\phi=e^{-\kappa s}\,ds/s$): $\ \displaystyle \mu^\kappa_X(C_X(\gamma^m))=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{e^{(\frac12-\sqrt{1/4+\kappa})L}}{e^L-1}.$
- **$\alpha$-stable** ($\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha/2}$): $\ \displaystyle \mu^\alpha_X(C_X(\gamma^m))=(\alpha/2)\mu_X(C_X(\gamma^m))$ — the stable case is just the Brownian case rescaled by $\alpha/2$, because both $dt/t$ and $\phi$ are scale-invariant so their combination forces $V^\phi\propto ds/s$.
- **Shifted $\alpha$-stable** ($\phi(\lambda)=(\lambda+\kappa)^{\alpha/2}$): $\ \displaystyle \mu^\phi_X(C_X(\gamma^m))=(\alpha/2)\mu^\kappa_X(C_X(\gamma^m))$.

Every mass has the shape (**constant**$/m)\cdot\exp((1-s)L)/(e^L-1)$ for some spectral parameter $s=\frac12+\sqrt{\frac14+\kappa}$ that will drive the whole rest of the paper. The killing case with $\kappa=-\frac14$ (the analytic-continuation boundary) is where $s=\frac12$, exactly the bottom of the $L^2$-spectrum of the hyperbolic Laplacian: a preview of the spectral geometry to come. See [[Remark - The Range of the Killing Parameter]].

### 3g. Poissonising: the loop soup

Any $\sigma$-finite measure can serve as the intensity of a Poisson point process, so for each intensity $c>0$ we can toss a *random configuration* of loops on $X$ whose mean number in any measurable set $A$ is $c\mu^\phi_X(A)$. This is Lawler and Werner's **loop soup** $\mathcal L_c$. The upgrade is real: the class-masses were expectations, but $\mathcal L_c$ makes them **means of Poisson counts** ([[Prop - Poissonian Structure of Homotopy Classes|Proposition 3.8]]) — the number of loops in the class $C_X(\gamma^m)$ is a Poisson random variable of mean $c\mu^\phi_X(C_X(\gamma^m))$, and counts in disjoint classes are independent. This is what lets us later ask for the *distribution* of the loop soup's homology, not merely its expectation.

### 3h. Length-spectrum consequences

The class-mass formula in the Brownian case is a scalar function of $\ell_\gamma$ alone: $\mu_X(C_X(\gamma))=1/(e^{\ell_\gamma}-1)$. Two immediate consequences. First, since restriction and conformal invariance of $\mu_X$ let you delete a polar set $P$ from $X$ and re-endow $X\setminus P$ with its own complete hyperbolic metric without changing the mass, the identity of masses on the two surfaces becomes an identity of geodesic length spectra — this is [[Thm - Wang-Xue Length Spectrum Identity|Wang and Xue's length-spectrum identity]]. Second, on any closed hyperbolic surface, inverting $1/(e^{\ell_\gamma}-1)$ recovers $\ell_\gamma$ from $\mu_X(C_X(\gamma))$ ([[Prop - Loop Masses Determine the Length Spectrum]]), so the loop masses **determine the marked length spectrum**, and Otal–Croke then gives ([[Cor - Loop Masses Determine the Hyperbolic Surface|Corollary 3.12]]) that they determine the point of Teichmüller space.

## 4. From class-masses to a Selberg zeta value

Now sum the class-masses over all classes. Each term $\mu^\kappa_X(C_X(\gamma^m))$ has that clean $(\text{const}/m)\cdot e^{(1-s)L}/(e^L-1)$ shape, and the whole double sum over $\gamma\in\mathcal P_X$ and $m\geq 1$ turns into $-\log$ of an Euler product. That product is the **Selberg zeta function**

$$Z_X(s):=\prod_{\gamma\in\mathcal P_X}\prod_{k=0}^\infty\big(1-e^{-(s+k)\ell_\gamma}\big),\qquad\mathrm{Re}(s)>\delta,$$

built from the primitive geodesic length spectrum $\mathcal P_X$ (see [[Def - Selberg and Ruelle Zeta Functions and the Critical Exponent]]). Here $\delta$ is the **critical exponent** of $\Gamma$: the abscissa of convergence of the Poincaré series $\sum_{h\in\Gamma}e^{-sd(z,hz)}$, measuring how fast the group orbit accumulates near the boundary. It has half a dozen equivalent identities — Hausdorff dimension of the limit set, topological entropy of the geodesic flow, the exponential proliferation rate of closed geodesics (**prime geodesic theorem** $N_X(R)\sim e^{\delta R}/(\delta R)$, [[Thm - Prime Geodesic Theorem]]), the bottom of the $L^2$-spectrum via $\lambda_0=\delta(1-\delta)$ when $\delta>\frac12$ — and the paper uses whichever is most convenient at each point. On finite-area surfaces $\delta=1$; on infinite-area surfaces (with funnels) $\delta<1$.

Why the sum turns into $-\log Z_X(s)$: taking the log of the Euler product and using $-\log(1-x)=\sum_m x^m/m$, we get

$$-\log Z_X(s)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal P_X}\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{e^{(1-s)m\ell_\gamma}}{e^{m\ell_\gamma}-1},$$

and this is *exactly* the total class-mass in the killing case with $s=\frac12+\sqrt{\frac14+\kappa}$. That is [[Thm - Selberg Zeta Identity for the Total Loop Mass|Corollary 4.3]]. The abstraction is [[Lemma - Selberg Zeta Criterion|the Selberg zeta criterion]]: **whenever a class-mass has the shape (const$/m)\cdot e^{(1-s)L}/(e^L-1)$, summing it over all classes gives $-C\log Z_X(s)$**, because that shape is exactly the log-expansion of the Selberg zeta. The four Bernstein cases all satisfy the criterion, with $s=1$ for Brownian and $\alpha$-stable, and $s=\frac12+\sqrt{\frac14+\kappa}$ for killing and shifted stable.

One conceptual side-note before moving on. The total mass has an entirely classical **statistical-mechanical reading** ([[Remark - Bosonic Partition Function Interpretation]]): $Z(s)=\prod_\gamma\prod_k(1-e^{-(s+k)\ell_\gamma})^{-1}=Z_X(s)^{-1}$ is the grand-canonical partition function of a free Bose gas whose modes are indexed by (primitive geodesic, non-negative integer $k$) with energies $(s+k)\ell_\gamma$, at zero chemical potential. So $-\log Z_X(s)=\log Z(s)$ is the free energy of a bosonic gas, and the whole zeta identity is a Wick-rotated path-integral reading of that gas's log-partition function — the same reading that will make Section 5 physical.

### 4a. The Ruelle twist by a representation

The zeta function has a family of twisted cousins built from finite-dimensional representations $\rho\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{GL}(V_\rho)$. The **twisted Ruelle zeta** is $R_X(s,\rho):=\prod_{\gamma\in\mathcal P_X}\det(I-\rho(\tau)e^{-s\ell_\gamma})$, and expanding its log by $-\log\det(I-M)=\sum_m\mathrm{tr}(M^m)/m$ gives ([[Thm - Twisted Ruelle Zeta Identity|Corollary 4.6]])

$$-\log R_X(s,\rho)=\sum_{\gamma,m}\mathrm{tr}\,\rho(\tau^m)\Big[\mu^{\kappa_-(s)}_X(C_X(\gamma^m))-\mu^{\kappa_+(s)}_X(C_X(\gamma^m))\Big],$$

with $\kappa_\pm(s)=s(s\pm 1)$. The difference of two killing masses at neighbouring shifts isolates the $e^{-sL}/m$ term, and the trace weights it by the representation. This is the algebraic device that will unlock the passage from homotopy to homology in §6: specialising $\rho$ to a *character* $\chi\colon H_1(X,\mathbb Z)\to S^1$ (composed with the abelianisation $\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow H_1(X,\mathbb Z)$) turns the twisted zeta into a Selberg $L$-function whose Fourier expansion regroups the loop mass by homology class.

### 4b. The finiteness dichotomy

Whether the whole double sum converges is a sharp dichotomy governed by the exponential-growth rate of the geodesic count. The proof in [[Thm - Finiteness of the Total Loop Mass|Corollary 4.7]] reduces the double sum to a single sum $\sum_\gamma e^{-s\ell_\gamma}$, and integration by parts against the prime-geodesic counting function $N_X(R)\asymp e^{\delta R}/R$ turns the question into whether $\int^\infty e^{-(s-\delta)R}/R\,dR$ converges. So:

**The total mass is finite iff $s>\delta$; at $s=\delta$ it blows up.**

Concretely: for the Brownian case ($s=1$) the total mass is finite on **infinite-area** surfaces ($\delta<1$), but is **infinite on finite-area** surfaces ($\delta=1$). To restore finiteness on a finite-area surface without giving up on Brownian motion entirely, you need a positive killing rate — pushing $s(\kappa)=\frac12+\sqrt{\frac14+\kappa}>1$. The interesting case for the rest of the paper is exactly the borderline one: finite-area surface, $s=1$, total mass just barely divergent. The paper's answer, in §5, is to renormalise it against a *canonical* divergent quantity — the zeta-regularised determinant of the Laplacian — so that the divergences cancel and a well-defined normalisation constant survives.

## 5. Renormalising the total mass

The setting: $X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb H^2$ is a *closed* hyperbolic surface. The Laplacian $\Delta_X$ has a discrete spectrum $0=\lambda_0<\lambda_1\leq\lambda_2\leq\dots$ with $\lambda_j\sim 4\pi j/\mathrm{Area}(X)$ (Weyl's law), and its determinant $\det\Delta_X=\prod_{j\geq 1}\lambda_j$ is a divergent formal product. The **zeta-regularised determinant** is the finite canonical substitute: form the spectral zeta $\zeta_X(s)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\lambda_j^{-s}$, meromorphically continue it to $s=0$ (regular there thanks to the heat-trace short-time expansion), and *define* $\log\det_\zeta\Delta_X:=-\zeta_X'(0)$. This is the classical Ray–Singer regularisation, and it is the natural stand-in for the divergent product $\sum_j\log\lambda_j$: a **finite canonical number extracted from the whole spectrum by analytic continuation**. See [[Def - Zeta-Regularised Determinant of the Laplacian]].

The bridge to loops is the **Schwinger proper-time representation** — the identity $-\log\det(\Delta_X+\kappa)=\int_0^\infty (dt/t)\,e^{-\kappa t}\,\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-t\Delta_X})$, where the trace of the heat semigroup is $\int_X p(t,x,x)\,d\mathrm{vol}$, the total mass of Brownian bridges from every point back to itself. So the log-determinant, up to a divergent short-time piece, is *literally* the total mass of the Brownian-with-killing loop measure, with the $dt/t$ weight and the killing weight $e^{-\kappa t}$ that put us squarely in §2's construction. The paper's Section 3.2 walks this in the language of Euclidean field theory: for a real scalar field of mass $\sqrt\kappa$ the partition function is $\det(\Delta_X+\kappa)^{-1/2}$, so the one-loop effective action is $\frac12\log\det(\Delta_X+\kappa)$, and this is $\frac12$ (regularised total mass of Brownian loops with killing rate $\kappa$). One-line version: **the zeta-regularised determinant is the regularised total mass of the Brownian-with-killing loop measure.**

The renormalisation the paper uses (following Naud, [[Remark - Naud's Geodesic Term as a Total Loop Mass]]) comes from the **Selberg trace formula**, which splits the heat trace $\sum_j e^{-\lambda_j t}$ into an *identity* term (a universal function of $\mathrm{Area}(X)$ and $t$) and a *geometric* term $S_X(t)$ that is a sum over primitive geodesics with exactly the summand of the strip formula in §3. Naud's evaluation of $-\log\det_\zeta\Delta_X$ writes it as

$$-\log\det_\zeta\Delta_X=-\mathrm{Area}(X)\cdot E-\gamma_\mathrm{EM}+\int_0^1\frac{S_X(t)}{t}dt+\int_1^\infty\frac{S_X(t)-1}{t}dt,$$

with $E\approx 0.0538$ an explicit spectral constant and $\gamma_\mathrm{EM}$ Euler–Mascheroni. Each ingredient of the integrand $S_X(t)/t$ is *exactly* the summand of the strip formula for the Brownian loop mass — the $e^{-t/4}(4\pi t)^{-1/2}$ factor is the on-diagonal ratio of the hyperbolic heat kernel and the $\ell_\gamma/(2\sinh(m\ell_\gamma/2))\cdot e^{-(m\ell_\gamma)^2/(4t)}$ factor is the length-dependence — so the whole integral **is a total loop mass** with the geometric piece isolated by hand.

### 5a. The two cleaner rewrites

Writing $S_X(t)=S_X^\mathrm{prim}(t)+(S_X-S_X^\mathrm{prim})(t)$ (splitting the primitive $m=1$ contribution from the non-primitive $m\geq 2$ tail), the non-primitive piece integrates without any renormalisation to $\sum_{\gamma\text{ non-primitive}}\mu_X(C_X(\gamma))$ — a total mass of non-primitive Brownian loops, finite because non-primitive contributions decay at rate $e^{-2\ell_\gamma}$. The primitive piece, though divergent, becomes finite after subtracting the "expected" logarithmic-integral count $\widetilde{\mathrm{Li}}(e^R)$ from the prime-geodesic counting function $N_X(R)$: because $|N_X(R)-\widetilde{\mathrm{Li}}(e^R)|=O(e^{(1-\epsilon)R})$, the difference is integrable. This gives the "long-primitive" form of [[Thm - Determinant as Renormalised Loop Mass|Theorem 5.1(i)]] for pure Brownian motion.

The cleaner story is the killing form. For $\kappa>0$ the total mass is *already finite* by §4, so no cutoff is needed:

$$-\log\det_\zeta\Delta_X=-\mathrm{Area}(X)\cdot E+\log\kappa+\sum_{\gamma,m}\mu^\kappa_X(C_X(\gamma^m))+O(\kappa)=-\mathrm{Area}(X)\cdot E+\log\kappa-\log Z_X\!\big(\frac12+\sqrt{\frac14+\kappa}\big)+O(\kappa),$$

and now the miracle: as $\kappa\downarrow 0$, the spectral parameter $s=\frac12+\sqrt{\frac14+\kappa}$ tends to $1$, and $Z_X$ has a **simple zero at $s=1$** forced by the zero eigenvalue $\lambda_0=0$ of $\Delta_X$. That zero contributes $-\log Z_X(s)\sim -\log Z_X'(1)-\log\kappa$ as $\kappa\downarrow 0$, so the $\log\kappa$ from the exponential integral *cancels the $\log\kappa$ from the zeta simple zero*, and the $O(\kappa)$ vanishes. Left over, the classical formula ([[Remark - D'Hoker-Phong Determinant Formula]])

$$\log\det_\zeta\Delta_X=\mathrm{Area}(X)\cdot E+\log Z_X'(1).$$

So on a closed hyperbolic surface, the zeta-regularised determinant is (up to the Euler-characteristic-driven universal constant $\mathrm{Area}(X)\cdot E=-4\pi(g-1)E$) *the derivative of the Selberg zeta at $s=1$*. See [[Remark - Why the Selberg Zeta Derivative Appears in Finite Area]] for the zero-cancellation.

### 5b. Polyakov's conformal anomaly

Theorem 5.1 computes $\log\det_\zeta\Delta$ on the hyperbolic representative of each conformal class. Polyakov's conformal anomaly formula ([[Thm - Polyakov Conformal Anomaly Formula]]) extends it to *any* metric $g=e^{2\sigma}g_\mathrm{hyp}$ in the conformal class, by adding an explicit **Polyakov correction** $P_X(\sigma)$ that is a quadratic form in $\sigma$ — a Liouville-action piece $\int|\nabla\sigma|^2$ plus a curvature-coupling piece $\int\sigma$ plus a volume-ratio piece. This gives [[Cor - Polyakov Formula via Brownian Loop Measure|Corollary 5.4]]: **the loop-measure formula for $\log\det_\zeta\Delta$ holds on any smooth metric in the conformal class**, with the correction $P_X(\sigma)$ made explicit.

### 5c. The cusped case

When $X$ is finite-area but not closed (say a modular surface, or a general geometrically finite hyperbolic surface with cusps), the construction breaks. The spectrum of $\Delta_X$ now has a **continuous part** $[\frac14,\infty)$ with multiplicity the number of cusps — the "Eisenstein series" solving $\Delta_X E_j=s(1-s)E_j$ but not living in $L^2$ — so the heat semigroup is no longer trace-class and there is no discrete list of eigenvalues to feed into $\sum\lambda_j^{-s}$. See [[Remark - Compact Determinant Construction Breaks in the Cusped Case]].

The replacement, following Melrose's microlocal analytic approach, is the **renormalised trace** ${}^0\mathrm{Tr}(e^{-t\Delta_X})$ — the integral of the heat kernel's diagonal in a Hadamard/Riesz-renormalised sense that removes the divergent short-time and cusp contributions — and the **renormalised determinant** $\det_0\Delta_X:=e^{-(\zeta^0_X)'(0)}$ built from its spectral zeta. The Borthwick–Judge–Perry formula ([[Thm - Borthwick-Judge-Perry Determinant Formula]]) then identifies $\det_0(\Delta_X-s(1-s))$ with the Selberg zeta up to explicit universal factors. Substituting into the same $\kappa\downarrow 0$ cancellation gives [[Thm - Determinant via Loop Measure, Finite-Area Case|Theorem 5.7]]: the analogue of the closed-surface formula holds, with $\det_0$ replacing $\det_\zeta$ and the simple zero at $s=1$ divided out to keep the answer finite. See [[Remark - The Infinite-Area Determinant Case]] for the infinite-area analogue.

The upshot is that on *any* geometrically finite hyperbolic surface, finite or infinite area, closed or with cusps, the renormalisation of the divergent total loop mass is a well-defined spectral invariant (a determinant of the Laplacian in the appropriate sense) and equals a Selberg-zeta value or derivative. That renormalised total is what will play the role of a partition function in the next section.

## 6. A probability measure on classes

Now the payoff. With the class-masses finite (in the killing case, or after renormalisation) and the total sum $-\log Z_X(s)$ known, define the **probability measure on free homotopy classes**

$$\mathbb P_s(C_X(\gamma^m)):=\frac{\mu^\kappa_X(C_X(\gamma^m))}{-\log Z_X(s)},\qquad s=\frac12+\sqrt{\frac14+\kappa}.$$

Every free homotopy class is now assigned a weight proportional to its Brownian-with-killing loop mass, and any function of the class becomes a genuine random variable. The most natural one is the **length** $L:=m\ell_\gamma$ of the geodesic representative. See [[Def - Probability Measure on Homotopy Classes]].

### 6a. The moment generating function is a shift in $s$

Here is the identity that makes the moments manageable: **shifting the spectral parameter is the same as tilting the class by $e^{-rL}$**. Because the class-mass depends on $s$ only through the factor $e^{(1-s)m\ell_\gamma}$, multiplying the summand by $e^{-rL}=e^{-rm\ell_\gamma}$ is *identical* to replacing $s$ by $s+r$. So

$$\mathbb E_s[e^{-rL}]=\frac{\sum_{\gamma,m}\mu^\kappa_X(C_X(\gamma^m))\,e^{-rm\ell_\gamma}}{-\log Z_X(s)}=\frac{-\log Z_X(s+r)}{-\log Z_X(s)}=\frac{\log Z_X(s+r)}{\log Z_X(s)}.$$

This is [[Remark - Moment Generating Function via a Selberg Zeta Shift]]. All moments are then derivatives at $r=0$ of $\log Z_X(s+r)/\log Z_X(s)$, and writing $F(s)=-\log Z_X(s)$ for the total mass,

$$\mathbb E_s[L^n]=(-1)^n\,\frac{F^{(n)}(s)}{F(s)},\qquad\mathrm{Var}_s(L)=\frac{d^2}{ds^2}\log F(s).$$

Strict convexity of $\log F$ on $(1,\infty)$ makes $s\mapsto\mathbb E_s[L]$ strictly decreasing — increasing the killing rate shortens the typical class, as expected. And as $s\to\infty$, the weight $e^{-sm\ell_\gamma}$ concentrates the measure on **systolic classes** — the primitive classes realising the systole $\ell_\mathrm{sys}=\min_\gamma\ell_\gamma$ — so $\mathbb P_s$ collapses to the uniform distribution on the $N_\mathrm{sys}\geq 2$ systolic geodesics (torsion-freeness forces a hyperbolic element and its inverse to be distinct, so $N_\mathrm{sys}\geq 2$). Both the systole and its multiplicity are recovered from the large-$s$ asymptotics of $-\log Z_X(s)$. See [[Remark - Systole Limit of the Homotopy Probability Measure]].

### 6b. Coarsening to homology

Free homotopy classes are conjugacy classes in $\Gamma=\pi_1(X)$, and for a genus-$g$ surface $\pi_1(X)$ is non-abelian: the class $C_X(\gamma^m)$ remembers not just the *amount* of winding around each handle but the *order* in which you wound them. **Homology forgets the order**: $H_1(X,\mathbb Z)$ is the abelianisation of $\pi_1(X)$, isomorphic to $\mathbb Z^{2g+n_C+n_F-1}$ for a non-compact geometrically finite $X$ with $n_C$ cusps and $n_F$ funnels (or $\mathbb Z^{2g}$ when closed), and it records only the net winding around each generator. See [[Def - First Homology, Characters, and Finite Fourier Analysis]]. The abelianisation map $\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow H_1(X,\mathbb Z)$ sends $\tau\mapsto[\gamma]$, and $[\gamma^m]=m[\gamma]$.

The mass of the Brownian loop measure in a fixed homology class $\beta\in H_1(X,\mathbb Z)$ is defined by grouping ([[Def - Mass in a Homology Class]]):

$$\mu^\kappa_X(\beta):=\sum_{\substack{\gamma\in\mathcal P_X,\,m\geq 1\\ m[\gamma]=\beta}}\mu^\kappa_X(C_X(\gamma^m)).$$

To extract this from the analytic side, twist the Selberg zeta by a unitary character $\chi\colon H_1(X,\mathbb Z)\to S^1$, obtaining the **Selberg $L$-function**

$$L_X(s,\chi):=\prod_{\gamma\in\mathcal P_X}\prod_{k=0}^\infty\big(1-\chi([\gamma])\,e^{-(s+k)\ell_\gamma}\big).$$

See [[Def - Selberg L-Function]]. This is the special case of the twisted Ruelle zeta of §4a with $\rho=\chi$ a one-dimensional character. The log-expansion works exactly as before ([[Cor - Selberg L-Function Identity|Corollary 6.4]]):

$$-\log L_X(s,\chi)=\sum_{\gamma,m}\chi([\gamma])^m\,\mu^\kappa_X(C_X(\gamma^m))=\sum_{\gamma,m}\frac{1}{m}\cdot\chi([\gamma])^m\cdot\frac{e^{(1-s)m\ell_\gamma}}{e^{m\ell_\gamma}-1},$$

with the character weight $\chi([\gamma])^m=\chi(m[\gamma])=\chi(\beta)$ *only* depending on the homology class $\beta=m[\gamma]$. That is the whole trick: **the character detects homology, not homotopy**, because it factors through the abelianisation. See [[Remark - Regrouping the L-Function Sum by Homology Class]].

Regrouping the sum by homology class gives the **Fourier expansion**

$$-\log L_X(s,\chi)=\sum_{\beta\in H_1(X,\mathbb Z)}\chi(\beta)\,\mu^\kappa_X(\beta),$$

and Fourier inversion on the character torus $\widehat{H_1(X,\mathbb Z)}\cong(S^1)^r$ (Pontryagin dual, using orthogonality of characters) extracts the homology-class mass ([[Thm - Fourier Inversion by Homology Class|Theorem 6.5]]):

$$\mu^\kappa_X(\beta)=\int_{\widehat{H_1(X,\mathbb Z)}}\big({-}\log L_X(s,\chi)\big)\,\overline{\chi(\beta)}\,d\chi.$$

For a closed surface, the character torus is Hodge-theoretically the Jacobian $\mathrm{Jac}(X)$ (the harmonic 1-forms modulo integer periods, with the pairing $\langle\beta,[\omega]\rangle=\int_\beta\omega\bmod\mathbb Z$), and the inversion can be written as an integral over $\mathrm{Jac}(X)$ against $e^{-2\pi i\int_\beta\omega}$ — see [[Remark - Jacobian Form of the Fourier Inversion]].

### 6c. The loop soup's total homology

Finally, run the loop soup $\mathcal L_\lambda$ at intensity $\lambda$ from §3g, and count its **total homology** $\beta(\lambda):=\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal L^*_\lambda}[\eta]\in H_1(X,\mathbb Z)$, the algebraic sum of the homology classes of all non-contractible, non-cusp-homotopic loops. The Poisson exponential formula (a total-mass calculation over the loop soup) then gives ([[Prop - Total Homology of the Loop Soup|Proposition 6.7]]) the explicit characteristic function

$$\mathbb E[\chi(\beta(\lambda))]=\bigg(\frac{Z_X(s)}{L_X(s,\chi)}\bigg)^\lambda.$$

Fourier inversion of this over the character torus gives the *distribution* of $\beta(\lambda)$: $\mathbb P(\beta(\lambda)=\beta)=Z_X(s)^\lambda\int_{\widehat{H_1(X,\mathbb Z)}}L_X(s,\chi)^{-\lambda}\,\overline{\chi(\beta)}\,d\chi$. So the loop soup's total homology has an explicit distribution written entirely in terms of the Selberg zeta and its twists — a beautiful bridge between the random loop model and classical analytic number theory.

One-line version: **the Selberg zeta gives the total mass; the Selberg $L$-function is the total mass twisted by a character; Fourier inverting over characters extracts the mass in each homology class.**

## 7. Lifting to hyperbolic 3-manifolds

Almost none of the machinery so far actually needed a surface. The loop-measure recipe used only the heat kernel and the volume measure; the homotopy decomposition of §3 used only that the universal cover has a $\Gamma$-invariant kernel, that non-elliptic non-parabolic elements have a canonical axis, and that the centraliser of a primitive element is cyclic. What tied us to surfaces were two things: **conformal invariance** of the loop measure (only holds in 2D) and the fact that $\mathbb H^2$'s heat kernel is available in a form we could quote from the literature (Wang–Xue). Once we work with a killing rate (or any nonlinear subordination), conformal invariance is no longer available anyway, so it does not obstruct the lift. And the analogous strip integral on $\mathbb H^3$ turns out to be *derived* by an explicit calculation, since $\mathbb H^3$'s heat kernel is *simpler* than $\mathbb H^2$'s and has a closed form. See [[Remark - Why the Surface Construction Lifts to 3-Manifolds]].

So set $X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb H^3$ with $\Gamma\subset\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb C)$ a torsion-free Kleinian group ([[Def - Hyperbolic 3-Space, Kleinian Groups, and Complex Length]]). The one new feature is this: a non-parabolic, non-elliptic element $\tau\in\Gamma$ is now **loxodromic** — it translates along a geodesic axis *and rotates about that axis*. Picture a screwdriver turning as it advances into wood: $\tau$ advances by a translation length $\ell_\gamma$ along the axis and simultaneously rotates by a holonomy angle $\theta_\gamma$ around it, and the natural bookkeeping is the **complex length**

$$L_\gamma:=\ell_\gamma+i\theta_\gamma,\qquad\theta_\gamma\in\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z.$$

The real part is the translation, the imaginary part is the rotation. When $\theta_\gamma=0$ it reduces to the surface case. See [[Remark - Complex Length as the Right Variable for 3-Manifolds]].

The standard form on $\mathbb H^3$ (upper-half-space model $\{(z,y):z\in\mathbb C,y>0\}$) sends the axis to the vertical geodesic and puts $\tau$ into $\tau(z,y)=(e^{L_\gamma}z,e^{\ell_\gamma}y)$: on the height it acts by real scaling $e^{\ell_\gamma}$ (so we get the same slab-fundamental-region $\mathcal F_\tau=\{1\le y<e^{\ell_\gamma}\}$ as before), on the horizontal complex coordinate it acts by *rotation with scaling* $e^{L_\gamma}$. The homotopy-decomposition theorem of §3 lifts verbatim ([[Thm - Homotopy Decomposition for 3-Manifolds|Theorem 7.1]]): same coset unfolding, same centraliser collapse, same strip formula, just in $\mathbb H^3$.

The evaluation of the strip integral is the piece that has to be *derived* rather than cited. The $\mathbb H^3$ Brownian heat kernel has the explicit closed form $p_{\mathbb H^3}(t,z,w)=(4\pi t)^{-3/2}(u/\sinh u)\,e^{-t-u^2/(4t)}$ with $u=d(z,w)$. Setting up the strip integral in polar coordinates on the horizontal plane, changing variables from $r$ (radial) to $u$ (hyperbolic distance) so the $\sinh u$ in the denominator cancels, and doing the resulting one-variable integral, the paper obtains ([[Lemma - Hyperbolic 3-Space Strip Integral]])

$$\int_{\mathcal F_\tau}p_{\mathbb H^3}(t,w,\tau^m w)\,d\mathrm{vol}(w)=\frac{\ell_\gamma}{|e^{mL_\gamma}-1|^2}\cdot 2\pi e^{m\ell_\gamma}\cdot\frac{2t\,e^{-t}}{(4\pi t)^{3/2}}\,e^{-(m\ell_\gamma)^2/(4t)}.$$

Collapsing the $dt/t$-integral against $V^\phi$ (as before) gives the general 3-manifold class-mass ([[Thm - Mass of Subordinate Loops on 3-Manifolds|Theorem 7.2]]), and specialising to Brownian motion the Gauss identity evaluates the remaining integral explicitly, giving the exceptionally clean formula ([[Cor - Brownian Mass on 3-Manifolds|Corollary 7.3]])

$$\mu_X(C_X(\gamma^m))=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{1}{|e^{mL_\gamma}-1|^2},\qquad mL_\gamma=m\ell_\gamma+im\theta_\gamma.$$

The mental picture is: **the 3-manifold Brownian mass is the surface Brownian mass with the denominator promoted from real to complex modulus, and *squared*.** The squaring reflects the extra spatial dimension in the strip (a complex coordinate rather than a real one). When the holonomy vanishes ($\theta_\gamma=0$), $|e^{mL_\gamma}-1|^2=(e^{m\ell_\gamma}-1)^2$ and the formula degenerates almost to the surface answer, differing by the extra factor of $(e^{m\ell_\gamma}-1)$ in the denominator that came from the extra dimension. Written differently, using $|e^{a+ib}-1|^2=(e^a-1)^2+4e^a\sin^2(b/2)$, the formula becomes

$$\mu_X(C_X(\gamma^m))=\frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{1}{(e^{m\ell_\gamma}-1)^2+4e^{m\ell_\gamma}\sin^2(m\theta_\gamma/2)},$$

exhibiting the holonomy as an additive positive correction to the surface answer — the more the geodesic twists, the smaller the class-mass. Everything else — the Selberg zeta identities of §4, the renormalisation of §5, the probability measure of §6, the passage to homology by Selberg $L$-functions — carries through in the same shape but with $\ell_\gamma$ replaced by the complex length $L_\gamma$ at every appearance, and the machinery of Kleinian-group Selberg theory (which is well-developed in the literature) as the analytic backbone. The paper stops with the class-mass formula as the punchline for the 3-manifold case.

## The whole picture in one paragraph

A hyperbolic surface's Brownian loop measure has infinite total mass, but every free homotopy class of loops on it corresponds to a conjugacy class in the surface's Fuchsian fundamental group and hence to a unique closed geodesic with a translation length, and sorting the loop measure by class turns each class-mass into a finite integral over a strip in the universal cover — collapsed by the cyclic centraliser of the class's group representative — that evaluates in every subordinate-Brownian case (Brownian, killing, $\alpha$-stable, shifted $\alpha$-stable) to the closed form $(\mathrm{const}/m)\cdot e^{(1-s)m\ell_\gamma}/(e^{m\ell_\gamma}-1)$ with a spectral parameter $s=\frac12+\sqrt{\frac14+\kappa}$; summing those class-masses over all classes is $-C\log Z_X(s)$ for the Selberg zeta $Z_X$ (that is the "Selberg zeta criterion"); finiteness of the sum is governed by the sharp dichotomy $s>\delta$ versus $s\leq\delta$ with $\delta$ the critical exponent equal to $1$ on finite-area surfaces, so the naive $s=1$ sum diverges there and must be renormalised; the right renormalisation, via Naud's evaluation of the Selberg trace formula, is the zeta-regularised determinant of the Laplacian — a finite canonical stand-in for the divergent product of eigenvalues — with the $\kappa\downarrow 0$ divergence in the exponential integral cancelling exactly against the simple zero of $Z_X$ at $s=1$ that the zero eigenvalue $\lambda_0=0$ forces, and Polyakov extends the formula off the hyperbolic representative to any conformally equivalent metric while a $\det_0$-and-Borthwick–Judge–Perry substitute handles the cusped case; dividing each class-mass by the total gives an honest probability measure $\mathbb P_s$ on free homotopy classes whose moment generating function is the shift identity $\mathbb E_s[e^{-rL}]=\log Z_X(s+r)/\log Z_X(s)$; coarsening from free homotopy to first homology is done by twisting $Z_X$ by a unitary character of $H_1(X,\mathbb Z)$ to form the Selberg $L$-function $L_X(s,\chi)$, whose log-expansion regroups by homology class because $\chi([\gamma])^m=\chi(m[\gamma])=\chi(\beta)$, and Fourier inversion over the character torus (which for closed surfaces is the Jacobian) extracts the mass $\mu^\kappa_X(\beta)$ in each homology class — with the loop soup's total homology having an explicit distribution given by $(Z_X/L_X(\cdot,\chi))^\lambda$; and finally the whole construction lifts almost verbatim from hyperbolic surfaces to hyperbolic three-manifolds, with the single new feature that closed geodesics now translate *and* rotate — a screw motion packaged as a complex length $L_\gamma=\ell_\gamma+i\theta_\gamma$ — and the class-mass in the Brownian case takes the exceptionally clean form $1/(m|e^{mL_\gamma}-1|^2)$, reducing to the surface answer squared when the holonomy vanishes.
