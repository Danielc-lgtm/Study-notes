# §2 — Preliminaries

Return to the [[Paper - Brownian Loops — Homotopy and Homology|hub]] for the shared notation table and prerequisites map. This section builds the paper's central object — a *measure on loops* — in three widening circles: first for ordinary Brownian motion on a surface (§2.1), then for any process coming from a [[Def - Dirichlet Form and its Operator and Semigroup|Dirichlet form]] (§2.2), then for the processes obtained by [[Def - Bernstein Function, Subordinator, and Subordination|subordination]] (§2.3–2.4). The payoff of §2.4 is a bookkeeping device, the *weighted potential measure*, that collapses a double integral into a single one — the computational lever the rest of the paper pulls. Nothing here is hard once the vocabulary is unpacked; the difficulty is entirely in the vocabulary, which is why every term below carries a recall.

**Symbols used on this page.** $(X,g)$ a Riemannian surface; $\operatorname{vol}_g$ its area measure; $\Delta_X$ the positive Laplacian; $p(t,x,y)$ the heat kernel; $\mathbb{W}^t_x$, $\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}$ the Wiener and bridge measures; $t>0$ loop duration, $s\ge0$ subordination time; $\phi$ a Bernstein function with data $(a,b,\nu)$ and subordinator law $\psi^\phi_t$. Full types in the [[Paper - Brownian Loops — Homotopy and Homology|hub]] table.

## §2.1 — The Brownian loop measure

The paper opens on a **complete orientable Riemannian surface** $(X,g)$, possibly with boundary; when there is a boundary, Brownian motion is *killed* on first hitting it (Dirichlet boundary conditions). The dynamics are fixed by the Laplacian.

> [!recall]- Laplace–Beltrami operator, heat kernel, Brownian motion
> **Formally:** $\Delta_X=-\operatorname{div}_g\operatorname{grad}_g$ is the positive Laplace–Beltrami operator on $(X,g)$ (spectrum in $[0,\infty)$); the [[Def - Heat Kernel and Heat Semigroup|heat semigroup]] $e^{-t\Delta_X}$ has integral kernel the **heat kernel** $p(t,x,y):(0,\infty)\times X\times X\to(0,\infty)$, so $(e^{-t\Delta_X}f)(x)=\int_X p(t,x,y)f(y)\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(y)$; **Brownian motion run at speed 2** is the process generated by $-\Delta_X$, whose transition density is $p(t,x,y)$, with law $\mathbb{W}^t_x$ on paths $C([0,t],X)$.
> **In words:** $p(t,x,y)$ is the chance-density that a random-walking particle goes from $x$ to $y$ in time $t$; "speed 2" and the minus-sign convention are bookkeeping that make $p(t,x,x)\sim 1/(4\pi t)$ for small $t$. See [[Def - Riemannian Surface, Laplace-Beltrami Operator, and Volume Measure]], [[Def - Heat Kernel and Heat Semigroup]], [[Def - Brownian Motion on a Riemannian Manifold]].

To turn paths into *loops* we fix both endpoints together. That needs the bridge measure, which is a conditioning of the Wiener measure on its endpoint.

> [!recall]- Bridge measure and disintegration
> **Formally:** disintegrating the Wiener measure over the endpoint map $\omega\mapsto\omega(t)$ gives $\mathbb{W}^t_x=\int_X \mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(y)$, where the **unnormalised bridge measure** $\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}$ is concentrated on paths ending at $y$ and has total mass $|\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}|=p(t,x,y)$; $\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}/p(t,x,y)$ is the Brownian bridge (the path conditioned to end at $y$).
> **In words:** "$\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}$" is "a Brownian path from $x$ that is required to land at $y$", weighted so its total weight equals the transition density. Taking $y=x$ gives *loops*. See [[Def - Disintegration and the Bridge Measure]].

The loop space itself is where these live:
$$C^*_X := \big\{(t,\omega) : t\ge0,\ \omega\in C([0,t];X),\ \omega(0)=\omega(t)\big\},$$
the **parametrised, oriented, rooted** loops — each carries a duration $t$, a direction of travel (orientation), and a marked basepoint $\omega(0)=\omega(t)$ (the "root"). The slice $\{t=0\}$ is the constant point-loops.

**The definition, motivated.** We want to weigh *all* loops on $X$, of every duration, on an equal footing. A loop of duration $t$ occurs with "density" $\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to x}$ (a Brownian bridge from $x$ back to $x$), and we must integrate this over the basepoint $x\in X$ and over all durations $t>0$. The only subtlety is the weight in $t$: to make the result *scale-invariant* — to not privilege any particular clock speed — we weight durations by the multiplicative measure $dt/t$ rather than $dt$.

> [!recall]- The multiplicative (Haar) measure dt/t
> **Formally:** $\frac{dt}{t}$ is the [[Def - Signed and Infinite Measures for Loop Measures|σ-finite measure]] on $(0,\infty)$ invariant under the scaling group $t\mapsto\lambda t$ (its Haar measure): $\int_{\lambda a}^{\lambda b}\frac{dt}{t}=\int_a^b\frac{dt}{t}$. It has infinite total mass ($\int_0^1=\int_1^\infty=\infty$) but is finite on each $[a,b]\subset(0,\infty)$.
> **In words:** $dt/t$ counts *ratios* of durations, not differences, so doubling every loop's duration leaves total weight unchanged — the natural "duration-blind" way to aggregate loops.

> **Definition 2.1 (rooted Brownian loop measure).** The **rooted Brownian loop measure** on $X$ is the infinite but [[Def - σ-Finite Measure|σ-finite]] measure on $C^*_X$
> $$\mu^*_X \;:=\; \int_0^\infty \frac{dt}{t}\int_X \mathbb{W}^t_{x\to x}\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(x).$$

**Concrete unpacking.** Its total mass is $\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int_X p(t,x,x)\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(x)$ (using $|\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to x}|=p(t,x,x)$). Near $t=0$, the diagonal heat kernel $p(t,x,x)\sim 1/(4\pi t)$, so the integrand behaves like $\frac{1}{t}\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi t}=\frac{\operatorname{Area}(X)}{4\pi t^2}$ — and $\int_0 \frac{dt}{t^2}=\infty$. **The total mass is infinite, and the divergence is a short-loop (small-$t$) effect.** This single observation is the seed of the whole paper: the total is infinite, but — as §3 will show — the part concentrated on any fixed topological type of loop is finite, because winding around a hole forces the loop to be long, cutting off the small-$t$ divergence.

**From rooted to unrooted.** Two loops should count as the same if they trace the same oriented curve, regardless of where the basepoint sits or how the loop is parametrised in time. Formally, identify $(t,\omega)\sim(s,\tilde\omega)$ whenever an increasing continuous bijection of the parametrising circles $i:[0,t]/_{0\sim t}\to[0,s]/_{0\sim s}$ satisfies $\tilde\omega(i(r))=\omega(r)$ — this forgets the root and the time-parametrisation but keeps the orientation. These identifications are generated by the **circular time-shift** $\mathrm{shift}_{s_0}(t,\omega)=(t,\omega_{s_0})$, $\omega_{s_0}(s)=\omega(s+s_0\bmod t)$ (slide the basepoint around the loop).

> **Definition 2.1′ (Brownian loop measure).** The **Brownian loop measure** $\mu_X$ is the [[Def - Signed and Infinite Measures for Loop Measures|pushforward]] of $\mu^*_X$ to the quotient $C_X:=C^*_X/\!\sim$ of unparametrised, oriented, unrooted loops.

**Two fundamental properties** (Lawler–Werner [LW04, §4]), stated because §3 leans on them:
- **Restriction.** If $X'\subseteq X$ is open, $\mu_{X'}$ is $\mu_X$ restricted to loops that stay inside $X'$: $d\mu_{X'}(\eta)=\mathbf 1_{\eta\subseteq X'}\,d\mu_X(\eta)$. (The killed-on-exit process's bridge measures are just the ambient ones restricted to paths staying in $X'$.)
- **Conformal invariance.** Replacing $g$ by a conformally equivalent metric $e^{2\sigma}g$ leaves $\mu_X$ unchanged, so $\mu_X$ depends only on the conformal class $[g]$ — one may take $X$ to be a Riemann surface.

> [!recall]- Conformal class / conformally equivalent metrics
> **Formally:** two metrics $g,\tilde g$ on $X$ are **conformally equivalent** if $\tilde g=e^{2\sigma}g$ for some smooth $\sigma:X\to\mathbb{R}$ (a pointwise rescaling that preserves angles but not lengths); the equivalence class $[g]$ is the **conformal class**, equivalently the structure of a **Riemann surface**.
> **In words:** conformal changes stretch the surface without shearing — they keep angles. In 2D, Brownian motion "sees" only angles (it is conformally invariant), so its loop measure ignores the rescaling. ⚠️ *Intuition, made precise by [LW04]:* this is special to two dimensions; the recall states the fact, the paper cites its proof.

The paper flags that this conformal invariance is **special to 2-D Brownian motion**: for a subordinate (jump) process the operator depends on $g$ itself, not just $[g]$, so from §2.2 on one keeps $(X,g)$ a genuine Riemannian surface. Reusable statements of the definitions above: [[Def - Brownian Loop Measure]].

## §2.2 — Dirichlet-form loop measures

Having built the loop measure from Brownian motion, the paper now abstracts away the specific process: everything used was a symmetric Markov process with a transition density, and those are exactly what **Dirichlet forms** produce. This is Le Jan's viewpoint [LJ11], and it is what lets §2.3 feed *jump* processes into the same machine.

> [!recall]- Regular symmetric Dirichlet form and its data
> **Formally:** on $L^2(X,\operatorname{vol}_g)$ a **regular symmetric Dirichlet form** $(\mathcal E,\mathcal F)$ is a symmetric, non-negative, closed, Markovian bilinear form on a dense domain $\mathcal F$; it determines a non-negative self-adjoint operator $A$ ($\mathcal F=\operatorname{Dom}(A^{1/2})$, $\mathcal E(u,v)=\langle A^{1/2}u,A^{1/2}v\rangle$), a semigroup $e^{-tA}$, a symmetric transition density $p^E(t,x,y)$ with $(e^{-tA}f)(x)=\int_X p^E(t,x,y)f(y)\,\operatorname{vol}_g(dy)$, and (via the Dirichlet-form↔Hunt-process correspondence) a symmetric Markov process.
> **In words:** a Dirichlet form is an "energy functional"; feeding it into a standard machine outputs a self-adjoint operator, a heat kernel, and a random process, all compatible. The Dirichlet-energy form $\int|\nabla u|^2$ gives back Brownian motion. See [[Def - Dirichlet Form and its Operator and Semigroup]].

The construction of Definition 2.1 carries over verbatim, with continuous paths $C([0,t];X)$ replaced by **càdlàg paths** $D([0,t];X)$ (right-continuous with left limits) to allow the jumps a general process may have.

> [!recall]- Càdlàg path
> **Formally:** a path $\omega:[0,t]\to X$ is **càdlàg** (French *continue à droite, limite à gauche*) if it is right-continuous and has left limits everywhere; $D([0,t];X)$ is the space of such paths, containing $C([0,t];X)$.
> **In words:** a path that may jump, but only cleanly — at a jump it takes the value on the right and leaves a well-defined value on the left. Diffusions are continuous; subordinate (jump) processes are merely càdlàg.

Disintegration gives $\mathbb{W}^{t,E}_x=\int_X \mathbb{W}^{t,E}_{x\to y}\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(y)$ with $|\mathbb{W}^{t,E}_{x\to y}|=p^E(t,x,y)$, exactly as before, and $C^*_X, C_X$ now denote càdlàg loop spaces.

> **Definition 2.2 (Dirichlet-form loop measure).** The **parametrised loop measure** on $C^*_X$ is the σ-finite measure
> $$\mu^{*,E}_X \;:=\; \int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int_X \mathbb{W}^{t,E}_{x\to x}\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(x),$$
> invariant under the circular time-shift [LJ11, Ch. 2]; its pushforward to $C_X$ is the **loop measure** $\mu^E_X$. Its total mass is $\int_0^\infty\frac{1}{t}\int_X p^E(t,x,x)\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(x)\,dt$, and the restriction property holds (the part form $\mathcal E_{X'}$ of the process killed on leaving an open $X'\subseteq X$ has bridge measures equal to the ambient ones restricted to paths staying in $X'$).

Reusable statement: [[Def - Dirichlet Form Loop Measure]].

## §2.3 — Bernstein functions and subordination

The paper now produces a *family* of Dirichlet forms from one, by running the process on a random clock — **subordination** (following [SSV12], [FOT11]). This is where killed Brownian motion and the $\alpha$-stable processes enter. The paper develops it in three steps: the clocks themselves (§2.3.1), how they act on a semigroup and Dirichlet form (§2.3.2), and the specialisation to the hyperbolic plane (§2.3.3).

### §2.3.1 — Bernstein functions and subordinators

> [!recall]- Bernstein function, subordinator, subordination
> **Formally:** a **Bernstein function** $\phi:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ is $C^\infty$ with $(-1)^{n-1}\phi^{(n)}\ge0$; it has a unique **Lévy–Khintchine representation** $\phi(\lambda)=a+b\lambda+\int_0^\infty(1-e^{-\lambda s})\,\nu(ds)$ with killing rate $a\ge0$, drift $b\ge0$, and Lévy measure $\nu$ ($\int(1\wedge s)\,\nu(ds)<\infty$). A **subordinator** $S_t$ is an increasing Lévy process (possibly killed at constant rate) with $\mathbb{E}[e^{-\lambda S_t}]=e^{-t\phi(\lambda)}$ and law $\psi^\phi_t$ on $[0,\infty)$; when $a=0$ it is *conservative* ($\psi^\phi_t$ a probability measure), when $a>0$ it is killed at rate $a$ ($|\psi^\phi_t|=e^{-at}$). See [[Def - Bernstein Function, Subordinator, and Subordination]].
> **In words:** watch the process at a random increasing time $S_t$ instead of at $t$; if the clock jumps, the observed process jumps. The exponent $\phi$ encodes how much killing/drift/jumping the clock carries.

**Assumption 2.3 (why it is there).** The paper assumes $b>0$ **or** $\nu(0,\infty)=\infty$; equivalently $S_t>0$ almost surely for every $t>0$, i.e. $\psi^\phi_t(\{0\})=0$. This excludes **compound Poisson** subordinators, whose clock has a positive chance of not having moved, giving the subordinate semigroup an atom and hence *no transition density* — and a density is exactly what the loop measure needs.

**Remark 2.4 (subordination versus time change by a PCAF).** Two constructions both "run the process on a different clock" but act differently on the data $(\mathcal E,\mathcal F,\operatorname{vol}_g)$, and it is worth keeping them apart. A **subordinator** $S_t$ is *independent* of the original process and *not adapted* to its filtration; it replaces the generator $-A$ by $-\phi(A)$, changing the Dirichlet form while **keeping the reference measure $\operatorname{vol}_g$**, and (when $\nu\ne0$) introduces jumps even into a diffusion. By contrast, a **positive continuous additive functional (PCAF)** clock is built from the process's *own* trajectory and *is* adapted; the resulting time change reparametrises the same path, so **continuity of paths is preserved**, but the reference measure is replaced by the **Revuz measure** of the additive functional (see [RY99, Ch. 10]). The canonical PCAF example is **Liouville Brownian motion** [GRV16]: planar Brownian motion $B_t$ time-changed by the inverse of the "quantum clock" $F_t=\int_0^t e^{\gamma\varphi(B_s)}\,ds$ — with $e^{\gamma\varphi}$ the exponential of the Gaussian free field $\varphi$ defined through Gaussian multiplicative chaos — giving $Z_t=B_{F^{-1}(t)}$, coupling constant $\gamma\in[0,2)$. The paper uses subordination, not PCAF time change.

**Example 2.5 (the Bernstein functions used in the paper).** Three clocks recur; their data $(a,b,\nu)$ and laws $\psi^\phi_t$ are:

| Resulting process | $\phi(\lambda)$ | $(a,b,\nu)$ | Law $\psi^\phi_t$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brownian motion | $\lambda$ | $(0,1,0)$ | $\delta_t$ |
| Brownian with killing ($\kappa>0$) | $\lambda+\kappa$ | $(\kappa,1,0)$ | $e^{-\kappa t}\,\delta_t$ |
| $\alpha$-stable ($\alpha\in(0,2)$) | $\lambda^{\alpha/2}$ | $(0,0,\nu_\alpha)$ | density $\eta^\alpha_t(s)=t^{-2/\alpha}\,g_{\alpha/2}(s\,t^{-2/\alpha})$ |

Here $\nu_\alpha(ds)=\frac{\alpha/2}{\Gamma(1-\alpha/2)}\,s^{-1-\alpha/2}\,ds$ is the $\alpha$-stable Lévy measure and $g_{\alpha/2}$ is the density of the standard $\alpha/2$-stable distribution on $(0,\infty)$ (see [BBK+09, Ch. 5] for the explicit form). The framework also covers gamma, inverse-Gaussian, and relativistic-stable subordinators.

### §2.3.2 — Subordination of semigroups and Dirichlet forms

Given a regular symmetric [[Def - Dirichlet Form and its Operator and Semigroup|Dirichlet form]] $(\mathcal E,\mathcal F)$ on $L^2(X,\operatorname{vol}_g)$ with operator $A$, and a Bernstein $\phi$ with subordinator independent of the process, the **subordinate semigroup** is the average of $e^{-sA}$ against the clock law,
$$e^{-t\phi(A)}=\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-sA}\,\psi^\phi_t(ds),\qquad \phi(A)=aI+bA+\int_0^\infty\big(I-e^{-sA}\big)\,\nu(ds),$$
so the generator of the subordinate process is $-\phi(A)$, and its **transition density** is the average of the original heat kernel against the clock law,
$$p^\phi(t,x,y)=\int_{[0,\infty)}p^E(s,x,y)\,\psi^\phi_t(ds).$$
This last identity is used in *every* subsequent mass computation — keep it in view. The subordinate process is again a $\operatorname{vol}_g$-symmetric Hunt process associated with a regular symmetric Dirichlet form $(\mathcal E^\phi,\mathcal F^\phi)$ on $L^2(X,\operatorname{vol}_g)$,
$$\mathcal E^\phi(u,u)=a\|u\|^2+b\,\mathcal E(u,u)+\int_0^\infty\big(\|u\|^2-\langle e^{-sA}u,u\rangle\big)\,\nu(ds),\qquad \mathcal F^\phi=\{u\in L^2(X,\operatorname{vol}_g):\mathcal E^\phi(u,u)<\infty\},$$
where $\|\cdot\|,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ are the $L^2(X,\operatorname{vol}_g)$ norm and inner product.

### §2.3.3 — Application to the hyperbolic plane

Applying the above to Brownian motion on the [[Def - Hyperbolic Plane|hyperbolic plane]] $\mathbb{H}^2$, whose volume measure is the hyperbolic area $\rho$: for a Bernstein $\phi$, the subordinate Dirichlet form $(\mathcal E^\phi,\mathcal F^\phi)$ on $L^2(\mathbb{H}^2,\rho)$ has operator $\phi(\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^2})$ and heat kernel
$$p^\phi_{\mathbb{H}^2}(t,z,w)=\int_{[0,\infty)}p_{\mathbb{H}^2}(s,z,w)\,\psi^\phi_t(ds),\qquad z,w\in\mathbb{H}^2.$$
Because $\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^2}$ is $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})$-invariant, so is $p^\phi_{\mathbb{H}^2}$ — the invariance §3 exploits to descend to a quotient surface.

**Example 2.6 (killing, $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda+\kappa$, $\kappa>0$).** The subordinate form is the killed Dirichlet energy and the kernel is a pure exponential tilt:
$$\mathcal E_\kappa(f,f)=\int_{\mathbb{H}^2}|\nabla f|^2\,d\rho+\kappa\int_{\mathbb{H}^2}f^2\,d\rho,\qquad p^\kappa_{\mathbb{H}^2}(t,z,w)=e^{-\kappa t}\,p_{\mathbb{H}^2}(t,z,w).$$

**Example 2.7 ($\alpha$-stable, $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha/2}$, $\alpha\in(0,2)$).** The operator is the **fractional Laplacian** $\Delta^{\alpha/2}_{\mathbb{H}^2}$ and the heat kernel is the $\alpha/2$-stable average,
$$p^\alpha_{\mathbb{H}^2}(t,z,w)=\int_0^\infty p_{\mathbb{H}^2}(s,z,w)\,\eta^\alpha_t(s)\,ds,$$
with $\eta^\alpha_t$ the density of the $\alpha/2$-stable subordinator law $\psi^\phi_t$. The process is **pure-jump** for every $\alpha\in(0,2)$; the boundary case $\alpha=2$ recovers Brownian motion.

## §2.4 — Subordinate Brownian loop measure and the weighted potential measure

Fix a Bernstein $\phi$ and its subordinate form $(\mathcal E^\phi,\mathcal F^\phi)$, kernel $p^\phi$, bridge masses $|\mathbb{W}^{t,\phi}_{x\to y}|=p^\phi(t,x,y)$.

> **Definition 2.8 (subordinate Brownian loop measure).** Applying Definition 2.2 to $(\mathcal E^\phi,\mathcal F^\phi)$, the rooted subordinate Brownian loop measure on $C^*_X$ is $\mu^{*,\phi}_X:=\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int_X \mathbb{W}^{t,\phi}_{x\to x}\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(x)$, with pushforward $\mu^\phi_X$ to $C_X$.

Reusable statement: [[Def - Subordinate Brownian Loop Measure]].

**The double integral, and the trick to collapse it.** Because $p^\phi$ is itself an integral of $p^E(s,\cdot,\cdot)$ against $\psi^\phi_t(ds)$, the loop mass carries *two* integrals, over $t$ (duration) and $s$ (subordination time). The mass only ever sees the combination $\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}(\cdots)$ applied to $\psi^\phi_t$. Collapsing that $t$-integral once and for all produces a single measure in $s$ — this is the technical heart of §2.4, from [SSV12, Ch. 5].

> **Definition 2.9 (weighted potential measure).** The **weighted potential measure** $V_\phi$ is the σ-finite measure on $(0,\infty)$ defined by
> $$\int_{(0,\infty)}h(s)\,V_\phi(ds) \;=\; \int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int_{(0,\infty)}h(s)\,\psi^\phi_t(ds)$$
> for every non-negative measurable $h$ making the right-hand side finite. When $V_\phi$ is absolutely continuous we write $V_\phi(ds)=V_\phi(s)\,ds$; this holds for all Bernstein functions used in the paper.

Reusable statement: [[Def - Weighted Potential Measure]].

**Example 2.10 (worked, the three cases).**
- **Brownian ($\phi=\lambda$):** $\psi^\phi_t=\delta_t$, so the $s$-integral localises at $s=t$ and $V_\phi(ds)=\frac{ds}{s}$ — the multiplicative Haar measure returns.
- **Killing ($\phi=\lambda+\kappa$):** $\psi^\phi_t=e^{-\kappa t}\delta_t$, same localisation, $V_\phi(ds)=e^{-\kappa s}\frac{ds}{s}$.
- **$\alpha$-stable ($\phi=\lambda^{\alpha/2}$):** $\psi^\phi_t(ds)=t^{-2/\alpha}g_{\alpha/2}(s\,t^{-2/\alpha})\,ds$. Substitute $u=s\,t^{-2/\alpha}$ (so at fixed $s$, $du=-\frac{2}{\alpha}s\,t^{-2/\alpha-1}\,dt$, i.e. $\frac{dt}{t}=-\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{du}{u}$) in $\int_0^\infty\frac1t\,t^{-2/\alpha}g_{\alpha/2}(s\,t^{-2/\alpha})\,dt=\frac{\alpha}{2s}\int_0^\infty g_{\alpha/2}(u)\,du=\frac{\alpha}{2s}$ (since $g_{\alpha/2}$ is a probability density). Hence $V_\phi(ds)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{ds}{s}$: the Haar measure again, up to the constant $\alpha/2$.

**Lemma 2.11 — the payoff.** The whole point of $V_\phi$ is that it turns the $t$-integral of the *subordinate* kernel into an $s$-integral of the *original* kernel.

> **Lemma 2.11.** For all $x,y\in X$,
> $$\int_0^\infty \frac{dt}{t}\,p^\phi(t,x,y) \;=\; \int_{(0,\infty)} p^E(s,x,y)\,V_\phi(ds).$$

**Why one should expect it.** $p^\phi(t,x,y)$ is a $\psi^\phi_t$-average of $p^E(s,x,y)$; the definition of $V_\phi$ is *precisely* "apply $\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}$ to $\psi^\phi_t$." So integrating $p^\phi$ against $\frac{dt}{t}$ must reproduce $p^E$ integrated against $V_\phi$. The only thing to check is that swapping the order of the $t$- and $s$-integrals is legal — which is what σ-finiteness buys.

> [!cite]- External input — Fubini–Tonelli theorem
> **Statement (typed):** if $(\Omega_1,\mu_1)$ and $(\Omega_2,\mu_2)$ are $\sigma$-finite measure spaces and $F:\Omega_1\times\Omega_2\to[0,\infty]$ is measurable and non-negative, then $\int_{\Omega_1}\!\int_{\Omega_2}F\,d\mu_2\,d\mu_1=\int_{\Omega_2}\!\int_{\Omega_1}F\,d\mu_1\,d\mu_2$ (both equal the double integral; **Tonelli**, no integrability needed for non-negative $F$).
> **Why it's true:** for non-negative integrands the iterated integrals both compute the measure of the region under the graph, which does not depend on the order of slicing. **Source:** [[Thm - Fubini-Tonelli Theorem|vault note]]; Folland, *Real Analysis*, Thm 2.37. Applicable here because $p^E\ge0$ and all measures ($\frac{dt}{t}$, $\psi^\phi_t$, $V_\phi$) are $\sigma$-finite.

> [!note]- Gap-free proof of Lemma 2.11
> **Step 1 — expand the subordinate kernel.** By the subordination formula (4), $p^\phi(t,x,y)=\int_{(0,\infty)}p^E(s,x,y)\,\psi^\phi_t(ds)$. (The integral is over $(0,\infty)$, not $[0,\infty)$: Assumption 2.3 gives $\psi^\phi_t(\{0\})=0$, so the atom at $s=0$ contributes nothing.) Substituting,
> $$\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\,p^\phi(t,x,y)=\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int_{(0,\infty)}p^E(s,x,y)\,\psi^\phi_t(ds).$$
> **Step 2 — swap the order of integration (Tonelli).** The integrand $(t,s)\mapsto p^E(s,x,y)$ is non-negative and jointly measurable, and both $\frac{dt}{t}$ on $(0,\infty)$ and the kernel $\psi^\phi_\bullet(\cdot)$ are $\sigma$-finite; by the Tonelli half of Fubini–Tonelli (no integrability hypothesis needed for a non-negative integrand) we may exchange $\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}$ and $\int_{(0,\infty)}\!\cdots\psi^\phi_t(ds)$:
> $$=\int_{(0,\infty)}\!\Bigg(\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\,\psi^\phi_t(ds)\Bigg)\,p^E(s,x,y)\;\text{``}=\text{''}\;\int_{(0,\infty)}p^E(s,x,y)\,\Bigg[\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\,\psi^\phi_t(ds)\Bigg].$$
> Here the bracketed object is not a number but the recipe that, tested against a function $h$, returns $\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int h\,d\psi^\phi_t$ — which is exactly Definition 2.9 of $V_\phi$.
> **Step 3 — recognise the weighted potential measure.** Apply Definition 2.9 with the test function $h(s)=p^E(s,x,y)$ (non-negative, and the right-hand side is finite for the Bernstein functions in play, which is where the definition's proviso is used). By definition,
> $$\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int_{(0,\infty)}p^E(s,x,y)\,\psi^\phi_t(ds)=\int_{(0,\infty)}p^E(s,x,y)\,V_\phi(ds).$$
> Chaining Steps 1–3 gives $\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\,p^\phi(t,x,y)=\int_{(0,\infty)}p^E(s,x,y)\,V_\phi(ds)$, as claimed. $\qquad\blacksquare$
>
> *(This is the paper's own two-line proof — "By (4) and Tonelli … taking $h(s)=p^E(s,x,y)$ in (7)" — written out with the Tonelli hypotheses checked and the $s=0$ atom accounted for.)*

Reusable statement with typing: [[Lemma - Collapsing the Time Integral of the Subordinate Kernel]].

**Where this goes.** Every free-homotopy-class mass in §3 is of the form $\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}(\text{subordinate kernel between }z\text{ and }\tau^m z)$; Lemma 2.11 rewrites each as a single $\int p^E(s,\cdot,\cdot)\,V_\phi(ds)$, and then the geometry of the geodesic lets the $s$-integral be evaluated in closed form.
