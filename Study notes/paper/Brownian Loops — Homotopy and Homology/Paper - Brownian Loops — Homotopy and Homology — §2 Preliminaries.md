# §2 — Preliminaries

Back to the [[Paper - Brownian Loops — Homotopy and Homology|hub]]. §2 is the paper's toolkit page: it builds the central object — a *measure on loops* on a surface — in three widening circles. First (§2.1) for ordinary Brownian motion on a Riemannian surface: pick a duration $t$ from the scale-invariant weight $\frac{dt}{t}$, pick a basepoint $x$ from the area, place a Brownian bridge from $x$ back to $x$. Then (§2.2) for any process coming from a **Dirichlet form** — the abstraction that lets killed and jump processes go through the same formulas, Le Jan's viewpoint. Then (§2.3–§2.4) for the processes obtained by **subordination** — running the ambient process on a random clock — which is how killed Brownian motion and $\alpha$-stable processes enter uniformly. The payoff of §2.4 is a bookkeeping device, the *weighted potential measure* $V_\phi$, that collapses the double integral $\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int p^\phi(t,\cdot,\cdot)$ into a single integral $\int p^E(s,\cdot,\cdot)\,V_\phi(ds)$ — the computational lever the rest of the paper pulls. Nothing here is hard once the vocabulary is in place; the difficulty is entirely in the vocabulary, which is why every above-floor term below carries a three-field recall.

**A reader landing on this section without having read §1 needs no earlier paper material** — the paper's Introduction is largely motivational. All that is used from outside §2 is undergraduate analysis, linear algebra, and elementary probability, plus the above-floor concepts recalled below.

## Prerequisites recap

Every above-floor concept §2 uses is recalled below with a three-field callout (formal / plain-language / concrete). A reader who has never seen these can close each callout with a picture, and none of the callout "In words" fields rely on above-floor jargon.

> [!recall]- Riemannian surface $(X,g)$, area measure $\operatorname{vol}_g$, positive Laplace–Beltrami operator $\Delta_X$
> **Formally:** $(X,g)$ is a two-dimensional smooth manifold $X$ equipped with a smoothly-varying positive-definite inner product $g$ on tangent vectors; $\operatorname{vol}_g$ is the induced Borel measure of area (in local coordinates $(x^1,x^2)$, $d\operatorname{vol}_g=\sqrt{\det g_{ij}}\,dx^1\,dx^2$). The **positive Laplace–Beltrami operator** is $\Delta_X:=-\operatorname{div}_g\operatorname{grad}_g$: apply the gradient, then take (minus) the divergence. In local coordinates, $\Delta_X f=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\partial_i(\sqrt{\det g}\,g^{ij}\partial_j f)$. Its spectrum lies in $[0,\infty)$.
> **In words:** a curved 2D surface with a way to measure lengths and angles smoothly across it. $\operatorname{vol}_g$ is the natural notion of area on that surface. $\Delta_X$ measures how much a function bulges away from its local average — bumps have positive $\Delta_X$, dips have negative $\Delta_X$ (with the paper's sign convention). The minus sign flips the usual Euclidean $\Delta$ so eigenvalues are non-negative.
> **Concretely:** on the flat plane $X=\mathbb{R}^2$, $g=dx^2+dy^2$, $\operatorname{vol}_g=dx\,dy$, and $\Delta_X f=-(f_{xx}+f_{yy})$: for $f=x^2+y^2$, $\Delta_X f=-4$ (the bowl bulges upward, so its "average deviation from centre" is positive by convention with the *positive* Laplacian). On the hyperbolic plane $\mathbb{H}^2$ (upper half-plane, $g=(dx^2+dy^2)/y^2$), $\operatorname{vol}_g=dx\,dy/y^2$; near $y=0$ the metric blows up and areas get large. See [[Def - Riemannian Surface, Laplace-Beltrami Operator, and Volume Measure]] and [[Def - Hyperbolic Plane]].

> [!recall]- Heat kernel $p(t,x,y)$ and heat semigroup $e^{-t\Delta_X}$
> **Formally:** $e^{-t\Delta_X}$ is the family of operators on $L^2(X,\operatorname{vol}_g)$, $t\ge 0$, defined by the spectral functional calculus of the non-negative self-adjoint $\Delta_X$; it solves the heat equation $\partial_t u=-\Delta_X u$. Its integral kernel with respect to $\operatorname{vol}_g$ is the **heat kernel** $p:(0,\infty)\times X\times X\to(0,\infty)$: $(e^{-t\Delta_X}f)(x)=\int_X p(t,x,y)f(y)\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(y)$. Symmetric, positive, Chapman–Kolmogorov ($p(t+r,x,z)=\int p(t,x,y)p(r,y,z)\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(y)$).
> **In words:** $p(t,x,y)$ is the probability density (with respect to area) that a random walker on $X$ starting at $x$ is found at $y$ after time $t$. The semigroup $e^{-t\Delta_X}$ acts by "convolve $f$ against $p(t,x,\cdot)$"; running heat for time $t$ then time $r$ is the same as running it for $t+r$.
> **Concretely:** on flat $\mathbb{R}^2$, $p(t,x,y)=\frac{1}{4\pi t}e^{-|x-y|^2/(4t)}$ — a Gaussian bell widening with $t$. At $t=1$, $x=0$, $y=(1,0)$: $p(1,0,(1,0))=\frac{1}{4\pi}e^{-1/4}\approx 0.062$. The on-diagonal value $p(t,x,x)=1/(4\pi t)$ blows up as $t\to 0$ and decays as $t\to\infty$. On any Riemannian surface, the same small-$t$ diagonal asymptotic $p(t,x,x)\sim 1/(4\pi t)$ holds — locally every surface looks flat. See [[Def - Heat Kernel and Heat Semigroup]] and [[Def - Brownian Motion on a Riemannian Manifold]].

> [!recall]- Wiener measure $\mathbb{W}^t_x$ and bridge measure $\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}$
> **Formally:** the **Wiener measure** $\mathbb{W}^t_x$ is the law of speed-2 Brownian motion on $X$ started at $x$, run for time $t$, on the path space $C([0,t],X)$. Its disintegration over the endpoint map $\omega\mapsto\omega(t)$ gives the family of unnormalised **bridge measures** $\{\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}\}_{y\in X}$, each concentrated on paths with $\omega(t)=y$, with $|\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}|=p(t,x,y)$ and $\mathbb{W}^t_x=\int_X\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(y)$. $\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}/p(t,x,y)$ is the normalised **Brownian bridge**.
> **In words:** $\mathbb{W}^t_x$ is a probability measure on paths — the law of a Brownian traveller. The bridge $\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to y}$ is the weighted family of those paths that end at $y$, scaled so the total weight equals the probability-density of landing there. Setting $y=x$ gives the *loops*.
> **Concretely:** to sample from the Brownian bridge from $(0,0)$ to $(0,0)$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ over $t=1$: throw many Brownian paths starting at the origin; keep those landing within Euclidean $\epsilon$ of the origin; the empirical distribution (divided by $\pi\epsilon^2$) converges as $\epsilon\to 0$ to the normalised bridge. That bridge, times $p(1,0,0)=1/(4\pi)$, is $\mathbb{W}^1_{0\to 0}$ — the "Brownian pretzel" of unit duration, total mass $1/(4\pi)$. See [[Def - Disintegration and the Bridge Measure]].

> [!recall]- The multiplicative (Haar) measure $\frac{dt}{t}$ on $(0,\infty)$
> **Formally:** $\frac{dt}{t}$ is the $\sigma$-finite Borel measure on $(0,\infty)$ invariant under $t\mapsto\lambda t$ for every $\lambda>0$: $\int_{\lambda a}^{\lambda b}\frac{dt}{t}=\int_a^b\frac{dt}{t}$. Infinite total mass ($\int_0^1=\int_1^\infty=+\infty$); finite on intervals bounded away from $0$ and $\infty$.
> **In words:** counts *ratios* of durations, not differences. Doubling every loop's duration keeps total weight unchanged — the natural duration-blind way to aggregate loops.
> **Concretely:** the substitution $u=\log t$ turns $\int_a^b f(t)\,\frac{dt}{t}$ into $\int_{\log a}^{\log b}f(e^u)\,du$, ordinary Lebesgue integration in $\log t$. So $\int_1^{e}\frac{dt}{t}=1$, $\int_1^{e^{10}}\frac{dt}{t}=10$: each factor of $e$ contributes one unit of mass. The divergences at $0$ and $\infty$ are $\log 0=-\infty$ and $\log\infty=+\infty$ respectively. See [[Def - Signed and Infinite Measures for Loop Measures]].

> [!recall]- $\sigma$-finite measure and signed measure
> **Formally:** a measure $\mu$ on $(\Omega,\mathcal F)$ is **$\sigma$-finite** if $\Omega=\bigcup_n\Omega_n$ with $\mu(\Omega_n)<\infty$; it is a **signed measure** if it takes values in $\mathbb{R}$ (possibly $\pm\infty$) subject to countable additivity, Hahn–Jordan-decomposable as $\mu=\mu^+-\mu^-$.
> **In words:** the space breaks into countably many finite-mass pieces (even if total mass is infinite), which is all you need for Tonelli/Fubini and disintegration to work. A signed measure allows negative values — useful for intermediate objects that arise as differences.
> **Concretely:** Lebesgue on $\mathbb{R}$ is $\sigma$-finite ($\mathbb{R}=\bigcup_n[-n,n]$); so is $\frac{dt}{t}$ on $(0,\infty)$ ($\bigcup_n[1/n,n]$, each of mass $2\log n$). The signed measure $f(x)\,dx$ with $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ integrable arbitrary sign is a standard example. See [[Def - Signed and Infinite Measures for Loop Measures]] and [[Def - σ-Finite Measure]].

> [!recall]- Conformal class $[g]$ / conformally equivalent metrics
> **Formally:** two metrics $g,\tilde g$ on $X$ are **conformally equivalent** if $\tilde g=e^{2\sigma}g$ for some smooth $\sigma:X\to\mathbb{R}$. The equivalence class $[g]$ is the **conformal class**, equivalently the structure of a **Riemann surface**.
> **In words:** a conformal change stretches the surface pointwise without twisting or shearing — angles between crossing curves stay the same, lengths change. In 2D, Brownian motion "sees" only angles (path shapes, not clock speed), so its loop measure is unaffected.
> **Concretely:** on the flat plane, $g=dx^2+dy^2$ rescaled by $\sigma(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}(x^2+y^2)$ gives $\tilde g=e^{x^2+y^2}(dx^2+dy^2)$: lengths grow super-fast far from the origin, but a right-angle crossing stays a right angle. A Brownian path in $\tilde g$ traces the same *shape* as in $g$, just at a rescaled clock — its (duration-blind $\frac{dt}{t}$-weighted) loop measure is unchanged. ⚠️ *Made rigorous by [LW04]; special to two dimensions.*

> [!recall]- Regular symmetric Dirichlet form $(\mathcal E,\mathcal F)$ and its data $(A,e^{-tA},p^E)$
> **Formally:** on $L^2(X,\operatorname{vol}_g)$, a **regular symmetric Dirichlet form** is a symmetric non-negative bilinear form $\mathcal E:\mathcal F\times\mathcal F\to\mathbb{R}$ on a dense linear subspace $\mathcal F\subseteq L^2$, closed (i.e. $\mathcal F$ is a Hilbert space under $\|u\|_{\mathcal E}^2:=\mathcal E(u,u)+\|u\|^2_{L^2}$) and Markovian ($u\in\mathcal F$ implies its clip $u^\#:=(0\vee u)\wedge 1$ is in $\mathcal F$ with $\mathcal E(u^\#,u^\#)\le\mathcal E(u,u)$). It determines a non-negative self-adjoint operator $A$ with $\mathcal E(u,v)=\langle A^{1/2}u,A^{1/2}v\rangle$, a semigroup $e^{-tA}$, a symmetric transition density $p^E$ (assumed to exist), and by Fukushima's theorem a symmetric Markov (Hunt) process.
> **In words:** a way to write down an "energy" for each function on $X$ — flat functions cost less energy than wiggly ones. Once you have such an energy, a general machine (Fukushima's theorem) automatically produces a random-walk process on $X$ whose typical paths dissipate that energy. Different energies give different processes: $\int|\nabla u|^2$ gives Brownian motion; $\int|\nabla u|^2+\kappa\int u^2$ gives killed Brownian motion; a non-local energy involving jumps gives a jump process.
> **Concretely:** on $X=\mathbb{R}$ take $\mathcal F=H^1(\mathbb{R})$ and $\mathcal E(u,v)=\int u'v'\,dx$: this is the Dirichlet energy, its operator is $-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$, its kernel is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}e^{-(x-y)^2/4t}$, its process is standard Brownian motion. Add a mass term $\int uv\,dx$ and you get Brownian motion killed at unit rate. See [[Def - Dirichlet Form and its Operator and Semigroup]].

> [!recall]- Càdlàg path
> **Formally:** $\omega:[0,t]\to X$ is **càdlàg** (French: *continue à droite, limite à gauche*) if for every $r\in[0,t)$ the right-limit exists and equals $\omega(r)$, and for every $r\in(0,t]$ the left-limit exists (but need not equal $\omega(r)$). $D([0,t],X)$ is the space of such paths; it contains $C([0,t],X)$.
> **In words:** a path that may jump, but only cleanly — at each jump the path's value equals the right side, and a left limit still exists. No wild oscillations.
> **Concretely:** $\omega(s)=\mathbf 1_{[1/2,1]}(s)$ on $[0,1]$ is càdlàg: at $s=1/2$ it jumps from $0$ to $1$, taking the right value $1$ at the jump; the left limit is $0$. Continuous paths are càdlàg with no jumps; a Poisson-process trajectory is càdlàg with countably many.

> [!recall]- Bernstein function $\phi$, subordinator $S_t$, subordinate semigroup $e^{-t\phi(A)}$
> **Formally:** $\phi:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)$ is a **Bernstein function** if $\phi\in C^\infty$ with $(-1)^{n-1}\phi^{(n)}\ge 0$ for all $n\ge 1$; equivalently $\phi(\lambda)=a+b\lambda+\int_0^\infty(1-e^{-\lambda s})\,\nu(ds)$ with $a,b\ge 0$, $\int(1\wedge s)\,\nu(ds)<\infty$ (Lévy–Khintchine). A **subordinator** is an increasing Lévy process (possibly killed) $S_t$ with $\mathbb{E}[e^{-\lambda S_t}]=e^{-t\phi(\lambda)}$; $\psi^\phi_t$ its law on $[0,\infty)$. **Subordination** of a Markov process $B_r$ (with generator $-A$) by an independent $S_t$ produces the process $Y_t:=B_{S_t}$, with generator $-\phi(A)$ (spectral calculus), semigroup $e^{-t\phi(A)}=\int e^{-sA}\psi^\phi_t(ds)$, and transition density $p^\phi(t,x,y)=\int p^E(s,x,y)\,\psi^\phi_t(ds)$.
> **In words:** $\phi$ is exactly the class of Laplace exponents of increasing random clocks. A subordinator only moves forward, sometimes with jumps (skipping ahead); observing an underlying process at those clock times gives a new process that jumps whenever the clock jumped. Data: drift $b$ = smooth advance rate; Lévy measure $\nu(ds)$ = intensity of jumps of size $s$; killing rate $a$ = clock-death rate.
> **Concretely:** $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda$ ($a=0,b=1,\nu=0$): $S_t=t$ deterministically ($\psi^\phi_t=\delta_t$), subordination does nothing. $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda+\kappa$: $\psi^\phi_t=e^{-\kappa t}\delta_t$, gives Brownian motion killed at rate $\kappa$. $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{1/2}$: $1/2$-stable subordinator with density $\frac{t}{2\sqrt\pi}s^{-3/2}e^{-t^2/(4s)}$; subordinating planar Brownian motion yields the Cauchy process. See [[Def - Bernstein Function, Subordinator, and Subordination]].

## Concept map

Every named paper item in §2, in the paper's order. Each parent bullet wikilinks to the item's fully self-contained atomic subpage; each child bullet unpacks the item in 3–5 sentences.

### §2.1 — The Brownian loop measure

- **[[Def - Brownian Loop Measure|Definition 2.1 (Brownian loop measure)]]** — Defines the rooted Brownian loop measure $\mu^*_X=\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int_X\mathbb{W}^t_{x\to x}\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(x)$ on the space $C^*_X$ of parametrised rooted loops on the Riemannian surface $(X,g)$, and its pushforward $\mu_X$ to unrooted, unparametrised, oriented loops on $C_X$. Weights durations by the scale-invariant $\frac{dt}{t}$; total mass is infinite, but the divergence is a small-$t$ (short-loop) effect that later gets cut off per-topological-type. Two properties bought from [LW04]: *restriction* (the killed-on-exit measure on $X'\subseteq X$ is $\mu_X$ cut to loops in $X'$) and *conformal invariance* (depends only on $[g]$, hence $X$ may be taken as a Riemann surface). Conformal invariance is special to 2-D Brownian motion; from §2.2 on the paper uses the genuine Riemannian metric $g$.
  - The mass-divergence computation: $p(t,x,x)\sim 1/(4\pi t)$ makes the integrand behave like $\frac{\operatorname{Area}(X)}{4\pi t^2}$ near $t=0$, so $\int_0\frac{dt}{t^2}=+\infty$. This is the "seed observation" that motivates the whole rest of the paper.

### §2.2 — Dirichlet-form loop measures

- **[[Def - Dirichlet Form Loop Measure|Definition 2.2 (Dirichlet-form loop measure)]]** — Same construction as Definition 2.1, with the specific Brownian motion replaced by *any* symmetric Markov process coming from a regular symmetric Dirichlet form $(\mathcal E,\mathcal F)$ on $L^2(X,\operatorname{vol}_g)$: pick a duration $t$ by $\frac{dt}{t}$, pick a basepoint by $\operatorname{vol}_g$, place the process's bridge $\mathbb{W}^{t,E}_{x\to x}$ (mass $p^E(t,x,x)$). Paths are now càdlàg (jumps allowed). This is Le Jan's abstraction [LJ11]; the payoff is that from §2.3 the same formula covers killed, $\alpha$-stable, gamma, and relativistic-stable processes at once. Restriction still holds; conformal invariance does not transfer beyond the Brownian case.
  - The invariance under the *circular time-shift* $\mathrm{shift}_{s_0}(t,\omega)=(t,\omega(\cdot+s_0\bmod t))$ is what makes the pushforward to unrooted loops well-defined.

### §2.3 — Bernstein functions and subordination

The paper's §2.3 has three sub-parts: (§2.3.1) the clocks themselves; (§2.3.2) how they act on a semigroup and Dirichlet form; (§2.3.3) the specialisation to the hyperbolic plane. The subordination formulas (both the semigroup identity and the transition-density identity) are stated in the prerequisites recap above and unpacked in each atomic subpage; on this section page they appear only through the four items below.

- **[[Remark - Non-Compound-Poisson Assumption on the Bernstein Function|Assumption 2.3 (non-compound-Poisson)]]** — Standing assumption on the Bernstein function: $b>0$ or $\nu(0,\infty)=\infty$, equivalently $S_t>0$ almost surely for every $t>0$, i.e. $\psi^\phi_t(\{0\})=0$. This excludes **compound Poisson** subordinators, whose clock has a positive chance of not having moved by time $t$, producing an atom in the subordinate semigroup at real time and hence *no transition density*. Since every construction in §2.4 needs a density (bridge measures, loop measure, the collapsing lemma), such subordinators are excluded by fiat. All four of the paper's working Bernstein functions satisfy the assumption.

- **[[Remark - Subordination versus PCAF Time Change|Remark 2.4 (subordination vs PCAF time change)]]** — Distinguishes the two constructions that both "run the process on a different clock". **Subordination**: the clock $S_t$ is an *independent* subordinator, not adapted to $B$'s filtration; the generator becomes $\phi(A)$; the reference measure $\operatorname{vol}_g$ is kept; jumps may appear even in a diffusion. **PCAF time change**: the clock $F_t$ is built from $B$'s own trajectory and is *adapted*; path continuity is preserved; the reference measure becomes the Revuz measure $\mu_F$ of the additive functional. Canonical PCAF example: **Liouville Brownian motion** [GRV16], planar Brownian motion time-changed by the quantum clock $F_t=\int_0^t e^{\gamma\varphi(B_s)}\,ds$ with $\varphi$ the Gaussian free field. The paper uses subordination, not PCAF time change.

- **[[Ex - The Four Bernstein Functions of the Paper|Example 2.5 (four important Bernstein functions)]]** — Tabulates the paper's Bernstein functions with their Lévy–Khintchine data $(a,b,\nu)$ and subordinator laws: (1) Brownian $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda$, $\psi^\phi_t=\delta_t$; (2) killed Brownian $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda+\kappa$, $\psi^\phi_t=e^{-\kappa t}\delta_t$; (3) $\alpha$-stable $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha/2}$ with Lévy measure $\nu_\alpha(ds)=\frac{\alpha/2}{\Gamma(1-\alpha/2)}s^{-1-\alpha/2}\,ds$ and law density $\eta^\alpha_t$; (4) shifted $\alpha$-stable $\phi(\lambda)=(\lambda+\kappa)^{\alpha/2}$, an exponentially-tilted $\alpha$-stable (introduced in §3.1.4 but naturally grouped here). The paper's Example 2.5 tables cases (1)–(3); case (4) is the essential jump-plus-killing case that breaks pure scale-invariance. The framework also covers gamma, inverse-Gaussian, and relativistic-stable subordinators.

- **Example 2.6 (killing, $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda+\kappa$, on $\mathbb{H}^2$) ([[Ex - The Subordinate Form of Brownian Motion with Killing]])** — For the killed Bernstein function $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda+\kappa$ on the hyperbolic plane $\mathbb{H}^2$: the subordinate Dirichlet form is the killed Dirichlet energy $\mathcal E_\kappa(f,f)=\int_{\mathbb{H}^2}|\nabla f|^2\,d\rho+\kappa\int_{\mathbb{H}^2}f^2\,d\rho$ (a mass term added to the ambient energy), and the subordinate kernel is a pure exponential tilt $p^\kappa_{\mathbb{H}^2}(t,z,w)=e^{-\kappa t}p_{\mathbb{H}^2}(t,z,w)$. The path is continuous up until the killing time, so this is *not* a jump process — the "subordination" here changes only the survival probability. The exponential tilt $e^{-\kappa t}$ cures the large-$t$ divergence in loop-mass but not the small-$t$ one.

- **Example 2.7 ($\alpha$-stable, $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha/2}$, on $\mathbb{H}^2$) ([[Ex - The Subordinate Form of the alpha-Stable Process]])** — For $\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha/2}$, $\alpha\in(0,2)$: the operator is the *fractional Laplacian* $\Delta_{\mathbb{H}^2}^{\alpha/2}$ (defined by spectral calculus), and the subordinate kernel is the $\alpha/2$-stable average $p^\alpha_{\mathbb{H}^2}(t,z,w)=\int_0^\infty p_{\mathbb{H}^2}(s,z,w)\,\eta^\alpha_t(s)\,ds$. The process is **pure-jump** (càdlàg with countably many jumps) for every $\alpha\in(0,2)$ — the fractional Laplacian is non-local, and the subordinator has $b=0$ with a genuine Lévy measure — with the boundary $\alpha=2$ recovering Brownian motion. This is the paper's primary jump-process test-bed.

### §2.4 — Subordinate Brownian loop measure and the weighted potential measure

- **[[Def - Subordinate Brownian Loop Measure|Definition 2.8 (subordinate Brownian loop measure)]]** — Applies [[Def - Dirichlet Form Loop Measure|Definition 2.2]] to $(\mathcal E^\phi,\mathcal F^\phi)$: the rooted subordinate Brownian loop measure is $\mu^{*,\phi}_X=\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int_X\mathbb{W}^{t,\phi}_{x\to x}\,d\operatorname{vol}_g(x)$, with pushforward $\mu^\phi_X$ to $C_X$. This is the paper's *working object*: every subsequent mass computation in §3–§7 is a mass in $\mu^\phi_X$ for a specific choice of Bernstein $\phi$. Because $p^\phi$ is an integral of $p^E(s,\cdot,\cdot)$ against $\psi^\phi_t$, every mass carries a *double* integral (in $t$ and in $s$) — the setup for Definition 2.9 and Lemma 2.11.

- **Definition 2.9 (weighted potential measure $V_\phi$) ([[Def - Weighted Potential Measure]])** — The $\sigma$-finite measure on $(0,\infty)$ characterised by $\int h(s)\,V_\phi(ds)=\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}\int h(s)\,\psi^\phi_t(ds)$ for every non-negative measurable $h$ making the right-hand side finite. It is the compressed record of "apply the multiplicative Haar $\frac{dt}{t}$ to the subordinator's law", packaged as a single measure on the subordination variable $s$. In every case of the paper it is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue, $V_\phi(ds)=V_\phi(s)\,ds$.

- **Example 2.10 ($V_\phi$ for the paper's four Bernstein functions) ([[Ex - Weighted Potential Measures of the Paper's Bernstein Functions]])** — Explicit densities: (a) Brownian $V_\phi(ds)=\frac{ds}{s}$; (b) killing $V_\phi(ds)=e^{-\kappa s}\frac{ds}{s}$; (c) $\alpha$-stable $V_\phi(ds)=\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{ds}{s}$; (d) shifted $\alpha$-stable $V_\phi(ds)=\frac{\alpha}{2}e^{-\kappa s}\frac{ds}{s}$. The paper's Example 2.10 tables (a)–(c); (d) is the shifted case worked out in §3.1.4. All four are of the form $c\cdot e^{-\kappa s}\frac{ds}{s}$: three are proportional to the scale-invariant Haar $\frac{ds}{s}$ (Brownian and $\alpha$-stable), and the two killed cases carry an $e^{-\kappa s}$ tilt that breaks scale-invariance.

- **Lemma 2.11 (collapsing the $t$-integral) ([[Lemma - Collapsing the Time Integral of the Subordinate Kernel]])** — For every $x,y\in X$, $\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}p^\phi(t,x,y)=\int_{(0,\infty)}p^E(s,x,y)\,V_\phi(ds)$. This is the payoff of §2.4: it turns the double integral $\int_0^\infty\frac{dt}{t}$ of the *subordinate* kernel into a single integral of the *original* kernel against $V_\phi$. The proof is one application of Tonelli (both measures are $\sigma$-finite and the integrand is non-negative) plus the definition of $V_\phi$. Used in *every* subsequent per-homotopy-class mass computation: [[Thm - Mass of a Free Homotopy Class|Theorem 3.2]] and its §3.1 evaluations, the zeta identities of §4, and the total-mass renormalisation of §5.
