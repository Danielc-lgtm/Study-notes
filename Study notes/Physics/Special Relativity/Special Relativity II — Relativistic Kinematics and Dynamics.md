# Notation Registry

A note on units before anything else. Throughout this topic we work in **natural units** with the speed of light $c = 1$: time and space are measured in the same unit (seconds and light-seconds, say), so $c$ disappears from every equation. This is not a physical assumption but a choice of ruler — exactly as one might measure both horizontal and vertical distances in metres rather than horizontal in metres and vertical in feet. If a final answer is wanted in conventional units, the factors of $c$ are restored by dimensional analysis at the end. Where the contrast with the Newtonian limit is the point, $c$ is reinstated explicitly and flagged.

- $\mu, \nu, \rho, \sigma \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ — spacetime indices; $0$ is the time component, $1,2,3$ the spatial components
- $i, j, k \in \{1,2,3\}$ — purely spatial indices
- $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)$ — the Minkowski metric (mostly-minus signature); $\eta^{\mu\nu}$ is its inverse, equal to it as a matrix
- $X^\mu = (t, \mathbf{x})$ — a spacetime point or **four-position**; $\mathbf{x} = (x^1,x^2,x^3)$ is the spatial part
- $A^\mu$ — a generic **four-vector**, a four-component object transforming as $A^\mu \to \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu A^\nu$ under a Lorentz transformation $\Lambda$
- $A^\mu B_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}A^\mu B^\nu = A^0 B^0 - \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ — the Minkowski inner product; also written $A\cdot B$
- $A_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}A^\nu$ — the same four-vector with index **lowered**; $A_\mu = (A^0, -\mathbf{A})$
- $\tau$ — **proper time**, the time measured by a clock carried along a worldline; the natural curve parameter for a massive particle
- $\mathbf{u} = d\mathbf{x}/dt$ — the ordinary (three-)velocity measured in a given inertial frame; $u = |\mathbf{u}|$ its speed
- $\gamma = \gamma_u = (1 - u^2)^{-1/2}$ — the Lorentz factor of a particle of speed $u$; satisfies $dt/d\tau = \gamma$
- $U^\mu = dX^\mu/d\tau = \gamma(1, \mathbf{u})$ — the **four-velocity**; normalised so that $U\cdot U = 1$
- $A^\mu = dU^\mu/d\tau$ — the **four-acceleration**; always satisfies $A\cdot U = 0$
- $m$ — the **rest mass** (invariant mass) of a particle, a Lorentz scalar
- $P^\mu = m U^\mu = (E, \mathbf{p})$ — the **four-momentum**; satisfies $P\cdot P = m^2$
- $E = \gamma m$ — the **total energy**; $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m \mathbf{u}$ — the **relativistic three-momentum**
- $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 + m^2$ — the energy–momentum relation (the "dispersion relation")
- $F^\mu = dP^\mu/d\tau$ — the **four-force**; satisfies $F\cdot U = 0$
- $\mathbf{f}$ — the ordinary three-force; the spatial part of $F^\mu$ is $\gamma\mathbf{f}$
- $K^\mu = \omega(1, \mathbf{n})$ — the **frequency four-vector** (wave four-vector) of a wave of angular frequency $\omega$ travelling in unit direction $\mathbf{n}$
- $\hbar$ — the reduced Planck constant; for a photon, $E = \hbar\omega$ and $P^\mu = \hbar K^\mu$
- $T^{\mu\nu}$ — the **energy–momentum tensor** (stress–energy tensor), a symmetric $(2,0)$ tensor
- $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu$ — a Lorentz transformation matrix, satisfying $\Lambda^T\eta\Lambda = \eta$

# Motivation

[[Special Relativity I — Lorentz Transformations and Minkowski Space|Special Relativity I]] rebuilt the stage. It replaced the Galilean shuffling of space and time by the [[Def - The Lorentz Transformation|Lorentz transformation]], identified the [[Def - The Spacetime Interval|spacetime interval]] as the one quantity all observers agree on, and packaged spacetime as [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|Minkowski space]] — a four-dimensional vector space with the indefinite metric $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)$. It also taught us what a [[Def - Four-Vector|four-vector]] is: any four-component object that transforms the way the coordinates do. That was the kinematics of *spacetime itself*. This topic is about the **kinematics and dynamics of particles moving through it** — and the entire subject is the answer to one question.

The question is this. Newtonian mechanics is built from velocity $\mathbf{u} = d\mathbf{x}/dt$, momentum $\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{u}$, acceleration $\mathbf{a} = d\mathbf{u}/dt$, and the law $\mathbf{f} = d\mathbf{p}/dt$. Every one of these is a *three-vector*, and every one is built by differentiating with respect to *time* $t$. But in special relativity time is not absolute — $t$ is a frame-dependent coordinate, not an invariant. Differentiating a four-vector with respect to a frame-dependent number does not produce a four-vector; it produces a messy object that looks different to every observer. So the Newtonian quantities, as they stand, are simply not the right objects: they do not have clean transformation laws, and a law written with them cannot be the same in every inertial frame, which Postulate 1 demands. **The whole of relativistic mechanics is the project of rebuilding velocity, momentum, acceleration, and force as genuine four-vectors**, so that the laws relating them are *tensor equations* — true in one frame if and only if true in all.

The key turns out to be a single new idea: **proper time**. Instead of differentiating with respect to the coordinate time $t$ of some observer, differentiate with respect to $\tau$, the time read by a clock travelling *with the particle*. Proper time is the spacetime interval along the worldline, divided by $c$; it is a Lorentz invariant, agreed on by everyone. Differentiate the four-position $X^\mu$ with respect to $\tau$ and you get the [[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration|four-velocity]] $U^\mu$, a true four-vector because $X^\mu$ is a four-vector and $\tau$ is a scalar. Multiply by the rest mass $m$ — itself an invariant — and you get the [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momentum]] $P^\mu$, the object that is actually conserved. Differentiate $U^\mu$ again and you get the [[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration|four-acceleration]]; set $dP^\mu/d\tau$ equal to a four-force and you have [[Thm - The Relativistic Equation of Motion|Newton's second law]], now Lorentz-covariant. The recurring move never changes: *to relativise a Newtonian quantity, differentiate with respect to proper time instead of coordinate time.*

This rebuilding is not bookkeeping. It forces two genuinely new pieces of physics. The first is **$E = mc^2$**: the time component of the four-momentum turns out to be the energy, and Taylor-expanding it reveals that a particle's energy contains a rest term $mc^2$ in addition to the familiar kinetic energy. Mass *is* energy. The second is the dissolution of two separate Newtonian conservation laws — conservation of mass and conservation of energy — into the single law of [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|four-momentum conservation]]. Mass is no longer separately conserved; it can be converted into kinetic energy and back. This is what makes particle decay, particle creation, nuclear binding energy, and the entire enterprise of particle physics possible, and §2.2 and §2.3 are largely the working-out of its consequences.

Finally there are the **massless particles**, the photon chief among them. A photon travels on a null worldline, along which proper time does not advance, so the proper-time machinery breaks down for it. The repair is elegant: a photon has no four-velocity and no rest mass, but it still has a [[Def - The Four-Momentum of a Photon|four-momentum]], a null four-vector $P\cdot P = 0$, supplied by quantum mechanics through $E = \hbar\omega$. With that one object the photon enters every conservation-of-four-momentum calculation on exactly the same footing as a massive particle, and the Compton effect, the relativistic Doppler shift, pair production, and the photon rocket all follow from the same algebra.

# Concept Map

## §2.1 Proper Time and Four-Velocity

- **[[Def - Proper Time]]**
	- The **proper time** $\tau$ along a timelike worldline is the time read by a clock carried along it, $\tau = \int \sqrt{dt^2 - d\mathbf{x}^2} = \int dt/\gamma$, equal to the [[Def - The Spacetime Interval|spacetime interval]] divided by $c$. It is a Lorentz **invariant** — every observer computes the same value — because it is built from the invariant interval. For a particle moving at speed $u$ in a frame, $dt/d\tau = \gamma$, so a moving clock runs slow. Proper time is the natural, frame-independent parameter for a worldline, and it is the device that makes four-velocity and four-momentum into genuine four-vectors. It is undefined along a null worldline, since the interval there vanishes.

- **[[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration]]**
	- The **four-velocity** is $U^\mu = dX^\mu/d\tau = \gamma(1,\mathbf{u})$, the derivative of the [[Def - Four-Vector|four-position]] with respect to proper time; it is a four-vector because $\tau$ is a scalar. It is normalised, $U\cdot U = c^2$ (i.e. $=1$ in natural units), so it carries only three independent components — the constraint absorbs the fourth. The **four-acceleration** $A^\mu = dU^\mu/d\tau$ is always Minkowski-orthogonal to the four-velocity, $A\cdot U = 0$, a consequence of differentiating the normalisation. Under a Lorentz transformation $U^\mu \to \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu$, which makes velocity addition a single matrix multiplication. Constant proper acceleration produces hyperbolic motion in spacetime.

- **[[Def - The Frequency Four-Vector]]**
	- The **frequency four-vector** $K^\mu = \omega(1,\mathbf{n})$ packages the angular frequency $\omega$ and propagation direction $\mathbf{n}$ of a wave; for light it is null, $K\cdot K = 0$. A plane wave is written $\cos(K\cdot X)$, and because the phase $K\cdot X$ is a Lorentz scalar, $K^\mu$ must be a four-vector. The frequency an observer of four-velocity $U^\mu$ measures is the invariant contraction $\omega_{\text{obs}} = U\cdot K$ (with $c=1$). For a photon, the [[Def - The Four-Momentum of a Photon|four-momentum]] is $P^\mu = \hbar K^\mu$. This single four-vector delivers the relativistic Doppler effect and aberration in one stroke.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Wave Four-Vector and Dispersion Relations *(from Quantum Field Theory)*
> Writing $K^\mu = (\omega, \mathbf{k})$ for a general wave, the condition $K\cdot K = m^2$ is the **dispersion relation** of a relativistic field — null for the photon, massive for an electron. The plane-wave factor $e^{-iK\cdot X}$ is the building block of every **quantum field**, and de Broglie's relation $P^\mu = \hbar K^\mu$ is exactly the bridge from this section's classical wave four-vector to the quantum particle.

- **[[Ex - Proper time of a clock in circular motion]]**
	- A clock orbits at constant speed $v$; integrate $d\tau=dt/\gamma$ along the curved worldline to get $\Delta\tau=T/\gamma$, compare with an inertial clock, and explain why only the speed — not the centripetal acceleration — enters (the CERN muon storage ring). (⭐⭐)

## §2.2 Four-Momentum and Relativistic Dynamics

- **[[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass]]**
	- The **four-momentum** of a particle is $P^\mu = m U^\mu = (E, \mathbf{p})$, the rest mass times the four-velocity. The **rest mass** $m$ is a Lorentz invariant — the mass measured in the particle's own rest frame — and is the modern meaning of "mass" (the older "relativistic mass" $\gamma m$ is just the energy $E$ in disguise). The time component is the energy $E = \gamma m$, the spatial part the relativistic three-momentum $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{u}$. The normalisation $U\cdot U = 1$ becomes $P\cdot P = m^2$, equivalently $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 + m^2$. As $u\to c$ both $E$ and $|\mathbf{p}|$ diverge, which is why no massive particle reaches the speed of light.

- **[[Thm - Mass-Energy Equivalence]]**
	- Expanding $E = \gamma m c^2 = mc^2 + \tfrac12 m u^2 + \cdots$ shows the energy of a particle is its rest energy $mc^2$ plus the Newtonian kinetic energy plus relativistic corrections. A particle at rest still has energy $E = mc^2$: mass *is* a form of energy. The invariant relation is $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4$, obtained cleanly by evaluating $P\cdot P$ in the rest frame. The practical consequence is that the rest mass of a system is *not* the sum of the rest masses of its parts — binding energy and kinetic energy contribute — and mass can be converted into kinetic energy and back.

- **[[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum]]**
	- In any process with no external forces, the **total four-momentum is conserved**: $\sum_{\text{in}} P_i^\mu = \sum_{\text{out}} P_j^\mu$, a single four-vector equation comprising conservation of energy ($\mu=0$) and conservation of three-momentum ($\mu=1,2,3$). It is frame-independent: if it holds in one inertial frame it holds in all, because both sides transform identically. It *replaces* the two separate Newtonian laws of mass conservation and energy conservation, which are recovered as its low-speed limit. This is the master tool of relativistic collision and decay problems.

- **[[Ex - Threshold energy for particle production]]**
	- Find the minimum energy to create a new particle in a collision; the trick is that at threshold all products are at rest in the centre-of-momentum frame, and the invariant $(\sum P)^2$ is then minimal. (⭐⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Compton scattering]]**
	- Derive the shift in a photon's frequency when it scatters off an electron, by eliminating the unknown recoil electron via $P_e'\cdot P_e' = m^2$. (⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Two-body decay kinematics]]**
	- A particle at rest decays into two; conservation of four-momentum fixes the energies and speeds of the products entirely in terms of the three masses. (⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Mandelstam variables for two-body scattering]]**
	- For $1+2\to3+4$, define $s,t,u$ as Minkowski squares of four-momentum sums and differences; prove they are Lorentz invariant and obey the sum rule $s+t+u=\sum m_i^2$, identify $\sqrt s$ as the centre-of-mass energy, and compute $s$ for a collider versus a fixed target. (⭐⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Whether a particle reaction is kinematically allowed]]**
	- Use conservation of four-momentum as a filter: show a free photon cannot decay to $e^+e^-$ (null cannot equal timelike), find the two-photon pair-production threshold $E_1E_2(1-\cos\theta)\ge2m_e^2$, and compute the fixed-target threshold for antiproton production. (⭐⭐)

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Energy–Momentum Tensor and Perfect Fluids *(from General Relativity and Continuum Mechanics)*
> A single particle carries a four-momentum $P^\mu$; a continuous distribution of matter carries an **energy–momentum tensor** $T^{\mu\nu}$, the flux of the $\mu$-component of four-momentum across a surface of constant $x^\nu$. For a **perfect fluid** it takes the form $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)U^\mu U^\nu + p\,\eta^{\mu\nu}$ with $\rho$ the energy density and $p$ the pressure. Its conservation $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is the field-theoretic version of [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|four-momentum conservation]], and $T^{\mu\nu}$ is the source term in Einstein's field equations.

## §2.3 The Photon and Relativistic Dynamics

- **[[Def - The Four-Momentum of a Photon]]**
	- A **photon** is a massless particle, $m = 0$, travelling on a null worldline at speed $c$ in every frame; proper time does not advance along it, so it has no four-velocity. It still has a four-momentum, the **null** four-vector $P^\mu = (E, \mathbf{p})$ with $P\cdot P = 0$, equivalently $E = |\mathbf{p}|c$. Quantum mechanics supplies the magnitude: $E = \hbar\omega$ and $P^\mu = \hbar K^\mu$, tying the photon to the [[Def - The Frequency Four-Vector|frequency four-vector]]. A photon enters [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|conservation of four-momentum]] on the same footing as a massive particle — this is what makes pair production, Compton scattering and the Doppler effect computable.

- **[[Thm - The Relativistic Equation of Motion]]**
	- Newton's second law, made Lorentz-covariant, is $F^\mu = dP^\mu/d\tau$, with $F^\mu$ a **four-force** satisfying $F\cdot U = 0$. Its spatial part is $\gamma\mathbf{f}$ where $\mathbf{f} = d\mathbf{p}/dt$ is the ordinary force acting on the relativistic momentum; its time part is $\gamma$ times the rate of working $dE/dt = \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{f}$. The single physically important relativistic force is the electromagnetic one, $F^\mu = q\,F^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu$, with $F^\mu{}_\nu$ the field-strength tensor. When $F^\mu = 0$ the law reduces to conservation of four-momentum, the basis of all of particle physics.

- **[[Ex - Pair production and the photon-photon threshold]]**
	- Two photons can collide to make an electron–positron pair, but a single photon in vacuum cannot; the difference is a statement about the invariant mass of the system. (⭐⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - The relativistic rocket]]**
	- A rocket that propels itself by emitting photons; conservation of four-momentum integrates to a relativistic analogue of the Tsiolkovsky equation. (⭐⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Photon emission and recoil]]**
	- An excited atom emits a photon and recoils; the photon's energy is slightly less than the level spacing because the atom must carry away momentum. (⭐⭐)

> [!tip] Unlocked: Gauge Fields and the Field-Strength Tensor *(from Electromagnetism and Gauge Theory)*
> The electromagnetic four-force $F^\mu = q F^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu$ contains the **field-strength tensor** $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$, built from the **four-potential** $A^\mu$. Maxwell's four equations collapse to two tensor equations, $\partial_\mu F^{\nu\mu} = J^\nu$ and $dF = 0$. The freedom $A^\mu \to A^\mu + \partial^\mu\lambda$ is **gauge invariance**, the seed of all of **gauge theory** and the Standard Model.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of relativistic kinematics and dynamics chase a small, recognisable set of goals, and naming the goal is the first step of every solution. The most common is **finding an energy or a momentum**: the energy of a decay product, the threshold energy to create a particle, the recoil energy of an atom, the frequency of a scattered photon. A second is **finding a speed, an angle, or a direction**: the speed of the fragments of a decay, the angle between two scattered particles, the aberration of a light ray. A third is **finding an invariant mass** — the rest mass of a composite system, which is emphatically not the sum of its parts' masses, and which is the cleanest single number characterising a collection of particles. A fourth is **establishing a kinematic inequality or a possibility/impossibility statement**: a particle can decay only if heavier than its products; a single photon cannot pair-produce in vacuum; a massive particle cannot reach $c$. A fifth, more structural, is **showing that a law is Lorentz-covariant** — that it is built from genuine four-vectors and tensors and so holds in every frame. The unifying remark is that all of these are questions about a *conserved four-vector* or a *Lorentz invariant*: you understand a relativistic process when you know the total four-momentum (which is conserved) and the invariant masses (which are frame-independent), and every target above is read off from those two.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **No external forces act** — then four-momentum is conserved, $\sum P_{\text{in}} = \sum P_{\text{out}}$, and this single four-vector equation is the richest source in the subject. **A particle is on a timelike worldline with known rest mass** — then $P\cdot P = m^2$ constrains its four-momentum to a hyperboloid, and squaring any expression for $P$ produces the scalar $m^2$. **A particle is massless** — then $P\cdot P = 0$, the photon's four-momentum is null, and squaring it gives zero, which is even more powerful as a simplifier. **A convenient frame is available** — the rest frame of a decaying particle, or the centre-of-momentum frame of a collision, in which the total spatial momentum vanishes and the algebra collapses. **A quantity is known to be a Lorentz scalar** — proper time, rest mass, an inner product $P\cdot Q$ — then it may be computed in whichever frame is easiest and the answer transported unchanged to any other. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: "no external forces" routes through conservation of four-momentum to an energy or angle; "known rest mass" routes through $P\cdot P = m^2$ to an invariant; "convenient frame" routes a hard vector equation into a trivial scalar one. The [[Special Relativity II — Relativistic Kinematics and Dynamics#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves almost every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything here is self-contained: a reader who knows only that a four-vector is a four-component object transforming as $A^\mu\to\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu A^\nu$, and that the Minkowski inner product $A\cdot B = A^0B^0 - \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ is the one combination every observer agrees on, should be able to follow each operation from its description alone.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Write down the total four-momentum and set it equal before and after.** In any process without external forces, [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|conservation of four-momentum]] gives $\sum_{\text{in}}P^\mu = \sum_{\text{out}}P^\mu$ — four scalar equations in one. This is the single most-used operation. The trigger is any collision, decay, or creation process; the first line of almost every solution is "conservation of four-momentum reads $P_1 + P_2 = P_3 + P_4$". It already contains conservation of energy (the $\mu=0$ component) and conservation of three-momentum (the spatial components), so you never invoke those separately.

2. **Square a four-momentum to extract an invariant mass.** For any four-momentum, $P\cdot P = m^2$ — a Lorentz scalar — and for a photon $P\cdot P = 0$. So whenever an equation can be brought to the form $P = (\text{stuff})$, taking the Minkowski square of both sides converts the left side into the *known number* $m^2$ and the right side into inner products you can evaluate. The trigger is the appearance of a particle whose four-momentum you do not want to know in detail: isolate it on one side, square, and it becomes $m^2$. This is the workhorse of [[Ex - Compton scattering|Compton scattering]] and [[Ex - Threshold energy for particle production|threshold]] problems.

3. **Go to the centre-of-momentum frame.** Choose the inertial frame in which the total spatial three-momentum vanishes, $\sum\mathbf{p} = 0$. In this frame the total four-momentum is purely temporal, $(\,E_{\text{tot}},\mathbf{0})$, and its square is simply $E_{\text{tot}}^2$ — the *invariant mass squared* of the whole system. The trigger is any multi-particle process, especially threshold and decay problems; the centre-of-momentum frame is "nearly always the smart frame to choose" because it kills the spatial components and turns vector bookkeeping into scalar arithmetic.

4. **Go to the rest frame of a chosen particle.** For a massive particle, the frame in which it is momentarily at rest gives it the four-momentum $(m,\mathbf{0})$ and four-velocity $(1,\mathbf{0})$ — the simplest possible components. The trigger is any problem featuring one distinguished massive particle (a decaying nucleus, the target electron in Compton scattering): compute in its rest frame, then, because the quantities of interest are invariants, transport the answer to any frame for free.

5. **Differentiate with respect to proper time, not coordinate time.** To build a four-vector dynamical quantity, differentiate a four-vector with respect to the scalar $\tau$: $U^\mu = dX^\mu/d\tau$, $A^\mu = dU^\mu/d\tau$, $F^\mu = dP^\mu/d\tau$. The trigger is any attempt to relativise a Newtonian rate of change. Using coordinate time $t$ instead is the standard illegal shortcut — see below — because $t$ is frame-dependent and the result is not a four-vector. Convert between the two with $d/d\tau = \gamma\,d/dt$.

6. **Use a Lorentz invariant to switch frames.** Any Minkowski inner product $P\cdot Q$, any rest mass $m$, the proper time $\tau$, the frequency $U\cdot K$ measured by an observer — all are Lorentz scalars, the *same number in every frame*. The trigger is a quantity that is awkward in the frame the problem is posed in: recognise it as an invariant, compute it in whatever frame makes it trivial, and equate the two expressions. This is exactly how [[Thm - Relativistic Velocity Addition|velocity addition]] is done painlessly with four-velocities: $U\cdot U' = c^2\gamma_w$ in one frame, $\gamma_u\gamma_v(c^2 - \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v})$ in another.

7. **Lorentz-transform a four-vector when a component is wanted in another frame.** If a four-vector's components are known in frame $S$ and a component is wanted in $S'$, apply the matrix: $A'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu A^\nu$. The trigger is a question of the form "what energy/frequency/angle does the *other* observer measure?" — the relativistic Doppler effect and aberration are exactly this operation applied to $K^\mu$, and the transformation of $P^\mu$ gives how energy and momentum mix between frames.

8. **Use the orthogonality $A\cdot U = 0$ and $F\cdot U = 0$.** Differentiating a normalisation constraint ($U\cdot U = 1$ or $P\cdot P = m^2$) shows the four-acceleration is Minkowski-orthogonal to the four-velocity, and the four-force likewise. The trigger is any problem involving acceleration or force: this orthogonality is a free equation, it constrains the four-acceleration to a three-dimensional subspace, and it is what guarantees the rest mass stays constant under an ordinary force.

9. **Raise and lower indices with the metric, and keep contractions paired.** Write $A_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}A^\nu$, so $A_\mu = (A^0,-\mathbf{A})$, and form invariants only as $A^\mu B_\mu$ with one index up and one down. The trigger is any index manipulation: an expression with a repeated index that is *not* one-up-one-down (such as $A^\mu B^\mu$ summed) is frame-dependent and should be regarded as illegal in relativity. Correctly paired contractions are automatically Lorentz scalars.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Using the Newtonian momentum $\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{u}$ and kinetic energy $\tfrac12 m u^2$
> Every instinct trained on Newtonian mechanics writes momentum as mass times velocity. Relativistically this is wrong: the conserved three-momentum is $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{u}$, with the extra factor $\gamma = (1-u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$, and the kinetic energy is $E - mc^2 = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$, not $\tfrac12 mu^2$. The Newtonian forms are only the leading terms of a Taylor expansion in $u/c$ and fail badly as $u\to c$ — where $\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{u}$ would stay finite but the true momentum diverges. The diverging $\gamma$ is precisely what enforces the speed limit; drop it and you "prove" particles can exceed $c$. Use $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{u}$ and $E = \gamma mc^2$ always; the Newtonian expressions are a limit, never an identity.

> [!warning] 2. Adding velocities linearly, $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$
> If a particle moves at $\mathbf{u}$ in a frame and the frame moves at $\mathbf{v}$, it is tempting to say the particle moves at $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}$. This is the Galilean law, and it is false — it would let two sub-light speeds add to a super-light one. The correct composition is the [[Thm - Relativistic Velocity Addition|relativistic velocity addition]] law, cleanest as a *four-velocity* statement: four-velocities do not add, they Lorentz-transform, $U^\mu \to \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu$. The linear law is the limit $c\to\infty$; at finite $c$ the composition of two speeds below $c$ is always below $c$, which is the whole point. Velocities are not vectors you add — boosts are a *group*, and you compose group elements.

> [!warning] 3. Treating rest mass as additive, $m_{\text{total}} = \sum m_i$
> The mass of a composite system is the most over-trusted quantity in the subject. Rest mass is *not* additive: the invariant mass of a system is $M^2 = (\sum P_i)\cdot(\sum P_i)$, and expanding this produces cross terms $2P_i\cdot P_j$ that depend on the relative motion and binding of the parts. Two photons each of zero mass can form a system of nonzero invariant mass; a bound nucleus weighs *less* than its free constituents by the binding energy over $c^2$; a hot gas weighs more than a cold one. What *is* conserved and additive is the four-momentum $\sum P_i^\mu$; mass is the (frame-independent) length of that vector sum, and the length of a sum is not the sum of lengths.

> [!warning] 4. Confusing total energy with rest energy, or rest mass with "relativistic mass"
> Two distinct slogans, $E = mc^2$ and $E = \gamma mc^2$, are constantly conflated. The first is the energy of a particle *at rest*; the second is its total energy *in motion*. A particle's energy is $E = \gamma mc^2 = mc^2 + (\text{kinetic energy})$, and only the rest part is $mc^2$. The older language called $\gamma m$ the "relativistic mass" and wrote $E = (\text{relativistic mass})c^2$ to rescue the famous formula — but "relativistic mass" is just energy divided by $c^2$, carries no new information, and obscures that the genuinely invariant, frame-independent mass is the rest mass $m$. Modern usage: *mass means rest mass*, it is a Lorentz scalar, and energy is a separate, frame-dependent quantity equal to $\gamma m c^2$.

> [!warning] 5. Differentiating a four-vector with respect to coordinate time $t$
> To get a velocity one differentiates position with respect to time — so why not set $V^\mu = dX^\mu/dt$? Because $t$ is the time coordinate of one particular observer, not a Lorentz scalar. Under a boost both $X^\mu$ and $t$ change, and $dX^\mu/dt$ transforms in a tangled, non-tensorial way: it is not a four-vector, and a law written with it is not the same in every frame. The fix is to differentiate with respect to **proper time** $\tau$, which *is* invariant — see [[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration|four-velocity]]. The relation $dt/d\tau = \gamma$ converts between the two; the factor of $\gamma$ that distinguishes $U^\mu = dX^\mu/d\tau$ from the naive $dX^\mu/dt$ is exactly what makes $U^\mu$ a genuine four-vector.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this topic are won the moment you classify what is being asked and recognise the conserved or invariant object that answers it. Begin there.

If the problem **describes a collision, decay, or creation process and asks for an energy, momentum, speed, or angle**, you are in the central problem class of the topic, and the first line of the solution is fixed: write [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|conservation of four-momentum]], $\sum_{\text{in}} P^\mu = \sum_{\text{out}} P^\mu$. This is one four-vector equation, hence four scalar equations, and it is almost always enough. The skill is in what comes next, and it is governed by a single question: *is there a four-momentum in the problem you do not care about?* If a decay produces a particle whose energy and direction you are not asked for, or Compton scattering recoils an electron you only need to eliminate, then **isolate that unwanted four-momentum on one side of the equation and square it**. Squaring converts it into the scalar $P\cdot P = m^2$ (or $0$ for a photon), the unwanted vector vanishes from the problem, and what remains is a scalar equation in the quantities you do want. This "rearrange to $P = \ldots$, then square" move is the most important technique in §2.2 and §2.3; it is how [[Ex - Compton scattering|Compton scattering]], [[Ex - Two-body decay kinematics|two-body decay]], and [[Ex - Threshold energy for particle production|threshold]] problems are all solved.

If the problem **asks for a threshold — the minimum energy for something to happen** — the governing idea is that *at threshold the products are all at rest in the centre-of-momentum frame*. There is no kinetic energy to spare: every bit of available energy goes into making rest mass. So the strategy is to evaluate the invariant $(\sum P)\cdot(\sum P)$ — the total four-momentum squared, which is the invariant mass squared of the whole system and is the *same in every frame*. Compute it in the lab frame, where the kinematics are given, and again in the centre-of-momentum frame, where at threshold $\sum P = (M_{\text{products}}, \mathbf{0})$ so the square is just $(\sum m_{\text{products}})^2$. Equating the two frame computations of the same invariant delivers the threshold condition in one line. The reason this works is operation 3 and operation 6 combined: the centre-of-momentum frame trivialises the right-hand side, and the invariance of $P\cdot P$ lets you import it into the lab frame.

If the problem **involves a photon or any massless particle**, the simplification is even sharper. A photon has no rest frame and no four-velocity, but it has a [[Def - The Four-Momentum of a Photon|four-momentum]] that is *null*: $P\cdot P = 0$. So whenever you square an expression containing a photon's four-momentum, that term simply drops out. In a problem with several photons, the photon–photon inner products $P_\gamma\cdot P_\gamma' = E_\gamma E_\gamma'(1-\cos\theta)$ survive but each photon's self-square vanishes. The photon also couples to the [[Def - The Frequency Four-Vector|frequency four-vector]] through $P^\mu = \hbar K^\mu$, so any question about the *colour* of light — the Doppler effect, Compton scattering — is answered by tracking $K^\mu$ and contracting it with the observer's four-velocity, $\omega_{\text{obs}} = U\cdot K$.

If the problem **asks you to relate measurements made by two different observers** — what frequency, energy, or angle the other observer sees — the route is to identify the relevant four-vector, write its components in the frame where they are known, and either Lorentz-transform it (operation 7) or contract it with the four-velocity of the second observer (operation 6). The relativistic Doppler effect is $K^\mu$ transformed or contracted; the transformation of energy and momentum between frames is $P^\mu$ transformed; aberration is the change in the spatial direction of $K^\mu$ under a boost. Do not transform coordinates and recompute — transform the four-vector directly. This is the entire advantage of the four-vector formalism: it eliminates the need to carry out explicit coordinate transformations, exactly as the dot product eliminates coordinates from Euclidean geometry.

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle. **The two things you can always trust are the conserved total four-momentum and the Lorentz-invariant scalars.** A specific component of a four-vector — an energy, a single momentum component — is frame-dependent and treacherous; the *vector* $\sum P^\mu$ is conserved, and the *scalars* $P\cdot Q$ and $m^2$ are frame-independent. So the disciplined approach to any problem is: write the conserved four-vector equation, then manipulate it only into statements about invariants (by squaring, by contracting with a chosen four-vector, by going to a frame that trivialises an invariant). Solve the problem in the world of invariants, where every observer agrees, and translate back to components only at the very end. A solution that stays in component-land frame-hops and accumulates $\gamma$ factors; a solution that lives in invariant-land is two or three lines long.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|Conservation of four-momentum]]**: $\sum_{\text{in}} P^\mu = \sum_{\text{out}} P^\mu$. This is the most-used single fact in the topic because it is the *only* dynamical input most problems need: in the absence of external forces it holds, it is exact (not an approximation), and it bundles conservation of energy and conservation of three-momentum into one frame-independent statement. The recognisable setup is any process — collision, decay, absorption, emission — and the first line of the solution is always to write it down. It is most powerful when combined with squaring (next item): conservation gives you a four-vector equation, squaring turns it into a scalar equation about invariants. Recognise its applicability whenever particles interact and nothing external pushes on them.

- **The mass-shell relation $P\cdot P = m^2$** (equivalently $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 + m^2$): every particle's four-momentum has Minkowski-length-squared equal to its rest mass squared, and *zero* for a photon. Its reusable content is that it converts a four-vector into a known scalar: whenever you can isolate a four-momentum on one side of an equation, squaring both sides replaces it by $m^2$. This is the engine of elimination — it removes uninteresting particles from a calculation — and it is also the relation that connects energy and momentum, so any problem giving one and asking for the other routes through it. The photon's version, $P\cdot P = 0$, is even more useful because the term vanishes outright.

- **[[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration|The four-velocity]] $U^\mu = \gamma(1,\mathbf{u})$, normalised $U\cdot U = 1$**: the basic kinematic four-vector, from which $P^\mu = mU^\mu$ and $A^\mu = dU^\mu/d\tau$ are built. Its reusable feature is the normalisation: $U\cdot U = c^2$ is a free equation, true for every particle, and differentiating it hands you the orthogonality $A\cdot U = 0$ at no cost. The contraction of two four-velocities, $U\cdot U' = c^2\gamma_{\text{rel}}$, is the relative Lorentz factor of two particles — the cleanest route to relativistic velocity addition and to relative speeds in collision problems. Reach for $U^\mu$ whenever a problem mentions a particle's velocity and you want a four-vector to compute with.

- **[[Def - Proper Time|Proper time]] $\tau$ and the relation $dt/d\tau = \gamma$**: proper time is the invariant clock-reading along a worldline, and the conversion factor $\gamma$ between it and coordinate time is the most frequently used small fact in the topic. Its reusable role is twofold: it is what makes every dynamical four-vector a genuine four-vector (differentiate with respect to $\tau$, not $t$), and the integral $T = \int d\tau = \int dt/\gamma$ computes the elapsed time on an accelerated clock, which is the twin paradox and every ageing-of-travellers problem. Recognise it whenever a problem asks "how much time passes for the particle / traveller / clock".

- **The Lorentz invariance of inner products**: any contraction $A^\mu B_\mu$ is the same number in every inertial frame. This is the most reusable structural fact, because it is the licence behind "compute in the convenient frame". Typical use: a quantity is awkward in the frame the problem is set in (the lab); recognise it as an inner product, evaluate it in a friendlier frame (rest frame, centre-of-momentum frame), and equate. Threshold problems, velocity addition, the Doppler effect, and Compton scattering are all this one move. The discipline it enforces — keep contractions one-index-up-one-index-down — is also what guarantees a written law is Lorentz-covariant.

# Bridges

1. **Geometric mechanics and the variational principle — the relativistic action is proper time.** A free relativistic particle extremises the functional $S = -mc^2\int d\tau$, the proper time along its worldline, weighted by $-mc^2$. This is the exact relativistic counterpart of the principle of least action: the integrand $-mc^2\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot X^\mu\dot X^\nu}$ plays the role of the Lagrangian, and the Euler–Lagrange equations are precisely the statement that the four-acceleration vanishes, $dU^\mu/d\tau = 0$, a straight worldline. A reader who knows geometric mechanics will recognise that the conjugate momentum $\partial L/\partial\dot X^\mu$ is exactly the [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momentum]] $P_\mu$, and that the mass-shell constraint $P\cdot P = m^2$ is a *primary constraint* in the Hamiltonian sense — the relativistic free particle is a constrained system, and the constraint is the Hamiltonian (up to a multiplier), generating reparametrisation of the worldline. The [[Thm - The Reversed Triangle Inequality|reversed triangle inequality]] of [[Special Relativity I — Lorentz Transformations and Minkowski Space|Special Relativity I]] then says the straight worldline is the one of *maximal* proper time — the variational principle is a maximum, not a minimum, because the Minkowski metric is indefinite. This is the cleanest entry point to the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulation of relativistic mechanics and, beyond it, of relativistic field theory.

2. **Quantum mechanics — the de Broglie relation and the wave–particle bridge.** The single equation $P^\mu = \hbar K^\mu$, relating a particle's [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momentum]] to the [[Def - The Frequency Four-Vector|frequency four-vector]] of an associated wave, is the relativistic form of de Broglie's hypothesis. Its time component is $E = \hbar\omega$ (Planck) and its spatial part is $\mathbf{p} = \hbar\mathbf{k}$ (de Broglie). Because both sides are four-vectors, the relation is automatically Lorentz-covariant — which is why it could only be stated cleanly *after* relativity. The mass-shell condition $P\cdot P = m^2$ becomes the dispersion relation $\omega^2 - \mathbf{k}^2 = m^2$, and a plane wave $e^{-iK\cdot X}$ with this dispersion is the free solution of the Klein–Gordon equation. Special relativity supplies the kinematic skeleton on which relativistic quantum mechanics and quantum field theory are built.

3. **Continuum mechanics and fluid dynamics — the energy–momentum tensor.** A single particle carries a four-momentum $P^\mu$; a continuous medium carries an **energy–momentum tensor** $T^{\mu\nu}$, defined as the flux of the $\mu$-component of four-momentum across a surface of constant $x^\nu$. For a **perfect fluid** — one with no viscosity and no heat conduction, isotropic in its rest frame — it has the form $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)U^\mu U^\nu + p\,\eta^{\mu\nu}$, where $\rho$ is the energy density and $p$ the pressure, both measured in the fluid's rest frame, and $U^\mu$ is the fluid four-velocity field. The conservation law $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is the local, field-theoretic form of [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|four-momentum conservation]]: its time component is the continuity equation for energy and its spatial components are the relativistic Euler equations. A reader from fluid dynamics will see the non-relativistic Euler and continuity equations emerge as the $c\to\infty$ limit. This tensor is also the source term of Einstein's field equations, so this bridge runs straight into general relativity.

4. **Electromagnetism and gauge theory — the field-strength tensor.** The one relativistic force that survives from the Newtonian world is the electromagnetic one, and [[Thm - The Relativistic Equation of Motion|the relativistic equation of motion]] for a charge is $dP^\mu/d\tau = q\,F^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu$, where $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ is the **field-strength tensor**, an antisymmetric $(0,2)$ tensor packaging the electric and magnetic fields, and $A^\mu$ is the **four-potential**. Maxwell's four three-vector equations collapse, in this language, to two tensor equations — $\partial_\mu F^{\nu\mu} = J^\nu$ and the identity $\partial_{[\mu}F_{\nu\lambda]} = 0$, which says $F$ is a closed two-form, $dF = 0$. Because Minkowski space is topologically trivial, closed implies exact, so $F = dA$ for a one-form potential $A$ — and the freedom $A\to A + d\lambda$ is **gauge invariance**. This connects directly to [[Multivariate Analysis IV — Differential Forms and Stokes' Theorem|differential forms]]: $F$ is a two-form, $A$ a one-form, the exterior derivative $d$ is the coordinate-free curl, and $d^2 = 0$ is the identity behind one pair of Maxwell's equations. Promoting the global gauge freedom to a local symmetry is the seed of all of gauge theory and the Standard Model.

5. **Group theory — boosts compose, they do not add.** The reason velocities do not add linearly is structural: the set of Lorentz transformations forms a [[Def - Group|group]], the [[Group Theory I — §1.1–1.2|Lorentz group]], and combining two boosts means *composing two group elements*, not adding two vectors. A boost in a fixed direction is parametrised by the [[Def - Rapidity|rapidity]] $\phi$, and rapidities — unlike velocities — *do* add, because the boosts along one axis form a one-parameter abelian subgroup isomorphic to $(\mathbb{R},+)$. The relativistic velocity addition formula is just the statement $\tanh(\phi_1 + \phi_2) = (\tanh\phi_1 + \tanh\phi_2)/(1 + \tanh\phi_1\tanh\phi_2)$ with $u = c\tanh\phi$. Four-vectors transform under the *defining representation* of the Lorentz group, and the classification of particles by rest mass and spin is the classification of its irreducible representations — the deep reason rest mass is a Lorentz invariant is that it is a Casimir of the Poincaré group.

6. **Quantum field theory — from particles to fields.** The mass-shell relation $P\cdot P = m^2$ is the hinge on which relativistic quantum mechanics turns. Quantising a particle replaces its four-momentum by the differential operator $P_\mu \to i\hbar\partial_\mu$ — this is the relativistic completion of the Schrödinger prescription $E\to i\hbar\partial_t$, $\mathbf{p}\to -i\hbar\nabla$, now packaged covariantly into one four-vector of operators. Feeding it into $P\cdot P = m^2$ turns the algebraic mass-shell condition into a wave equation, $(\Box + m^2)\phi = 0$, the **Klein–Gordon equation**, with $\Box = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu$ the d'Alembertian. The plane waves $e^{-iK\cdot X}$ that solve it are exactly the wave four-vector of §2.1, with the dispersion relation $K\cdot K = m^2$. But the Klein–Gordon equation is second order in time, which spoils the probability interpretation of single-particle quantum mechanics, and Dirac sought instead a *first*-order equation whose square reproduces it; the only way to do this forces the wavefunction to carry several components and the coefficients to be matrices, producing the **Dirac equation** and, as unavoidable bonuses, spin-$\tfrac12$ and a doubling of states that Dirac read as **antimatter**. The structural reason a particle carries exactly the two labels mass and spin is the **Wigner classification**: the irreducible unitary representations of the Poincaré group — the symmetry group of Minkowski space, boosts and rotations and translations together — are labelled by precisely two invariants, the Casimir $P\cdot P = m^2$ and a spin (or helicity) quantum number. A particle *is* an irreducible representation of the Poincaré group; that is what fixes its quantum numbers. Finally, $E = mc^2$ makes particle number unstable: with enough energy concentrated in a small region, pair creation produces new particles, so the number of particles is not conserved and a fixed-particle-number wavefunction cannot describe the physics. The repair is to promote the wavefunction itself to an operator-valued **field** whose excitations are particles — single-particle quantum mechanics is replaced by **quantum field theory**, in which creation and annihilation of particles is built in from the start.

7. **General relativity — from free particles to geodesics.** A free relativistic particle obeys the equation of motion $dU^\mu/d\tau = 0$: its four-velocity is constant, so its worldline is a straight line in flat Minkowski space, the relativistic restatement of Newton's first law. General relativity keeps this principle — a free-falling particle still moves "as straight as possible" — but on a *curved* spacetime, where "straight" must be defined intrinsically. The flat-space derivative $d/d\tau$ acquires correction terms encoding how the coordinate basis itself twists from point to point, and the equation of motion becomes the **geodesic equation** $dU^\mu/d\tau + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}U^\nu U^\rho = 0$, where the **Christoffel symbols** $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}$ are the components of the connection, built from first derivatives of the metric $g_{\mu\nu}$. In flat space and Cartesian coordinates $\Gamma = 0$ and the geodesic equation collapses back to $dU^\mu/d\tau = 0$; the curvature lives entirely in the $\Gamma$ term, and gravity is the statement that these straightest-possible worldlines are bent relative to a flat-space grid. What bends them is matter: the **energy–momentum tensor** $T^{\mu\nu}$ of §2.2's bridge, the field-theoretic carrier of four-momentum, is the source of spacetime curvature through **Einstein's field equations** $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$, with $G_{\mu\nu}$ the Einstein tensor measuring curvature. The flat-space conservation law $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ — the local form of [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|four-momentum conservation]] — upgrades to the covariant statement $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, where $\nabla$ is the same $\Gamma$-corrected derivative; and the fact that the Einstein tensor automatically satisfies $\nabla_\mu G^{\mu\nu} = 0$ (the contracted Bianchi identity) is exactly what makes the field equations consistent with energy–momentum conservation. Special relativity is thus the tangent-space approximation to general relativity: every freely-falling observer carries a local Minkowski frame, and the whole of this topic is what they see in it.

8. **Particle physics and collider kinematics.** [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|Conservation of four-momentum]] is not merely one tool among many in particle physics — it is the computational backbone of the entire subject, the input to every cross-section calculation, every decay-rate prediction, every event reconstruction at a detector. The reason it is so workable is that the kinematics of any $2\to 2$ process is captured by just three Lorentz-invariant numbers, the **Mandelstam variables** $s$, $t$, $u$, formed as the squared four-momentum sums and differences of the external legs. The first, $s = (P_1 + P_2)^2$, is the square of the total [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momentum]], hence the invariant mass squared of the whole system — and since in the centre-of-momentum frame the total four-momentum is purely temporal, $s$ equals the centre-of-mass energy squared, $s = E_{\text{cm}}^2$, the single most important number characterising a collision (the "$\sqrt{s} = 13\text{ TeV}$" of the LHC is exactly $\sqrt{s}$). The other two, $t = (P_1 - P_3)^2$ and $u = (P_1 - P_4)^2$, are momentum transfers and encode scattering angle. They are not independent: conservation of four-momentum forces the constraint $s + t + u = \sum_i m_i^2$, so two of them suffice to fix the kinematics completely. Their deeper role is **crossing symmetry**: a single analytic scattering amplitude $\mathcal{M}(s,t,u)$, regarded as a function of these invariants, describes *several* physical processes at once — the $s$-channel reaction $1+2\to 3+4$, the $t$-channel reaction $1+\bar3\to\bar2+4$, and the $u$-channel — each obtained by continuing the same function into a different region of the $(s,t,u)$ plane. The reaction in which an incoming particle is "crossed" to an outgoing antiparticle is described by the *same* amplitude evaluated elsewhere, so computing one channel gives the others for free. This is why the relativistic kinematics of this topic is the indispensable preliminary to a course in particle physics: it supplies the invariant language — $s$, $t$, $u$, and the conservation law that relates them — in which all of scattering theory is written.

9. **Quantum field theory — from particles to fields.** The mass-shell condition $P\cdot P = m^2$, which in §2.2 is just the geometric statement that the [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momentum]] has Minkowski length the rest mass, is the seed from which all of relativistic quantum theory grows. The bridge is the substitution $P_\mu \to i\hbar\partial_\mu$ — the relativistic, covariant completion of the Schrödinger prescription $E\to i\hbar\partial_t$ and $\mathbf{p}\to -i\hbar\nabla$, now packaged into a single four-vector of operators. Feeding this operator into the algebraic constraint $P\cdot P = m^2$ converts it into a differential equation, $(\Box + m^2)\phi = 0$, the **Klein–Gordon equation**, with $\Box = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu$ the d'Alembertian wave operator; its plane-wave solutions $e^{-iK\cdot X}$ are exactly the wave four-vector of §2.1, with the dispersion relation $K\cdot K = m^2$. But the Klein–Gordon equation is second order in time, which spoils the conserved positive probability density that single-particle quantum mechanics requires, and **Dirac** therefore looked for a *first*-order-in-time equation whose square would reproduce Klein–Gordon. The only way to factorise the second-order operator into a first-order one forces the wavefunction to carry several components and the coefficients to be matrices satisfying a Clifford algebra — and out of this come, with no extra input, the **Dirac equation**, spin-$\tfrac12$, and a doubling of the solution space that Dirac was forced to interpret as **antimatter**. The structural reason a particle carries exactly the two labels mass and spin is the **Wigner classification**: the irreducible unitary representations of the **Poincaré group** — boosts, rotations and spacetime translations together, the full symmetry group of Minkowski space — are labelled by precisely two invariants, the Casimir $P\cdot P = m^2$ and a spin (or, for massless particles, helicity) quantum number. A particle simply *is* an irreducible representation of the Poincaré group, and that is the deep reason it carries those and only those two labels. Finally, $E = mc^2$ makes particle number unstable: concentrate enough energy in a small region and pair creation converts that energy into new massive particles, so the number of particles is not conserved. A wavefunction with a fixed number of arguments cannot describe a process that changes the particle count, and the repair is to promote the wavefunction itself to an operator-valued **quantum field** whose excitations are the particles — single-particle relativistic quantum mechanics must give way to **quantum field theory**, in which creation and annihilation are built in from the start.

10. **General relativity — from free particles to geodesics.** A free relativistic particle obeys $dU^\mu/d\tau = 0$: its [[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration|four-velocity]] is constant, so its worldline is a straight line in flat Minkowski space — Newton's first law, rewritten covariantly. **General relativity** keeps the principle that a free-falling particle moves "as straight as possible" but transfers it to a *curved* spacetime, where "straight" must be defined intrinsically rather than by reference to a background grid. The flat-space proper-time derivative $d/d\tau$ then acquires correction terms encoding how the coordinate basis vectors themselves twist from point to point, and the equation of motion becomes the **geodesic equation** $dU^\mu/d\tau + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}U^\nu U^\rho = 0$, where the **Christoffel symbols** $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}$ are the components of the connection, built from first derivatives of the metric $g_{\mu\nu}$. In flat space and Cartesian coordinates every $\Gamma$ vanishes and the geodesic equation collapses back to $dU^\mu/d\tau = 0$; all of the curvature lives in the $\Gamma$ term. What curves spacetime is matter: the **energy–momentum tensor** $T^{\mu\nu}$ — the field-theoretic carrier of four-momentum from the §2.2 bridge — is the source of curvature through **Einstein's field equations** $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$, with the **Einstein tensor** $G_{\mu\nu}$ measuring the curvature of the geometry. The flat-space conservation law $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ — the local form of [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|four-momentum conservation]] — upgrades to the covariant $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, where $\nabla$ is the same $\Gamma$-corrected derivative; and the identity $\nabla_\mu G^{\mu\nu} = 0$ (the contracted Bianchi identity) is precisely what makes the field equations consistent with energy–momentum conservation. Special relativity is in this sense the tangent-space approximation to general relativity: every freely-falling observer carries a local Minkowski frame, and the whole of this topic is the physics they read off in it.

11. **Particle physics and collider kinematics — the Mandelstam invariants.** [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|Conservation of four-momentum]] is the computational backbone of every collider calculation, and the structure that makes it tractable is that a two-body process $1+2\to 3+4$ is described, up to overall rotations, by just three Lorentz-invariant numbers — the **Mandelstam variables** $s = (P_1+P_2)^2$, $t = (P_1-P_3)^2$, $u = (P_1-P_4)^2$. Each is the Minkowski square of a sum or difference of external [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momenta]], hence a scalar every observer agrees on. The variable $s$ is the square of the *total* four-momentum, so it is the invariant mass squared of the whole system; evaluated in the centre-of-momentum frame, where the total four-momentum is purely temporal, it equals the centre-of-mass energy squared, $s = E_{\text{cm}}^2$ — the $\sqrt s$ that headlines a collider. The variables $t$ and $u$ are momentum transfers and carry the scattering-angle information. They are not independent: combining the four mass-shell conditions $P_i\cdot P_i = m_i^2$ with conservation $P_1+P_2 = P_3+P_4$ forces the constraint $s+t+u = \sum_i m_i^2$, so two of the three fix the kinematics completely. Their deeper role is **crossing symmetry**: a single analytic amplitude $\mathcal{M}(s,t,u)$ describes several physical processes at once — the $s$-channel reaction $1+2\to 3+4$ and the $t$- and $u$-channel reactions obtained by continuing the same function into a different region of the $(s,t,u)$ plane, with an incoming particle "crossed" to an outgoing antiparticle. Computing one channel therefore delivers the others for free. This invariant language — $s$, $t$, $u$, and the conservation law relating them — is the one in which all of scattering theory is written; see [[Ex - Mandelstam variables for two-body scattering]].

# Insights

**The unifying frame: relativistic mechanics is Newtonian mechanics with proper time as the clock.** Every quantity in this topic is obtained from a Newtonian quantity by one substitution — replace differentiation with respect to coordinate time $t$ by differentiation with respect to proper time $\tau$. Velocity $d\mathbf{x}/dt$ becomes four-velocity $dX^\mu/d\tau$; acceleration becomes four-acceleration; Newton's law $\mathbf{f} = d\mathbf{p}/dt$ becomes $F^\mu = dP^\mu/d\tau$. The reason this single substitution does all the work is that $\tau$ is a Lorentz *scalar* while $t$ is a frame-dependent coordinate: differentiating a four-vector with respect to a scalar yields a four-vector, so every relativised quantity is automatically a genuine four-vector and every relativised law is automatically a tensor equation, true in one frame if true in all. Once this is seen, the topic has no surprises — it is the disciplined transfer of Newtonian mechanics into Minkowski space, with proper time as the bridge. The factor $\gamma = dt/d\tau$ that appears everywhere is just the exchange rate between the two clocks.

**The true name of mass is "the length of the four-momentum vector".** The textbook definition of rest mass — the mass measured in the rest frame — is correct but operationally clumsy, because it forces you into a particular frame. The useful characterisation is geometric: the four-momentum $P^\mu$ is a four-vector, and its Minkowski length is the rest mass, $P\cdot P = m^2$. This reframing explains at once every counterintuitive fact about mass. Mass is not additive because the length of a sum of vectors is not the sum of their lengths — $|P_1 + P_2| \neq |P_1| + |P_2|$, just as for ordinary vectors. The invariant mass of a system of photons can be nonzero even though each photon has zero length, because two null vectors can sum to a timelike one. A bound system is lighter than its parts because the binding energy is a negative contribution to the length of the total four-momentum. And mass is frame-independent for the same reason a vector's length is basis-independent. Stop thinking of mass as "amount of stuff" and start thinking of it as "the Minkowski norm of the energy–momentum vector", and the entire chapter's worth of paradoxes evaporates.

**Conservation of four-momentum is a statement about an invariant vector sum, and that is why it can do so much.** Newtonian physics had two separate conservation laws — mass and momentum (and, for elastic processes, energy) — and they were logically independent. Relativity unifies them into one law, $\sum P^\mu = \text{const}$, and the unification is not cosmetic. Because the conserved object is a *four-vector*, the law is the same equation in every inertial frame, so you may verify it or exploit it in whichever frame is convenient — and "go to the convenient frame" is therefore not a trick but a theorem. Because the conserved object is a *sum of four-vectors*, you may take its Minkowski square and obtain a conserved *scalar*, the invariant mass of the whole system, and squaring is what eliminates the particles you do not care about. Almost every problem in §2.2 and §2.3 is solved by these two consequences alone: write the conserved vector, then either change to the frame that trivialises it or square it to get the invariant. The Newtonian laws, by contrast, were frame-dependent in their natural statement and had no squaring trick. The whole computational power of relativistic kinematics flows from the conserved quantity being a genuine four-vector rather than a frame-dependent triple of numbers.

**Massless particles are the degenerate limit, and the degeneracy is a feature.** It is tempting to regard the photon as a problem case — no rest frame, no proper time, no four-velocity, the whole proper-time machinery inapplicable. But the photon is better thought of as the *boundary* of the theory, the $m\to 0$ limit of a massive particle, and the limit is clean precisely because the four-momentum survives it. A massive particle has $P\cdot P = m^2 > 0$ (timelike) and a particle of imaginary mass would have $P\cdot P < 0$ (spacelike, a tachyon — and tachyons are excluded because they would travel faster than light); the photon sits exactly on the dividing null cone, $P\cdot P = 0$. Everything that needed the four-velocity is rephrased in terms of the four-momentum, which the photon has; the four-momentum's magnitude, which the proper-time construction would have fixed, is instead supplied by quantum mechanics through $E = \hbar\omega$. The lesson is general: when a construction degenerates, find the object that survives the degeneration and re-express everything through it. For the photon, that object is the null four-momentum, and once you have it the photon participates in every conservation law on equal terms with massive matter.
