# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we set $c = 1$**, and we use the **mostly-minus** signature $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, so a timelike four-vector has $X \cdot X > 0$. Gourgoulhon, whose Chapter 8 is the backbone of this chapter, uses the opposite mostly-plus signature $\mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$ and writes the Poincaré group as $\mathrm{IO}(3,1)$ and the restricted Lorentz group as $\mathrm{SO}_o(3,1)$; we translate his formulas by flipping the overall sign of the metric and write the Poincaré group as $\mathrm{ISO}(1,3)$ and the restricted Lorentz group as $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$, matching the rest of this series. Where a formula is more recognisable with $c$ restored, it is given both ways; to restore $c$, replace $x^0 = t$ by $ct$ and insert factors of $c$ by dimensional analysis.

- $\mathscr{E}$, $\mathbb{M}$ — Minkowski spacetime, the four-dimensional real affine space with metric $\eta$; its points are **events**
- $E$ — the vector space of displacements underlying $\mathscr{E}$ (the "tangent space", here global because $\mathscr{E}$ is flat)
- $\mathcal{O}$, $\mathcal{O}'$ — observers; $\mathscr{L}$, $\mathscr{L}'$ — their worldlines
- $U$, $U'$ — four-velocities (Gourgoulhon's $\vec{u}$); a unit future-timelike vector, $U \cdot U = 1$
- $a$ — four-acceleration $a = \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}\tau$; $\omega$ — four-rotation of a local frame (both orthogonal to $U$)
- $(e_0, e_1, e_2, e_3)$ — the orthonormal local frame (tetrad) carried by an observer, with $e_0 = U$
- $\tau$ or $t$ — proper time along a worldline; $(ct, x^1, x^2, x^3)$ — inertial coordinates of an observer
- $\mathscr{E}_u(t)$ — the local rest space of the observer at proper time $t$ (its $U^\perp$ hyperplane)
- $\Sigma_u(t)$ — the simultaneity hypersurface at proper time $t$ (Einstein–Poincaré simultaneous events)
- $\Lambda$, $\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta$ — a Lorentz transformation and its matrix; restricted means $\Lambda \in \mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$
- $f = (\boldsymbol{v}, \Lambda)$ — a Poincaré transformation: translation vector $\boldsymbol{v} \in E$ and Lorentz part $\Lambda$
- $T$ — a translation; $\Lambda_O$ — a Lorentz transformation pointed at the event $O$ (fixing $O$)
- $\mathrm{ISO}(1,3)$, $\mathrm{IO}(1,3)$ — the **Poincaré group** (inhomogeneous Lorentz group); $\mathrm{ISO}^+(1,3)$ its restricted subgroup
- $\rtimes$ — semidirect product; $\mathbb{R}^4 \rtimes \mathrm{O}(1,3)$ is the Poincaré group
- $\mathfrak{iso}(1,3)$ — the Poincaré Lie algebra, $E \times \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$, dimension $10$
- $P_\alpha$, $K_i$, $J_i$ — generators of the Poincaré algebra: translations, boosts, rotations ($\alpha = 0\ldots3$, $i = 1,2,3$)
- $P^\mu$ — the four-momentum operator (the translation generator promoted to an observable); $J^{\mu\nu}$ — the angular-momentum tensor
- $W^\mu$ — the **Pauli–Lubanski** four-vector, $W^\mu = -\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}J_{\nu\rho}P_\sigma$
- $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ — the Levi-Civita symbol, totally antisymmetric, $\varepsilon^{0123} = +1$
- $m$, $s$ — the rest mass and spin of a representation; the two Casimir labels
- $\delta_{ij}$ — Kronecker delta; $\epsilon_{ijk}$ — three-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol; Einstein summation throughout (repeated index once up once down)

# Motivation

Here is the whole chapter in one sentence: the inertial observers are the unaccelerated ones, and the group that relates them — together with the freedom to move the origin of space and time — is the Poincaré group, whose irreducible representations are the elementary particles. Everything below is an unpacking of that sentence.

We have spent five chapters (Special Relativity VI through XI) developing the apparatus of *general* observers: an observer is any future-timelike unit worldline carrying an orthonormal frame, and such an observer may accelerate, rotate, and carry a local rest space that twists from instant to instant. This was the right level of generality, and it is the level at which the equivalence principle later attaches general relativity. But it is more generality than the founders of special relativity needed, and more than most of physics uses day to day. Special relativity was forged entirely in terms of the simplest observers — the inertial ones — and §12.1 returns to them, now as a distinguished *subclass* of the general observers rather than as a primitive notion. An [[Def - Inertial Observer|inertial observer]] is one whose carried frame is constant along the worldline, $\mathrm{d}e_\alpha/\mathrm{d}\tau = 0$, which is equivalent to demanding that both the four-acceleration and the four-rotation vanish: $a = 0$ and $\omega = 0$. The first consequence is geometric and clean — the worldline is a straight line of Minkowski space — and the second is the one that makes the inertial observer special among all observers: its local rest space, which for a general observer is only a *local* object that bends and may even self-intersect, becomes **global**. The simultaneity hyperplanes are parallel, never meet, and slice the whole of spacetime into a single consistent family of "nows". This is what makes a global inertial coordinate system possible, and it is the precise sense in which an inertial observer can pretend, à la Newton, that there is one time coordinate for all of space.

Section §12.2 asks the natural follow-up: how are two inertial observers related, and what is the group of all such relations? Two inertial observers assign coordinates $(x^\alpha)$ and $(x'^\alpha)$ to each event, and the relation between them is

$$x'^\alpha = \Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta\, x^\beta + x_0'^\alpha,$$

a Lorentz rotation $\Lambda$ composed with a constant shift $x_0'$. The shift is the new ingredient. The Lorentz group $\mathrm{O}(1,3)$ moves the *directions* of spacetime; allowing also a translation of the *origin* of spacetime enlarges it to the **Poincaré group** $\mathrm{ISO}(1,3)$, the full symmetry group of Minkowski space as an affine space. The structural surprise — and the central algebraic content of the chapter — is that the Poincaré group is *not* simply the product of the Lorentz group and the translation group. When you compose two Poincaré transformations, the Lorentz part of the first acts on the translation part of the second:

$$(\boldsymbol{v}_1, \Lambda_1)\,(\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_2) = (\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2,\; \Lambda_1\Lambda_2).$$

That twist in the composition law — $\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2$ rather than $\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \boldsymbol{v}_2$ — is the signature of a **semidirect product**, $\mathrm{ISO}(1,3) = \mathbb{R}^4 \rtimes \mathrm{O}(1,3)$. It makes the translations a *normal* subgroup acted on by the Lorentz group, and it makes the Poincaré group non-simple where the Lorentz group is simple. The chapter dwells on this because the semidirect structure is exactly what the representation theory of §12.3 exploits.

Section §12.3 is the climax, and it goes beyond Gourgoulhon's chapter into the physics that the Poincaré group exists to support. Eugene Wigner asked the question that turns this group from a piece of geometry into the organising principle of all of particle physics: *what are the irreducible unitary representations of the Poincaré group?* The answer is the **Wigner classification**, and it is one of the deepest results in mathematical physics. The Poincaré group has exactly two **Casimir invariants** — operators commuting with everything in the group, hence taking a single scalar value on each irreducible representation. They are $P^2 = P_\mu P^\mu$, whose value is the squared **mass** $m^2$, and $W^2 = W_\mu W^\mu$ built from the **Pauli–Lubanski** vector, whose value is $-m^2 s(s+1)$ and so encodes the **spin** $s$. Every irreducible unitary representation is labelled by a pair $(m, s)$, and the punchline, due to Wigner, is the definition of what a particle *is*: an elementary particle is an irreducible unitary representation of the Poincaré group. Mass and spin are not properties a particle "has"; they are the two labels that *name* the representation, the two Casimir eigenvalues. This is the sense in which the title of the chapter is not a piece of mathematics bolted onto physics — the group *is* the physics.

The reader is assumed to have worked through the general-observer machinery of [[Special Relativity VI — Observers, Local Rest Spaces and Local Frames]] (four-velocity, four-acceleration, four-rotation, local rest space), the Lorentz group and its classification ([[Special Relativity IX — The Lorentz Group, Structure and Classification]]), and crucially the Lie-theoretic treatment of the Lorentz group ([[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]]: the algebra $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$, its generators $K_i$ and $J_i$, the brackets $[J,J] = J$, $[J,K] = K$, $[K,K] = -J$). The pure Lie-theory background — what a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] and a [[Def - Lie Algebra|Lie algebra]] are, the exponential map, the bracket-as-commutator on a matrix algebra — is assumed from [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map]]. The genuinely new mathematics is small: the semidirect product, which is one twisted composition law, and the Casimir operators, which are two specific quadratic combinations of generators. The conceptual reach, by contrast, is enormous — this is the chapter where special relativity becomes the kinematic skeleton of quantum field theory.

# Concept Map

## §12.1 Inertial Observers

- **[[Def - Inertial Observer]]**
	- An inertial observer is an [[Def - Observer and Local Rest Space|observer]] whose orthonormal local frame is constant along its worldline, $\mathrm{d}e_\alpha/\mathrm{d}\tau = 0$, equivalently one with vanishing four-acceleration *and* vanishing four-rotation: $a(\tau) = 0$ and $\omega(\tau) = 0$ for all $\tau$. Vanishing acceleration alone forces the worldline to be a straight line of Minkowski space, $x^\alpha(\tau) = U^\alpha \tau + x_0^\alpha$ (with $c$: $x^\alpha = c\,U^\alpha t + x_0^\alpha$); vanishing rotation in addition forces the carried frame not to spin. The converse to "straight worldline" is therefore false: a straight worldline is only an unaccelerated observer, and may still carry a rotating frame. The four-velocity of an inertial observer is a single constant vector of $E$, the same at every event of the worldline, which is the property that makes the rest space global.

- **[[Thm - Globality of the Local Rest Space for Inertial Observers]]**
	- For an inertial observer the simultaneity hypersurface and the local rest space coincide, $\Sigma_u(t) = \mathscr{E}_u(t)$, and these hyperplanes are mutually parallel and never intersect. The coincidence follows because the gap between "simultaneous to $O(t)$" and "orthogonal to $U$ at $O(t)$" is induced by the curvature of the worldline, which vanishes for a straight line; the parallelism follows because $U$ is constant, so all the $U^\perp$ hyperplanes are translates of one another. The consequence is decisive: there is no obstruction to labelling *every* event of spacetime by the coordinates $(ct, x^i)$ of the inertial observer, so the rest space and frame are global objects and the qualifier "local" may be dropped. This is the precise statement that an inertial observer's notion of "now" extends consistently across all of space.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Geodesics and the Equivalence Principle *(from General Relativity)*
> The inertial worldline — straight, zero four-acceleration — is the flat-spacetime instance of a **geodesic**, the curve of zero covariant acceleration $\nabla_U U = 0$. In a curved spacetime the geodesics are the worldlines of freely-falling bodies, and the **equivalence principle** asserts that a freely-falling observer is *locally* inertial: the frame they carry is unaccelerated and non-rotating to first order, so special relativity holds exactly along their worldline. The globality that this section establishes is exactly what *fails* in the presence of gravity — the locally inertial frames at different events no longer fit into a single global coordinate system, and that failure is curvature. See [[General Relativity I — Einstein's Equations and Schwarzschild]].

- **[[Ex - Inertial worldline is a straight line]]** (⭐)
	- Integrate the defining condition $\mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}\tau = 0$ twice to obtain $x^\alpha(\tau) = U^\alpha \tau + x_0^\alpha$, recognise it as the parametric equation of a straight line of $\mathscr{E}$, and identify the eight integration constants $(U^\alpha, x_0^\alpha)$ as the initial four-velocity and initial position.

- **[[Ex - A straight worldline need not be inertial]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Construct an observer whose worldline is a straight line but whose carried frame rotates — a constant four-velocity with a spatial triad that precesses in proper time — and verify it has $a = 0$ but $\omega \neq 0$, so it is not inertial; conclude that "straight worldline" captures only the vanishing of four-acceleration, not of four-rotation.

- **[[Ex - Parallel rest spaces of an inertial observer]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that the rest-space hyperplanes $\mathscr{E}_u(t_1)$ and $\mathscr{E}_u(t_2)$ of an inertial observer are parallel and disjoint for $t_1 \neq t_2$, using the constancy of $U$, and contrast with an accelerated observer whose rest spaces intersect, producing the coordinate breakdown (Rindler horizon) studied later.

## §12.2 The Poincaré Group

- **[[Def - The Poincaré Group]]**
	- The Poincaré group $\mathrm{ISO}(1,3)$ is the group of all affine transformations $f : \mathscr{E} \to \mathscr{E}$ of Minkowski spacetime whose linear part is a [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz transformation]]: $\overrightarrow{f(M)f(N)} = \Lambda(\overrightarrow{MN})$ for some $\Lambda \in \mathrm{O}(1,3)$. Each such $f$ decomposes uniquely as a Lorentz transformation pointed at a chosen origin followed by a translation, written $f = (\boldsymbol{v}, \Lambda)$, and the composition law is $(\boldsymbol{v}_1, \Lambda_1)(\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_2) = (\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2,\, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$. This twist makes the group the **semidirect product** $\mathbb{R}^4 \rtimes \mathrm{O}(1,3)$, ten-dimensional, in which the translations form a normal abelian subgroup and the Lorentz group acts on them. It is the full isometry group of $(\mathscr{E}, \eta)$ and the group governing changes of inertial observer.

- **[[Thm - The Poincaré Group as a Lie Group]]**
	- The Poincaré group is a Lie group of dimension $10 = 4 + 6$, with Lie algebra $\mathfrak{iso}(1,3) = E \times \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ spanned by the ten generators $P_\alpha$ (translations), $K_i$ (boosts), $J_i$ (rotations). The bracket cannot be borrowed from a matrix commutator — the algebra is not a priori a subalgebra of endomorphisms — so it is defined intrinsically by $[(\boldsymbol{v}_1, L_1), (\boldsymbol{v}_2, L_2)] = (L_1\boldsymbol{v}_2 - L_2\boldsymbol{v}_1,\, [L_1, L_2])$, which one shows is bilinear, antisymmetric, and satisfies the Jacobi identity. Computing it on the generators yields the structure constants: $[P_\alpha, P_\beta] = 0$ (translations commute), $[K_i, P_0] = P_i$ and $[K_i, P_j] = \delta_{ij}P_0$ (boosts mix time- and space-translations), $[J_i, P_0] = 0$ and $[J_i, P_j] = \epsilon_{ijk}P_k$ (rotations rotate the spatial translations).

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Poincaré Algebra as the Skeleton of Quantum Field Theory *(from QFT)*
> Promoting the generators to Hermitian operators on a Hilbert space turns $P_\alpha$ into the **energy–momentum** operator $P^\mu = (H, \boldsymbol{P})$, $J_i$ into **angular momentum**, and $K_i$ into the **boost** generators; the structure constants become the commutation relations of relativistic quantum mechanics. The bracket $[J_i, P_j] = \epsilon_{ijk}P_k$ is the statement that momentum is a vector under rotations; $[K_i, P_0] = P_i$ is the statement that energy and momentum mix under boosts. Every relativistic field theory is built so that its fields carry a representation of exactly this algebra — the **Poincaré algebra** is the kinematic backbone onto which all dynamics is hung.

- **[[Ex - The semidirect product group law of the Poincaré group]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compose two Poincaré transformations $f_1 = (\boldsymbol{v}_1, \Lambda_1)$ and $f_2 = (\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_2)$ by applying $\overrightarrow{Of(M)} = \Lambda(\overrightarrow{OM}) + \boldsymbol{v}$ twice, and derive the law $(\boldsymbol{v}_1, \Lambda_1)(\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_2) = (\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2,\, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$, identifying precisely where the factor $\Lambda_1$ enters.

- **[[Ex - Translations form a normal abelian subgroup]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Verify the translations $(\boldsymbol{v}, \mathrm{Id})$ form a subgroup isomorphic to $(\mathbb{R}^4, +)$, that it is abelian, and that it is *normal* by computing the conjugate $(\boldsymbol{w}, \Lambda)(\boldsymbol{v}, \mathrm{Id})(\boldsymbol{w}, \Lambda)^{-1} = (\Lambda\boldsymbol{v}, \mathrm{Id})$ — a translation again — and conclude the Poincaré group is not simple.

- **[[Ex - Active versus passive Poincaré transformations]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Distinguish the *passive* transformation $x'^\alpha = \Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta x^\beta + x_0'^\alpha$ relating the coordinates of one event in two inertial frames from the *active* transformation $\overrightarrow{Of(M)} = \Lambda(\overrightarrow{OM}) + \boldsymbol{v}$ moving one event to another within a single frame, show the two have identical algebraic form but opposite physical meaning, and exhibit the inverse-matrix relationship between them.

- **[[Ex - The Poincaré group is not a direct product]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Show that if the Poincaré group were the direct product $\mathbb{R}^4 \times \mathrm{O}(1,3)$ the composition would read $(\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$; exhibit two boosts whose product translates a point to demonstrate $\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2 \neq \boldsymbol{v}_1 + \boldsymbol{v}_2$, and explain why the semidirect twist is forced by the requirement that translating-then-boosting differ from boosting-then-translating.

## §12.3 Casimirs, Mass and Spin

- **[[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group]]**
	- The two Casimir invariants of the Poincaré group are $P^2 = P_\mu P^\mu$ and $W^2 = W_\mu W^\mu$, where $W^\mu = -\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}J_{\nu\rho}P_\sigma$ is the **Pauli–Lubanski** four-vector built from the angular-momentum tensor $J^{\mu\nu}$ and the four-momentum $P^\mu$. They are the only independent operators that commute with every Poincaré generator, so each takes a single scalar value on an irreducible representation: $P^2 = m^2$ fixes the **rest mass** and, for $m > 0$, $W^2 = -m^2 s(s+1)$ fixes the **spin** $s \in \{0, \tfrac{1}{2}, 1, \ldots\}$. The Pauli–Lubanski vector is always orthogonal to the momentum, $W \cdot P = 0$, which is why for a massive particle (timelike $P$) it is spacelike and reduces in the rest frame to the spin three-vector. These two numbers $(m, s)$ are the complete label of an elementary particle.

> [!tip] Unlocked: An Elementary Particle Is a Representation of the Poincaré Group *(from QFT)*
> This is the conceptual summit of the whole special-relativity series, and it deserves to be stated without hedging. Eugene Wigner proved in 1939 that the irreducible unitary representations of the Poincaré group are classified by the two Casimir eigenvalues — mass and spin — together with the choice of **little group**, the subgroup of Lorentz transformations fixing a standard momentum. For a massive particle the little group is $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, the rotations of the rest frame, whose finite-dimensional representations are labelled by spin $s$ with dimension $2s+1$; this is *why* a spin-$s$ particle has $2s+1$ polarisation states. For a massless particle the momentum is null, the little group is $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ — the Euclidean group of the plane — and demanding finite-dimensionality collapses the continuous parameter to a single discrete **helicity** $\pm s$; this is *why* the photon ($s=1$) has only two polarisations, $h = \pm 1$, not three, and the graviton ($s=2$) has $h = \pm 2$.
>
> The definition Wigner extracted is the one modern physics actually uses: **an elementary particle is an irreducible unitary representation of the Poincaré group.** A "particle" is not a little ball; it is a way the symmetries of spacetime can act on a quantum state space without that space splitting into independent pieces. Mass and spin are then not contingent attributes — they are the names of the representation, the two Casimir labels, as intrinsic to the particle as the dimension is to a vector space. This reframes the entire ontology of quantum field theory. When a field theorist writes down a Klein–Gordon field they are realising the $(m, 0)$ representation; a Dirac field realises $(m, \tfrac{1}{2})$; the electromagnetic field realises $(0, 1)$. The free-field equations — Klein–Gordon, Dirac, Maxwell, Proca, Rarita–Schwinger — are nothing but the projection conditions that pick out a single irreducible $(m,s)$ from the reducible space of all fields. The classification also draws the boundary of the possible: there are exotic representations (the "continuous-spin" or infinite-spin representations of the massless ISO(2) little group, and the tachyonic spacelike-$P$ representations) that are excluded from physics precisely because they would violate locality or positivity of energy, and seeing *why* they are excluded is seeing what makes a sensible particle. The Coleman–Mandula theorem later shows that the largest symmetry a non-trivial relativistic theory can have is the Poincaré group times internal symmetries — and the only loophole, supersymmetry, is the **super-Poincaré** group whose representations (supermultiplets) bundle particles of different spin together. All of this is downstream of the two Casimirs defined on this page.

- **[[Ex - Computing the Pauli-Lubanski vector]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Evaluate $W^\mu = -\tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}J_{\nu\rho}P_\sigma$ in the rest frame $P = (m, 0, 0, 0)$ of a massive particle, show it reduces to $W^0 = 0$ and $W^i = m J^i$ with $J^i = \tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon^{ijk}J_{jk}$ the spin three-vector, confirm $W \cdot P = 0$ in general, and hence derive $W^2 = -m^2 s(s+1)$.

- **[[Ex - The two Casimir invariants commute with the Poincaré generators]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Prove $[P^2, P_\mu] = [P^2, J_{\mu\nu}] = 0$ directly from the algebra, then show $W^\mu$ transforms as a four-vector ($[J_{\mu\nu}, W_\rho]$ is the vector rule) and commutes with $P_\mu$, so $W^2$ is a Lorentz scalar built from Poincaré-covariant pieces and hence Casimir; conclude these are the two labels of an irreducible representation.

- **[[Ex - Classifying massive versus massless representations]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For a massive momentum $P = (m, \mathbf{0})$ identify the little group as $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ and the representations as spin-$s$ multiplets of dimension $2s+1$; for a massless momentum $P = (E, 0, 0, E)$ identify the little group as $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$, show the two "translation" generators must act trivially on physical states (else continuous spin), and conclude the label is a single discrete helicity $h = \pm s$.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The problems of this chapter pursue a recognisable handful of goals. The most basic is to **characterise an observer or worldline** — to decide whether a given motion is inertial, by checking the two conditions $a = 0$ and $\omega = 0$, and to extract the consequences (straightness of the worldline, parallelism of the rest spaces, globality of the coordinates). This is a verification target: one is handed a worldline or a frame and asked which of the defining properties hold. A second, more algebraic target is to **establish a group-structural fact** — that the Poincaré transformations close under composition, that the translations are normal, that the group is a semidirect rather than a direct product, that it is not simple, that it has dimension ten. These are the proofs that make the Poincaré group a genuine group and a genuine Lie group, and they are won by manipulating the composition law $(\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$. A third target is to **compute a structure constant or a commutator**, reading off a bracket of generators from the abstract definition or from the matrix realisation — the bridge between the geometry of the group and the algebra of physics. A fourth, the deepest, is to **classify a representation**: given a value of the mass and spin, or given a standard momentum, to identify the little group and the multiplet structure, and conversely to recognise a physical particle as a specific irreducible representation. A fifth target, threading the others, is to **distinguish the active from the passive viewpoint** — to take a transformation that has been written down and decide whether it moves events within a frame or relabels one event across frames, since the same formula serves both and confusing them is the commonest error. Across all five, the unifying remark is that this chapter is where special relativity stops being about specific effects (time dilation, length contraction) and becomes about the *group* that produces them; the targets are correspondingly structural rather than numerical.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A worldline or frame is specified** — most usefully by its four-velocity and four-acceleration — and one immediately tests $a = 0$, $\omega = 0$ to decide inertiality, then reaches for the integrated straight-line form. **A constant four-velocity is given**, which is the richest geometric source: it makes the rest-space hyperplanes parallel and unlocks global inertial coordinates in one step. **The composition law of the Poincaré group is given** — the semidirect $(\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$ — and essentially every group-theoretic target is reached by substituting into it: normality of the translations is one conjugation, the failure of the direct-product structure is one comparison of $\Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2$ with $\boldsymbol{v}_2$. **The Lie algebra brackets are given** — the $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ relations from [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]] together with the action of generators on basis vectors — and structure-constant targets are reached by the intrinsic bracket formula $[(\boldsymbol{v}_1, L_1), (\boldsymbol{v}_2, L_2)] = (L_1\boldsymbol{v}_2 - L_2\boldsymbol{v}_1, [L_1, L_2])$. **A standard momentum is given** — timelike $P = (m, \mathbf{0})$ for the massive case, null $P = (E, 0, 0, E)$ for the massless case — and the representation-theoretic target is reached by identifying the subgroup of Lorentz transformations that fixes it, the little group, and decomposing. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a four-velocity routes through its constancy to global coordinates; the composition law routes through one substitution to a structural fact; the brackets route through the intrinsic formula to a structure constant; a standard momentum routes through its stabiliser to a particle classification. The [[Special Relativity XII — Inertial Observers and the Poincaré Group#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves almost every problem in the chapter is assembled from. The chapter is more algebraic than its predecessors, so several operations are group- and algebra-theoretic rather than kinematic; each states its trigger so the reader can scan the list when stuck.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Test inertiality by the two conditions $a = 0$ and $\omega = 0$.** An observer is inertial exactly when its four-acceleration *and* its four-rotation vanish along the whole worldline; see [[Def - Inertial Observer]]. *Trigger:* a worldline or a carried frame is handed to you and you must decide whether it is inertial. *Pattern:* compute $a = \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}\tau$ first — if it is nonzero the observer accelerates and is not inertial; if it vanishes, the worldline is straight, but you must still check that the spatial triad does not precess ($\omega = 0$) before concluding inertiality. Forgetting the second check is the trap that [[Ex - A straight worldline need not be inertial]] is built to expose.

2. **Integrate $\mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}\tau = 0$ to a straight worldline.** When the four-acceleration vanishes, $U$ is constant and the worldline integrates to $x^\alpha(\tau) = U^\alpha\tau + x_0^\alpha$; see [[Ex - Inertial worldline is a straight line]]. *Trigger:* you have established $a = 0$ and want the explicit worldline. *Pattern:* two integrations in proper time, eight constants of integration $(U^\alpha, x_0^\alpha)$ fixed by initial four-velocity and initial event.

3. **Use the constancy of $U$ to make the rest spaces parallel and the coordinates global.** A constant four-velocity has all its $U^\perp$ hyperplanes mutually parallel, so they never intersect and slice spacetime consistently; see [[Thm - Globality of the Local Rest Space for Inertial Observers]]. *Trigger:* you must show an inertial observer's coordinates extend over all of spacetime, or that its "now"-slices are consistent. *Pattern:* parallel hyperplanes through a one-parameter family of points on a straight line tile the affine space, giving the global chart $(ct, x^i)$.

4. **Decompose a Poincaré transformation as $f = (\boldsymbol{v}, \Lambda) = T \circ \Lambda_O$.** Any Poincaré transformation splits uniquely, given a chosen origin $O$, into a Lorentz transformation pointed at $O$ followed by a translation of vector $\boldsymbol{v} = \overrightarrow{Of(O)}$; see [[Def - The Poincaré Group]]. *Trigger:* a Poincaré transformation appears and you want its data. *Pattern:* read $\Lambda$ off the linear part and $\boldsymbol{v}$ off the image of the chosen origin; the pair $(\boldsymbol{v}, \Lambda)$ is then the working coordinate for all group computations.

5. **Compose using the semidirect law $(\boldsymbol{v}_1, \Lambda_1)(\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_2) = (\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$.** This is the master computation of §12.2; see [[Ex - The semidirect product group law of the Poincaré group]]. *Trigger:* any product, inverse, or conjugate of Poincaré transformations. *Pattern:* the Lorentz parts multiply as usual; the translation part of the *first* factor receives the translation of the second factor *rotated by* $\Lambda_1$. The inverse is $(\boldsymbol{v}, \Lambda)^{-1} = (-\Lambda^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}, \Lambda^{-1})$.

6. **Prove normality by conjugation.** A subgroup $N$ is normal when $g n g^{-1} \in N$ for all $g \in G$, $n \in N$; for the translations, $(\boldsymbol{w}, \Lambda)(\boldsymbol{v}, \mathrm{Id})(\boldsymbol{w}, \Lambda)^{-1} = (\Lambda\boldsymbol{v}, \mathrm{Id})$; see [[Ex - Translations form a normal abelian subgroup]]. *Trigger:* you must show a subgroup is normal, or that the group is not simple. *Pattern:* conjugate a generic element of the subgroup and check the result lands back inside; for the Poincaré group the conjugate of a translation is the translation by the Lorentz-rotated vector, manifestly still a translation.

7. **Read structure constants from the intrinsic bracket.** The Poincaré bracket is $[(\boldsymbol{v}_1, L_1), (\boldsymbol{v}_2, L_2)] = (L_1\boldsymbol{v}_2 - L_2\boldsymbol{v}_1, [L_1, L_2])$, and the generators $P_\alpha, K_i, J_i$ are plugged into it; see [[Thm - The Poincaré Group as a Lie Group]]. *Trigger:* you want a commutator of generators. *Pattern:* identify each generator as a pair $(\boldsymbol{v}, L)$ — $P_\alpha = (e_\alpha, 0)$, $K_i = (0, K_i)$, $J_i = (0, J_i)$ — substitute, and use the known action of $K_i, J_i$ on the basis vectors $e_\alpha$ to evaluate $L_1\boldsymbol{v}_2 - L_2\boldsymbol{v}_1$.

8. **Identify a Casimir by checking it commutes with all generators.** An operator is Casimir when it commutes with translations *and* Lorentz generators; see [[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group]]. *Trigger:* you must show some quadratic combination of generators is a representation label. *Pattern:* $P^2$ is manifestly translation-invariant and a Lorentz scalar; for $W^2$ verify $W^\mu$ transforms as a four-vector and commutes with $P_\mu$, so its square is a Lorentz scalar that commutes with everything.

9. **Classify a representation via its little group.** Fix a standard momentum, find the subgroup of $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ fixing it, and decompose the representation over that little group; see [[Ex - Classifying massive versus massless representations]]. *Trigger:* you are asked for the spin content or polarisation count of a particle of given mass. *Pattern:* timelike standard momentum $\Rightarrow$ little group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ $\Rightarrow$ spin-$s$ multiplet of $2s+1$ states; null standard momentum $\Rightarrow$ little group $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ $\Rightarrow$ two helicity states $\pm s$.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Concluding "straight worldline, therefore inertial"
> It is tempting to identify the inertial observers with the straight worldlines, since a straight worldline certainly has zero four-acceleration. But inertiality requires *two* conditions, $a = 0$ and $\omega = 0$, and a straight worldline guarantees only the first. The counterexample is an observer moving uniformly along a straight line while its carried spatial triad rotates in proper time — a perfectly good observer with $a = 0$ but $\omega \neq 0$ (constructed explicitly in [[Ex - A straight worldline need not be inertial]]). The operation becomes legal exactly when one adds the hypothesis that the frame is Fermi–Walker transported with no spatial rotation, i.e. $\omega = 0$; only then does straightness of the worldline imply inertiality of the observer. Gourgoulhon flags this as Remark 8.1: the converse of "inertial $\Rightarrow$ straight" is false.

> [!warning] 2. Treating the Poincaré group as $\mathbb{R}^4 \times \mathrm{O}(1,3)$
> The decomposition $f = (\boldsymbol{v}, \Lambda)$ looks like a Cartesian product, and one is tempted to compose componentwise: $(\boldsymbol{v}_1, \Lambda_1)(\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_2) \overset{?}{=} (\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$. This is wrong, and the error is not cosmetic — it changes the group. The correct law has the Lorentz part of the first factor acting on the translation of the second, $(\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$, because applying $f_2$ then $f_1$ first translates by $\boldsymbol{v}_2$ and *then* rotates that translation by $\Lambda_1$. The concrete demonstration ([[Ex - The Poincaré group is not a direct product]]) takes $\Lambda_1$ a boost and $\boldsymbol{v}_2$ spacelike and finds $\Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2 \neq \boldsymbol{v}_2$. The componentwise law becomes legal only in the degenerate case where every $\Lambda_i = \mathrm{Id}$ (the pure-translation subgroup) or every $\boldsymbol{v}_i = 0$ (the pure-Lorentz subgroup); on the full group the product is irreducibly semidirect, written $\rtimes$, not $\times$.

> [!warning] 3. Confusing active and passive transformations
> The passive law $x'^\alpha = \Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta x^\beta + x_0'^\alpha$ and the active law $\overrightarrow{Of(M)} = \Lambda(\overrightarrow{OM}) + \boldsymbol{v}$ are algebraically identical, and it is tempting to treat them as the same statement. They are not: the passive transformation relabels *one event* in two inertial coordinate systems (nothing physically moves), while the active transformation maps *one event to a different event* within a single coordinate system (something physically moves). Conflating them inverts the role of $\Lambda$ — the matrix relating the *bases* is the inverse of the matrix relating the *coordinates*, $e_\alpha = \Lambda^\beta{}_\alpha e'_\beta$ versus $x'^\alpha = \Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta x^\beta$ — and a sign or transpose error in a boost almost always traces to this confusion (worked through in [[Ex - Active versus passive Poincaré transformations]]). The operation of "applying $\Lambda$" is unambiguous only once you have declared which viewpoint you are in; the repair is always to state, before writing a matrix, whether the event moves or the frame does.

> [!warning] 4. Forgetting $W \cdot P = 0$ when handling the spin Casimir
> The Pauli–Lubanski vector $W^\mu$ is not an independent four-vector floating free of the momentum: it is orthogonal to it, $W \cdot P = 0$, identically, by the antisymmetry of $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ against $P_\sigma P_\mu$. Forgetting this leads to nonsense such as treating $W$ as timelike for a massive particle (it is spacelike, having only spatial components in the rest frame) or expecting four independent spin components (there are three, the constraint $W\cdot P = 0$ removing one). The constraint becomes the *content* of the massless case: when $P$ is null, $W \cdot P = 0$ together with $W^2 = 0$ forces $W = h P$ for a scalar $h$, the helicity — the entire massless representation theory rides on this orthogonality. Any manipulation of $W$ that does not respect $W \cdot P = 0$ has gone wrong.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this chapter divide cleanly into three families — observer characterisation, group structure, and representation classification — and the first decision is always which family you are in, because the techniques do not overlap.

If the problem **concerns an observer or a worldline** — is this motion inertial, what are its rest spaces, do its coordinates cover spacetime — the strategy is the two-condition test followed by integration. Compute the four-acceleration $a = \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}\tau$ first; if it does not vanish the observer accelerates and the question reduces to the accelerated-observer machinery of a later chapter. If $a = 0$, integrate twice to the straight worldline $x^\alpha(\tau) = U^\alpha\tau + x_0^\alpha$, but do not yet declare the observer inertial — check the four-rotation $\omega$, because a straight worldline may carry a precessing frame ([[Ex - A straight worldline need not be inertial]]). Once both conditions hold, the constancy of $U$ does all the remaining work: the rest-space hyperplanes are parallel translates of a single $U^\perp$, they never intersect, and the coordinates $(ct, x^i)$ are global ([[Thm - Globality of the Local Rest Space for Inertial Observers]]). The reason this works is that everything special about an inertial observer flows from the single fact that its four-velocity is one constant vector rather than a varying field; whenever you are stuck, return to that fact and ask what the constancy of $U$ gives you.

If the problem **concerns the group structure** — closure, normality, simplicity, the direct-versus-semidirect distinction, dimension — the strategy is to write every transformation as a pair $(\boldsymbol{v}, \Lambda)$ and substitute into the composition law. Almost every structural target is one substitution away. To show the translations are normal, conjugate: $(\boldsymbol{w}, \Lambda)(\boldsymbol{v}, \mathrm{Id})(\boldsymbol{w}, \Lambda)^{-1} = (\Lambda\boldsymbol{v}, \mathrm{Id})$, still a translation ([[Ex - Translations form a normal abelian subgroup]]). To show the group is not a direct product, compare $\Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2$ with $\boldsymbol{v}_2$ and find them unequal ([[Ex - The Poincaré group is not a direct product]]). To find the Lie algebra and its brackets, pass to infinitesimal transformations $\Lambda = \mathrm{Id} + \varepsilon L$, $\boldsymbol{v} = \varepsilon\boldsymbol{v}$, expand the composition law to second order in $\varepsilon$, and read the bracket off the antisymmetric part ([[Thm - The Poincaré Group as a Lie Group]]). The unifying observation is that the semidirect law $(\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$ encodes *all* of the group structure; the entire family of structural problems is the study of that one formula.

If the problem **concerns representations, mass, or spin** — what is the spin content of a field, how many polarisations does a particle have, is a given operator a Casimir — the strategy is to fix a standard momentum and find its little group. For a massive particle take the rest-frame momentum $P = (m, \mathbf{0})$; the Lorentz transformations fixing it are exactly the spatial rotations $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, whose irreducible representations are the spin-$s$ multiplets of dimension $2s+1$, and the Pauli–Lubanski vector reduces to the spin three-vector with $W^2 = -m^2 s(s+1)$ ([[Ex - Computing the Pauli-Lubanski vector]]). For a massless particle take $P = (E, 0, 0, E)$; the little group is the non-compact $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$, and the physical requirement that its non-compact "translations" act trivially collapses the label to a single helicity $\pm s$ ([[Ex - Classifying massive versus massless representations]]). To check that a quadratic operator is Casimir, verify it commutes with the translations (easy for $P^2$, immediate from $[P,P]=0$) and with the Lorentz generators (verify the four-vector transformation law of its building blocks). The reason the little-group method works is Wigner's induction theorem: a representation of the whole Poincaré group is determined by a representation of the little group of any one momentum, so the hard infinite-dimensional problem reduces to the easy finite-dimensional representation theory of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ or $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$.

Threading all three families is one meta-question, the single question this chapter asks in every guise: **which symmetry are we looking at, and how does it act?** An inertial observer is the geometry seen by the *trivial* element of the group; a change of inertial observer is a *passive* group element; a physical motion of matter is an *active* group element; and a particle is an *irreducible* way the group acts on a quantum state space. The whole chapter is the study of one group, $\mathrm{ISO}(1,3)$, from four sides — its fixed points (inertial frames), its passive action (coordinate changes), its active action (motions), and its representations (particles) — and every problem is the question of which side you are on.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **The semidirect composition law** $(\boldsymbol{v}_1, \Lambda_1)(\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_2) = (\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$ ([[Def - The Poincaré Group]]): the single most-used fact of the chapter, because every group-theoretic question reduces to it. **Typical use:** substitute to prove closure, compute an inverse $(\boldsymbol{v},\Lambda)^{-1} = (-\Lambda^{-1}\boldsymbol{v}, \Lambda^{-1})$, conjugate to prove normality, or expand infinitesimally to extract the Lie bracket. The decisive feature is the lone factor of $\Lambda_1$ in front of $\boldsymbol{v}_2$ — recognise that factor as the whole difference between $\rtimes$ and $\times$, and as the reason the translations are normal but the Lorentz subgroup is not.

- **Inertial $\Leftrightarrow a = 0$ and $\omega = 0$** ([[Def - Inertial Observer]]): the operational test for the central object of the chapter. **Typical use:** handed any worldline or frame, decide inertiality by these two checks, and remember that the first alone gives only straightness. Its most powerful corollary is the constancy of the four-velocity, from which globality of the rest space, parallelism of the simultaneity hyperplanes, and the existence of global inertial coordinates all follow in one step. Reach for it whenever a problem hinges on whether an observer can pretend to be Newtonian.

- **The two Casimirs** $P^2 = m^2$ and $W^2 = -m^2 s(s+1)$ ([[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group]]): the complete invariant label of a Poincaré representation. **Typical use:** to attach a $(m, s)$ to a field or particle, and to recognise mass and spin not as attributes but as Casimir eigenvalues. The reusable skill is reading the mass off $P\cdot P$ — which is also why a particle's rest mass is the Lorentz-invariant norm of its four-momentum — and reading the spin off the Pauli–Lubanski square, remembering the constraint $W \cdot P = 0$ that makes $W$ the spin in the rest frame. This property is the gateway from the geometry of this chapter to the particle content of quantum field theory.

- **The little group decomposition** (massive $\to \mathrm{SO}(3)$, massless $\to \mathrm{ISO}(2)$): the engine of representation classification. **Typical use:** to count polarisation states ($2s+1$ for massive, $2$ for massless) and to explain the qualitative difference between a massive vector boson (three polarisations) and a photon (two). The reusable content is Wigner's reduction — the representation theory of the ten-dimensional non-compact Poincaré group is governed by the representation theory of a *three-dimensional* little group — which turns an intractable problem into the familiar angular-momentum theory of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$. Recognise its applicability whenever the spin content of a relativistic field is in question.

- **The intrinsic Lie bracket** $[(\boldsymbol{v}_1, L_1), (\boldsymbol{v}_2, L_2)] = (L_1\boldsymbol{v}_2 - L_2\boldsymbol{v}_1, [L_1, L_2])$ ([[Thm - The Poincaré Group as a Lie Group]]): the bracket of the Poincaré algebra, defined without a matrix commutator. **Typical use:** to compute any structure constant by plugging in the generators $P_\alpha = (e_\alpha, 0)$, $K_i = (0, K_i)$, $J_i = (0, J_i)$ and using the action of $K_i, J_i$ on basis vectors. The reusable insight is *why* the bracket must be defined this way rather than copied from the Lorentz case — the Poincaré algebra is not a priori an algebra of endomorphisms, so the commutator is unavailable and the bracket must be read off the group composition law instead. This is a model for how one builds the Lie algebra of any abstract Lie group.

# Bridges

1. **Lie theory — the Poincaré group is a ten-dimensional non-compact Lie group, and its algebra is built by a general procedure.** A [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] is a group that is also a smooth manifold with smooth multiplication, and its [[Def - Lie Algebra|Lie algebra]] is the tangent space at the identity equipped with a bracket measuring infinitesimal non-commutativity ([[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group]]). For a *matrix* Lie group that bracket is the commutator of matrices ([[Ex - The Lie Bracket on a Matrix Lie Algebra is the Commutator]]), but the Poincaré group is not naturally a matrix group — its elements are affine maps $f = (\boldsymbol{v}, \Lambda)$ of spacetime, not linear endomorphisms — so the commutator is not available directly. The chapter therefore reconstructs the bracket from the group composition law itself, by expanding $(\boldsymbol{v}_1, \Lambda_1)(\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_2)$ to second order in an infinitesimal parameter and reading off the antisymmetric part; the result $[(\boldsymbol{v}_1, L_1), (\boldsymbol{v}_2, L_2)] = (L_1\boldsymbol{v}_2 - L_2\boldsymbol{v}_1, [L_1, L_2])$ is exactly what the general functorial construction of the Lie algebra of a Lie group ([[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map]]) produces when applied to $\mathrm{ISO}(1,3)$. The exponential map ([[Def - Exponential Map of a Lie Group]]) then carries $\mathfrak{iso}(1,3)$ back to the connected component of the group, so a finite Poincaré transformation is the exponential of a sum $\boldsymbol{v}\cdot P + \tfrac{1}{2}\omega_{\mu\nu}J^{\mu\nu}$ of generators. The non-compactness — inherited from the boosts $K_i$ and the translations $P_\alpha$, both of which generate one-parameter subgroups isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$ rather than to a circle — is the technical fact that forces the unitary representations to be infinite-dimensional, which is why Wigner's classification is genuinely subtle.

2. **Group theory — the semidirect product, normality, and the failure of simplicity.** The Poincaré group is the cleanest physically-occurring example of a [[Def - Group|group]] that is a *semidirect* rather than a *direct* product. The direct product $\mathbb{R}^4 \times \mathrm{O}(1,3)$ would compose componentwise, $(\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$; the actual law inserts $\Lambda_1$ in front of $\boldsymbol{v}_2$, and the meaning of that insertion is precisely that the Lorentz group *acts* on the translation group by its defining action on $\mathbb{R}^4$. The general definition of a semidirect product $N \rtimes_\varphi H$ requires a homomorphism $\varphi : H \to \mathrm{Aut}(N)$ giving the action; here $N = (\mathbb{R}^4, +)$, $H = \mathrm{O}(1,3)$, and $\varphi$ is the tautological action of Lorentz matrices on four-vectors. The structural payoff is that $N$ is a *normal* subgroup — conjugating a translation by any Poincaré element yields the translation by the Lorentz-rotated vector, still a translation — whereas the Lorentz subgroup is not normal. Consequently the Poincaré group is **not simple** (it has the proper normal subgroup of translations), in sharp contrast to the restricted Lorentz group $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$, which *is* simple. This is the relativistic analogue of the Euclidean group $\mathrm{ISO}(3) = \mathbb{R}^3 \rtimes \mathrm{O}(3)$ of rigid motions of space, and recognising the common semidirect skeleton is how one transfers intuition between the two.

3. **Quantum field theory — fields are representations, and the free-field equations are projection conditions.** The deepest bridge runs from the two Casimirs of §12.3 to the entire content of relativistic quantum field theory. Wigner's theorem identifies an elementary particle with an irreducible unitary representation of the Poincaré group, labelled by mass $m = \sqrt{P^2}$ and spin $s$ from $W^2 = -m^2 s(s+1)$; a quantum field is then a device for carrying such a representation on a space of functions on spacetime. The free-field wave equations are exactly the conditions that select a single irreducible $(m, s)$ from the reducible field space: the Klein–Gordon equation $(\Box + m^2)\phi = 0$ is the condition $P^2 = m^2$ realised on a scalar field (the $(m, 0)$ representation); the Dirac equation $(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi = 0$ realises $(m, \tfrac{1}{2})$, its first-order form being the square root of the Klein–Gordon condition that also fixes the spin; the source-free Maxwell equations realise the massless $(0, 1)$ representation, the two physical polarisations being the two helicities $h = \pm 1$ that survive once the gauge redundancy of the massless $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ little group is removed. The spin Casimir even explains the polarisation count that the equivalence principle and gauge invariance otherwise impose by hand: a massive spin-1 field (Proca, $(m,1)$) has $2s+1 = 3$ polarisations because its little group is $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, while the massless photon has only $2$ because its little group is $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ and the third would-be state is pure gauge. This is why the Lie-theoretic study of one spacetime symmetry group is the organising principle of all of particle physics, and why the present chapter, geometric though it looks, is the true foundation of the quantum theory built on top of it.

4. **General relativity — global inertial frames exist only in flat spacetime, and their failure is gravity.** The globality theorem of §12.1 is the precise statement of what gravitation destroys. In Minkowski spacetime an inertial observer's rest-space hyperplanes are parallel and the coordinates $(ct, x^i)$ blanket the whole manifold, because the four-velocity is one constant vector and the spacetime is flat. The bridge to gravitation is the **equivalence principle**: a freely-falling observer in a curved spacetime is *locally* inertial — along their worldline, which is a geodesic $\nabla_U U = 0$, the carried frame is unaccelerated and non-rotating, and special relativity holds exactly. But the locally inertial frames at *different* events can no longer be assembled into one global chart: parallel-transporting the rest space of one event to another is path-dependent, the simultaneity hyperplanes at neighbouring events tilt relative to one another, and the obstruction to making them globally parallel is exactly the Riemann curvature tensor. So the inertial observer of this chapter is the $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ limit of the freely-falling observer of general relativity, and the global inertial coordinate system — the thing that makes special relativity tractable — is the one luxury that curvature takes away. The straight worldline becomes the geodesic, the constant four-velocity becomes the covariantly-constant one, and the Poincaré group, which acts globally on Minkowski space, survives only as a *local* symmetry acting in the tangent space at each event ([[General Relativity I — Einstein's Equations and Schwarzschild]]).

5. **Mechanics — the Galilean group is the non-relativistic contraction of the Poincaré group, and the difference is one central extension.** The Poincaré group has a non-relativistic limit, obtained by letting $c \to \infty$ in the boost generators, and that limit is the **Galilean group** of classical mechanics — the symmetries of Newtonian spacetime, generated by time translations, space translations, rotations, and Galilean boosts. The contraction is instructive because the structure constants change: the relativistic bracket $[K_i, P_0] = P_i$ (boosts mixing time-translation into space-translation) survives, but $[K_i, P_j] = \delta_{ij}P_0$ — boosts mixing space-translation into time-translation — is suppressed by a factor $1/c^2$ and vanishes in the limit, which is exactly the statement that time becomes absolute. The subtle and beautiful fact is that the Galilean group, unlike the Poincaré group, admits a non-trivial *central extension*: the mass appears in quantum mechanics not as a Casimir but as the central charge of the extended Bargmann algebra, $[K_i, P_j] = i m \delta_{ij}$, which is why a Galilean-invariant wavefunction picks up a mass-dependent phase under boosts and why superselection by mass holds non-relativistically but not relativistically. Tracing how mass migrates from a Casimir of the Poincaré group to a central charge of the Galilean group, as $c \to \infty$, is one of the most illuminating exercises in seeing how relativistic and classical kinematics fit together; the relativistic story, where mass is simply $P^2$, is the cleaner one, and the Newtonian central charge is the fossil it leaves behind.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: one group, seen from four sides.** It is tempting to read this chapter as three loosely-related topics — a bit of observer geometry, a bit of group theory, a bit of representation theory — but they are one subject viewed from four angles, and the angle is the choice of *how the Poincaré group acts*. The inertial observers are the geometry that the group's identity element sees: a frame with no acceleration and no rotation, the fixed point around which the group's action is organised. A change of inertial observer is a *passive* group element, relabelling a single event in two coordinate systems. A physical motion of matter — a rigid displacement of an apparatus, a boost of a beam of particles — is an *active* group element, moving events to other events. And a species of particle is an *irreducible representation*, a way the group can act on a quantum state space without that space decomposing. Once you see that inertial frames, coordinate changes, physical motions, and particles are the fixed points, the passive action, the active action, and the representations of one and the same group $\mathrm{ISO}(1,3)$, the chapter stops being a list and becomes a single idea. The recurring confusion — active versus passive, geometry versus algebra — dissolves the moment you ask, for any object in front of you, *which face of the group am I looking at?*

**The true name of the Poincaré group is "the isometry group of Minkowski affine space".** The definition by generators and brackets is what you *compute* with, but the right way to *think* about the Poincaré group is as the complete symmetry group of $(\mathscr{E}, \eta)$ — every transformation of spacetime that preserves the interval between every pair of events. From this one characterisation the whole structure is forced. The transformations must be affine (they preserve a flat metric, and the metric-preserving maps of an affine space are affine), so each splits into a linear part and a translation. The linear part must preserve the metric, so it is a Lorentz transformation. The translations are forced to be normal because translating, then applying any isometry, then untranslating is again a translation — geometry, not algebra, dictates the semidirect structure. The dimension is forced: four translations plus six Lorentz parameters. Even the two Casimirs are forced, as the invariants any isometry group of this signature must have. When you see "Poincaré group", do not picture ten generators — picture the rigid motions of spacetime, the maps that slide and rotate the whole of Minkowski space while keeping all intervals fixed, exactly as the Euclidean group $\mathrm{ISO}(3)$ is the rigid motions of ordinary space. The generators are the bookkeeping; the isometry is the object.

**Mass and spin are not properties — they are names.** The single most reorienting idea in the chapter is Wigner's, and it inverts the naive ontology of physics. One naturally imagines a particle as a tiny object that *possesses* a mass and *carries* a spin, the way a billiard ball has a weight and a colour. Wigner's classification says the opposite: the particle *is* the representation, and the mass and spin are the two labels that pick the representation out, the two Casimir eigenvalues $P^2 = m^2$ and $W^2 = -m^2 s(s+1)$. They are no more "had" by the particle than the dimension is "had" by a vector space — they are constitutive, the very data that says which representation, hence which particle, you are talking about. This is why every electron in the universe has exactly the same mass and spin: they are not electrons that happen to weigh the same, they are all the same irreducible representation $(m_e, \tfrac{1}{2})$ of the one spacetime symmetry group. It is also why the polarisation counts come out as they do without any further input: a massive spin-$s$ particle has $2s+1$ states because that is the dimension of the spin-$s$ representation of its little group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, and a massless particle has just two helicities because its little group is the non-compact $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$. The lesson for a problem-solver is to stop asking "what is the spin of this thing?" as if probing a hidden attribute, and start asking "which representation is this?" — the spin is then read off, not measured.

**Why the bracket had to be reinvented, and what that teaches about Lie algebras.** A small but deep point sits at the heart of §12.2, easy to pass over. When we built the Lie algebra of the Lorentz group in [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]], the bracket came for free: $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ is a group of matrices, its Lie algebra is a space of matrices, and the bracket is the matrix commutator $[A, B] = AB - BA$. For the Poincaré group this gift is withdrawn, because the Poincaré group is *not* naturally a group of matrices — its elements are affine maps of spacetime, and an affine map is not a linear endomorphism whose commutator you can take. So the chapter does something more fundamental: it defines the bracket as the measure of how much two infinitesimal group elements fail to commute, read directly off the group composition law. This is not a trick special to the Poincaré group; it is the *general* definition of the Lie algebra of any Lie group, which the matrix case merely specialises. Seeing the bracket forced out of the composition law $(\boldsymbol{v}_1 + \Lambda_1\boldsymbol{v}_2, \Lambda_1\Lambda_2)$, rather than handed down as a commutator, is the clearest possible illustration of what a Lie bracket *is* — and it is worth holding onto, because the same construction is what gives Lie algebras to diffeomorphism groups, gauge groups, and every other infinite- or non-matrix Lie group in physics. (One can embed the Poincaré group in $5\times 5$ matrices using homogeneous coordinates, recovering the commutator, but the point stands: the bracket is intrinsic, and the matrix embedding is a convenience, not its source.)
