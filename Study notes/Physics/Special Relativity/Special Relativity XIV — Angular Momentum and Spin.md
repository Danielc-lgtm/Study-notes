# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we set $c = 1$** and use the **mostly-minus** signature $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, so that a timelike vector $X$ has $X \cdot X > 0$. Where a formula is more recognisable with $c$ restored, it is given both ways; to restore $c$, replace $t$ by $ct$, every velocity $v$ by $v/c$, and insert factors of $c$ by dimensional analysis.

> [!warning] Convention
> Gourgoulhon's *Special Relativity in General Frames* — the source for this chapter — uses the **opposite** signature, mostly-plus $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$, with timelike $g(v,v) < 0$ and a four-velocity normalised to $\vec u_0 \cdot \vec u_0 = -c^2$. Every scalar product, every norm, and several intermediate signs differ from ours. In particular his time-projection $h = -\vec u_0 \cdot \overrightarrow{CM}$ and energy $E = -c\langle p, \vec u_0\rangle$ carry signs that flip in our convention to $h = \vec u_0 \cdot \overrightarrow{CM}$ (up to the $c$ factor) and $E = P \cdot U_0$. The genuinely convention-free object is the **contravariant tensor** $J^{\alpha\beta} = x^\alpha p^\beta - x^\beta p^\alpha$: it is built with upper indices and no metric, so it reads identically in both signatures. We take it as the primary definition and derive every observer-relative quantity natively in mostly-minus.

- $\mathscr{P}$ — a particle, massive or massless; $\mathscr{L}$ — its [[Def - Worldline of a Particle|worldline]]; $\mathscr{S}$ — a system of particles
- $C, C' \in \mathscr{E}$ — reference events (points of Minkowski space $\mathscr{E}$) about which angular momentum is taken
- $M, M_a$ — events on worldlines; $x^\alpha$ — coordinates of an event; $\overrightarrow{CM}$ — the displacement vector from $C$ to $M$
- $p, P$ — the [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momentum]] of a particle (lower-case for a single particle, $P$ for a system total); $P^\alpha = (E, \mathbf{p})$ in a frame
- $m$ — rest mass; $E$ — energy relative to an observer; $\mathbf{p}, \mathbf{P}$ — spatial three-momentum (bold); $U, U_0$ — four-velocities
- $J_C$, $J_{\alpha\beta}$, $J^{\alpha\beta}$ — the **angular momentum** of $\mathscr{P}$ (or $\mathscr{S}$) with respect to $C$, as a two-form / its components
- $\vec\sigma_C$, $\sigma_C^i$ — the **angular momentum vector** relative to an observer, $\vec\sigma_C = \overrightarrow{CM} \times \mathbf{p}$ in the observer's rest space
- $\vec D$ — the **mass-energy dipole moment** of a system relative to an observer, $\vec D = \sum_a E_a \overrightarrow{OM_a}$ (with $c$: divided by $c^2$)
- $G$, $G_\mathcal{O}$ — the **centre of inertia** of an isolated system (intrinsic) and the **centroid** relative to observer $\mathcal{O}$ (observer-dependent)
- $\vec\sigma$, $S$, $S^\mu$ — the **spin vector** (independent of $C$ for an isolated system), the **spin two-form**, the **spin four-vector**; $S \cdot U = 0$
- $W^\mu$ — the [[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group|Pauli–Lubanski vector]], $W^\mu = -\tfrac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} J_{\nu\rho} P_\sigma$; for a massive system $S^\mu = W^\mu/(mc)$
- $N_C$ — the **four-torque**, $N_C = dJ_C/d\tau$; $f$ — the [[Def - Four-Force|four-force]] $dp/d\tau$
- $R_0$ — the **Møller radius** $R_0 = \|\vec\sigma\|/(mc)$, the lower bound on the size of a spinning system
- $\times$, $\times_{u_0}$ — the spatial cross product induced by the [[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor|Levi-Civita tensor]] $\epsilon$ on an observer's rest space; $\wedge$ — the exterior product $a \wedge b = a \otimes b - b \otimes a$
- $D^{\mathrm{FW}}_u$ — the [[Def - Fermi-Walker Derivative|Fermi–Walker derivative]] along a worldline of four-velocity $U$; $\perp_u$ — orthogonal projector onto the rest space $E_u$
- $g$ — the **Landé factor** of a particle; $gq/(2m)$ — the gyromagnetic ratio; $F$, $F_{\mu\nu}$ — the **electromagnetic field tensor** (introduced fully in [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field]])
- Einstein summation: a repeated index, once up once down, is summed over $0,1,2,3$ (Greek) or $1,2,3$ (Latin)

# Motivation

The previous chapter, [[Special Relativity XIII — Energy and Momentum]], built the first great conservation law of relativistic dynamics — the conservation of four-momentum — out of the single object $P = mU$. This chapter builds the *second*: the conservation of angular momentum. In Newtonian mechanics these are two independent first principles, and they remain two independent first principles here, because relativity has no action-reaction law to derive one from the other. But the relativistic version has a twist that has no Newtonian shadow, and that twist is the whole reason this chapter is worth a careful read.

In three dimensions, angular momentum is the vector $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$. The cross product is a three-dimensional accident — it exists because $\binom{3}{2} = 3$, so the antisymmetric pairs of three axes can be relabelled as three axes again. In four dimensions $\binom{4}{2} = 6 \ne 4$, the relabelling fails, and the honest object is not a vector but an **antisymmetric tensor**:

$$
J^{\alpha\beta} \;=\; x^\alpha p^\beta - x^\beta p^\alpha,
$$

the structural backbone of the chapter. This single equation contains everything. Its six independent components split, relative to any observer, into two three-vectors exactly as the electromagnetic tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$ splits into $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$: the space-space block is the familiar angular momentum vector $\vec\sigma = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$, and the time-space block is a new object with no Newtonian name, the **mass-energy dipole moment**, which measures how the system's energy is distributed in space. The mass-energy dipole is to angular momentum what the electric field is to the magnetic field: the piece that a boost rotates into the other piece.

That mass-energy dipole is where the surprise lives. In Newtonian mechanics the centre of mass is an objective point — every observer agrees on where it is. In relativity the analogous point, the **centroid**, is *observer-dependent*: two observers in relative motion locate the centre of mass of the *same* system at *different* points in spacetime. The displacement between their answers is governed by the spin, and it is bounded: the centroid can wander, but only within a region of radius $R_0 = \|\vec\sigma\|/(mc)$ — the **Møller radius**. Turned around, this says a spinning body cannot be smaller than its Møller radius, because to carry a fixed spin in an ever-smaller body the constituents would have to move faster than light. This is a genuinely relativistic theorem with no classical counterpart: relativity puts a floor on the size of anything that spins.

The chapter ends with evolution. When a system is not isolated, its angular momentum changes at a rate set by the **four-torque** $N_C = \overrightarrow{CM} \wedge f$, the relativistic moment of the four-force. Specialising to a small spinning body — a gyroscope, an electron — the spin obeys a beautifully simple law: in the absence of torque it is **Fermi–Walker transported**, the relativistic notion of "not rotating", and a free gyroscope's spin precesses purely because of the geometry of its worldline. Put the gyroscope in an electromagnetic field and you get the **BMT equation**, the equation that governs the precession of an electron's or muon's spin and underlies every measurement of the anomalous magnetic moment $g-2$.

The reader is assumed to be fluent in the four-momentum of a particle and a system from [[Special Relativity XIII — Energy and Momentum]], comfortable with the observer formalism — four-velocity, local rest space, orthogonal projector — of [[Special Relativity VI — Observers, Local Rest Spaces and Local Frames]], and to have met the [[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group|Pauli–Lubanski vector]] in passing in [[Special Relativity XII — Inertial Observers and the Poincaré Group]], where mass and spin first appeared as the two Casimir invariants of the Poincaré group. The new ingredients here are the antisymmetric-tensor packaging of angular momentum and the willingness to take seriously a centre of mass that depends on who is looking.

# Concept Map

## §14.1 Angular Momentum of a Particle and System

- **[[Def - Angular Momentum Four-Tensor]]**
	- The angular momentum of a particle with four-momentum $p$ about an event $C$ is the antisymmetric tensor $J^{\alpha\beta} = x^\alpha p^\beta - x^\beta p^\alpha$, where $x^\alpha$ are the components of $\overrightarrow{CM}$; equivalently the two-form $J_C = \overrightarrow{CM}^\flat \wedge p$. Its six components split relative to an observer into the **angular momentum vector** $\vec\sigma_C = \overrightarrow{CM} \times \mathbf{p}$ (space-space block, the relativistic $\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}$) and the **mass-energy dipole moment** (time-space block) — exactly the way the electromagnetic tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$ splits into $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{E}$. For a system it is the sum $J_C = \sum_a \overrightarrow{CM_a}^\flat \wedge p_a$ over particles, evaluated on a spacelike hypersurface. The tensor is intrinsic to the particle and the point $C$; the split into the two three-vectors is what depends on the observer.

- **[[Thm - Conservation of Angular Momentum]]**
	- For an isolated particle the angular momentum about any fixed event is constant along the worldline, $J_C(M) = \text{const}$, a consequence of the law of inertia $dp/d\tau = 0$ together with $\overrightarrow{CM}^\flat \wedge p \mapsto cU \wedge p = 0$. For an isolated *system* of two or more particles this is an independent first principle, on the same footing as four-momentum conservation, because relativity has no strong action–reaction law. Under a change of reference point, $J_{C'} = J_C + \overrightarrow{C'C}^\flat \wedge P$ with $P$ the total four-momentum; the angular momentum vector relative to an inertial observer is separately conserved, $d\vec\sigma_C/dt = 0$. The law is the conservation that Noether's theorem will tie to Lorentz invariance in [[Special Relativity XV — The Principle of Least Action]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Mass and Spin as Poincaré Casimirs *(from Quantum Field Theory)*
> The antisymmetric tensor $J^{\alpha\beta}$ is the conserved current of the **Lorentz** part of the Poincaré group, exactly as $P^\alpha$ is the current of the translation part. Contracting the two produces the [[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group|Pauli–Lubanski vector]] $W^\mu = -\tfrac12\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}J_{\nu\rho}P_\sigma$, whose square $W^2$ is the **second Casimir** of the Poincaré group. Wigner's classification of relativistic particles is precisely the statement that an elementary particle is an irreducible representation labelled by the two Casimirs $P^2 = m^2$ and $W^2 = -m^2 s(s+1)$ — **mass and spin**. This chapter is the classical skeleton of that quantum-mechanical bone.

## §14.2 Centre of Inertia and Spin

- **[[Def - Centre of Inertia]]**
	- The **centroid** of a system relative to an observer $\mathcal{O}$ is the energy-weighted mean position of its particles in $\mathcal{O}$'s rest space, $\overrightarrow{OG_\mathcal{O}} = \tfrac{1}{E}\sum_a E_a \overrightarrow{OM_a} = \tfrac{c^2}{E}\vec D$ — and it is **observer-dependent**: two observers in relative motion place it at genuinely different events. For an isolated system one removes the ambiguity by choosing observers comoving with the system (four-velocity equal to the system's $\vec u$); the resulting intrinsic point is the **centre of inertia** $G$, whose worldline is a straight timelike line, the worldline of the centre-of-momentum frame. The observer-dependence is the single most surprising fact of the chapter: there is no frame-independent "where the mass is" once the system spins.

- **[[Def - Spin Four-Vector]]**
	- For an isolated system the angular momentum vector $\vec\sigma_C$ relative to a barycentric observer turns out to be **independent of the reference point** $C$; this common value is the **spin vector** $\vec\sigma$, and the corresponding two-form $S = \epsilon(\vec u, \vec\sigma, \cdot, \cdot)$ is the **spin**. As a four-vector $S^\mu$ it is the [[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group|Pauli–Lubanski vector]] divided by the mass, $S^\mu = W^\mu/(mc)$, and is orthogonal to the four-velocity, $S \cdot U = 0$ — a spacelike vector living in the rest space. The spin is the part of the angular momentum that survives after the orbital part (which depends on $C$) is stripped away; it is the relativistic upgrade of "intrinsic angular momentum".

- **[[Thm - König Theorem (Relativistic)]]**
	- The angular momentum of an isolated system about any point splits as $J_C = S + \overrightarrow{CG}^\flat \wedge P$ — the **spin** $S$ (the angular momentum about the centre of inertia, independent of $C$) plus the **orbital angular momentum** $\overrightarrow{CG}^\flat \wedge P$ (the angular momentum of a fictitious point particle sitting at the centre of inertia $G$ carrying the total four-momentum $P$). This is the relativistic form of König's theorem, the decomposition "total $=$ spin $+$ orbital" familiar from the Newtonian centre-of-mass theorem. The orbital part carries all the $C$-dependence; the spin part carries none.

- **[[Thm - Minimal Size of a Spinning System]]**
	- The displacement between the centre of inertia $G$ and the centroid $G_\mathcal{O}$ relative to a moving observer is $\overrightarrow{GG_\mathcal{O}} = \tfrac{1}{mc^2}\vec\sigma \times \vec V_\mathcal{O}$, so the set of all possible centroids fills a disk of radius $R_0 = \|\vec\sigma\|/(mc)$ — the **Møller radius** — perpendicular to the spin. Since every particle of the system must lie inside the body, the body's radius $R$ satisfies $R \geq R_0 = \|\vec\sigma\|/(mc)$: **a spinning system cannot be arbitrarily small.** The intuition is that holding a fixed spin in a shrinking body forces the constituents to circulate faster, and the speed of light caps that. The centroids coincide with $G$ for *all* observers if and only if the spin vanishes.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Compton Wavelength as a Spin Length *(from Quantum Field Theory)*
> Promote the classical Møller bound $R \geq \|\vec\sigma\|/(mc)$ to the quantum regime by replacing $\|\vec\sigma\|$ with the spin eigenvalue $\sqrt{s(s+1)}\,\hbar$. The minimal size becomes $R_0 = \sqrt{s(s+1)}\,\hbar/(mc) = \sqrt{s(s+1)}\,\lambda_C$, where $\lambda_C = \hbar/(mc)$ is the **Compton wavelength**. This is the precise classical reason a spinning particle cannot be localised below its Compton wavelength — the same length below which pair creation makes a single-particle position meaningless. The Møller radius is the bridge between "spin needs room to circulate" and "you cannot probe an electron below $\lambda_C$".

## §14.3 Evolution — Torque and Precession

- **[[Def - Four-Torque]]**
	- The **four-torque** on a particle about a point $C$ is the proper-time derivative of its angular momentum two-form, $N_C := dJ_C/d\tau$, and it equals the relativistic moment of the four-force, $N_C = \overrightarrow{CM}^\flat \wedge f$ with $f = dp/d\tau$ the [[Def - Four-Force|four-force]]. It is the relativistic upgrade of the Newtonian torque $\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{F}$: vanishing torque is equivalent to conservation of angular momentum, and the time-space and space-space blocks of $N_C$ drive the evolution of the dipole moment and the angular momentum vector respectively.

- **[[Thm - Evolution of the Angular Momentum Vector]]**
	- For an inertial observer measuring the angular momentum of a particle about a point $C$ fixed in the observer's reference space, the angular momentum vector obeys $d\vec\sigma_C/dt = \overrightarrow{CM} \times \mathbf{F}$ — the Newtonian torque law, recovered exactly. For a general (accelerated, rotating) observer or a moving point the full law replaces $d/dt$ by the Fermi–Walker derivative and adds corrections for the observer's four-acceleration and four-rotation; setting the force to zero recovers $d\vec\sigma_C/dt = 0$. This is the bridge from the covariant four-torque to the three-vector torque of laboratory mechanics.

- **[[Thm - The BMT Equation]]**
	- A small spinning body whose spin is not torqued is a **free gyroscope**: its spin four-vector is Fermi–Walker transported, $D^{\mathrm{FW}}_u \vec s = 0$, which keeps $\|\vec s\|$ constant and makes the spin *precess* purely from the bending of the worldline. Place the body, carrying charge $q$ and Landé factor $g$, in an electromagnetic field, and the torque on the spin gives the **Bargmann–Michel–Telegdi equation** $\frac{d\vec s}{d\tau} = \frac{q}{mc}\big[\frac{g}{2}\vec F(\cdot,\vec s) + (\frac{g}{2}-1)F(\vec u,\vec s)\,\vec u\big]$, which preserves $\|\vec s\|$ and governs the precession of an electron's or muon's spin. The $(\frac g2 - 1)$ term is the Thomas precession; for $g=2$ the equation collapses to $\frac{d\vec s}{d\tau} = \frac{q}{mc}\vec F(\cdot,\vec s)$. The electromagnetic field tensor $F$ is developed fully in [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field]]; here it enters only kinematically.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Muon Anomalous Magnetic Moment *(from Particle Physics)*
> The BMT equation is the working tool of the muon $g-2$ experiments. In a storage ring the muon's spin and momentum both precess, and the *difference* of the two precession rates is proportional to the **anomalous magnetic moment** $a = (g-2)/2$. Because the $(\frac g2 - 1)$ Thomas term in the BMT equation isolates exactly the deviation of $g$ from $2$, the spin-momentum angle grows at a rate that measures $a$ directly. The current ${\sim}0.2$ ppm measurements of the muon anomaly — a precision test of the Standard Model — are read off from the BMT precession of the muon spin; the equation in this chapter is the classical kinematics those experiments are built on.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Thomas Precession and Spin–Orbit Coupling *(from Atomic Physics)*
> The $(\frac g2 - 1)$ term, present even when $g$ has its "naive" value $1$, is the **Thomas precession**: a purely kinematic rotation that an accelerated spin undergoes because successive instantaneous rest frames are related by boosts whose composition is a boost *times a rotation* (the Wigner rotation of [[Special Relativity IX — The Lorentz Group, Structure and Classification]]). In an atom the electron's spin, accelerated by the nucleus's electric field, precesses by exactly this; the Thomas factor of $\tfrac12$ is what reconciles the naive spin–orbit coupling energy with the observed fine structure of hydrogen. The kinematic free-gyroscope precession of this chapter is the same effect seen from a covariant standpoint, developed dynamically in [[Special Relativity XVI — Accelerated Observers]].

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The problems of this chapter chase a recognisable handful of goals. The first is to **establish a conservation or invariance law**: that an isolated system's angular momentum tensor is constant, that the spin four-vector is a Lorentz invariant of the system, that the BMT evolution preserves the spin's norm. These are proved by exhibiting the right derivative as zero — by the law of inertia for a single particle, by the first-principle conservation for a system, or by the antisymmetry of a Levi-Civita or field tensor that kills a contraction. A second target is **to decompose a covariant object into observer-relative pieces and read off their physics**: to split $J^{\alpha\beta}$ into angular-momentum vector and mass-energy dipole, or $J_C$ into spin and orbital parts (the König theorem). The skill is the same bookkeeping that splits $F_{\mu\nu}$ into $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. A third, distinctly relativistic, target is **to locate where a thing is, and to discover that the answer depends on the observer**: to compute the centroid relative to a moving observer and find it displaced from the centre of inertia by $\vec\sigma\times\vec V/(mc^2)$. A fourth is **to derive an evolution law** — the four-torque, the relativistic torque law $d\vec\sigma/dt = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}$, the Fermi–Walker transport of a free gyroscope, the BMT precession — typically by differentiating a defining relation and using a constraint such as $S\cdot U = 0$. A fifth, more structural, is **to connect the classical object to its quantum or group-theoretic meaning**: to identify the spin four-vector with the Pauli–Lubanski vector, or the Møller radius with the Compton wavelength. Across all five, the unifying remark is that you understand a relativistic angular-momentum situation when you know which part of $J^{\alpha\beta}$ is intrinsic (the tensor, the spin) and which part is observer-relative (the vector split, the centroid).

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **The system is isolated** is the richest source: it makes the angular momentum tensor conserved, independent of hypersurface and observer, and it licenses the definition of the spin vector as a $C$-independent quantity and of the centre of inertia as an intrinsic point. **An observer is prescribed** — most usefully a *barycentric* (centre-of-momentum) observer, in whose frame the total three-momentum vanishes, the mass-energy dipole vanishes, and the energy reduces to the rest mass $E = mc^2$; the physics is simplest there and one transforms out. **A four-vector or tensor is recognised as built from $\overrightarrow{CM}$ and $p$** — then its transformation law is automatic (it transforms as a tensor) and its contractions with $U$ or $P$ are constrained by orthogonality, $S\cdot U = 0$ and $J_G(P,\cdot) = 0$. **A constraint is given** — the spin supplementary condition $S(p,\cdot) = 0$, the normalisation $U\cdot U = 1$, the antisymmetry of $F$ or $\epsilon$ — and differentiating the constraint pins down an otherwise-free scalar (this is how the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda = a\cdot s$ in the spin evolution is found). **The law of inertia or a force law is given** — $dp/d\tau = 0$ for a free particle, $f = qF(\cdot,U)$ for a charge — feeding directly into the four-torque. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: isolation routes through the law of inertia to conservation; a barycentric observer routes through "three-momentum is zero" to a clean spin; a recognised tensor routes through orthogonality constraints to an evolution law. The [[Special Relativity XIV — Angular Momentum and Spin#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in the chapter is assembled from. The recurring difficulty is not the algebra but keeping straight which quantities are intrinsic to the system (the tensor, the spin) and which are relative to an observer (the vector split, the centroid, the energy). When stuck, scan the list.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Build angular momentum as $\overrightarrow{CM}^\flat \wedge p$, then read its tensor components.** The two-form $J_C = \overrightarrow{CM}^\flat \wedge p$ has components $J_{\alpha\beta} = x_\alpha p_\beta - x_\beta p_\alpha$ (raise to get $J^{\alpha\beta} = x^\alpha p^\beta - x^\beta p^\alpha$); see [[Def - Angular Momentum Four-Tensor]]. *Trigger:* any quantity that should be "moment of momentum". *Pattern:* assemble $\overrightarrow{CM}$ and $p$ as four-vectors, antisymmetrise, and the result transforms as a rank-two tensor — every component-level claim follows from the tensor transformation law $J'^{\alpha\beta} = \Lambda^\alpha{}_\mu \Lambda^\beta{}_\nu J^{\mu\nu}$.

2. **Split a two-form into its observer-relative vectors.** Relative to a four-velocity $U_0$, an antisymmetric two-form decomposes into a "magnetic" part (a vector in the rest space, here the angular momentum vector $\vec\sigma_C = \overrightarrow{CM}\times\mathbf{p}$) and an "electric" part (here the mass-energy dipole); this is the same decomposition that splits $F_{\mu\nu}$ into $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{E}$. *Trigger:* you have a covariant two-form and want the three-vectors an observer measures. *Pattern:* contract once with $U_0$ to extract the electric part, and identify the residual space-space block as the cross product giving the magnetic part.

3. **Change the reference point with the inhomogeneous rule.** Moving $C \to C'$ shifts angular momentum by $\overrightarrow{C'C}^\flat \wedge P$, the wedge of the displacement with the *total* four-momentum: $J_{C'} = J_C + \overrightarrow{C'C}^\flat \wedge P$; see [[Thm - Conservation of Angular Momentum]]. *Trigger:* the problem asks for angular momentum about a different point, or asks whether a quantity is point-independent. *Pattern:* the shift vanishes precisely when $P = 0$ (barycentric frame) or when the displacement is along $P$ — which is why the spin is point-independent.

4. **Go to the barycentric (centre-of-momentum) frame.** For an isolated system choose observers comoving with it; then the total three-momentum $\mathbf{P} = 0$, the mass-energy dipole $\vec D = 0$, the energy is the rest mass $E = mc^2$, and the centroid coincides with the centre of inertia $G$; see [[Def - Centre of Inertia]]. *Trigger:* any computation of spin, or of an intrinsic property of an isolated system. *Pattern:* compute in the centre-of-momentum frame where most terms vanish, then transform out — the relativistic analogue of "work in the rest frame".

5. **Strip orbital from spin via König.** Write $J_C = S + \overrightarrow{CG}^\flat \wedge P$, isolating the $C$-independent spin $S$ from the orbital part carried by the centre of inertia; see [[Thm - König Theorem (Relativistic)]]. *Trigger:* you want the intrinsic angular momentum and the problem hands you the total about some point. *Pattern:* subtract the orbital angular momentum of a point particle at $G$ carrying the total momentum; what remains is the spin.

6. **Impose and differentiate the spin supplementary condition.** The spin obeys $S(p,\cdot) = 0$, equivalently $S\cdot U = 0$; differentiating this constraint along the worldline determines otherwise-free scalars; see [[Def - Spin Four-Vector]]. *Trigger:* a spin evolution law with an undetermined multiplier. *Pattern:* differentiate $U\cdot S = 0$, use $U\cdot U = 1$ and the known $dU/d\tau$, and solve for the multiplier (this is how $\lambda = a\cdot s$ appears in $d\vec s/d\tau$).

7. **Compute a four-torque as $\overrightarrow{CM}^\flat \wedge f$.** The evolution of angular momentum is driven by $N_C = dJ_C/d\tau = \overrightarrow{CM}^\flat \wedge f$, with $f$ the four-force; see [[Def - Four-Torque]]. *Trigger:* the system is not isolated and you need $dJ/d\tau$. *Pattern:* the $\overrightarrow{CM}\wedge dp/d\tau$ term is the torque; the $d\overrightarrow{CM}/d\tau \wedge p$ term vanishes because $d\overrightarrow{CM}/d\tau = cU$ is parallel to $p$.

8. **Use Fermi–Walker transport for "non-rotating".** A free gyroscope's spin satisfies $D^{\mathrm{FW}}_u\vec s = 0$; the Fermi–Walker derivative is the proper-time derivative corrected to keep vectors orthogonal to $U$ and is the covariant meaning of "not rotating"; see [[Def - Fermi-Walker Derivative]] and [[Thm - The BMT Equation]]. *Trigger:* a torque-free spin, or any transported frame along an accelerated worldline. *Pattern:* $D^{\mathrm{FW}}_u\vec s = \frac{d\vec s}{d\tau} - (a\cdot s)\,U$; setting it to zero gives precession with $\|\vec s\|$ constant.

9. **Kill a contraction with antisymmetry.** A symmetric object contracted with an antisymmetric one vanishes; this is the workhorse that makes norms conserved and constraints consistent. *Trigger:* you need to show a derivative is zero, e.g. $\frac{d}{d\tau}(s\cdot s) = 0$. *Pattern:* $s\cdot \frac{ds}{d\tau} \propto F(s,s) = 0$ because $F$ is antisymmetric and $s\cdot s$ is symmetric — the reason the BMT equation preserves $\|\vec s\|$.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating angular momentum as a vector in four dimensions
> The Newtonian instinct is that angular momentum is a vector, so one reaches for a four-vector $L^\mu$. There is none: in four dimensions the antisymmetric object $\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}$ has $\binom{4}{2}=6$ independent components, not $4$, and they form a **tensor** $J^{\alpha\beta}$, not a vector. The cross product is a three-dimensional coincidence ($\binom{3}{2}=3$). A genuine four-*vector* does emerge — the spin $S^\mu$ — but only for an isolated system, only as the Hodge dual of the spin part of the tensor contracted with the four-velocity, and it carries three components' worth of information, not six. Writing "$L^\mu = \epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}x_\nu p_\rho \dots$" without a four-velocity to contract against is meaningless. The operation becomes legal exactly when you have an isolated system and contract with its four-velocity to form the Pauli–Lubanski vector.

> [!warning] 2. Assuming the centre of mass is observer-independent
> Every Newtonian reflex says the centre of mass is a fixed point of the system, the same for all observers. In relativity it is not: the **centroid** $G_\mathcal{O} = \frac{1}{E}\sum E_a M_a$ depends on $\mathcal{O}$ in two ways at once — through the rest space in which the positions $M_a$ are taken, and through the energies $E_a$ that weight them, both observer-relative. Two observers in relative motion locate the centre of mass of the same spinning body at points differing by $\vec\sigma\times\vec V/(mc^2)$ ([[Def - Centre of Inertia]]). The intrinsic point — the **centre of inertia** $G$ — exists only because one *selects* the comoving observers and agrees to use their answer. The operation "the centre of mass is at $\dots$" is legal without qualification only for a *non-spinning* system; the moment the spin is nonzero, you must say *relative to which observer*.

> [!warning] 3. Forgetting the spin supplementary condition
> It is tempting to treat the spin four-vector $S^\mu$ as four independent functions of proper time. It is not free: it must satisfy the **spin supplementary condition** $S\cdot U = 0$ (equivalently $S(p,\cdot)=0$), which removes the time-component in the rest frame and leaves the three physical spin components. Dropping it produces an extra, unphysical degree of freedom and an evolution law that fails to preserve $\|\vec s\|$. The condition is not a convention — it is what selects the centre-of-inertia worldline as the one the spin is referred to (the Tulczyjew/Synge condition $J_G(p,\cdot)=0$). Any spin evolution must be checked against $\frac{d}{d\tau}(S\cdot U) = 0$; if your law violates it, you have either the wrong torque or the wrong multiplier.

> [!warning] 4. Using the ordinary time derivative for a transported spin
> For a gyroscope on an accelerated worldline it is tempting to write "no torque, so $\frac{d\vec s}{d\tau} = 0$". That is wrong: the naive derivative does not respect the constraint $\vec s\cdot U = 0$, because $U$ itself is changing. The correct statement of "no torque" is that the **Fermi–Walker derivative** vanishes, $D^{\mathrm{FW}}_u\vec s = 0$, which equals $\frac{d\vec s}{d\tau} - (a\cdot s)U$; the extra term is exactly what keeps $\vec s$ in the rest space as the rest space tilts ([[Thm - The BMT Equation]]). Setting $\frac{d\vec s}{d\tau}=0$ instead of $D^{\mathrm{FW}}_u\vec s = 0$ predicts no precession for a free gyroscope, contradicting the Thomas precession. The naive derivative is legal only along an *inertial* worldline, where $a = 0$ and the two derivatives coincide.

> [!warning] 5. Carrying over Gourgoulhon's signs without flipping the signature
> The source for this chapter works in the mostly-plus signature, where $\vec u_0\cdot\vec u_0 = -c^2$, timelike vectors have negative norm, and the time-projection reads $h = -\vec u_0\cdot\overrightarrow{CM}$. Transcribing his formulas verbatim into our mostly-minus convention flips the sign of every scalar product and several intermediate quantities. The energy he writes as $E = -c\langle p,\vec u_0\rangle$ is, in our signature, $E = P\cdot U_0$; his spin norm $S\cdot S$ (positive in his timelike-negative world for a spacelike $S$) becomes negative for us. The safe operation is to define each observer-relative quantity natively in mostly-minus and only *cross-check* against Gourgoulhon's structure; the tensor $J^{\alpha\beta} = x^\alpha p^\beta - x^\beta p^\alpha$, having no metric in it, is the one object you may copy across unchanged.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this chapter are won by classifying what kind of object you are being asked about — a frame-independent tensor, an observer-relative three-vector, an intrinsic invariant, or an evolution rate — and routing through the move that produces it. The single recurring discipline is keeping the intrinsic and the observer-relative apart.

If the problem **asks you to prove a conservation or invariance law**, the decisive move is to write the object as a derivative and show the derivative vanishes. For a single isolated particle, differentiate $J_C = \overrightarrow{CM}^\flat\wedge p$ along the worldline: the $d\overrightarrow{CM}/d\tau\wedge p$ term dies because $d\overrightarrow{CM}/d\tau = cU \parallel p$, and the $\overrightarrow{CM}\wedge dp/d\tau$ term dies by the [[Thm - Conservation of Angular Momentum|law of inertia]]. For a system, conservation is a first principle, but you still use the same algebra to verify point-independence and observer-independence. For the spin's norm under BMT evolution, the move is operation 9 — contract the evolution against $s$ and watch the field tensor's antisymmetry kill it. The general lesson: a relativistic conservation law is almost always "the right derivative is zero", and the right derivative is found by differentiating the defining wedge or contraction.

If the problem **asks you to decompose a covariant object and interpret the pieces**, go to operation 2 and split with respect to a four-velocity. The angular momentum tensor splits into the angular momentum vector (space-space) and the mass-energy dipole (time-space) exactly as the field tensor splits into $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{E}$ — and the analogy is not loose, because both are antisymmetric two-forms and the decomposition is the same theorem. The [[Thm - König Theorem (Relativistic)|König decomposition]] is a second split, this time of the tensor into a $C$-independent spin and a $C$-dependent orbital part; reach for it whenever you need the intrinsic angular momentum out of a total about an arbitrary point. The skill in both is to track which pieces are observer- or point-relative and which are not.

If the problem **asks where something is**, brace for the answer to depend on the observer. Compute the centroid as the energy-weighted mean position, $\overrightarrow{OG_\mathcal{O}} = \frac1E\sum E_a\overrightarrow{OM_a}$, in the frame the problem specifies — and then, if a second observer is involved, do *not* assume they agree. The displacement between two observers' centroids is $\vec\sigma\times\vec V/(mc^2)$, bounded by the [[Thm - Minimal Size of a Spinning System|Møller radius]]. The intrinsic point, the centre of inertia, is recovered only by selecting comoving observers; its defining property is the Tulczyjew condition $J_G(P,\cdot)=0$. The trap to avoid is the unstated Newtonian premise that the centre of mass is objective (illegal operation 2).

If the problem **asks for an evolution law**, differentiate the defining relation and impose the constraint. The four-torque $N_C = \overrightarrow{CM}^\flat\wedge f$ ([[Def - Four-Torque]]) is the master object; for an inertial observer and a fixed point it reduces to the laboratory torque $d\vec\sigma/dt = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}$ ([[Thm - Evolution of the Angular Momentum Vector]]). For spin, the constraint $S\cdot U = 0$ is what makes the problem tractable: differentiate it to find the Lagrange multiplier, recognise the Fermi–Walker derivative, and read off precession. The BMT equation is exactly this recipe with the torque supplied by the electromagnetic field; the only subtlety is the $(\frac g2 - 1)$ Thomas term, which comes from the difference between the lab-frame field and the rest-frame field.

Threading through everything is one meta-principle: **decide whether the quantity in front of you is intrinsic or observer-relative before you compute anything.** The tensor $J^{\alpha\beta}$, the spin $S^\mu$, the rest mass, the Møller radius are intrinsic; the angular momentum vector, the mass-energy dipole, the centroid, the energy are relative to an observer. Almost every error in the chapter — the wrong sign, the assumed-objective centre of mass, the violated spin constraint — is a category error between these two. Name the category first, and the right operation follows.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Def - Angular Momentum Four-Tensor|The angular momentum tensor]]** $J^{\alpha\beta} = x^\alpha p^\beta - x^\beta p^\alpha$. This is the most-used single object in the chapter because it is the *intrinsic* carrier of all angular momentum information, free of any observer. Reach for it whenever you need a frame-independent statement, whenever you must transform angular momentum between frames (it transforms as an ordinary rank-two tensor, $J'^{\alpha\beta} = \Lambda^\alpha{}_\mu\Lambda^\beta{}_\nu J^{\mu\nu}$), and whenever the Newtonian "angular momentum vector" is leading you astray in four dimensions. **Typical use:** to prove that some quantity built from positions and momenta is conserved or invariant, assemble it as $J^{\alpha\beta}$ and use that the tensor's vanishing (or constancy) in one frame holds in all.

- **The two-form/three-vector split.** Relative to a four-velocity, an antisymmetric two-form splits into an "electric" rest-space vector (extracted by one contraction with $U_0$) and a "magnetic" rest-space vector (the residual space-space block as a cross product). **Typical use:** to recover the laboratory three-vectors an observer measures — the angular momentum vector $\vec\sigma_C = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}$ and the mass-energy dipole — from the covariant tensor. The decisive content is that this is *the same* decomposition that produces $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ from $F_{\mu\nu}$, so every intuition about how boosts mix $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ transfers verbatim to how boosts mix angular momentum and mass-energy dipole.

- **[[Thm - König Theorem (Relativistic)|The König decomposition]]** $J_C = S + \overrightarrow{CG}^\flat\wedge P$. Its recognisable setup is "total angular momentum about an arbitrary point, want the intrinsic part". The reusable content is twofold: the spin $S$ is the *whole* of the $C$-independent angular momentum, and the orbital part is exactly the angular momentum of a fictitious point mass at the centre of inertia carrying the total momentum. **Typical use:** strip the orbital contribution to isolate spin, or — run backwards — assemble the total angular momentum of a composite by adding the spin of each part to the orbital angular momentum of its centre of inertia. This is the relativistic upgrade of the Newtonian centre-of-mass theorem, and it is the organising identity of the whole "centre of inertia and spin" circle of ideas.

- **The spin supplementary condition** $S\cdot U = 0$ (equivalently $S(p,\cdot)=0$, the Tulczyjew condition $J_G(p,\cdot)=0$). Its typical use is to cut the spin four-vector down from four components to the three physical ones and, crucially, to *determine evolution*: differentiating the constraint along the worldline pins down the Lagrange multiplier in any spin equation of motion. **Typical use:** in deriving $\frac{d\vec s}{d\tau}$ for a free gyroscope or under BMT, differentiate $U\cdot S = 0$ to find the coefficient of the $U$ term, then recognise the Fermi–Walker derivative. It is also the condition that fixes *which* worldline the spin is referred to — the centre-of-inertia worldline.

- **[[Def - Fermi-Walker Derivative|Fermi–Walker transport]]** $D^{\mathrm{FW}}_u\vec s = \frac{d\vec s}{d\tau} - (a\cdot s)U$. This is the covariant meaning of "the spin is not being torqued", and it is the workhorse of every gyroscope and spin-precession calculation. **Typical use:** set $D^{\mathrm{FW}}_u\vec s = 0$ for a free gyroscope to get precession with constant $\|\vec s\|$; set $D^{\mathrm{FW}}_u\vec s$ equal to the electromagnetic torque to get the BMT equation. The reusable skill is recognising that "no rotation" along an accelerated worldline is *not* a vanishing ordinary derivative but a vanishing Fermi–Walker derivative — the extra $(a\cdot s)U$ term is the source of the Thomas precession.

# Bridges

1. **Electromagnetism — the angular momentum tensor is the mechanical twin of the field tensor.** The electromagnetic field is the antisymmetric two-form $F_{\mu\nu}$ whose six components an observer reads as the electric field $\mathbf{E}$ (the time-space block) and the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ (the space-space block); a boost rotates one into the other. The [[Def - Angular Momentum Four-Tensor|angular momentum tensor]] $J_{\alpha\beta}$ is the identical structure for mechanics: its time-space block is the mass-energy dipole moment $\vec D$ and its space-space block is the angular momentum vector $\vec\sigma$, and a boost mixes them just as it mixes $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. The shared mathematics is the decomposition of an antisymmetric two-form with respect to a four-velocity, established once and used in both places. The analogy even extends to invariants: just as $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} \propto B^2 - E^2$ and ${\star}F\cdot F \propto \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ are the two Lorentz scalars of the field, $J_{\alpha\beta}J^{\alpha\beta} \propto \sigma^2 - D^2$ and the dual contraction $\propto \vec\sigma\cdot\vec D$ are the two Lorentz scalars of the angular momentum. The field tensor is built in [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field]]; the parallel is not a coincidence but a statement that "antisymmetric two-form" is the universal home of a quantity that splits into two observer-relative three-vectors.

2. **The Poincaré group and quantum field theory — spin is the second Casimir.** The conserved currents of the [[Def - The Poincaré Group|Poincaré group]] are the four-momentum $P^\alpha$ (from translations) and the angular momentum tensor $J^{\alpha\beta}$ (from Lorentz transformations). Contracting them through the Levi-Civita tensor produces the [[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group|Pauli–Lubanski vector]] $W^\mu = -\tfrac12\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}J_{\nu\rho}P_\sigma$, and the two scalars $P^2$ and $W^2$ are the two Casimir invariants — the only quantities commuting with every Poincaré generator. Wigner's theorem says an elementary particle *is* an irreducible unitary representation of the Poincaré group, and such representations are labelled by exactly these two numbers: $P^2 = m^2$ fixes the **mass**, $W^2 = -m^2 s(s+1)$ fixes the **spin**. The classical [[Def - Spin Four-Vector|spin four-vector]] of this chapter, $S^\mu = W^\mu/(mc)$, is the classical shadow of that quantum spin: it obeys $S\cdot U = 0$ because $W\cdot P = 0$ identically, and its magnitude is the classical $|\vec\sigma|$ that becomes $\sqrt{s(s+1)}\,\hbar$ upon quantisation. This chapter is therefore the classical mechanics that the representation theory of [[Special Relativity XII — Inertial Observers and the Poincaré Group]] quantises.

3. **Differential geometry — Fermi–Walker transport is parallel transport along an accelerated worldline.** On a curved manifold a vector is carried "without rotation" along a curve by parallel transport with respect to the [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation|connection]]; the covariant derivative along the curve vanishes. In flat spacetime there is no curvature, so an *inertial* observer transports a vector simply by keeping its components constant. But an *accelerated* observer cannot: a vector constrained to the rest space must tilt as the rest space tilts, and the correct "no rotation" rule is the [[Def - Fermi-Walker Derivative|Fermi–Walker derivative]], $D^{\mathrm{FW}}_u\vec s = \frac{d\vec s}{d\tau} - (a\cdot s)U + (U\cdot s)a$. The extra terms beyond the ordinary derivative are exactly the minimal correction that keeps the transported vector orthogonal to $U$ and undoes the spurious rotation a boost would otherwise introduce. A free gyroscope's spin is Fermi–Walker transported, and the residual rotation it accumulates around a closed accelerated loop — the Thomas precession — is the flat-spacetime analogue of the holonomy a parallel-transported vector picks up around a loop on a curved surface. The bridge runs forward into [[General Relativity I — Einstein's Equations and Schwarzschild|general relativity]], where gyroscope precession (geodetic and frame-dragging) becomes a measurable probe of spacetime curvature.

4. **Atomic and particle physics — the BMT equation measures $g-2$.** The [[Thm - The BMT Equation|Bargmann–Michel–Telegdi equation]] $\frac{d\vec s}{d\tau} = \frac{q}{mc}[\frac g2\vec F(\cdot,\vec s) + (\frac g2 - 1)F(\vec u,\vec s)\vec u]$ governs how a charged spin precesses in an electromagnetic field, and the structure of its two terms is precisely tuned to experiment. The $\frac g2$ term is the magnetic-moment precession (Larmor), and the $(\frac g2 - 1)$ term is the kinematic Thomas precession that survives even for a "naive" $g=1$. In a storage ring the spin precesses at the rate set by $g$ while the momentum precesses at the rate set by the cyclotron motion; the *difference* of the two rates is proportional to the **anomalous magnetic moment** $a = (g-2)/2$, because the cyclotron term is exactly the part of the BMT precession that tracks the momentum. The muon $g-2$ experiments measure $a$ to sub-ppm precision by watching this differential precession, and the BMT equation is the classical kinematics they invert. In an atom the same Thomas term, applied to the electron accelerated by the nuclear Coulomb field, supplies the factor of $\tfrac12$ that makes the spin–orbit coupling energy match the observed hydrogen fine structure — the historical origin of Thomas's 1927 calculation.

5. **Newtonian mechanics — the relativistic theorems collapse to the textbook ones.** Every result here has a Newtonian limit, and tracking the limit is the surest check on the relativistic formula. The angular momentum tensor's space-space block is the Newtonian $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}$ exactly; the time-space block, the mass-energy dipole $\vec D = \frac{1}{c^2}\sum E_a\overrightarrow{OM_a}$, reduces as $E_a \to m_ac^2$ to $\sum m_a\overrightarrow{OM_a}$, the *first moment of mass* whose vanishing defines the Newtonian centre of mass. The [[Thm - König Theorem (Relativistic)|König theorem]] $J_C = S + \overrightarrow{CG}^\flat\wedge P$ is literally the Newtonian theorem "total angular momentum equals spin about the centre of mass plus orbital angular momentum of the centre of mass" — the same statement, lifted from three-vectors to two-forms. The [[Thm - Evolution of the Angular Momentum Vector|torque law]] $d\vec\sigma/dt = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}$ is recovered verbatim for an inertial observer and a fixed point. What has no Newtonian shadow is the observer-dependence of the centroid and the Møller bound on size — both vanish in the $c\to\infty$ limit, where $\vec\sigma\times\vec V/(mc^2)\to 0$ and the centre of mass becomes objective again. The relativistic theory is the Newtonian one plus exactly the corrections of order $v^2/c^2$ that turn an objective centre of mass into an observer-relative one.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: angular momentum is an antisymmetric two-form, and everything else is bookkeeping.** The temptation is to think of relativistic angular momentum as "the Newtonian $\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}$ with relativistic corrections". The right picture is that it was never a vector at all — it is the antisymmetric tensor $J^{\alpha\beta} = x^\alpha p^\beta - x^\beta p^\alpha$, and the three-dimensional vector was a low-dimensional disguise that worked only because $\binom 32 = 3$. Once you internalise this, the entire chapter is predictable. A two-form relative to an observer splits into two three-vectors, so angular momentum must split into two three-vectors — and the second one, the mass-energy dipole, is not a relativistic correction to anything Newtonian but a genuinely new object that was hiding in the time-space components all along. The same two-form structure makes the bridge to electromagnetism exact rather than analogical: $J_{\alpha\beta}$ and $F_{\mu\nu}$ are the same kind of object, so they have the same decomposition, the same boost-mixing, the same pair of Lorentz invariants. And the same structure makes the spin a *vector* again — but a four-vector, the Hodge dual of the spin part contracted with the four-velocity, recovering three components from six because the supplementary condition $S\cdot U=0$ removes one and antisymmetry removes two. The single sentence to carry away: in four dimensions the home of angular momentum is $\Lambda^2$ (the antisymmetric two-tensors), and every named object in the chapter is a projection of one element of $\Lambda^2$.

**The true name of spin is "the angular momentum that does not depend on where you measure it from".** The official definition of spin goes through the centre of inertia, the barycentric frame, the Pauli–Lubanski vector — machinery that obscures what spin *is for*. The operational characterisation is far simpler: the spin is the part of the angular momentum that is *independent of the reference point $C$*. Change the point you take moments about, and the orbital angular momentum $\overrightarrow{C'C}^\flat\wedge P$ changes, but the spin does not — that point-independence is the [[Def - Spin Four-Vector|definition]] that matters in practice. This is why the spin deserves to be called *intrinsic*: it is the angular momentum you cannot make go away by choosing a clever origin, the residue after all orbital motion is subtracted. The same characterisation explains the supplementary condition: $S\cdot U = 0$ is precisely the statement that the spin has been referred to the one worldline — the centre of inertia — about which the orbital part vanishes. When you meet "spin" in a problem, do not picture a spinning top; picture the $C$-independent piece of $J_C$, and reach for the König decomposition.

**Indefiniteness strikes again: the centre of mass is observer-dependent, and the spin bounds how far it can wander.** The most counterintuitive fact in the chapter — that two observers disagree on where a body's centre of mass is — has the same root as every other relativistic surprise: the indefinite metric and the finite speed of light. The centroid is an energy-weighted average of positions, and *both* the positions (taken in a rest space that tilts with the observer) and the weights (energies, which transform) depend on the observer. The displacement between two observers' answers is $\vec\sigma\times\vec V/(mc^2)$ — manifestly of order $v^2/c^2$ once you note $E = mc^2$ at leading order, hence invisible to Newton and unavoidable to Einstein. What makes this more than a curiosity is that it is *bounded*: the centroid ranges over a disk of radius $R_0 = \|\vec\sigma\|/(mc)$, the Møller radius, and never further. This bound is the chapter's deepest theorem because it reverses the usual direction of inference. Normally relativity tells you that some quantity you thought was absolute is relative; here it tells you that the *spread* of the relative answers is controlled by an absolute invariant, the spin, and that this same invariant forbids a spinning body from being smaller than $R_0$. A point particle can carry momentum and energy, but it cannot carry spin — the classical theory says so through the Møller bound, and the quantum theory agrees by setting $R_0$ equal to the Compton wavelength below which "position" loses meaning.

**The free gyroscope already knows about Thomas precession — it is geometry, not dynamics.** A surprising amount of spin physics is purely kinematic. A free gyroscope feels no torque, yet its spin precesses; one is tempted to look for a force, but there is none. The precession is geometry: the spin is Fermi–Walker transported, and Fermi–Walker transport along a bent worldline accumulates a rotation for the same reason a vector parallel-transported around a loop on a sphere comes back rotated. The dynamical inputs — the electromagnetic field, the charge, the Landé factor — enter only when the gyroscope is *not* free; and even then, the BMT equation cleanly separates the dynamical magnetic-moment precession (the $\frac g2$ term) from the kinematic Thomas precession (the $(\frac g2-1)$ term, present at $g=1$). This separation is what makes $g-2$ measurable: the kinematic part is universal and known, so any *extra* precession is a clean readout of the deviation of $g$ from $2$. The lesson for a problem-solver is to distinguish, in any spin-precession problem, the part that comes from the worldline's shape (always there, computable from the four-acceleration alone) from the part that comes from a genuine magnetic moment (proportional to the field and to $g$). Mistaking one for the other is the classic error, and it is exactly the error Thomas corrected in 1927 when the factor of two in the hydrogen fine structure refused to come out right.
