# Problem Statement

Use the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula to show that the composition of two boosts in *different* directions is a boost times a rotation — the Thomas rotation — and that the rotation is generated by the $[K,K] = -J$ structure relation.

1. State the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula to second order and apply it to $\Lambda_1 = \exp(\psi_1 K_1)$ (a boost along $x$) and $\Lambda_2 = \exp(\psi_2 K_2)$ (a boost along $y$).
2. Show that the leading correction term $\tfrac12[\psi_1 K_1, \psi_2 K_2]$ is a *rotation* generator, and compute it.
3. Conclude that to leading order in the rapidities, $\Lambda_1\Lambda_2$ is a boost composed with a rotation about $z$ by angle $\approx \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2$ (the **Thomas–Wigner rotation angle** at small rapidity).
4. Explain why this rotation is absent for *collinear* boosts, and why it is the source of Thomas precession.

**Recall:**

![[Thm - The Exponential Map Generates the Restricted Lorentz Group#Statement]]

The structure relation $[K_1, K_2] = -J_3$ ([[Def - Generators and Structure Constants]]) says two boost generators commute into minus a rotation generator. The Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula gives $\exp(X)\exp(Y) = \exp(X + Y + \tfrac12[X,Y] + \cdots)$. The [[Def - Thomas Rotation|Thomas rotation]] is the spatial rotation produced when two non-collinear boosts are composed.

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** An *extract-a-composition-law* problem: compose two boosts via Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff and find the Thomas rotation. The [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic strategy]] says non-commuting compositions route through BCH, whose leading correction is a single commutator from the structure constants.

**Assumption pattern.** Two boosts in perpendicular directions, with generators $K_1$ and $K_2$ that do *not* commute: $[K_1, K_2] = -J_3 \ne 0$. The signpost is the non-vanishing boost-boost commutator, which guarantees a non-trivial BCH correction — and that correction is a *rotation* generator, not a boost.

**Theorem routing.** Part 1: write $\Lambda_1\Lambda_2 = \exp(\psi_1 K_1)\exp(\psi_2 K_2) = \exp(\psi_1 K_1 + \psi_2 K_2 + \tfrac12[\psi_1 K_1, \psi_2 K_2] + \cdots)$ ([[Thm - The Exponential Map Generates the Restricted Lorentz Group]]). Part 2: $\tfrac12[\psi_1 K_1, \psi_2 K_2] = \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2[K_1, K_2] = -\tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2 J_3$, a rotation generator ([[Def - Generators and Structure Constants]]). Part 3: the exponent contains boost generators ($\psi_1 K_1 + \psi_2 K_2$) plus a rotation generator ($-\tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2 J_3$), so the product is a boost times a rotation by $\approx \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2$. Part 4: collinear boosts have $[K, K] = 0$, no correction.

**Key decision point.** The crux is recognising that the BCH correction term $\tfrac12[K_1, K_2] = -\tfrac12 J_3$ is a *rotation* generator appearing in a composition of two *boosts* — manufactured entirely by the non-commutativity recorded in $[K, K] = -J$. The temptation is to think the product of two boosts is a boost (as for collinear boosts); the non-vanishing commutator is precisely the obstruction, and it produces a real, physical rotation. The subtlety in part 3 is that the exponent is not purely a boost — the rotation generator's presence means the product factors as boost $\times$ rotation, the Thomas rotation.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff: $\exp(X)\exp(Y) = \exp(X + Y + \tfrac12[X,Y] + \tfrac1{12}[X,[X,Y]] - \cdots)$. With $X = \psi_1 K_1$, $Y = \psi_2 K_2$, keep through the $\tfrac12[X,Y]$ term for the leading effect.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> $[X, Y] = [\psi_1 K_1, \psi_2 K_2] = \psi_1\psi_2[K_1, K_2]$. Now use the structure relation $[K_1, K_2] = -J_3$. So $\tfrac12[X,Y] = -\tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2 J_3$ — a *rotation* generator.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> The exponent is $\psi_1 K_1 + \psi_2 K_2 - \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2 J_3 + \cdots$. The first two terms are boost generators; the third is a rotation generator. So the product is (to leading order) a boost times a rotation about $z$ by angle $\approx \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2$.

> [!note]- Hint 4
> For collinear boosts (both along $x$, say), the generators are $\psi_1 K_1$ and $\psi_2 K_1$, which commute ($[K_1, K_1] = 0$). Then BCH truncates to $\exp((\psi_1+\psi_2)K_1)$ — a pure boost, no rotation. The Thomas rotation requires *non-collinear* boosts.

# Solution

Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff gives the exponent of $\Lambda_1\Lambda_2$ as the sum of the boost generators plus the correction $\tfrac12[\psi_1 K_1, \psi_2 K_2] = -\tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2 J_3$ — a rotation generator. So two perpendicular boosts compose into a boost times a rotation about $z$ by $\approx \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2$. Collinear boosts commute, giving no rotation.

**Step 1: Apply Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Take $\Lambda_1 = \exp(\psi_1 K_1)$ (boost of rapidity $\psi_1$ along $x$) and $\Lambda_2 = \exp(\psi_2 K_2)$ (boost of rapidity $\psi_2$ along $y$). By [[Thm - The Exponential Map Generates the Restricted Lorentz Group|Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff]],
> $$\Lambda_1\Lambda_2 = \exp(\psi_1 K_1)\exp(\psi_2 K_2) = \exp\!\Big(\psi_1 K_1 + \psi_2 K_2 + \tfrac12[\psi_1 K_1, \psi_2 K_2] + \cdots\Big),$$
> where the omitted terms are higher-order iterated commutators (cubic and beyond in the rapidities). For small rapidities $\psi_1, \psi_2$, the displayed terms dominate, with the $\tfrac12[\cdot,\cdot]$ being the leading correction to the naive guess $\exp(\psi_1 K_1 + \psi_2 K_2)$.

**Step 2: The correction is a rotation generator.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Evaluate the commutator using bilinearity and the structure relation:
> $$\tfrac12[\psi_1 K_1, \psi_2 K_2] = \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2[K_1, K_2] = \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2\cdot(-J_3) = -\tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2\,J_3.$$
> This is a *rotation* generator — $J_3$, generating rotations in the $x$–$y$ plane — even though both inputs $K_1, K_2$ are *boost* generators. The appearance of $J_3$ is forced by the structure relation $[K_1, K_2] = -J_3$: the commutator of two boost generators is minus a rotation generator. So the exponent of $\Lambda_1\Lambda_2$ contains a rotation piece that was in neither factor.

**Step 3: The product is a boost times a Thomas rotation.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> The exponent is
> $$\psi_1 K_1 + \psi_2 K_2 - \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2\,J_3 + O(\psi^3).$$
> The first two terms are boost generators (combining to a boost in some direction in the $x$–$y$ plane); the third is a rotation generator. To leading order the product factors as
> $$\Lambda_1\Lambda_2 \approx \underbrace{\exp(\psi_1 K_1 + \psi_2 K_2)}_{\text{a boost}}\cdot\underbrace{\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2 J_3\big)}_{\text{a rotation about } z},$$
> a boost composed with a **rotation about the $z$-axis by angle**
> $$\Delta\theta \approx \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2$$
> (the sign indicating the sense of rotation). This is the **Thomas–Wigner rotation**: the composition of two non-collinear boosts is *not* a pure boost but a boost together with a spatial rotation. The angle $\tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2$ is the small-rapidity (low-velocity) limit; for general rapidities the exact angle is larger, obtained by summing the full BCH series or by the [[Def - Thomas Rotation|Thomas rotation]] formula, but the *existence* and the leading magnitude are entirely fixed by the single structure relation $[K_1, K_2] = -J_3$.

**Step 4: Collinear boosts and Thomas precession.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> *Collinear case.* If both boosts are along $x$, the generators are $\psi_1 K_1$ and $\psi_2 K_1$, which commute: $[K_1, K_1] = 0$. The BCH series truncates,
> $$\exp(\psi_1 K_1)\exp(\psi_2 K_1) = \exp\big((\psi_1 + \psi_2)K_1\big),$$
> a *pure boost* of rapidity $\psi_1 + \psi_2$ — no rotation. So the Thomas rotation is absent for collinear boosts; it requires the boosts to point in *different* directions, so that $[K_a, K_b] \ne 0$. This is the precise sense in which the Thomas rotation is a consequence of the *non-commutativity* of boosts.
>
> *Thomas precession.* A particle moving on a curved trajectory — for instance an electron orbiting a nucleus, or a gyroscope in a circular orbit — has a velocity whose *direction* continuously changes. In its instantaneous rest frame, advancing along the trajectory is a sequence of infinitesimal boosts in *slightly different directions*, and each successive pair fails to commute, contributing a small Thomas rotation. Integrated around the orbit, these accumulate into a net rotation of the particle's spin (or gyroscope axis) relative to the lab frame — the **Thomas precession**, with rate $\boldsymbol\Omega_{\text{Thomas}} = \tfrac{\gamma^2}{\gamma+1}\,\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{v}$ (in the $c=1$ convention). This precession is observable: it contributes a factor of $\tfrac12$ to the spin–orbit coupling in atomic fine structure, a discrepancy that puzzled physicists until Thomas explained it in 1926 as exactly this relativistic-kinematic effect. The whole phenomenon traces back to the structure relation $[K, K] = -J$: boosts in different directions commute into a rotation, and a curving worldline integrates those rotations into a precession.

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> For $\Lambda_1 = \exp(\psi_1 K_1)$, $\Lambda_2 = \exp(\psi_2 K_2)$, Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff gives $\Lambda_1\Lambda_2 = \exp(\psi_1 K_1 + \psi_2 K_2 + \tfrac12[\psi_1 K_1, \psi_2 K_2] + \cdots)$. The correction is $\tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2[K_1, K_2] = \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2(-J_3) = -\tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2 J_3$, a rotation generator. So the exponent is (boost generators) $+ (-\tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2 J_3)$, and the product factors to leading order as a boost times a rotation about $z$ by angle $\approx \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2$ — the Thomas rotation. For collinear boosts the generators commute ($[K_1, K_1] = 0$), BCH truncates to $\exp((\psi_1+\psi_2)K_1)$, a pure boost with no rotation. The Thomas rotation is thus a direct consequence of $[K, K] = -J$ (non-commuting boosts), and integrated around a curved worldline it produces the Thomas precession of a particle's spin, with rate $\tfrac{\gamma^2}{\gamma+1}\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{v}$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**Two non-collinear boosts compose into a boost plus a rotation, and Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff extracts the rotation from one commutator.** The composition of boosts in different directions is *not* a boost — it is a boost times a rotation, the Thomas–Wigner rotation — and the entire effect is visible in the leading BCH correction $\tfrac12[K_1, K_2] = -\tfrac12 J_3$. The reusable method: to compose two non-commuting group elements, write each as an exponential, apply BCH, and read the leading correction as a single commutator computed from the structure constants. The trigger for expecting a surprise in a composition is a non-vanishing commutator of the generators; the magnitude of the surprise is the size of that commutator. For boosts, the commutator $[K, K] = -J$ is a rotation, so composing boosts produces rotations — a purely relativistic effect with no Newtonian analogue, since Galilean boosts commute. The Thomas rotation angle $\approx \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2$ at small rapidity is the direct numerical output of this one commutator.

**The Thomas rotation is the physical face of "$[K, K] = -J$", the most consequential structure relation in the chapter.** The abstract algebraic fact that two boost generators commute into minus a rotation generator is not a curiosity — it is the reason the boosts do not form a subgroup, the reason the algebra splits over $\mathbb{C}$ (the minus sign is $i^2$), and the reason a particle on a curved trajectory undergoes Thomas precession. The reusable principle is that structure constants have physical consequences: a non-vanishing $[K_a, K_b]$ means boosts in different planes interfere, and integrated along a worldline this interference becomes a measurable precession of spin. The chain runs: the structure relation $[K, K] = -J$ $\Rightarrow$ the BCH correction is a rotation $\Rightarrow$ composing boosts yields the Thomas rotation $\Rightarrow$ a curving worldline accumulates Thomas precession $\Rightarrow$ the factor of $\tfrac12$ in atomic spin–orbit coupling. This is a model for how the deepest material of the chapter — the Lie algebra structure — connects to observable physics, and it is why the minus sign in $[K, K] = -J$ deserves to be singled out as the algebraic heart of the Lorentz group.

**Collinear composition is trivial precisely because the generators commute — the contrast isolates what causes the rotation.** When two boosts are collinear, their generators are proportional and commute, so BCH truncates to ordinary addition of rapidities and the product is a pure boost with no rotation. This contrast is diagnostic: it shows that the Thomas rotation is caused *specifically* by the non-commutativity of boosts in different directions, not by boosting per se. The reusable insight is that a composition law is "simple" (parameters add) exactly when the generators commute, and "complicated" (with extra induced transformations) exactly when they do not — and the extra transformation is always the commutator. For boosts, collinear means commuting means no rotation; non-collinear means non-commuting means a Thomas rotation. The same dichotomy governs every Lie group: coaxial rotations add angles (commuting), but rotations about different axes do not (non-commuting), and the failure is again a commutator. Recognising "do the generators commute?" as the first question in any composition is the transferable lesson, and the Thomas rotation is the cleanest physical example of what happens when the answer is no.
