# Notation

We set $c = 1$, $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$. The [[Def - Lie Algebra of the Lorentz Group|Lorentz algebra]] is $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ with generators $K_i$ (boosts) and $J_i$ (rotations); the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|restricted Lorentz group]] is $SO^+(1,3)$. The **matrix exponential** of a real $4\times 4$ matrix $\omega$ is $\exp(\omega) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \omega^n/n!$, convergent for every $\omega$. A unit spatial vector is $\mathbf{n} = n^i\mathbf{e}_i$ with $\sum_i (n^i)^2 = 1$; rapidity is $\psi$, rotation angle is $\varphi$. Full registry on [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]].

---

# Statement

> **Theorem (exponential map onto the restricted Lorentz group).** The matrix exponential restricts to a well-defined map
> $$\exp : \mathfrak{so}(1,3) \longrightarrow SO^+(1,3), \qquad \omega \longmapsto \exp(\omega) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\omega^n}{n!},$$
> sending every Lorentz generator to a restricted (proper orthochronous) Lorentz transformation. The boost generators exponentiate to boosts and the rotation generators to rotations:
> $$\exp(\psi\,n^i K_i) = \text{boost of rapidity } \psi \text{ in the plane } \mathrm{Span}(\mathbf{e}_0,\mathbf{n}), \qquad \exp(\varphi\,n^i J_i) = \text{rotation by } \varphi \text{ about } \mathbf{n}.$$
> Moreover the map is **surjective**: every element of $SO^+(1,3)$ is the exponential of a single element of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$.

> **Corollary (Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff and the structure constants).** For $\Lambda_1 = \exp(L_1)$ and $\Lambda_2 = \exp(L_2)$ in $SO^+(1,3)$,
> $$\Lambda_1\Lambda_2 = \exp\!\Big(L_1 + L_2 + \tfrac12[L_1,L_2] + \tfrac{1}{12}[L_1,[L_1,L_2]] - \tfrac{1}{12}[L_2,[L_1,L_2]] + \cdots\Big),$$
> a series of iterated brackets, so the entire group law of $SO^+(1,3)$ is determined by the [[Def - Generators and Structure Constants|structure constants]].

# Motivation

The [[Def - Lie Algebra of the Lorentz Group|Lie algebra]] $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is the infinitesimal shadow of the Lorentz group — the tangent space at the identity, capturing transformations infinitely close to doing nothing. The pressing question is whether this shadow is enough: can the *finite* transformations, the actual boosts and rotations that move events by macroscopic amounts, be recovered from the infinitesimal generators? If they cannot, the Lie algebra is a curiosity; if they can, the Lie algebra *is* the group, repackaged as linear data, and the entire study of the Lorentz group reduces to the linear algebra of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$.

This theorem is the affirmative answer, and it is the bridge that makes the whole Lie-theoretic programme work. The mechanism is the exponential map. Intuitively, a finite boost of rapidity $\psi$ is built by applying an infinitesimal boost of rapidity $\psi/N$ a total of $N$ times and letting $N \to \infty$; the limit $\lim_{N\to\infty}(\mathrm{Id} + \psi K/N)^N$ is exactly $\exp(\psi K)$. So the exponential is the integral of the infinitesimal — the finite transformation assembled from infinitely many infinitesimal ones — and the theorem says this assembly lands precisely in the restricted Lorentz group and reaches all of it.

The surjectivity claim is the deep and surprising part. For a *compact* group such as $SO(3)$, surjectivity of the exponential is standard (every rotation has an axis and an angle, hence is $\exp$ of an angular-velocity generator). For the Lorentz group, which is *non-compact*, surjectivity is not automatic and in fact fails for many non-compact groups — for the related group $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ the exponential is *not* onto. The remarkable fact, special to $SO^+(1,3)$, is that the exponential is nonetheless surjective: a single exponential of a single generator suffices to reach any restricted Lorentz transformation. This is what licenses the slogan that the restricted Lorentz group is "generated by its Lie algebra" and underwrites the use of Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff to compute compositions — including the composition of two boosts, where the formula produces the Thomas rotation as a corollary.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The theorem's precondition is "$\omega \in \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$", i.e. $\omega$ is an $\eta$-skew generator. The point is to recognise the disguises.

The first disguised source is **"a physical boost or rotation is specified by a continuous parameter"**. Any time a problem gives a boost by its rapidity, or a rotation by its angle, the transformation is $\exp(t\,G)$ for the corresponding generator $G$ and parameter $t$ — so the exponential representation is available even when no generator is written down. The bridge is that a one-parameter family of Lorentz transformations $\Lambda(t)$ with $\Lambda(0) = \mathrm{Id}$ and $\Lambda(s)\Lambda(t) = \Lambda(s+t)$ is necessarily $\exp(tG)$ with $G = \dot\Lambda(0) \in \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$. *Example problem:* show that the set of boosts in a fixed plane is a one-parameter subgroup isomorphic to $(\mathbb{R},+)$, parametrised by rapidity.

The second disguised source is **"a matrix $\omega$ satisfies $\eta\,\omega$ antisymmetric"**, however it arose. A generator may appear not as "a boost generator" but as the velocity $\dot\Lambda(0)$ of some curve, or as the $\omega$ in $\Lambda = \mathrm{Id} + \omega + \cdots$ extracted from a near-identity transformation ([[Def - Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations]]). Once $\eta\,\omega$ is checked to be antisymmetric, the theorem guarantees $\exp(\omega)$ is a genuine restricted Lorentz transformation. *Example problem:* given the antisymmetric 4-rotation $\Omega$ of an observer's [[Def - Local Frame and Four-Rotation|local frame]], conclude that the frame's evolution over a proper-time step is the Lorentz transformation $\exp(\mathrm{d}\tau\,\Omega)$.

The third disguised source is **"a transformation lies in the identity component"**. To *apply surjectivity* — to write a given $\Lambda$ as $\exp(\omega)$ — one needs $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$, the connected component of the identity. The bridge is that $\det\Lambda = +1$ and $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$ together place $\Lambda$ in the restricted group, hence in the range of $\exp$. *Example problem:* show that the proper orthochronous transformation produced by composing two boosts (a boost times a Thomas rotation) is itself $\exp(\omega)$ for some single generator $\omega$.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$\exp(\omega) \in SO^+(1,3)$, and every element of $SO^+(1,3)$ is reached".

Combine the conclusion with **the addition law for commuting generators**. If $[L_1,L_2] = 0$ then $\exp(L_1)\exp(L_2) = \exp(L_1 + L_2)$, so collinear boosts (same plane, hence $[K,K]$-commuting in that plane) compose by *adding rapidities* and coaxial rotations by *adding angles*. The further result is the entire one-parameter-subgroup structure: the boosts in a fixed plane form a copy of $(\mathbb{R},+)$ under composition, recovering [[Thm - Boosts Compose by Adding Rapidities]]. The combination is useful because it turns a matrix-multiplication problem into scalar addition.

Combine the conclusion with **the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula for non-commuting generators**. When $[L_1,L_2]\ne 0$, the product $\exp(L_1)\exp(L_2)$ is $\exp$ of a series of iterated brackets. The further result, applied to two boosts in *different* planes, is that their product is a boost times a rotation — the [[Def - Thomas Rotation|Thomas rotation]] — read off from the $[K,K] = -J$ relation appearing in the BCH series. The combination is nonobvious because the rotation appears even though neither factor is a rotation; it is manufactured entirely by the non-commutativity recorded in the structure constants.

Combine the conclusion with **the determinant identity** $\det\exp(\omega) = e^{\mathrm{tr}\,\omega}$. Since every generator has $\mathrm{tr}\,\omega = 0$, every exponential has $\det = +1$, confirming the image lands in the *proper* Lorentz group. The further result is a clean proof that $\exp$ cannot reach the improper components ($\det = -1$) or the antichronous ones: those are disconnected from the identity, and the continuous image of the connected vector space $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ must stay in one connected component.

# Why Is It True

The theorem has two halves — that exponentials land in $SO^+(1,3)$, and that they fill it — and each has a clean intuition.

That **exponentials land in the Lorentz group** is a transcription of the algebra condition into a group condition. The generator condition is $\eta\,\omega = -(\eta\,\omega)^{\mathsf T}$, equivalently $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta = -\eta\,\omega$, equivalently $\omega^{\mathsf T} = -\eta\,\omega\,\eta$ (using $\eta^2 = \mathrm{Id}$). Exponentiate: $\exp(\omega^{\mathsf T}) = \exp(-\eta\,\omega\,\eta)$. The left side is $(\exp\omega)^{\mathsf T}$ because transposition commutes with the power series, and the right side is $\eta\exp(-\omega)\eta = \eta(\exp\omega)^{-1}\eta$ because conjugation commutes with the power series and $\exp(-\omega) = (\exp\omega)^{-1}$. So $(\exp\omega)^{\mathsf T} = \eta(\exp\omega)^{-1}\eta$, which rearranges to $(\exp\omega)^{\mathsf T}\eta\,(\exp\omega) = \eta$ — the defining equation of the Lorentz group. **The single mechanism is that exponentiation turns the *infinitesimal* skew condition $\omega^{\mathsf T} = -\eta\omega\eta$ into the *finite* isometry condition $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$, because $\exp$ commutes with both transposition and $\eta$-conjugation.** That $\det = +1$ and the result lands in the *identity component* then follows because $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is connected (it is a vector space), $\exp$ is continuous, and $\exp(0) = \mathrm{Id}$, so the whole image is trapped in the connected component containing $\mathrm{Id}$, namely $SO^+(1,3)$.

That **exponentials fill the restricted group** is the harder, non-compact statement. The intuition rests on the [[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group|polar decomposition]]: every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ factors as a boost times a rotation, $\Lambda = B\,R$. Boosts are exponentials of boost generators (a boost of rapidity $\psi$ in a plane is $\exp(\psi K)$, shown below), and rotations are exponentials of rotation generators (every rotation has an axis and angle, so $R = \exp(\varphi\,\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{J})$). The non-trivial fact — that the *product* $BR$ is again a single exponential rather than merely a product of two — is special to $SO^+(1,3)$ and is what fails for $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$; it can be proved by a careful analysis of eigenvalues, but the conceptual takeaway is that the Lorentz group is "exponential" in the strong sense that one generator always suffices. For computations one rarely needs the single-exponential form; the polar form $B\,R$ with each factor an exponential is enough, and that much is elementary.

The reason boosts come out *hyperbolic* and rotations *circular* under the exponential is the sign of the generator's square. For a boost, $K^2$ is a positive projector ($K_1^2 = \mathrm{diag}(1,1,0,0)$), so the even powers in $\exp(\psi K) = \sum \psi^n K^n/n!$ reassemble into $\cosh\psi$ and the odd into $\sinh\psi$. For a rotation, $J^2$ is a *negative* projector ($J_3^2 = \mathrm{diag}(0,-1,-1,0)$), and the alternating signs reassemble the series into $\cos\varphi$ and $\sin\varphi$. The hyperbolic-versus-circular dichotomy of relativity is, at this level, nothing but the sign of $G^2$.

# What Makes This Hard

The landing-in-the-group half is a short manipulation, and the place people stumble is keeping track of where $\eta$ sits: the identity $\omega^{\mathsf T} = -\eta\,\omega\,\eta$ must be exponentiated with conjugation-by-$\eta$ preserved, and it is easy to drop a factor of $\eta$ or to forget that $\eta^2 = \mathrm{Id}$ is what makes the conjugation close up. The genuinely hard part is **surjectivity for a non-compact group** — the non-obvious fact that a single exponential reaches every restricted Lorentz transformation, which is false for the lookalike group $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ and requires either the polar decomposition plus an eigenvalue argument or a dedicated theorem; the common error is to assume surjectivity is automatic "because it works for $SO(3)$", forgetting that compactness was doing the work there.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire proof.**

**High-level strategy:**
Show $\exp$ maps generators into the group by exponentiating the skew condition $\omega^{\mathsf T} = -\eta\,\omega\,\eta$ and using that $\exp$ commutes with transpose and with $\eta$-conjugation; show the image is in the *restricted* component by connectedness and $\det = +1$; get surjectivity from the polar decomposition (boost $\times$ rotation, each an exponential), citing the single-exponential refinement.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Rewrite the generator condition.** Show $\omega \in \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is equivalent to $\omega^{\mathsf T} = -\eta\,\omega\,\eta$.
   - *Hint:* Start from $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\,\omega = 0$ and multiply by $\eta$ on the right, using $\eta^2 = \mathrm{Id}$.
   - *Why needed:* This is the form that exponentiates cleanly.

2. **Exponentiate to the isometry condition.** Show $(\exp\omega)^{\mathsf T}\eta\,(\exp\omega) = \eta$.
   - *Hint:* $\exp(\omega^{\mathsf T}) = (\exp\omega)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\exp(-\eta\,\omega\,\eta) = \eta\,(\exp\omega)^{-1}\eta$; equate and rearrange.
   - *Why needed:* It places $\exp\omega$ in $O(1,3)$.

3. **Land in the restricted component.** Show $\exp\omega \in SO^+(1,3)$.
   - *Hint:* $\det\exp\omega = e^{\mathrm{tr}\,\omega} = e^0 = 1$; and $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is connected, $\exp$ continuous, $\exp(0) = \mathrm{Id}$.
   - *Why needed:* It pins the image to the identity component, not merely $O(1,3)$.

4. **Surjectivity via polar decomposition.** Show every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ is an exponential.
   - *Hint:* Write $\Lambda = B\,R$ with $B = \exp(\psi\,\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{K})$ a boost and $R = \exp(\varphi\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{J})$ a rotation; cite the refinement that $B\,R$ is itself a single exponential for $SO^+(1,3)$.
   - *Why needed:* It is the "fills the group" half.

5. **Boost and rotation forms.** Show $\exp(\psi K_1)$ is hyperbolic, $\exp(\varphi J_3)$ circular.
   - *Hint:* $K_1^2 = \mathrm{diag}(1,1,0,0)$ resums to $\cosh,\sinh$; $J_3^2 = \mathrm{diag}(0,-1,-1,0)$ resums to $\cos,\sin$.
   - *Why needed:* It identifies the exponentials with the physical boosts and rotations.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: The matrix exponential commutes with transposition and with conjugation
> **Statement:** For any real $4\times 4$ matrix $A$ and any invertible $P$, $\exp(A^{\mathsf T}) = (\exp A)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\exp(PAP^{-1}) = P(\exp A)P^{-1}$.
>
> **Hint:** Apply the operations termwise to the power series, using $(A^n)^{\mathsf T} = (A^{\mathsf T})^n$ and $(PAP^{-1})^n = PA^nP^{-1}$.
>
> **Why needed:** These are the two facts that let the skew condition on $\omega$ pass through $\exp$ to become the isometry condition on $\exp\omega$.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Transposition is linear and continuous, so it passes through the convergent series: $\exp(A^{\mathsf T}) = \sum_n (A^{\mathsf T})^n/n! = \sum_n (A^n)^{\mathsf T}/n! = \big(\sum_n A^n/n!\big)^{\mathsf T} = (\exp A)^{\mathsf T}$, using $(A^{\mathsf T})^n = (A^n)^{\mathsf T}$. Conjugation by $P$ is likewise linear and continuous, and $(PAP^{-1})^n = PA^nP^{-1}$ (the inner factors $P^{-1}P$ telescope), so $\exp(PAP^{-1}) = \sum_n PA^nP^{-1}/n! = P\big(\sum_n A^n/n!\big)P^{-1} = P(\exp A)P^{-1}$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: Exponentials of generators are Lorentz transformations
> **Statement:** If $\omega \in \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ then $(\exp\omega)^{\mathsf T}\eta\,(\exp\omega) = \eta$.
>
> **Hint:** Rewrite the generator condition as $\omega^{\mathsf T} = -\eta\,\omega\,\eta$, exponentiate using Lemma 1, and rearrange.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the "lands in the group" half of the theorem.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > The generator condition $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\,\omega = 0$ gives $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta = -\eta\,\omega$; multiply on the right by $\eta$ and use $\eta^2 = \mathrm{Id}$ to get $\omega^{\mathsf T} = -\eta\,\omega\,\eta$. Exponentiate both sides. By Lemma 1, the left is $\exp(\omega^{\mathsf T}) = (\exp\omega)^{\mathsf T}$. The right is $\exp(-\eta\,\omega\,\eta) = \exp(\eta(-\omega)\eta) = \eta\,\exp(-\omega)\,\eta = \eta\,(\exp\omega)^{-1}\eta$, using conjugation-invariance (Lemma 1) and $\exp(-\omega) = (\exp\omega)^{-1}$. Hence $(\exp\omega)^{\mathsf T} = \eta\,(\exp\omega)^{-1}\eta$. Multiply on the left by nothing and on the right by $\eta\,(\exp\omega)$: $(\exp\omega)^{\mathsf T}\eta\,(\exp\omega) = \eta\,(\exp\omega)^{-1}\eta\,\eta\,(\exp\omega) = \eta\,(\exp\omega)^{-1}(\exp\omega) = \eta$. So $\exp\omega \in O(1,3)$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The image lies in the identity component SO⁺(1,3)
> **Statement:** For $\omega \in \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$, $\exp\omega$ has $\det = +1$ and $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$, so $\exp\omega \in SO^+(1,3)$.
>
> **Hint:** Use $\det\exp\omega = e^{\mathrm{tr}\,\omega}$ and the connectedness of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$.
>
> **Why needed:** Lemma 2 only gives membership in $O(1,3)$; this refines it to the restricted group.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Every generator has zero trace ($\mathrm{tr}\,K_i = \mathrm{tr}\,J_i = 0$, hence $\mathrm{tr}\,\omega = 0$ for all $\omega \in \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$), so $\det\exp\omega = e^{\mathrm{tr}\,\omega} = e^0 = 1$ — the result is proper. For orthochronicity, observe that $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is a real vector space, hence connected, and $\exp$ is continuous, so the image $\exp(\mathfrak{so}(1,3))$ is a connected subset of $O(1,3)$. It contains $\exp(0) = \mathrm{Id}$, which lies in $SO^+(1,3)$. The four components of $O(1,3)$ are disjoint and (relatively) open, so a connected set meeting $SO^+(1,3)$ is entirely contained in it. Therefore $\exp\omega \in SO^+(1,3)$ for every $\omega$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 4: A boost generator exponentiates to a hyperbolic boost
> **Statement:** $\exp(\psi K_1) = \mathrm{Id} + \sinh\psi\,K_1 + (\cosh\psi - 1)K_1^2$, the boost of rapidity $\psi$ in the $t$–$x$ plane.
>
> **Hint:** Use $K_1^2 = \mathrm{diag}(1,1,0,0)$ and $K_1^3 = K_1$, so the series splits into even and odd powers.
>
> **Why needed:** It identifies the exponential of a boost generator with the physical boost, and exhibits the hyperbolic functions.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Direct computation gives $K_1^2 = \mathrm{diag}(1,1,0,0)$, and then $K_1^3 = K_1\cdot K_1^2 = K_1$ (since $K_1$ is supported on the $t$–$x$ block where $K_1^2$ acts as the identity), so $K_1^{2m+1} = K_1$ and $K_1^{2m} = K_1^2$ for $m \ge 1$. The series
> > $$\exp(\psi K_1) = \mathrm{Id} + \sum_{m\ge 0}\frac{\psi^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}K_1 + \sum_{m\ge 1}\frac{\psi^{2m}}{(2m)!}K_1^2 = \mathrm{Id} + \sinh\psi\,K_1 + (\cosh\psi - 1)K_1^2,$$
> > using $\sum_{m\ge 0}\psi^{2m+1}/(2m+1)! = \sinh\psi$ and $\sum_{m\ge 1}\psi^{2m}/(2m)! = \cosh\psi - 1$. In the $t$–$x$ block this is $\begin{pmatrix}\cosh\psi & \sinh\psi\\ \sinh\psi & \cosh\psi\end{pmatrix}$, the boost of rapidity $\psi$ (compare [[Def - Boosts as Hyperbolic Rotations]]). $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 5: A rotation generator exponentiates to a rotation
> **Statement:** $\exp(\varphi J_3) = \mathrm{Id} + \sin\varphi\,J_3 + (1 - \cos\varphi)J_3^2$, the rotation by $\varphi$ in the $x$–$y$ plane.
>
> **Hint:** Use $J_3^2 = \mathrm{diag}(0,-1,-1,0)$ and $J_3^3 = -J_3$, so the alternating signs build circular functions.
>
> **Why needed:** It identifies the exponential of a rotation generator with the physical rotation, exhibiting the circular functions and the sign contrast with the boost.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Compute $J_3^2 = \mathrm{diag}(0,-1,-1,0)$, and $J_3^3 = J_3\cdot J_3^2 = -J_3$ (on the $x$–$y$ block $J_3^2$ acts as $-\mathrm{Id}$), so $J_3^{2m+1} = (-1)^m J_3$ and $J_3^{2m} = (-1)^{m-1}J_3^2 \cdot(-1)$... more simply $J_3^{2m} = (-1)^m(-J_3^2)$ giving the alternating pattern. Resumming,
> > $$\exp(\varphi J_3) = \mathrm{Id} + \sum_{m\ge0}\frac{(-1)^m\varphi^{2m+1}}{(2m+1)!}J_3 + \sum_{m\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{m}\varphi^{2m}}{(2m)!}(-J_3^2) = \mathrm{Id} + \sin\varphi\,J_3 + (1-\cos\varphi)J_3^2.$$
> > In the $x$–$y$ block this is $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi\\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}$, the ordinary rotation by $\varphi$. The sign of $J_3^2$ (negative, versus $K_1^2$ positive) is exactly what turns $\cosh,\sinh$ into $\cos,\sin$. $\blacksquare$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> **Step 0 — convergence and well-definedness.** The series $\exp(\omega) = \sum_n \omega^n/n!$ converges for every real $4\times 4$ matrix in any submultiplicative norm (it is dominated by $\sum_n \|\omega\|^n/n! = e^{\|\omega\|}$), so $\exp$ is defined on all of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$.
>
> **Landing in the group.** Let $\omega \in \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$. By Lemma 2, $(\exp\omega)^{\mathsf T}\eta\,(\exp\omega) = \eta$, so $\exp\omega \in O(1,3)$. By Lemma 3, $\det\exp\omega = e^{\mathrm{tr}\,\omega} = 1$ and the image is connected to $\mathrm{Id}$, so $\exp\omega \in SO^+(1,3)$. Thus $\exp : \mathfrak{so}(1,3) \to SO^+(1,3)$ is well-defined.
>
> **Boosts and rotations.** By Lemma 4, $\exp(\psi K_1)$ is the boost of rapidity $\psi$ in the $t$–$x$ plane; conjugating by a spatial rotation aligns the boost with any unit $\mathbf{n}$, giving the result that $\exp(\psi\,n^iK_i)$ is the boost of rapidity $\psi$ in $\mathrm{Span}(\mathbf{e}_0,\mathbf{n})$. By Lemma 5, $\exp(\varphi J_3)$ is the rotation by $\varphi$ in the $x$–$y$ plane; similarly $\exp(\varphi\,n^iJ_i)$ is the rotation by $\varphi$ about $\mathbf{n}$.
>
> **Surjectivity.** Let $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$. By the [[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group|polar decomposition]], $\Lambda = B\,R$ where $B$ is a boost and $R$ a rotation. By the previous paragraph $B = \exp(\psi\,\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{K})$ and $R = \exp(\varphi\,\mathbf{m}\cdot\mathbf{J})$, so $\Lambda$ is a product of two exponentials. A classical refinement, special to $SO^+(1,3)$ (it fails for $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$), shows that this product is itself a single exponential $\exp(\omega)$ for some $\omega \in \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$; the proof analyses the eigenvalues of $\Lambda$ and constructs the logarithm $\omega = \log\Lambda$ within the algebra. Hence $\exp$ is surjective onto $SO^+(1,3)$.
>
> **The composition law.** For $\Lambda_1 = \exp(L_1)$, $\Lambda_2 = \exp(L_2)$, the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula gives $\Lambda_1\Lambda_2 = \exp\big(L_1 + L_2 + \tfrac12[L_1,L_2] + \tfrac1{12}[L_1,[L_1,L_2]] - \tfrac1{12}[L_2,[L_1,L_2]] + \cdots\big)$, a series whose every term is an iterated commutator, hence computable from the [[Def - Generators and Structure Constants|structure constants]] $C_{ab}{}^c$ alone. When $[L_1,L_2] = 0$ the series truncates to $\exp(L_1 + L_2)$, giving the addition law for collinear boosts and coaxial rotations. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**The matrix logarithm in numerical relativity.** Computing $\omega = \log\Lambda$ for a given restricted Lorentz transformation — inverting the exponential — is the practical content of surjectivity, and it appears whenever one interpolates between two Lorentz frames (for instance in relativistic ray-tracing or in the geometric integration of spin-precession equations). The application is nonobvious because "take the logarithm of a boost" sounds purely formal until one needs a continuous path of frames, which is exactly $\exp(s\,\log\Lambda)$ for $s \in [0,1]$.

**One-parameter subgroups and Stone's theorem.** In quantum mechanics, a strongly continuous one-parameter group of unitaries $U(t)$ is $e^{-iHt}$ for a self-adjoint generator $H$ (Stone's theorem) — the infinite-dimensional analogue of "every one-parameter subgroup is an exponential". The boost-as-exponential statement here is the finite-dimensional, indefinite-signature instance, and recognising the parallel is what connects the Lorentz generators to the boost *operators* on a quantum field's Hilbert space.

**The exponential of the angular-velocity tensor in rigid-body dynamics.** A rotating rigid body has orientation $R(t) = \exp\big(\int_0^t \Omega(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\big)$ only when the angular-velocity tensors $\Omega(s)$ commute; otherwise the ordering matters and one needs the time-ordered exponential. The Lorentz analogue — that $\exp(\psi K_1 + \varphi J_3) \ne \exp(\psi K_1)\exp(\varphi J_3)$ because $K_1$ and $J_3$ do not commute — is the same phenomenon, and the Thomas rotation is its relativistic signature.

# Bridges

- **[[Def - Exponential Map of a Lie Group]]** — this theorem is the Lorentz-group instance of the general exponential map $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ defined for any [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] as the time-one flow of the left-invariant vector field generated by $\omega$. For matrix groups that abstract construction collapses to the matrix power series used here, and the general theorem that $\exp$ is a local diffeomorphism near $0$ specialises to the statement that boosts and rotations near the identity are parametrised by their generators.

- **[[Thm - Boosts Compose by Adding Rapidities]]** — the addition law for collinear boosts is the commuting case $[K,K]_{\text{same plane}} = 0$ of the composition corollary: $\exp(\psi_1 K)\exp(\psi_2 K) = \exp((\psi_1+\psi_2)K)$. This page derives that law as a special case of Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff, explaining *why* rapidities add — because the boost generators in a fixed plane commute and the BCH series truncates to ordinary addition.

- **[[Def - Thomas Rotation]]** — the non-commuting case is the source of the Thomas rotation: composing two boosts in different planes, the BCH series produces a term $\tfrac12[K_a,K_b] = -\tfrac12\epsilon_{abc}J_c$, a *rotation* generator, so the product is a boost times a rotation. The $[K,K] = -J$ structure relation is the precise origin of the rotation, and this theorem is what turns that relation into a statement about finite composed boosts.

- **[[Ex - The Lie Algebra of SO(3) is Antisymmetric Matrices]]** — the rotation half of this theorem is exactly the $SO(3)$ exponential: $\exp(\varphi\,\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{J})$ restricted to the spatial block is the Rodrigues formula for a rotation, and the rotation generators $J_i$ are the embedding of the antisymmetric $3\times 3$ matrices $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ into $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] The (A,B) Decomposition and Field Representations *(from §10.3 and Quantum Field Theory)*
> With the exponential map in hand, a representation of the algebra exponentiates to a representation of the group, and the [[Thm - The Complexification of so(1,3) and the (A,B) Decomposition|(A,B) split]] organises these: a field in the $(j_A,j_B)$ representation transforms by $\exp$ of the corresponding generators. **Scalars $(0,0)$, Weyl spinors $(\tfrac12,0)$ and $(0,\tfrac12)$, four-vectors $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$** are all built this way. See [[Special Relativity XI — SL(2,C), Spinors and the Spinor Map]].

> [!tip] The Failure of Surjectivity Elsewhere, and Spinors *(from Spinors)*
> That $\exp$ is surjective here but the group is *not simply connected* ([[Thm - Topology of the Lorentz Group]]) means the exponential, while onto, is not injective and the group has a non-trivial **double cover** $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$. Representations of the cover that are not representations of $SO^+(1,3)$ itself are the **spinors**, on which a $2\pi$ rotation acts as $-1$. This is the algebraic reason half-integer spin exists.
