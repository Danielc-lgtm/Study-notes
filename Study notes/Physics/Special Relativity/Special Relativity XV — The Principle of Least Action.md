# Notation Registry

Two standing conventions hold for the whole topic. First, **we set $c = 1$**, restoring it only where a formula is more recognisable with the dimensionful constant; to restore $c$, write $t \to ct$, $v \to v/c$, and insert factors of $c$ by dimensional analysis. Second, **the signature is mostly-minus**, $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)$, so a timelike vector has $X \cdot X > 0$ and a clock carried along a timelike worldline reads proper time $d\tau = \sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu dx^\nu}$ (with $c$: $d\tau = c^{-1}\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu dx^\nu}$). The source for this chapter (Gourgoulhon, *Special Relativity in General Frames*, Chapter 11) uses the opposite, mostly-plus signature $\mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$, in which the free action reads $S = -mc\int\sqrt{-g_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta}\,d\lambda$ with timelike $\dot x \cdot \dot x < 0$. Every transcribed formula has been translated: the overall sign of the metric is flipped, the minus under the square root is dropped, and the four-velocity normalisation $g_{\alpha\beta}u^\alpha u^\beta = -c^2$ becomes $U \cdot U = c^2$ (or $U \cdot U = 1$ with $c = 1$).

- $\mathcal{P}$ — a point particle; $\mathcal{L}$ — its worldline (a curve in spacetime), written $x^\mu = x^\mu(\lambda)$
- $\lambda$ — an arbitrary parameter increasing along $\mathcal{L}$; $\tilde\lambda$ — a second parameter, with $\lambda = f(\tilde\lambda)$
- $\tau$ — proper time, the arc length of a timelike worldline; the physical parameter, but *not* assumed a priori
- $\dot x^\mu$ — the parameter-derivative $dx^\mu/d\lambda$; the components of the tangent vector $V = \dot x^\mu e_\mu$ to $\mathcal{L}$
- $L$, $L(x^\mu, \dot x^\mu)$ — the **Lagrangian**, a function of position and parameter-velocity; for a relativistic particle, homogeneous of degree one in $\dot x^\mu$
- $S$, $S = \int_{\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2} L\,d\lambda$ — the **action** between two events $A_1, A_2$ of the worldline; reparametrisation-invariant
- $\delta x^\mu$, $\delta S$ — the variation of the worldline (vanishing at the endpoints) and the resulting first variation of the action
- $m$ — the rest mass of the particle (constant along $\mathcal{L}$ for a free particle)
- $U$, $U^\mu$ — the four-velocity, $U = dX/d\tau$, with $U \cdot U = 1$ (with $c$: $= c^2$); $u_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}U^\nu$ its covariant components
- $P$, $p_\mu$ — the four-momentum (a linear form); $p_\mu = \partial L/\partial \dot x^\mu$ is the **generalized four-momentum**; free particle $P = mU$
- $q$ — a coupling constant: the electric charge in a vector field, the scalar/tensor charge otherwise
- $A$, $A_\mu$ — the **potential one-form** of a vector field; $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ the associated field-strength two-form
- $\Phi$, $h_{\mu\nu}$ — a scalar field, a symmetric tensor field (other interaction Lagrangians)
- $G^\mu$, $G$ — the generators of an infinitesimal coordinate change $x'^\mu = x^\mu + \varepsilon G^\mu$; $G = G^\mu e_\mu$ the associated vector
- $\varepsilon$ — an infinitesimal parameter
- $J^{\mu\nu} = x^\mu p^\nu - x^\nu p^\mu$ — the angular-momentum four-tensor; $E = p_0$, $\mathbf{P} = (p_i)$ — energy and three-momentum relative to an inertial observer
- $H$, $H(x^\mu, p_\mu)$ — the **Hamiltonian**; $\{f, g\}$ — the Poisson bracket; $\mathsf{P}$ — phase space
- $P_{\alpha_0}$, $K_i$, $J_i$ — the ten generators of the Poincaré group: four translations, three boosts, three rotations
- $\perp_U$ — the orthogonal projector $\delta^\mu_{\;\nu} - U^\mu u_\nu$ (with $c=1$) onto the particle's local rest space
- Einstein summation: a repeated index, once up once down, is summed over $0,1,2,3$; Greek indices $\mu,\nu = 0,\dots,3$, Latin $i,j = 1,2,3$

# Motivation

Here is the whole chapter in one equation: a free relativistic particle moves so as to make the quantity
$$S = -mc^2\!\int_{A_1}^{A_2} d\tau \qquad (c=1:\ S = -m\!\int_{A_1}^{A_2} d\tau)$$
stationary, and everything else — the geodesic equation, the conservation of energy, momentum, and angular momentum, the coupling to electromagnetism, the passage to a Hamiltonian and thence to quantum mechanics — is a consequence of varying that single number. This is the chapter where relativistic dynamics stops being a list of equations of motion and becomes a *variational principle*, and the reorganisation is worth the price of admission three times over.

The first payoff is unification. In the two preceding chapters the equation of motion of a free particle (the worldline is straight) and the conservation laws (four-momentum, angular momentum) were separate facts, each established by its own argument. The action principle derives all of them from one object. The straight worldline is what you get by demanding $\delta S = 0$; the conservation laws are what you get from **Noether's theorem**, which converts each symmetry of the action into a conserved quantity. The reader will recognise the structure from non-relativistic analytical mechanics, and indeed the relativistic theory is the same machine with the Newtonian absolute time $t$ replaced by an arbitrary parameter $\lambda$ along the worldline — a replacement forced by the central fact of relativity, that there is no absolute time to integrate against.

The second payoff is the sign. The action of a free particle is $-mc^2$ times the proper time, and the minus sign is not a convention: it is the statement that **the free particle maximises proper time**. We already know from [[Thm - Inertial Worldlines Maximise Proper Time|Special Relativity V]] that the inertial worldline between two timelike-separated events is the *longest* in proper time — the geometric content of the twin paradox. To turn that *maximum* of proper time into a *minimum* of the action (so that "least action" is literally true) one multiplies by a negative constant, and dimensional analysis fixes the constant to be $-mc^2$. So the much-quoted "principle of least action" for a free particle is the principle that the worldline is a *timelike geodesic*, a curve of *extremal* — here maximal — Lorentzian length, exactly the variational characterisation of geodesics from [[Riemannian Geometry II — Geodesics, the Exponential Map, and Variational Principles|Riemannian Geometry II]] specialised to the flat indefinite metric.

The third payoff is the bridge forward. The action that couples a particle to a vector field,
$$S = -m\!\int d\tau + q\!\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu,$$
is the **minimal-coupling** prescription, and varying it produces the Lorentz force $f = qF(\cdot, U)$ with $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\nu$. This is the entire dynamical content of electromagnetism as seen by a charged particle, written down before the field equations themselves — a preview of [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field]] and [[Special Relativity XXII — Maxwell's Equations]], where the same potential one-form $A$ becomes the protagonist. And the Legendre transform of the free Lagrangian gives the relativistic Hamiltonian $H = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2}$ (with $c$: $H = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4}$), the energy-momentum relation read as a function on phase space — the launching point for canonical quantisation and the relativistic wave equations.

The reader is assumed to be fluent with the non-relativistic Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formalisms and, ideally, with their geometric repackaging in [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics|symplectic mechanics]] — phase space as a cotangent bundle, the [[Def - The Legendre Transform|Legendre transform]], the [[Def - Poisson Bracket|Poisson bracket]], canonical transformations. The relativistic novelties are two and only two: the action is parametrised by an arbitrary $\lambda$ rather than by time, which forces the Lagrangian to be homogeneous of degree one in the velocities (and makes the naive Hamiltonian vanish identically, a difficulty Dirac taught us to handle); and the symmetry group whose conservation laws Noether's theorem extracts is the [[Def - The Poincaré Group|Poincaré group]] rather than the Galilei group. Everything else is the analytical mechanics the reader already owns.

# Concept Map

## §15.1 The Action of a Relativistic Particle

- **[[Def - Relativistic Action of a Free Particle]]**
	- The action of a free particle between two events of its worldline is $S = -mc^2\int d\tau = -mc^2(\tau_2 - \tau_1)$, the proper time multiplied by $-mc^2$. Written over an arbitrary parameter $\lambda$ it is $S = \int L\,d\lambda$ with Lagrangian $L = -mc\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu \dot x^\nu}$ (mostly-minus; Gourgoulhon's $-mc\sqrt{-g_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta}$). Because the action must not depend on how the worldline is parametrised, $L$ is forced to be a **positive homogeneous function of degree one** in $\dot x^\mu$, $L(x, \mu\dot x) = \mu L(x, \dot x)$, which by Euler's identity gives $\dot x^\mu\,\partial L/\partial \dot x^\mu = L$. Relative to an inertial observer, $L = -mc^2\sqrt{1 - \mathbf{v}^2/c^2}$, the form found in introductory textbooks; it is *not* kinetic minus potential energy.

- **[[Thm - Free-Particle Worldline Extremises Proper Time]]**
	- The Euler–Lagrange equations of the free Lagrangian $L = -mc\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}$ reduce, using $\partial L/\partial \dot x^\mu = mc\,u_\mu$ and $\partial L/\partial x^\mu = 0$, to $du_\mu/d\lambda = 0$: the four-velocity is constant, so the worldline is a straight line — a **timelike geodesic** of Minkowski spacetime. Because the action is $-mc^2$ times proper time and the constant is negative, the stationary worldline is the one of *maximal* proper time, matching [[Thm - Inertial Worldlines Maximise Proper Time|Special Relativity V]]. The four Euler–Lagrange equations are not independent: the homogeneity identity $\dot x^\mu[\text{EL}]_\mu = 0$ leaves only three, reflecting the three physical degrees of freedom and the freedom in choosing $\lambda$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Geodesic Equation of General Relativity *(from General Relativity)*
> Replacing the flat metric $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ by a curved $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ in the action $S = -mc\int\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\,d\lambda$ and varying gives the **geodesic equation** $\ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\;\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho = 0$, with the Christoffel symbols $\Gamma$ appearing automatically from the $\partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho}$ terms. A freely-falling body in a gravitational field follows a timelike geodesic of $g_{\mu\nu}$, and the special-relativistic straight worldline is the flat-space limit $\Gamma = 0$. The variational derivation here is the exact template; only the metric changes. See [[General Relativity I — Einstein's Equations and Schwarzschild]].

- **[[Def - Lagrangian for a Particle in a Vector Field]]**
	- A particle coupled to a **vector field** has Lagrangian $L = -mc\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu} + \tfrac{q}{c}A_\mu(x)\dot x^\mu$, equivalently the action $S = -m\int d\tau + q\int A_\mu\,dx^\mu$ (with $c=1$) — the free part plus the integral of the potential one-form $A$ along the worldline, weighted by the **charge** $q$. The interaction term $\tfrac{q}{c}A_\mu\dot x^\mu$ is the simplest homogeneous-degree-one function of $\dot x^\mu$ buildable from a one-form. Varying gives $mc^2 a_\mu = qF_{\mu\nu}U^\nu$, the **Lorentz four-force** $f = qF(\cdot, U)$, with $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ the antisymmetric field-strength tensor. This is **minimal coupling**, the entire interaction of a charge with electromagnetism.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Gauge Invariance and the Electromagnetic Connection *(from Electromagnetism and Gauge Theory)*
> The action $q\int A_\mu dx^\mu$ is unchanged, up to endpoint terms, when $A \mapsto A + d\chi$ for any scalar $\chi$ — because $\int d\chi$ depends only on the endpoints, which are held fixed. This is **gauge invariance**, and it forces the physical content to lie in $F = dA$, not in $A$ itself. The same one-form $A$ is the **electromagnetic connection** of [[Gauge Theory I — Connections on Vector Bundles and the Electromagnetic Connection|gauge theory]]: minimal coupling is the statement that the charge's phase is parallel-transported by $A$. See [[Special Relativity XXII — Maxwell's Equations]].

- **[[Def - Generalized Four-Momentum and the Relativistic Hamiltonian]]**
	- The **generalized four-momentum** is $p_\mu := \partial L/\partial \dot x^\mu$, a linear form along the worldline; homogeneity of $L$ makes it independent of the parametrisation. For a free particle $P = mcU$ (with $c=1$, $P = mU$), recovering the four-momentum of [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|Special Relativity XIII]] with $P \cdot P = m^2$ (Gourgoulhon's $-m^2c^2$). For a particle in a vector field $P = mcU + \tfrac{q}{c}A$, so the canonical momentum differs from the kinetic momentum by $\tfrac{q}{c}A$, and the **kinetic** momentum $P - \tfrac{q}{c}A$ satisfies $(P-\tfrac{q}{c}A)\cdot(P-\tfrac{q}{c}A) = m^2c^2$. The relativistic Hamiltonian, as a function of the three-momentum, is $H = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2}$ (charged: $H = \sqrt{(\mathbf{p}-q\mathbf{A})^2 + m^2} + q\phi$).

> [!tip] Unlocked: Relativistic Wave Equations *(from Quantum Field Theory)*
> Promoting $p_\mu \to i\hbar\,\partial_\mu$ in the mass-shell constraint $P\cdot P = m^2$, i.e. $\eta^{\mu\nu}p_\mu p_\nu = m^2c^2$, gives the **Klein–Gordon equation** $(\Box + m^2c^2/\hbar^2)\psi = 0$; doing the same to the minimal-coupling momentum $p_\mu - \tfrac{q}{c}A_\mu$ gives the coupling of a quantum charged field to electromagnetism. The relativistic Hamiltonian $H = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2}$, made into an operator, is the seed of the **Dirac equation** (its "square root"). The generalized four-momentum of this chapter is what gets quantised.

## §15.2 Noether's Theorem

- **[[Thm - Noether Theorem (Relativistic Particle)]]**
	- If the Lagrangian is invariant under an infinitesimal coordinate change $x'^\mu = x^\mu + \varepsilon G^\mu(x)$ — meaning $L(x + \varepsilon G, \dot x + \varepsilon\dot G) = L(x, \dot x)$ — then the quantity $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x^\mu}G^\mu = p_\mu G^\mu$ is **constant along the worldline**. The proof is two lines: expand the invariance to first order in $\varepsilon$, use the Euler–Lagrange equations to replace $\partial L/\partial x^\mu$, and recognise a total derivative. Each one-parameter symmetry of the action yields one conserved quantity; the conserved quantity is the generalized momentum *contracted with the generator vector* $G$, i.e. the linear form $P$ evaluated on $G$. This is the mechanism that turns the symmetries of spacetime into the conservation laws of mechanics.

- **[[Thm - Noether Theorem (Relativistic Particle)|Application to a free particle (Poincaré conservation laws)]]**
	- The free-particle Lagrangian $L = -mc\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}$ is invariant under the full ten-parameter [[Def - The Poincaré Group|Poincaré group]] — four translations, three boosts, three rotations — because $\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x'^\mu\dot x'^\nu = \eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu$ under any Lorentz matrix and any constant shift. Noether's theorem then delivers ten conserved quantities: the four **translations** give conservation of the four-momentum $p_\mu = mc\,u_\mu$ (energy $E$ and three-momentum $\mathbf{P}$, see [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|Special Relativity XIII]]); the three **rotations** give conservation of the spatial angular momentum $J_{ij}$ (see [[Def - Angular Momentum Four-Tensor|Special Relativity XIV]]); and the three **boosts** give conservation of $J_{i0}$, equivalent to the **centre-of-inertia theorem** that the centre of mass moves uniformly, $x^i - V^i t = \text{const}$. The energy-momentum linear form and the angular-momentum two-tensor are exactly the Noether charges of the symmetries of Minkowski space.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Mass and Spin as Poincaré Casimirs *(from Quantum Field Theory)*
> The ten conserved quantities here are the *classical* charges of the Poincaré symmetry; their quantum counterparts are the generators of the Poincaré group acting on a Hilbert space. The two **Casimir invariants** built from them — $P_\mu P^\mu = m^2$ (from the translation charges) and the square of the Pauli–Lubanski vector $W_\mu W^\mu = -m^2 s(s+1)$ (from the full angular-momentum tensor) — are the *mass* and *spin* that, by **Wigner's classification**, define what an elementary particle is. The conservation laws of this section are the seed of that classification. See [[Special Relativity XII — Inertial Observers and the Poincaré Group]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Moment Map and Symplectic Reduction *(from Geometric Mechanics)*
> Noether's theorem is, geometrically, the statement that a symmetry group $G$ acting on phase space has a **moment map** $\mu$ whose components are the conserved charges — here the ten components of energy-momentum and angular momentum form the moment map of the Poincaré action. Quotienting the constraint surface by the symmetry (**Marsden–Weinstein reduction**) produces a smaller phase space carrying the genuine degrees of freedom. The single-particle relativistic system is the prototype; see the moment-map discussion in [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics]].

## §15.3 Hamiltonian Formulation

- **[[Thm - Hamiltonian Formulation (Relativistic Particle)]]**
	- The naive Legendre transform $H = p_\mu\dot x^\mu - L$ **vanishes identically** for a reparametrisation-invariant Lagrangian, by Euler's homogeneity identity $\dot x^\mu\,\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu = L$; and the map $\dot x^\mu \mapsto p_\mu$ is **not invertible**, since $\dot x^\mu$ lies in the kernel of the Hessian $\partial^2 L/\partial\dot x^\mu\partial\dot x^\nu$. Both failures express the same fact: the four functions $x^\mu(\lambda)$ carry only three physical degrees of freedom, and the momenta obey a **primary constraint**, $P\cdot P = m^2$ for a free particle (Gourgoulhon's $-m^2c^2$). Dirac's resolution is to take a Hamiltonian *proportional to the constraint*, $H = \tfrac{1}{2m}\eta^{\mu\nu}\big[p_\mu - \tfrac{q}{c}A_\mu\big]\big[p_\nu - \tfrac{q}{c}A_\nu\big]$; the canonical equations $\dot x^\mu = \partial H/\partial p_\mu$, $\dot p_\mu = -\partial H/\partial x^\mu$ then *select* the proper time as parameter ($\lambda = \tau$) and reproduce the Lorentz-force motion. The physically transparent alternative is the energy Hamiltonian $H = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2}$, the time component of the four-momentum expressed through the three-momentum.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Constrained Hamiltonian Systems and BRST *(from Gauge Theory and Quantisation)*
> The vanishing Hamiltonian and the primary constraint $P\cdot P - m^2 = 0$ are the defining features of a **constrained Hamiltonian system** in the sense of **Dirac and Bergmann**. The reparametrisation freedom of the worldline is a *gauge symmetry*, and the constraint is its generator; the systematic treatment — first-class constraints, the Dirac bracket, gauge-fixing, and ultimately the **BRST** cohomological formalism — grows directly out of this example. The relativistic particle is the simplest reparametrisation-invariant theory, and as such it is the standard toy model for the constraint quantisation of gravity and string theory.

- **[[Thm - Hamiltonian Formulation (Relativistic Particle)|Systems of particles and the no-interaction theorem]]**
	- Many relativistic particles need many time parameters, one per worldline. A general interacting action is the **Tetrode–Fokker** action, the sum of free actions plus a double integral $\sum_{a<b}q_aq_b\iint K\,d\lambda_a d\lambda_b$ describing **action at a distance** with no intervening field; choosing $K$ with a light-cone delta function gives **Wheeler–Feynman electrodynamics**, equivalent (ignoring radiation reaction) to Maxwell theory using the half-sum of retarded and advanced potentials. Two obstructions appear at the Hamiltonian level. The equations of motion are **integro-differential**, not a Cauchy problem. And the **no-interaction theorem** (Currie–Jordan–Sudarshan) forbids any Poincaré-invariant Hamiltonian system in which the canonical coordinates are the particles' spacetime positions *and* the particles interact — forcing the canonical coordinates to be abstract phase-space variables rather than literal positions.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Symplectic Geometry of Phase Space *(from Geometric Mechanics)*
> The Poisson bracket, canonical transformations, and generating functions reviewed in this section are the *coordinate* face of a coordinate-free structure: phase space is a **symplectic manifold** $(\mathsf{P}, \omega)$, a cotangent bundle $T^*Q$ carrying the canonical two-form $\omega = dp_\mu \wedge dx^\mu$, the Poisson bracket is $\{f,g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g)$, and a canonical transformation is a **symplectomorphism**. Hamilton's equations are $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$, and the conservation of phase-space volume (Liouville) is $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$, a one-line consequence of $d\omega = 0$. The relativistic particle is a worked example of this geometry; the full development is [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics]].

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of this chapter pursue a recognisable set of goals, all downstream of a single variational principle. The most basic is to **derive an equation of motion from an action**: write down $\delta S = 0$, extract the Euler–Lagrange equations, and simplify them into a recognisable dynamical law — the geodesic equation for a free particle, the Lorentz force for a charged one, a force involving the four-acceleration for scalar or tensor couplings. A second target is to **read off a conserved quantity from a symmetry**: identify a transformation under which the Lagrangian is invariant, apply Noether's theorem, and name the resulting constant of the motion (four-momentum from translations, angular momentum from rotations, the centre-of-inertia law from boosts). A third is to **compute the generalized momentum and the Hamiltonian**: differentiate $L$ with respect to $\dot x^\mu$, perform the Legendre transform, and arrive at $H = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2}$ or its charged generalisation, then verify that Hamilton's equations reproduce the original motion. A fourth, more delicate, is to **diagnose a degeneracy**: show that a Lagrangian is homogeneous of degree one and therefore has a vanishing canonical Hamiltonian and a primary constraint, and resolve the difficulty by Dirac's method or by switching to an inertial-observer parametrisation. A fifth is to **establish parametrisation invariance** of an action, the property that makes the variational principle well-posed in the absence of absolute time. Across all five the unifying observation is that you understand a relativistic dynamical system once you know its action: the equations of motion, the conserved quantities, and the Hamiltonian are all encoded in that one functional, and every problem is an exercise in extracting them.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **An action or Lagrangian is given**, which is the richest source: it supplies the equations of motion (by variation), the generalized momentum (by differentiation), and the Hamiltonian (by Legendre transform), so a single functional unlocks the entire dynamics. **A symmetry of the action is given or recognised** — invariance under translation, rotation, boost, or any one-parameter family of coordinate changes — and Noether's theorem converts it directly into a conserved quantity; this is the most labour-saving source, because it produces constants of the motion without ever solving a differential equation. **A potential is specified** — a one-form $A$ for a vector field, a scalar $\Phi$, a tensor $h_{\mu\nu}$ — and the minimal-coupling recipe writes down the interaction Lagrangian, from which the force follows. **The homogeneity of the Lagrangian is given** (degree one in $\dot x^\mu$), which simultaneously guarantees parametrisation invariance, forces Euler's identity $\dot x^\mu\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu = L$, and predicts the vanishing of the canonical Hamiltonian and the existence of a primary constraint. **The four-velocity normalisation $U\cdot U = c^2$ is available** as a constraint to be imposed *a posteriori*, after the Euler–Lagrange equations are written, never before — a subtlety that distinguishes the relativistic problem from its Newtonian counterpart. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a given action routes through variation to an equation of motion, through Noether to a conservation law, and through Legendre to a Hamiltonian; a given symmetry routes through Noether to a conserved charge; a given potential routes through minimal coupling to a force. The [[Special Relativity XV — The Principle of Least Action#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in the chapter is assembled from. The reader already commands the analytical mechanics; what is new is the careful handling of the arbitrary parameter $\lambda$ and the indefinite metric, so each operation states where those enter. When stuck, scan the list and try each one.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Vary the action and read off the Euler–Lagrange equations.** Perturb the worldline $x^\mu \mapsto x^\mu + \delta x^\mu$ with $\delta x^\mu$ vanishing at the two endpoints, integrate the $\delta\dot x^\mu$ term by parts, and demand $\delta S = 0$ for all variations to obtain $\partial L/\partial x^\mu - \tfrac{d}{d\lambda}(\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu) = 0$; see [[Def - Relativistic Action of a Free Particle]]. *Trigger:* an action is given and an equation of motion is wanted. *Pattern:* the boundary term $[\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu\,\delta x^\mu]_{\lambda_1}^{\lambda_2}$ vanishes because the endpoints are fixed, leaving the bulk integrand, which must vanish pointwise.

2. **Use Euler's homogeneity identity.** Because the relativistic Lagrangian is homogeneous of degree one in $\dot x^\mu$, it satisfies $\dot x^\mu\,\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu = L$; differentiating once more gives $\dot x^\mu\,\partial^2 L/\partial\dot x^\mu\partial\dot x^\nu = 0$. *Trigger:* you need to show the four Euler–Lagrange equations are dependent, or that the canonical Hamiltonian vanishes, or that the Legendre map is non-invertible. *Pattern:* the identity is the algebraic fingerprint of parametrisation invariance, and it is the source of every degeneracy in the chapter.

3. **Apply Noether's theorem.** Find a one-parameter family of coordinate changes $x'^\mu = x^\mu + \varepsilon G^\mu$ leaving $L$ invariant; then $p_\mu G^\mu$ is conserved, where $p_\mu = \partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu$; see [[Thm - Noether Theorem (Relativistic Particle)]]. *Trigger:* a symmetry is present and a conserved quantity is wanted. *Pattern:* read the generator $G^\mu$ off the transformation, contract it with the generalized momentum, and the result is automatically constant along the worldline — no differential equation need be solved.

4. **Couple to a field by minimal coupling.** To put a particle of charge $q$ in a vector field with potential one-form $A$, add $\tfrac{q}{c}A_\mu\dot x^\mu$ to the free Lagrangian, i.e. add $q\int A_\mu dx^\mu$ to the action; see [[Def - Lagrangian for a Particle in a Vector Field]]. *Trigger:* a particle interacts with an external field given by a potential. *Pattern:* the interaction term is the unique homogeneous-degree-one scalar built from the potential and $\dot x^\mu$; varying it produces $qF_{\mu\nu}U^\nu$ with $F = dA$.

5. **Compute the generalized four-momentum.** Differentiate the Lagrangian, $p_\mu = \partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu$; for the free particle this is $mc\,u_\mu$, for a charged particle $mc\,u_\mu + \tfrac{q}{c}A_\mu$; see [[Def - Generalized Four-Momentum and the Relativistic Hamiltonian]]. *Trigger:* you need the canonical momentum, a conserved Noether charge, or the input to a Legendre transform. *Pattern:* the canonical momentum and the kinetic momentum $mcU$ differ by the potential term, and only the canonical one is conserved under translations.

6. **Perform the Legendre transform — carefully.** Form $H = p_\mu\dot x^\mu - L$ only after checking the Legendre map is invertible; for a homogeneous-degree-one $L$ it is not, and $H \equiv 0$, so instead take a Hamiltonian proportional to the constraint (Dirac) or pass to the inertial-observer Lagrangian $L = -mc^2\sqrt{1-\mathbf{v}^2/c^2}$ and Legendre-transform in the three-velocity to get $H = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2}$; see [[Thm - Hamiltonian Formulation (Relativistic Particle)]]. *Trigger:* a Hamiltonian or a phase-space description is wanted. *Pattern:* the degeneracy is a feature, not a bug — it is the reparametrisation gauge symmetry.

7. **Choose the parameter last, and impose $U\cdot U = c^2$ a posteriori.** Keep $\lambda$ arbitrary while deriving the Euler–Lagrange equations; only after they are written may you fix $\lambda = \tau$ and impose the four-velocity normalisation $\eta_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu = c^2$. *Trigger:* any free-particle or field-coupling variation. *Pattern:* imposing $\lambda = \tau$ first collapses the free Lagrangian to the constant $-mc^2$ and yields the empty equation $0 = 0$; the constraint must be a consequence of the equations of motion, not an input to them.

8. **Switch to the inertial-observer Lagrangian for explicit computation.** With $\lambda = t$ (an inertial observer's time) the free Lagrangian becomes $L = -mc^2\sqrt{1 - \mathbf{v}^2/c^2}$, a function of the three-velocity $\mathbf{v}$ alone, and ordinary three-dimensional analytical mechanics applies. *Trigger:* you want energy, three-momentum, or a Hamiltonian as explicit functions of $\mathbf{v}$ or $\mathbf{p}$. *Pattern:* this trades manifest Lorentz covariance for computational transparency; $\mathbf{p} = \partial L/\partial\mathbf{v} = m\gamma\mathbf{v}$ and $H = \mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{v} - L = m\gamma c^2$.

9. **Recognise a conserved quantity as a momentum contracted with a generator.** When Noether gives $p_\mu G^\mu = \text{const}$, identify the geometric object: $G = e_{\alpha_0}$ (a translation) makes $p_{\alpha_0}$ conserved (energy or a momentum component); $G^\mu = (J_i)^\mu_{\;\nu}x^\nu$ (a rotation) makes the angular-momentum component $J_{ij}$ conserved; $G^\mu = (K_i)^\mu_{\;\nu}x^\nu$ (a boost) makes $J_{i0}$ conserved (centre-of-inertia motion). *Trigger:* a Poincaré symmetry is at hand. *Pattern:* the ten generators of the Poincaré Lie algebra give the ten conserved quantities, and the conserved quantity is always the linear form $P$ evaluated on the generator vector $G$.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Imposing the four-velocity normalisation before varying
> The relation $\eta_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu = c^2$ (Gourgoulhon's $g_{\alpha\beta}u^\alpha u^\beta = -c^2$) is true on the physical worldline, so it is tempting to substitute it into the action before varying. This is illegal and produces nonsense: with $\lambda = \tau$ enforced a priori, the free Lagrangian $L = -mc\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}$ collapses to the constant $-mc^2$, whose Euler–Lagrange equations are the empty statement $0 = 0$ — all dynamical content has been thrown away. The constraint restricts the variations, and a constrained variation is not a free one. The operation becomes legal only if you keep $\lambda$ unconstrained throughout the variation and impose $U\cdot U = c^2$ afterwards, as a consequence of the equations of motion (this is Gourgoulhon's Remark 11.3), or if you implement the constraint honestly with a Lagrange multiplier.

> [!warning] 2. Forming the canonical Hamiltonian $H = p_\mu\dot x^\mu - L$ for a free particle
> The reflex from non-relativistic mechanics is to define the Hamiltonian by the Legendre transform $H = p_\mu\dot x^\mu - L$. For the reparametrisation-invariant relativistic Lagrangian this gives $H = p_\mu\dot x^\mu - L = \dot x^\mu\,\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu - L = 0$ identically, by Euler's homogeneity identity — a useless Hamiltonian carrying no dynamics. The deeper symptom is that the Legendre map $\dot x^\mu \mapsto p_\mu$ is non-invertible (the Hessian of $L$ annihilates $\dot x^\mu$), so $\dot x^\mu$ cannot be solved for in terms of $p_\mu$ at all. The operation becomes legal in two ways: take a Hamiltonian *proportional to the primary constraint* $P\cdot P - m^2$ (Dirac), whose numerical value is constant but whose functional form drives the canonical equations; or break manifest covariance, single out a time, and Legendre-transform the inertial-observer Lagrangian in the three-velocity to get the genuine energy $H = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2}$.

> [!warning] 3. Treating the canonical momentum as the kinetic momentum for a charged particle
> For a particle in a vector field the generalized momentum is $P = mcU + \tfrac{q}{c}A$, *not* the kinetic momentum $mcU$, and conflating them is a classic error. The conserved Noether charge under a translation is the *canonical* momentum $P = mcU + \tfrac{q}{c}A$; the quantity satisfying the mass-shell relation $\,\cdot\,= m^2c^2$ is the *kinetic* momentum $mcU = P - \tfrac{q}{c}A$. Writing $P\cdot P = m^2c^2$ is wrong; the correct constraint is $(P - \tfrac{q}{c}A)\cdot(P - \tfrac{q}{c}A) = m^2c^2$, which is exactly why the Dirac Hamiltonian carries the combination $p_\mu - \tfrac{q}{c}A_\mu$ throughout. The distinction is the source of the minimal-coupling substitution $p_\mu \to p_\mu - \tfrac{q}{c}A_\mu$ that recurs in quantum mechanics. The operation becomes legal only once you keep the canonical and kinetic momenta strictly separate and remember that gauge transformations $A \mapsto A + d\chi$ shift the canonical momentum but not the kinetic one.

> [!warning] 4. Assuming "least action" means the action is a minimum
> The name "principle of least action" suggests the physical worldline minimises $S$, and for the free particle this is even arranged to be true by the sign $-mc^2$. But the principle is really one of *stationarity*, $\delta S = 0$, and the physical worldline is in general only a *critical point*, not a minimum — over a long enough worldline, or in the presence of a field, conjugate points appear and the action becomes a saddle. The underlying geometric fact is that the inertial worldline *maximises* proper time ([[Thm - Inertial Worldlines Maximise Proper Time]]), the opposite of the Euclidean intuition that a geodesic is shortest. The operation of "checking the second variation to confirm a minimum" is legal only locally and only for the free particle; in general one must speak of stationary action, and the correct statement is always $\delta S = 0$.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

Every problem in this chapter begins from an action, and the first move is always to ask which of the three things you want — an equation of motion, a conserved quantity, or a Hamiltonian — because each is a different operation on the same functional.

If the problem **asks for an equation of motion**, vary the action. Write the worldline as $x^\mu(\lambda)$ with $\lambda$ an arbitrary parameter, perturb it by $\delta x^\mu$ vanishing at the endpoints, and demand $\delta S = 0$; the [[Def - Relativistic Action of a Free Particle|Euler–Lagrange machinery]] then gives $\partial L/\partial x^\mu - \tfrac{d}{d\lambda}(\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu) = 0$. The decisive discipline is to keep $\lambda$ unconstrained until the equations are written: only afterwards may you set $\lambda = \tau$ and impose $U\cdot U = c^2$. For a free particle the result is $du_\mu/d\lambda = 0$, the [[Thm - Free-Particle Worldline Extremises Proper Time|geodesic equation]]; for a charged particle, $mc^2 a_\mu = qF_{\mu\nu}U^\nu$, the Lorentz force; for a scalar or tensor coupling, a force that involves the four-acceleration through the projector $\perp_U$ onto the rest space. The reason variation works so cleanly is that the boundary term vanishes by the fixed-endpoint condition, isolating the bulk integrand, which must vanish pointwise.

If the problem **asks for a conserved quantity**, do not solve the equations of motion — find a symmetry and apply [[Thm - Noether Theorem (Relativistic Particle)|Noether's theorem]]. Identify a one-parameter family of coordinate changes $x'^\mu = x^\mu + \varepsilon G^\mu$ leaving the Lagrangian invariant, read off the generator $G^\mu$, and write down $p_\mu G^\mu = \text{const}$. For a free particle the symmetries are the ten Poincaré transformations, and the routine is mechanical: a translation generator gives a conserved four-momentum component, a rotation generator a conserved angular-momentum component, a boost generator the centre-of-inertia law. This is the single most labour-saving technique in the chapter, because it manufactures constants of the motion algebraically, bypassing the differential equations entirely. The conserved quantity is always the linear form $P$ contracted with the generator vector $G$, a fact worth holding onto: the four-momentum's status as a *one-form* rather than a vector is exactly what makes the contraction $p_\mu G^\mu$ natural.

If the problem **asks for a Hamiltonian or a phase-space description**, the strategy forks on whether the Lagrangian is homogeneous of degree one. If it is — the manifestly covariant free or minimally-coupled Lagrangian — the naive Legendre transform fails: $H \equiv 0$ and the Legendre map is non-invertible, both because of Euler's identity. You must then either follow [[Thm - Hamiltonian Formulation (Relativistic Particle)|Dirac]] and take a Hamiltonian proportional to the primary constraint $P\cdot P - m^2$, or break covariance by choosing an inertial observer's time as parameter, which gives the non-homogeneous Lagrangian $L = -mc^2\sqrt{1-\mathbf{v}^2/c^2}$ whose ordinary Legendre transform yields the genuine energy $H = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 + m^2}$. The recognition step is to check homogeneity *first*: if $L(x, \mu\dot x) = \mu L(x, \dot x)$, expect the degeneracy and plan around it. The geometric content — phase space as a cotangent bundle, the Poisson bracket, canonical transformations — is the [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics|symplectic mechanics]] the reader already knows; the only relativistic novelty is the constraint.

If the problem **asks to establish parametrisation invariance** or to diagnose a degeneracy, the instrument is Euler's homogeneity identity. Show that $L$ is homogeneous of degree one in $\dot x^\mu$ — usually by inspection, since the relativistic Lagrangians are built from $\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}$ and $A_\mu\dot x^\mu$, both manifestly homogeneous — and then the chain of consequences follows: the action is reparametrisation-invariant, the four Euler–Lagrange equations are dependent (only three are independent), the canonical Hamiltonian vanishes, and the momenta obey a primary constraint. This cluster of facts is not four separate observations but one, viewed from four angles, and recognising the homogeneity is the key that unlocks all of them.

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle: **the action contains everything.** Unlike Newtonian mechanics, where the equation of motion, the energy, and the conserved quantities are separate inputs, here a single functional $S$ encodes the dynamics (by variation), the symmetries-and-conservation-laws (by Noether), and the canonical structure (by Legendre). When you are handed a relativistic system, ask first for its action; once you have it, every question in the chapter is a different way of differentiating it.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Noether Theorem (Relativistic Particle)|Noether's theorem]]**: a symmetry $x'^\mu = x^\mu + \varepsilon G^\mu$ of the Lagrangian yields the conserved charge $p_\mu G^\mu$. This is the most-used single tool in the chapter because it converts the hardest kind of question — "what is conserved?" — into the easiest kind of computation, contracting a momentum with a generator, with no differential equation to solve. Reach for it whenever a problem mentions invariance, symmetry, or "show that ... is constant," and whenever you can spot a coordinate change leaving the action alone. Its most powerful disguised use is in field theory, where the same theorem (with multi-dimensional integrals) gives the energy-momentum tensor and every conserved current; the particle case is the one-dimensional prototype. Recognise its applicability the instant a symmetry appears, and remember that the conserved quantity is the four-momentum *one-form* evaluated on the generator *vector*.

- **The free action $S = -mc^2\int d\tau$**: the proper time, weighted by $-mc^2$. It is the foundation everything else is built on, and its three features each carry weight. The proportionality to proper time makes the geodesic principle of [[Thm - Inertial Worldlines Maximise Proper Time|Special Relativity V]] a variational statement; the negative sign turns the *maximum* of proper time into a *minimum* of action, so "least action" is literally true for the free particle; and the rest mass $m$ as the proportionality constant is fixed uniquely by requiring the action to have the right dimension. Reach for the free action as the starting point of any single-particle problem, add interaction terms to it for coupled problems, and use its inertial-observer form $-mc^2\sqrt{1-\mathbf{v}^2/c^2}$ whenever explicit three-dimensional computation is needed.

- **Minimal coupling $S = -m\int d\tau + q\int A_\mu dx^\mu$**: the prescription that puts a charge in a field by adding the line integral of the potential one-form. Its recognisable setup is any particle interacting with an external field given by a potential rather than a force. The reusable content is twofold: varying it produces the Lorentz force $qF(\cdot,U)$ with $F = dA$ automatically, so the field equations are not needed to find the motion; and it is gauge-invariant, $A \mapsto A + d\chi$ changing the action only by endpoint terms, which forces the physics into $F$ and seeds the entire gauge principle. Reach for it whenever a coupling to electromagnetism (or any one-form field) is wanted, and remember that the canonical momentum it produces, $mcU + \tfrac{q}{c}A$, is the one that enters the Hamiltonian and gets quantised.

- **Euler's homogeneity identity $\dot x^\mu\,\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu = L$**: the algebraic fingerprint of parametrisation invariance. Its typical use is diagnostic: it is the reason the four Euler–Lagrange equations are dependent, the reason the canonical Hamiltonian vanishes, and the reason the Legendre map is non-invertible — three facts that are really one. Reach for it whenever you need to show a degeneracy, whenever a manifestly covariant Lagrangian is in play, and whenever the naive Hamiltonian construction is about to be attempted (so you can anticipate its failure). The identity is what distinguishes a reparametrisation-invariant theory from an ordinary one, and recognising it is the key to handling the relativistic Hamiltonian correctly.

- **The generalized four-momentum $p_\mu = \partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu$**: the canonical momentum, a linear form along the worldline. It is the workhorse that links the three faces of the formalism: it is the conserved Noether charge (contracted with a generator), the input to the Legendre transform, and the object that gets promoted to $i\hbar\partial_\mu$ in quantisation. The recognisable setup is any problem needing a momentum — for a conservation law, a Hamiltonian, or a quantum transcription. The reusable move is to compute it by differentiation and then to keep straight whether it is the canonical momentum (conserved, gauge-dependent) or the kinetic momentum $mcU$ (mass-shell, gauge-invariant). It is the single object through which the Lagrangian, Hamiltonian, Noether, and quantum pictures all communicate.

# Bridges

1. **Geometric mechanics — the Hamiltonian formulation is symplectic geometry on a cotangent bundle.** The Poisson brackets, canonical transformations, and generating functions reviewed in §15.3 are the coordinate expression of a coordinate-free structure developed in [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics]]. Phase space is the [[Def - The Canonical Symplectic Form on a Cotangent Bundle|cotangent bundle]] $T^*Q$ carrying the canonical two-form $\omega = dp_\mu\wedge dx^\mu$; the [[Def - Poisson Bracket|Poisson bracket]] of two functions is $\{f,g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g)$ where $X_f$ is the Hamiltonian vector field of $f$; a [[Def - Symplectomorphism (Canonical Transformation)|canonical transformation]] is exactly a symplectomorphism, a diffeomorphism preserving $\omega$; and Hamilton's equations are the single geometric statement $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$. The fact, noted in §15.3, that the Poisson bracket is antisymmetric, bilinear, and satisfies the Jacobi identity — making the smooth functions a [[Def - Lie Algebra|Lie algebra]] — is the statement that $\{\cdot,\cdot\}$ is the Lie bracket induced by $\omega$. Even Gourgoulhon's remark that conservation of phase-space "volume" follows from the formalism is [[Thm - Liouville's Theorem on Phase Space Volume|Liouville's theorem]], which in the symplectic picture is the one-line consequence $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d\iota_{X_H}\omega + \iota_{X_H}d\omega = d(dH) + 0 = 0$ of $d\omega = 0$. The relativistic particle of this chapter is a worked example sitting inside that general theory, with the single twist that its Hamiltonian is constrained.

2. **The Legendre transform — the same bridge between Lagrangian and Hamiltonian, with a relativistic obstruction.** The passage from the Lagrangian $L(x,\dot x)$ to the Hamiltonian $H(x,p)$ via $p_\mu = \partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu$ and $H = p_\mu\dot x^\mu - L$ is the [[Def - The Legendre Transform|Legendre transform]] of [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics|geometric mechanics]], and the [[Thm - Equivalence of Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Formalisms|equivalence of the two formalisms]] holds whenever the transform is invertible — that is, whenever the Hessian $\partial^2 L/\partial\dot x^\mu\partial\dot x^\nu$ is non-degenerate. The relativistic free Lagrangian violates exactly this regularity condition: its Hessian annihilates $\dot x^\mu$, so the Legendre transform is singular and the equivalence theorem does not apply directly. This is not a relativistic pathology but a clean instance of the general theory of *degenerate* Lagrangians, whose Legendre transform lands on a constraint submanifold of phase space rather than on all of it. The resolution — Dirac's constrained Hamiltonian — is the general machinery for degenerate Legendre transforms, and the relativistic particle is its simplest non-trivial example.

3. **Riemannian geometry — the free particle is a geodesic, by the variation-of-length argument.** The free action $S = -mc\int\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\,d\lambda$ is (a constant times) the Lorentzian arc length of the worldline, and demanding it be stationary is the variational characterisation of a geodesic studied in [[Riemannian Geometry II — Geodesics, the Exponential Map, and Variational Principles]]. There the first variation of the length functional of a Riemannian metric is computed and shown to vanish exactly for curves satisfying the geodesic equation $\ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\;\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho = 0$; the special-relativistic computation is the same, specialised to the constant metric $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ where all Christoffel symbols vanish, so the geodesic equation collapses to $\ddot x^\mu = 0$, a straight line. The one sign difference — the metric is indefinite, so the stationary curve is a *maximum* of length rather than a minimum — is precisely the [[Thm - Inertial Worldlines Maximise Proper Time|reversed extremum]] of [[Special Relativity V — Worldlines, Proper Time and Four-Velocity]]. The bridge runs both ways: the geodesic flow on a Riemannian manifold is the prototype Hamiltonian system, and the relativistic free particle is the prototype geodesic.

4. **Electromagnetism — the action $q\int A_\mu dx^\mu$ is the source of the Lorentz force and the gateway to gauge theory.** Minimal coupling adds the line integral of the potential one-form $A$ to the action, and varying it produces the Lorentz four-force $qF_{\mu\nu}U^\nu$ with the field strength $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ — the antisymmetric tensor that, in [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field]], is shown to package the electric and magnetic fields. The construction reveals two structural facts before Maxwell's equations are even written. First, the field strength is exact, $F = dA$, so the homogeneous Maxwell equations $dF = 0$ hold automatically by $d^2 = 0$ — a preview of [[Special Relativity XXII — Maxwell's Equations]]. Second, the action is invariant under $A \mapsto A + d\chi$ up to endpoint terms, because $\int d\chi$ is a boundary integral; this **gauge invariance** is the same redundancy that, in [[Gauge Theory I — Connections on Vector Bundles and the Electromagnetic Connection|gauge theory]], identifies $A$ as a connection one-form whose curvature is $F$. The charged particle's worldline is the parallel transport of its quantum phase along $A$, and minimal coupling is the classical shadow of that geometric statement.

5. **The Poincaré group — Noether's ten conserved quantities are the moment map of the spacetime symmetry.** The free Lagrangian's invariance under the ten-parameter [[Def - The Poincaré Group|Poincaré group]] produces, by Noether's theorem, ten conserved quantities: the four-momentum (translations) and the angular-momentum tensor (boosts and rotations). These are not an ad hoc collection but the components of a single object — the **moment map** of the Poincaré action on phase space, in the language of [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics|symplectic geometry]]. The four-momentum is the moment map of the translation subgroup, the angular-momentum tensor that of the Lorentz subgroup, and their conservation is the geometric content of Noether's theorem read symplectically. Quantising the system replaces these classical charges by the generators of a unitary representation of the Poincaré group, whose two Casimir invariants — mass $P_\mu P^\mu$ and spin (the squared Pauli–Lubanski vector) — are, by [[Special Relativity XII — Inertial Observers and the Poincaré Group|Wigner's classification]], the defining labels of an elementary particle. The conservation laws derived here are the classical seed of the quantum notion of "particle."

6. **Constrained dynamics and quantisation — the relativistic particle is the toy model for gauge constraints.** The vanishing of the canonical Hamiltonian and the primary constraint $P\cdot P = m^2$ are the hallmarks of a *reparametrisation-invariant* theory, and their systematic treatment is the **Dirac–Bergmann constraint formalism**. The reparametrisation freedom of the worldline is a gauge symmetry; the mass-shell constraint is its first-class generator; gauge-fixing it amounts to choosing the parameter (for instance $\lambda = \tau$). This is the simplest example of the constraint structure that pervades **general relativity** (whose Hamiltonian is also a sum of constraints, by diffeomorphism invariance) and **string theory** (the relativistic string being the two-dimensional generalisation of the relativistic particle). The constraint-quantisation of these theories — promoting the constraints to operator conditions on physical states, and ultimately the cohomological BRST construction — is built on the pattern that the single relativistic particle exhibits in its simplest form. Anyone who understands why $H \equiv 0$ here understands the first and hardest conceptual step of canonical quantum gravity.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: the action is the single source of dynamics, symmetry, and quantisation.** The deepest reorganisation this chapter offers is that one object — the action functional $S$ — generates everything. In Newtonian mechanics the equation of motion is a separate postulate from the energy, which is separate from the list of conserved quantities; here they are three operations on one functional. Vary $S$ and you get the equation of motion. Find a symmetry of $S$ and Noether's theorem gives a conserved quantity. Legendre-transform $S$ and you get the Hamiltonian, which quantises. This is why the variational formulation is "usually a first step to the quantum version of a classical theory," as Gourgoulhon puts it: the action survives into the path integral $\int e^{iS/\hbar}\mathcal{D}x$, where the classical worldline is the stationary-phase point and the principle of least action is recovered as the $\hbar \to 0$ limit. When you meet a new relativistic system, do not ask for its equations of motion — ask for its action, and then differentiate it in whichever of the three ways the problem requires.

**The true name of the free action is "proper time, with a sign that makes geodesics maximal."** The formula $S = -mc^2\int d\tau$ is the right thing to compute with, but the conceptual content is hidden in the minus sign. Strip it away and the statement is: the free particle extremises its proper time, and the extremum is a *maximum* (the inertial worldline is the longest, [[Thm - Inertial Worldlines Maximise Proper Time]]). The minus sign is the bridge between two cultures of language — the geometer's "geodesics extremise length" and the physicist's "nature minimises action" — and it exists precisely because the Lorentzian extremum is a maximum where the Euclidean one would be a minimum. This single sign is doing real work: it makes "least action" literally true for the free particle while the underlying geometry is one of maximal proper time, and it is the same sign that distinguishes the timelike geodesics of relativity (longest) from the geodesics of Riemannian geometry (shortest). To remember the free action, remember the geometry: it is proper time, signed so that straight worldlines win.

**The vanishing Hamiltonian is not a defect but the gauge symmetry of time.** A reader meeting $H \equiv 0$ for the first time may suspect an error: a Hamiltonian that is identically zero seems to generate no motion. The resolution is that the relativistic particle has no preferred time, and "evolution in the worldline parameter $\lambda$" is a *gauge transformation*, not a physical change — so the generator of $\lambda$-evolution, the Hamiltonian, must vanish on the constraint surface. This is the first appearance of a phenomenon that dominates general relativity and string theory: in any theory with no absolute time, the Hamiltonian is a constraint, and "what is the energy?" and "what does it mean for time to flow?" become subtle. The relativistic particle exhibits this in miniature and resolves it cleanly: the genuine dynamics lives in the relation between the physical degrees of freedom (three of them), and the fourth "equation" $\dot x^\mu[\text{EL}]_\mu = 0$ is the redundancy of having parametrised the worldline. Once you see that the degeneracy is the reparametrisation gauge symmetry, the formalism stops being mysterious and becomes the template for constrained quantisation.

**Four-momentum is a one-form, and Noether's theorem is the proof.** It is easy to regard the distinction between vectors and covectors as pedantry, and to think of the four-momentum as "just a four-vector." Gourgoulhon's Remark 11.11 makes the case that it is fundamentally a *linear form*, and Noether's theorem supplies the cleanest argument. The conserved quantity Noether produces is $p_\mu G^\mu$, where $G^\mu$ are the components of the generator *vector* $G$ pointing along the symmetry direction; the only way to extract a number from a vector $G$ and a momentum $P$ is for $P$ to be a linear form *acting on* $G$, $\langle P, G\rangle = p_\mu G^\mu$. The momentum is the thing you contract *against* displacement vectors, which is the defining behaviour of a covector. The same point recurs in the coupling to electromagnetism, where the potential $A$ is a one-form (you integrate it along the worldline, $\int A_\mu dx^\mu$) precisely so that the field strength $F = dA$ can be defined by the exterior derivative, an operation available on forms but not on vectors. The chapter is, among other things, an extended argument that the natural home of momentum is the cotangent space — an insight that becomes indispensable the moment one writes Hamilton's equations on a cotangent bundle.

**Action at a distance and the no-interaction theorem mark the boundary of the particle picture.** The systems-of-particles material is brief but conceptually sharp: it shows where the single-particle formalism runs out. The [[Thm - Hamiltonian Formulation (Relativistic Particle)|Wheeler–Feynman]] theory of action at a distance is a genuine, Lorentz-invariant, field-free formulation of electromagnetism — but its equations are integro-differential, not a Cauchy problem, so the comforting uniqueness-from-initial-data of ordinary mechanics is lost. And the no-interaction theorem proves that one cannot even keep the particles' positions as canonical coordinates in a Poincaré-invariant interacting Hamiltonian theory: relativity, interaction, and "position is canonical" are mutually incompatible. The lesson is that the field is not a convenience but a necessity. The single particle in an external field is a coherent and powerful theory — the whole of this chapter — but the moment two particles interact *relativistically*, the natural description ceases to be particles-at-positions and becomes fields, mediating the interaction locally and restoring a well-posed initial-value problem. This is the conceptual pressure that pushes relativistic dynamics from the particle picture of this chapter into the field theory of [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field|the chapters that follow]].
