# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we set $c = 1$** and use the **mostly-minus** signature $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)$, so a timelike vector $X$ has $X\cdot X > 0$, a spacelike one has $X\cdot X < 0$, and a null one has $X\cdot X = 0$. Where a formula is more recognisable with $c$ restored, both forms are given. To restore $c$, replace $x^0 = t$ by $ct$ and insert factors of $c$ by dimensional analysis; the metric four-volume element gains one factor, $dU = c\sqrt{|g|}\,d^4x$.

> [!warning] Convention
> Gourgoulhon's *Special Relativity in General Frames*, the source for this chapter, uses the opposite signature $g = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$ with $\det g < 0$ and writes the volume element as $\sqrt{-\det g}\,d^4x$. We translate to mostly-minus throughout. The one mercy is that **the metric four-volume element is signature-robust**: in any four-dimensional Lorentzian metric the determinant is negative (one $+$, three $-$, product $<0$) in *either* convention, so $\sqrt{|\det g|} = \sqrt{-\det g}$ is the same object, and $dU = \sqrt{|g|}\,d^4x$ needs no sign change. What does flip are the unit-vector normalisations: a future-directed unit normal to a spacelike hypersurface has $n\cdot n = +1$ here (Gourgoulhon's $-1$), and a unit timelike tangent (a four-velocity) has $u\cdot u = +1$ here. Watch every normalisation $n\cdot n$, $u\cdot u$, $s\cdot s$ in a transcribed formula.

- $\mathscr{E}$ — Minkowski spacetime, the affine space $\mathbb{R}^4$ with metric $\eta$; $E$ — its underlying vector space of displacements
- $x^\mu$ — coordinates of an event, $\mu = 0,1,2,3$; $x^0 = t$ (or $ct$) in an inertial frame, but a general coordinate $x^0$ on a submanifold need not be a time coordinate
- $(x^\alpha)$ — an arbitrary (possibly curvilinear) coordinate system; $(\vec{e}_\alpha)$ — its coordinate basis, $\vec{e}_\alpha = \partial/\partial x^\alpha$
- $g_{\mu\nu}$, $g$ — the metric components in arbitrary coordinates and their determinant $\det(g_{\mu\nu})$; in an inertial frame $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}$ and $g = -1$
- $\sqrt{|g|}$ — the metric volume factor $\sqrt{|\det(g_{\mu\nu})|} = \sqrt{-\det(g_{\mu\nu})}$
- $\epsilon$, $\epsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}$ — the **Levi-Civita tensor** (volume 4-form), with $\epsilon_{0123} = \sqrt{|g|}$ in a right-handed coordinate basis; $\epsilon_{0123} = +1$ in a right-handed inertial frame
- $\star$ — the **Hodge star**; $\star A$ is the Hodge dual of the form $A$
- $\mathrm{d}$ — the **exterior derivative**, taking a $p$-form to a $(p+1)$-form, with $\mathrm{d}\circ\mathrm{d} = 0$
- $\nabla$, $\nabla\cdot\vec{v} = \nabla_\mu v^\mu$ — the **covariant derivative** and the **four-divergence** of a vector field
- $\mathscr{V}$ — a submanifold of $\mathscr{E}$ (a region of dimension $p \in \{1,2,3,4\}$, possibly with boundary); $\partial\mathscr{V}$ — its boundary, of dimension $p-1$
- $\underline{X}$, $X_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}X^\nu$ — the **metric dual** (1-form) of a vector $X$; $\underline{v} = v_\mu\,\mathrm{d}x^\mu$
- $\mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}_\alpha$ — the infinitesimal displacement vectors $\mathrm{d}x^\alpha\,\vec{e}_\alpha$ (no sum) generating a coordinate "hyperparallelepiped"
- $\mathrm{d}U$ — the four-volume element; $\mathrm{d}V$ — the volume (3-form) element of a hypersurface; $\mathrm{d}S$ — the area (2-form) element of a surface; $\mathrm{d}\ell$ — the length element of a curve
- $\vec{n}$ — the unit normal to a hypersurface; $\vec{u}$ — the unit tangent to a curve; $(\vec{n},\vec{s})$ — an orthonormal normal pair of a 2-surface
- $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$ — the **volume/area/length element form** of $\mathscr{V}$: a $p$-form on $\mathscr{V}$ ($\star\underline{n}$ for a hypersurface, $\star(\underline{n}\wedge\underline{s})$ for a 2-surface, $\pm\underline{u}$ for a curve)
- $\Phi_{\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v})$ — the **flux** of a vector field $\vec{v}$ through a hypersurface $\mathscr{V}$
- $\mathrm{vol}\,\mathscr{V}$, $\mathrm{int}_{\mathscr{V}}(f)$ — the four-volume of $\mathscr{V}$ and the integral of a scalar field $f$ over $\mathscr{V}$
- $J$, $J^\mu = (\rho, \mathbf{j})$ — the **electric four-current** (used in the charge-conservation application, owned by [[Special Relativity XXII — Maxwell's Equations]])
- Einstein summation: a repeated index, once up once down, is summed over $0,1,2,3$ (Greek) or $1,2,3$ (Latin $i,j$)

# Motivation

Everything physical that lives in spacetime is a field, and the laws those fields obey are conservation laws — of charge, of energy, of momentum, of baryon number. A conservation law is, at heart, a statement that a certain quantity does not leak: whatever flows out through the walls of a region equals the rate at which the contents decrease. To make such a statement precise you need to be able to integrate — over a four-dimensional region of spacetime, over a three-dimensional slice of "space at an instant", over the two-dimensional surface a flux passes through. This chapter builds that integration theory from the ground up, and it culminates in the single theorem that turns "the divergence vanishes" into "the flux through any closed boundary vanishes" — Stokes' theorem.

The backbone of the whole chapter is one equation, in which the exterior derivative trades places with the boundary operator:
$$\int_{\mathscr{V}} \mathrm{d}A \;=\; \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A.$$
Read it slowly. On the left, the exterior derivative $\mathrm{d}A$ of a $(p-1)$-form $A$ is integrated over a $p$-dimensional region $\mathscr{V}$. On the right, the form $A$ itself is integrated over the $(p-1)$-dimensional boundary $\partial\mathscr{V}$. The two are *equal*. This is the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem, the Kelvin–Stokes curl theorem, and the divergence theorem, all at once — they are the cases $p = 1, 2, 2, 3$ of this one identity, distinguished only by the dimension and by which classical object the abstract form $A$ unpacks into. Penrose liked it so much he suggested dropping Stokes' name and calling it *the fundamental theorem of exterior calculus*. The chapter's job is to assemble the machinery this equation needs — what it means to integrate a form, what a region and its boundary and their orientations are — and then to harvest its consequences.

The first thing to settle is what "volume" means when the metric is indefinite. In Euclidean space the volume of the box built on three vectors is their scalar triple product; the generalisation to four dimensions is the **Levi-Civita tensor** $\epsilon$, a totally antisymmetric 4-form, and the four-volume of the infinitesimal box built on $\mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}_0, \dots, \mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}_3$ is just $\epsilon$ evaluated on them. In coordinates this is the metric volume element $\mathrm{d}U = \sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}x^0\mathrm{d}x^1\mathrm{d}x^2\mathrm{d}x^3$, the factor $\sqrt{|g|}$ being exactly what makes the answer independent of which coordinates you chose. Section §20.1 sets this up and shows that integrating *any* 4-form $A$ over a four-region means integrating its single component $A_{0123}$ as an ordinary Lebesgue integral — and, remarkably, that this needs no metric at all.

Section §20.2 descends to lower dimensions. A region of spacetime of dimension less than four — a hypersurface, a surface, a curve — is a **submanifold**, and to integrate over it you must first orient it and equip it with the right volume form. The pattern is uniform and beautiful: the volume element of a hypersurface with unit normal $\vec{n}$ is the Hodge dual $\star\underline{n}$ of the normal's 1-form; the area element of a 2-surface with normal pair $(\vec{n},\vec{s})$ is $\star(\underline{n}\wedge\underline{s})$; the length element of a curve with unit tangent $\vec{u}$ is $\pm\underline{u}$. Once these are in hand, the **flux** of a vector field $\vec{v}$ through a hypersurface is the integral of $\star\underline{v}$, and a flux integral is the only kind of integral a conservation law ever really needs.

Section §20.3 states and proves Stokes' theorem and then extracts the two corollaries that the rest of relativistic physics runs on: the three-dimensional Gauss–Ostrogradsky (divergence) theorem inside a spatial slice, and the four-dimensional Gauss–Ostrogradsky theorem, $\Phi_{\partial\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}} \nabla\cdot\vec{v}\,\mathrm{d}U$, which says the flux of a vector field out through the closed boundary of a four-region equals the integral of its four-divergence inside. That last identity is the entire content of "$\nabla\cdot J = 0 \Leftrightarrow$ charge is conserved", and it is why this purely mathematical chapter is the indispensable prerequisite for [[Special Relativity XXII — Maxwell's Equations|Maxwell's equations]] and the [[Special Relativity XXIII — The Energy-Momentum Tensor and Field Energy|conservation laws]] that follow.

The reader is assumed to be comfortable with differential forms, the exterior derivative, the wedge product, and the Hodge star on spacetime — the machinery of [[Special Relativity XVIII — Tensors, Alternate Forms and Hodge Duality]] and [[Special Relativity XIX — Fields on Spacetime and the Covariant Derivative]] — and to know the manifold version of Stokes' theorem from [[Differential Geometry IX — Orientation, Integration, and Stokes' Theorem]]. This chapter is the affine-spacetime specialisation: the manifold is flat $\mathbb{R}^4$, the metric is constant (so $\sqrt{|g|}$ varies only because the *coordinates* are curvilinear), and every abstract construction acquires an explicit Minkowski formula. The genuinely new conceptual ingredient is not the integration theory — that is differential geometry — but the systematic translation of "flux", "divergence", and "conservation" into the language of forms and the Hodge star, because *that* is the language in which the field theories of the next chapters are written.

# Concept Map

## §20.1 Integration over Four-Volumes

- **[[Def - Integration of Forms and the Volume Element]]**
	- The **four-volume element** of the infinitesimal box built on displacement vectors $\mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}_0,\dots,\mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}_3$ is $\mathrm{d}U = \epsilon(\mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}_0,\dots,\mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}_3)$, the Levi-Civita 4-form evaluated on them; in coordinates $\mathrm{d}U = \sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}x^0\mathrm{d}x^1\mathrm{d}x^2\mathrm{d}x^3$, reducing to $\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z$ in an inertial frame. The four-volume of a region $\mathscr{V}$ is $\mathrm{vol}\,\mathscr{V} = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\epsilon$, and more generally the integral of any 4-form $A$ is $\int_{\mathscr{V}} A = \int_{\mathscr{V}} A_{0123}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ — a plain Lebesgue integral of the single independent component. The factor $\sqrt{|g|}$ is precisely the Jacobian compensator that makes the answer coordinate-independent; the integral of a *4-form* needs no metric at all, while the *volume* of a region does.

- **[[Ex - Integrating a four-form over a region of spacetime]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute the four-volume of a coordinate box and the integral of a given 4-form, in inertial and in spherical coordinates, checking that the $\sqrt{|g|} = r^2\sin\theta$ factor restores the familiar $\mathrm{d}t\,r^2\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi$ and that the answer is coordinate-independent. (⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Coordinate-independence of the four-volume]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show directly from the change-of-variables formula that $\mathrm{vol}\,\mathscr{V} = \int\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ does not depend on the coordinate system, using $\sqrt{-\det g'} = |J|\sqrt{-\det g}$ with $J$ the Jacobian, and explain why the integral of a 4-form is independent of coordinates *without* invoking the metric at all. (⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Only an antisymmetric form integrates coordinate-independently]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that the single component $A_{0123}$ of a 4-form integrates to a coordinate-independent number, but the analogous integral $\int T_{0123}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ of one component of a generic (non-antisymmetric) type-(0,4) tensor does not, by tracking the change-of-variable transformation and seeing that only the alternating combination matches the Jacobian — the structural reason forms, not tensors, are the objects of integration. (⭐⭐)

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Action Integral of a Field Theory *(from Classical Field Theory and QFT)*
> The metric four-volume element $\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ is the measure with respect to which every **field action** $S = \int \mathcal{L}\,\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ is built. Lorentz invariance of the action is exactly the statement that $\mathcal{L}$ is a scalar and $\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ is an invariant measure; varying such an action gives the **Euler–Lagrange field equations**, and in curved spacetime the very same $\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ is the measure of the Einstein–Hilbert action whose variation yields **Einstein's equations**.

## §20.2 Submanifolds and Their Integration

- **[[Def - Submanifolds of Spacetime]]**
	- A **submanifold** $\mathscr{V}$ of $\mathscr{E}$ of dimension $p \in \{1,2,3\}$ is a region that, near each of its points, is cut out by $4-p$ coordinate conditions $x^A = \mathrm{const}$ in some adapted coordinate system — a curve ($p=1$), a surface ($p=2$), or a hypersurface ($p=3$). A **submanifold with boundary** adds one inequality $x^{4-p}\le K$; its **boundary** $\partial\mathscr{V}$, where $x^{4-p}=K$, is a submanifold of dimension $p-1$, with the coordinate basis vector $\vec{e}_{4-p}$ pointing outward. An **orientation** is a nowhere-vanishing $p$-form $\rho$; a manifold's orientation induces the **outward-normal-first** orientation $\rho(\vec{e}_{4-p},\,\cdot\,)$ on its boundary, the convention that makes Stokes' theorem hold with a $+$ sign.

- **[[Def - Volume, Area, Length Elements and Flux Integrals]]**
	- To integrate over a submanifold you equip it with a $p$-form built from the Levi-Civita tensor and the submanifold's normals: the **volume element** of a hypersurface with future unit normal $\vec{n}$ is $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}} = \epsilon(\vec{n},\cdot,\cdot,\cdot) = \star\underline{n}$; the **area element** of a spacelike 2-surface with orthonormal normal pair $(\vec{n},\vec{s})$ is $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}} = \star(\underline{n}\wedge\underline{s})$; the **length element** of a curve with unit tangent $\vec{u}$ is $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}} = \pm\underline{u}$, giving $\mathrm{d}\ell = \|\mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}\|_g$. The integral of a scalar field is $\mathrm{int}_{\mathscr{V}}(f) = \int_{\mathscr{V}} f\,\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$, and the **flux** of a vector field through a hypersurface is $\Phi_{\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v}) = \pm\int_{\mathscr{V}}\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\,\mathrm{d}V = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\star\underline{v}$ — the integral of the Hodge dual of the vector's 1-form.

- **[[Ex - The flux of a vector field through a hypersurface]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute the flux $\Phi_\Sigma(\vec{v}) = \int_\Sigma \star\underline{v}$ of a vector field through a constant-time hyperplane $\Sigma$, show it reduces to $\int_\Sigma v^0\,\mathrm{d}^3x$ (the "amount of $\vec{v}$ crossing the slice"), and verify that a vector tangent to $\Sigma$ has zero flux. (⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - The volume element of a hypersurface is the Hodge dual of its normal]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}} = \star\underline{n}$ for a hypersurface by computing $(\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}})_{\alpha\beta\gamma} = n^\mu\epsilon_{\mu\alpha\beta\gamma}$ and comparing with the component formula for the Hodge star of a 1-form; deduce $\mathrm{d}V = n^0\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}x^1\mathrm{d}x^2\mathrm{d}x^3$ and recover the round-sphere area element. (⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Length, area, and volume elements in spherical coordinates]]** (⭐)
	- Compute $\sqrt{|g|}$ for inertial spherical coordinates and read off the length element of a radial curve, the area element of a sphere $t=\mathrm{const}, r=R$, and the volume element of a spatial ball, confirming each against the elementary formulas $\mathrm{d}\ell = \mathrm{d}r$, $\mathrm{d}S = R^2\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi$, $\mathrm{d}V = r^2\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi$. (⭐)

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Conserved Charge of a Symmetry *(from Noether's Theorem and Field Theory)*
> A flux integral $Q_\Sigma = \int_\Sigma \star\underline{J}$ of a conserved current $J$ through a spacelike slice $\Sigma$ is exactly a **Noether charge**. The integral-of-a-form construction here is what makes "the total charge at time $t$" a well-defined, frame-aware object: it is the integral of the 3-form $\star\underline{J}$ over the slice, and §20.3 will show it is independent of which slice you choose precisely when $\nabla\cdot J = 0$. This is the template for electric charge, energy, momentum, and angular momentum as integrals over a Cauchy surface.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Cauchy Surfaces and the Initial-Value Problem *(from General Relativity and PDE Theory)*
> A spacelike hypersurface — a "space at an instant", whose timelike unit normal $\vec{n}$ is an observer's four-velocity — is the relativistic notion of an **instant of time**, and the data a field carries on it (the field and its normal derivative) are the **initial data** for its evolution. The volume element $\star\underline{n}$ built here is the measure in which energy and charge on the slice are counted; in general relativity such a slice is a **Cauchy surface**, and the whole subject of the gravitational initial-value problem (the ADM formulation) is the study of how geometry propagates from one such slice to the next.

## §20.3 Stokes' Theorem and Its Applications

- **[[Thm - Stokes Theorem on Spacetime]]**
	- For an oriented compact submanifold-with-boundary $\mathscr{V}$ of dimension $p$ and a differential $(p-1)$-form $A$, Stokes' theorem states $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$, with $\partial\mathscr{V}$ carrying the induced (outward-normal-first) orientation. It is metric-independent — both sides are integrals of forms, defined without $g$. Its low-dimensional cases are the fundamental theorem of calculus ($p=1$), the Green–Riemann formula ($p=2$ in a plane), and the Kelvin–Stokes curl theorem ($p=2$ surface): a single identity, $\mathrm{d}$ on one side traded for $\partial$ on the other, of which all the classical integral theorems are shadows.

- **[[Thm - Gauss-Ostrogradsky Theorem (3D and 4D)]]**
	- Stokes' theorem applied to flux integrals gives the two divergence theorems. The **3D Gauss–Ostrogradsky theorem** $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\nabla\cdot\vec{v}\,\mathrm{d}V = \oint_{\partial\mathscr{V}}\vec{v}\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec{S}$ holds for a vector field tangent to a spatial slice and is the ordinary divergence theorem. The **4D Gauss–Ostrogradsky theorem** $\Phi_{\partial\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\nabla\cdot\vec{v}\,\mathrm{d}U$ relates the flux of a vector field out through the *closed hypersurface* $\partial\mathscr{V}$ bounding a four-region to the integral of its four-divergence inside, via the identity $\mathrm{d}\star\underline{v} = (\nabla\cdot\vec{v})\,\epsilon$. Unlike Stokes' theorem itself, these depend on the metric (through the divergence, the volume element, and the flux).

- **[[Ex - Stokes' theorem for a three-form gives the four-dimensional Gauss theorem]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Starting from the flux of $\vec{v}$ through $\partial\mathscr{V}$ as $\int_{\partial\mathscr{V}}\star\underline{v}$, apply Stokes' theorem and the identity $\mathrm{d}\star\underline{v} = (\nabla\cdot\vec{v})\,\epsilon$ to derive the four-dimensional divergence theorem $\Phi_{\partial\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\nabla\cdot\vec{v}\,\mathrm{d}U$, and verify the analogous 3D statement inside a spatial slab. (⭐⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Charge conservation as a flux statement]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Show that the local conservation law $\nabla\cdot J = 0$ for the electric four-current is equivalent to the vanishing of the flux $\int_{\partial\mathscr{V}}\star\underline{J}$ through every closed hypersurface, and deduce that the total charge $Q_\Sigma = \int_\Sigma \star\underline{J}$ is the same on every spacelike slice $\Sigma$ — total charge is conserved and slice-independent — by applying the 4D Gauss theorem to a slab between two slices with the current vanishing at spatial infinity. (⭐⭐⭐)

- **[[Ex - Green and Kelvin-Stokes as cases of the master theorem]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Specialise $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$ to a 1-form $A = P\,\mathrm{d}x + Q\,\mathrm{d}y$ on a planar region to recover the Green–Riemann formula, and to a 1-form on a spacelike 2-surface to recover $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{curl}\,\vec{A}\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec{S} = \oint_{\partial\mathscr{V}}\vec{A}\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}$ (Kelvin–Stokes), identifying which component of $\mathrm{d}A$ each classical theorem reads off. (⭐⭐)

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Generalised Stokes Theorem and de Rham Cohomology *(from Differential Geometry and Algebraic Topology)*
> Because $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$ holds for *every* form on *every* oriented region, it makes **closed forms** ($\mathrm{d}A=0$) and **exact forms** ($A=\mathrm{d}B$) into the natural pairing partners of **cycles** ($\partial\mathscr{V}=0$) and **boundaries**: integrating a closed form over a cycle gives a number that depends only on the cohomology class of the form and the homology class of the cycle. This pairing is **de Rham cohomology**, and it is the reason a flux can be topologically protected — the integral of a closed but non-exact form over a non-bounding cycle (a magnetic monopole's flux through a sphere surrounding it, say) cannot be deformed to zero.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Conservation Laws as Vanishing Boundary Flux *(from Energy-Momentum and General Relativity)*
> The 4D Gauss theorem is the master template for *every* conservation law in relativistic physics. **Energy-momentum conservation** $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ becomes, by exactly this argument, the statement that the flux of four-momentum through any closed hypersurface vanishes, so the total four-momentum on a spacelike slice is conserved. The same flux argument underlies **baryon-number conservation** in relativistic hydrodynamics and, in general relativity, the construction of conserved quantities (Komar mass, ADM momentum) as flux integrals over a sphere at spatial infinity — the only conserved charges a curved spacetime generally admits.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The problems of this chapter chase a handful of recurring goals. The most basic is to **compute an integral over a region of spacetime** — a four-volume, the volume of a spatial slice, the area of a surface, the length of a curve — and the entire skill is in choosing coordinates adapted to the region and writing down the correct volume factor $\sqrt{|g|}$ and the correct $p$-form $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$. A second target, the one the chapter is really built for, is to **express a conservation law as a vanishing flux**: given that some four-divergence vanishes, prove that a total charge is conserved and independent of the slice on which it is measured. A third is to **recover a classical integral theorem** — the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem, the curl theorem, the divergence theorem — as a special case of $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$, which both demystifies the classical theorems and confirms that the abstract machinery reduces correctly. A fourth is **structural**: to prove that a definition (the four-volume, the integral of a $p$-form) is independent of the coordinates or the mesh used to compute it, the prototype being the $\sqrt{-\det g'} = |J|\sqrt{-\det g}$ calculation. A fifth, more conceptual, is to **identify the right geometric object** for a physical quantity — that a flux is the integral of a Hodge dual, that a charge is the integral of a 3-form over a slice, that the "amount of stuff crossing a surface" is intrinsically a form, not a number times a measure. Across all five the unifying remark is that the natural objects of integration are *differential forms*, and the metric enters only when you want a *volume* — the integral of a form is metric-free, the volume of a region is not.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A region with adapted coordinates is given** — a coordinate box, a ball, a sphere, a worldline segment — and the adapted coordinates immediately supply the metric components $g_{\mu\nu}$, the volume factor $\sqrt{|g|}$, and the normals $\vec{n}, \vec{s}, \vec{u}$ that build $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$. **A four-divergence is given to vanish**, $\nabla\cdot\vec{v} = 0$ or $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, which is the precondition that turns the 4D Gauss theorem into a conservation statement — this is the richest source, because it converts a local differential identity into a global statement about fluxes through boundaries. **A vector or tensor field is specified**, and its metric dual, its Hodge dual, and its divergence are then all computable, so any flux it produces can be evaluated. **A form is recognised as exact or closed** — $A = \mathrm{d}B$ makes $\int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A$ collapse usefully, and $\mathrm{d}A=0$ makes its integral over a boundary vanish. **A symmetry or a normalisation is given** — that $\vec{n}$ is a unit normal, that $\vec{u}$ is a four-velocity with $\vec{u}\cdot\vec{u}=1$, that the coordinates are right-handed — and the normalisation fixes the form $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$ up to the orientation sign. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a region with adapted coordinates routes through $\sqrt{|g|}$ and $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$ to a computed integral; a vanishing divergence routes through the 4D Gauss theorem to a conserved charge; a recognised exact form routes through Stokes' theorem to a boundary integral; a specified field routes through its Hodge dual to a flux. The [[Special Relativity XX — Integration in Spacetime and Stokes' Theorem#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in the chapter is assembled from. The integration is differential-geometric, so each operation states explicitly *which form lives on which dimension* and *whether the metric is involved* — that bookkeeping is where the errors hide. When stuck, scan the list and try each.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Write the four-volume element as $\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$.** In any right-handed coordinate system the four-volume element is $\mathrm{d}U = \sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}x^0\mathrm{d}x^1\mathrm{d}x^2\mathrm{d}x^3$, where $g = \det(g_{\mu\nu})$; see [[Def - Integration of Forms and the Volume Element]]. *Trigger:* you must integrate a scalar over a four-region, or compute a four-volume. *Pattern:* compute the metric components in your coordinates, take the determinant, and the square root of its absolute value is the Jacobian-compensating factor that makes the integral coordinate-independent. In an inertial frame this is simply $\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z$.

2. **Integrate a $p$-form by reading off its single tangential component.** To integrate a $p$-form $A$ over a $p$-dimensional submanifold with adapted coordinates, integrate the single component $A_{(4-p)\dots 3}$ as a Lebesgue integral over the internal coordinates; see [[Def - Integration of Forms and the Volume Element]]. *Trigger:* an integral of a form (not a scalar) over a submanifold. *Pattern:* only the component of $A$ applied to the tangent vectors of the submanifold survives, so identify the adapted coordinates, pick out that one component, and integrate it. This operation needs no metric — it is the metric-free heart of the theory.

3. **Build the submanifold's volume form from its normals.** Equip a hypersurface with $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}} = \star\underline{n}$ (future unit normal $\vec{n}$), a 2-surface with $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}} = \star(\underline{n}\wedge\underline{s})$, a curve with $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}} = \pm\underline{u}$; see [[Def - Volume, Area, Length Elements and Flux Integrals]]. *Trigger:* you must integrate a *scalar* (a density, an energy, a charge density) over a lower-dimensional region. *Pattern:* find the unit normal(s) or tangent, dualise and Hodge-star to get the $p$-form $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$, then integrate $f\,\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$. The metric is essential here — a scalar integral over a submanifold is a volume, hence metric-dependent.

4. **Express a flux as the integral of a Hodge dual.** The flux of a vector field $\vec{v}$ through a hypersurface is $\Phi_{\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\star\underline{v}$, equivalently $\pm\int_{\mathscr{V}}\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\,\mathrm{d}V$; see [[Def - Volume, Area, Length Elements and Flux Integrals]]. *Trigger:* any "amount of stuff crossing a surface" — charge crossing a slice, energy through a wall, particles through a screen. *Pattern:* dualise $\vec{v}$ to the 1-form $\underline{v}$, Hodge-star to the 3-form $\star\underline{v}$, integrate over the hypersurface. This recasts a flux as a form integral, which is exactly the form Stokes' theorem consumes.

5. **Apply Stokes' theorem to trade $\mathrm{d}$ for $\partial$.** For a $(p-1)$-form $A$ and a $p$-region $\mathscr{V}$, $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$; see [[Thm - Stokes Theorem on Spacetime]]. *Trigger:* you have an integral of a derivative ($\mathrm{d}A$) over a region, or an integral over a boundary, and want to convert one to the other. *Pattern:* recognise the integrand as an exterior derivative, swap the region for its boundary (or vice versa) and drop one $\mathrm{d}$. The induced orientation on $\partial\mathscr{V}$ is outward-normal-first; getting that sign right is the whole subtlety.

6. **Use the identity $\mathrm{d}\star\underline{v} = (\nabla\cdot\vec{v})\,\epsilon$.** The exterior derivative of the Hodge dual of a vector's 1-form is its four-divergence times the volume form; see [[Thm - Gauss-Ostrogradsky Theorem (3D and 4D)]] and [[Special Relativity XIX/Def - The Exterior Derivative|Def - The Exterior Derivative]]. *Trigger:* a flux integral $\int_{\partial\mathscr{V}}\star\underline{v}$ that you want to convert to a volume integral, or a divergence you want to express as a boundary flux. *Pattern:* this is the bridge between operation 4 (flux as Hodge-dual integral) and operation 1 (volume integral) — applying Stokes to $\star\underline{v}$ and then this identity gives the 4D Gauss theorem in one line.

7. **Turn a vanishing divergence into a slice-independent charge.** If $\nabla\cdot\vec{v} = 0$, the flux of $\vec{v}$ through any closed hypersurface vanishes, so the flux through any two spacelike slices (with the field dying at spatial infinity) agree; see [[Thm - Gauss-Ostrogradsky Theorem (3D and 4D)]]. *Trigger:* a conservation law in the form $\nabla\cdot\vec{v}=0$ or $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0$. *Pattern:* enclose the spacetime region between two slices in a closed boundary, apply the 4D Gauss theorem, and the vanishing four-divergence makes the two slice-fluxes equal — that equality *is* the conservation of the total charge.

8. **Compute the normal of a hypersurface as the metric dual of a coordinate gradient.** A hypersurface $x^0 = \mathrm{const}$ has normal $\vec{m}$ dual to the 1-form $\nabla x^0 = \mathrm{d}x^0$; a vector $\vec{v}$ is tangent iff $\vec{m}\cdot\vec{v}=0$; see [[Def - Volume, Area, Length Elements and Flux Integrals]]. *Trigger:* you need the normal to a level set. *Pattern:* the gradient of the defining coordinate is normal; raise its index with the metric, normalise, and you have the unit normal $\vec{n}$. Whether $\vec{n}$ is timelike, spacelike, or null determines whether the hypersurface is spacelike, timelike, or null.

9. **Classify a hypersurface by its normal.** A hypersurface is spacelike if its normal is timelike, timelike if its normal is spacelike, and null if its normal is null (and then the normal is also tangent); see [[Def - Volume, Area, Length Elements and Flux Integrals]]. *Trigger:* a flux or integral over a hypersurface whose causal character matters, or a sign ambiguity in a flux. *Pattern:* a spacelike slice ("space at an instant") has a timelike unit normal that is some observer's four-velocity; a timelike wall has a spacelike normal; the sign in $\Phi = \pm\int\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\,\mathrm{d}V$ is fixed by which case you are in.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Forgetting the $\sqrt{|g|}$ in a curvilinear-coordinate integral
> In an inertial frame $\sqrt{|g|}=1$ and the four-volume element is just $\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}z$, which lulls you into writing $\int f\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ in *any* coordinates. In spherical coordinates this is wrong by a factor $r^2\sin\theta$: the correct element is $\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x = \mathrm{d}t\,r^2\sin\theta\,\mathrm{d}r\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\varphi$, and dropping the factor gives a coordinate-dependent, physically meaningless answer. The operation becomes legal exactly when you either are in an inertial (Cartesian) frame, where $\sqrt{|g|}=1$, or you reinstate the $\sqrt{|g|}$ factor that compensates the Jacobian of the coordinate change. The factor is *not* optional decoration — it is what makes the integral a geometric quantity.

> [!warning] 2. Integrating a generic tensor component and expecting coordinate-independence
> It is tempting to think that, just as the single component $A_{0123}$ of a 4-form integrates to a coordinate-independent number, the integral $\int T_{0123}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ of one component of *any* type-$(0,4)$ tensor is meaningful. It is not. The proof of coordinate-independence (the $A'_{23} = A_{23}J$ step) uses the *antisymmetry* of the form crucially: a generic tensor's component transforms with extra terms and the change-of-variable Jacobian no longer matches. The operation is legal only for **alternating** forms — that is exactly why differential forms, and not generic tensors, are the natural objects of integration ([[Def - Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product]]). To integrate a tensor field invariantly you must instead use the metric volume element, $\int T^{\mu\nu}\,\mathrm{d}U$, contracting against $\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$.

> [!warning] 3. Using the wrong induced orientation on the boundary
> Stokes' theorem $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$ holds with a $+$ sign *only* when $\partial\mathscr{V}$ carries the **outward-normal-first** induced orientation: a basis $(\vec{v}_1,\dots,\vec{v}_{p-1})$ of the boundary is positively oriented iff $(\vec{e}_{\mathrm{out}}, \vec{v}_1,\dots,\vec{v}_{p-1})$ is positively oriented in $\mathscr{V}$. Choosing the boundary orientation by some other convention — "counterclockwise", or inward-normal-first — flips the sign and turns the theorem into $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = -\int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$. The operation is legal only with the outward-first convention; for a four-region bounded by two spacelike slices this means the *future* slice gets its natural orientation and the *past* slice gets the reversed one, which is exactly the sign that makes "charge in minus charge out" come out right ([[Def - Submanifolds of Spacetime]]).

> [!warning] 4. Treating a null hypersurface like a spacelike or timelike one
> The clean story — unit normal $\vec{n}$, volume element $\star\underline{n}$, flux $\pm\int\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\,\mathrm{d}V$ — assumes the normal can be normalised, i.e. $\vec{m}\cdot\vec{m}\ne 0$. On a **null** hypersurface (a light cone, a horizon) the normal is null, $\vec{m}\cdot\vec{m}=0$, so it *cannot* be normalised and is simultaneously normal *and* tangent. The construction of a unit normal breaks down, and a separate (degenerate) treatment is needed. The operation is legal only for spacelike or timelike hypersurfaces; for a null hypersurface one must abandon the unit-normal apparatus and work with the null normal directly, a subtlety that becomes central when integrating fluxes across horizons in general relativity.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this chapter are won the moment you decide two things: what dimension you are integrating over, and whether the quantity you want is a *form* (metric-free) or a *volume/scalar integral* (metric-dependent). Almost every error is a confusion between these two, so name them first.

If the problem **asks for an integral over a region** — a four-volume, a spatial volume, an area, a length, or the integral of a density over one of these — the decisive first move is to find coordinates adapted to the region, in which the region is a coordinate box. Then write down the right volume form. For a four-region the element is $\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ ([[Def - Integration of Forms and the Volume Element]]); for a lower-dimensional region you build $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$ from the normals — $\star\underline{n}$ for a hypersurface, $\star(\underline{n}\wedge\underline{s})$ for a surface, $\pm\underline{u}$ for a curve ([[Def - Volume, Area, Length Elements and Flux Integrals]]) — and integrate $f\,\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$. The reason adapted coordinates work is that they reduce the geometric integral to an ordinary Lebesgue integral over a rectangular domain, with the geometry entirely absorbed into the $\sqrt{|g|}$ factor. The single most common mistake is to forget that factor in curvilinear coordinates; the sanity check is that with $f=1$ you must recover the elementary volume of the region.

If the problem **asks for a flux** — how much of a vector field crosses a surface — recast it immediately as the integral of a Hodge dual: $\Phi_{\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\star\underline{v}$ ([[Def - Volume, Area, Length Elements and Flux Integrals]]). This is worth doing even when the elementary form $\pm\int\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\,\mathrm{d}V$ would compute the answer, because the Hodge-dual form is exactly what Stokes' theorem acts on, and almost every flux problem is secretly a conservation problem in disguise. Through a constant-time slice the flux of $\vec{v}$ reduces to $\int v^0\,\mathrm{d}^3x$ — the integral of the time component, which is the "density" being transported.

If the problem **is a conservation law** — you are told some four-divergence vanishes and asked to prove a quantity is conserved — the instrument is the four-dimensional Gauss–Ostrogradsky theorem ([[Thm - Gauss-Ostrogradsky Theorem (3D and 4D)]]). The route is mechanical once you see it: the total charge at a time is the flux $Q_\Sigma = \int_\Sigma\star\underline{J}$ through the spacelike slice $\Sigma$; to compare $Q_{\Sigma_1}$ and $Q_{\Sigma_2}$, enclose the four-region between the two slices in a closed hypersurface (capped off at spatial infinity, where the current dies), and apply the 4D Gauss theorem. The integral of $\nabla\cdot J$ over the interior vanishes by hypothesis, so the total flux out of the closed boundary is zero, which — once you account for the outward orientation flipping the past slice's sign — says $Q_{\Sigma_1} = Q_{\Sigma_2}$. The local statement $\nabla\cdot J = 0$ and the global statement "total charge is conserved and slice-independent" are the *same fact*, related by this one theorem. This is the deepest pattern in the chapter and the reason it exists.

If the problem **asks to recover a classical theorem** — Green, the curl theorem, the divergence theorem, the fundamental theorem of calculus — the strategy is to specialise the master identity $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$ ([[Thm - Stokes Theorem on Spacetime]]) to the right dimension and the right form. The work is in identifying which component of $\mathrm{d}A$ the classical "curl" or "divergence" corresponds to, and writing the boundary integral $\int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$ in the classical "$\vec{A}\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}$" or "$\vec{v}\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec{S}$" notation. The payoff is conceptual: all four classical theorems are one theorem, and the apparatus of "div, grad, curl" is just the three-dimensional Hodge star and exterior derivative in disguise.

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle: **forms are what you integrate; the metric is what you need for a volume.** The integral of a $p$-form over a $p$-region is a coordinate-free, metric-free number — that is the content of operation 2 and the reason Stokes' theorem is metric-independent. The moment you want to integrate a *scalar* (a density) over a region, or compute a *volume*, you need the metric, because "how big is this region" is a metric question. Every question in this chapter is, at bottom, the question: **am I integrating a form, or am I measuring a volume?** — and knowing which tells you immediately whether $\sqrt{|g|}$ and $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$ enter or not.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Stokes Theorem on Spacetime|Stokes' theorem]]**: $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$. This is the most-used single fact in the chapter and in every field-theory chapter that follows, because it is the universal device for converting between "what happens inside a region" and "what crosses its boundary". Reach for it whenever an integrand is (or can be written as) an exterior derivative, whenever a boundary integral appears, and whenever a conservation law must be turned from local to global. Its disguised uses are the most valuable: the fundamental theorem of calculus, Green's theorem, the curl theorem, and the divergence theorem are all this one identity, and the 4D Gauss theorem — the engine of every relativistic conservation law — is Stokes applied to the Hodge dual of a current. Recognise its applicability the instant you see $\mathrm{d}$ of something integrated, or a flux through a closed surface.

- **The metric four-volume element** $\mathrm{d}U = \sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$: the invariant measure on spacetime. Its reusable content is that $\sqrt{|g|}$ is precisely the Jacobian compensator that makes any scalar integral coordinate-independent, so $\int f\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ is a genuine geometric quantity while $\int f\,\mathrm{d}^4x$ is not. **Typical use:** it is the measure of every field action $\int\mathcal{L}\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$, the measure in which energy and charge densities are integrated to totals, and the object whose square-root structure carries over verbatim to curved spacetime. Whenever you integrate a scalar over a four-region in anything but Cartesian inertial coordinates, this factor is the first thing to write down.

- **The flux-as-Hodge-dual identity** $\Phi_{\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\star\underline{v}$: the recognition that a flux is intrinsically the integral of a 3-form ([[Def - Volume, Area, Length Elements and Flux Integrals]]). Its recognisable setup is any "amount crossing a surface" quantity, and its reusable power is that it puts the flux into the exact shape Stokes' theorem consumes — so a flux problem becomes a conservation problem with one application of $\mathrm{d}$. **Typical use:** combine it with the identity $\mathrm{d}\star\underline{v} = (\nabla\cdot\vec{v})\epsilon$ and Stokes to get the 4D Gauss theorem; combine it with $\nabla\cdot\vec{v}=0$ to get a conserved charge. Reach for it whenever a flux must be related to a divergence or compared across surfaces.

- **The 4D Gauss–Ostrogradsky theorem** $\Phi_{\partial\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\nabla\cdot\vec{v}\,\mathrm{d}U$ ([[Thm - Gauss-Ostrogradsky Theorem (3D and 4D)]]): the bridge between local conservation ($\nabla\cdot\vec{v}=0$) and global conservation (vanishing flux through every closed hypersurface). Its reusable content is the whole logic of conserved charges in relativistic physics: a vanishing four-divergence makes the total charge on a spacelike slice independent of the slice. **Typical use:** charge conservation in electromagnetism, energy-momentum conservation $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=0$, baryon-number conservation in hydrodynamics — each is this theorem with a different current. Recognise its applicability the moment a problem gives you a vanishing divergence and asks about a total quantity.

- **The volume forms $\star\underline{n}$, $\star(\underline{n}\wedge\underline{s})$, $\pm\underline{u}$** of hypersurfaces, surfaces, and curves: the uniform Hodge-star recipe for the measure on a submanifold ([[Def - Volume, Area, Length Elements and Flux Integrals]]). The reusable insight is that the volume element of a $p$-submanifold is always the Hodge dual of the wedge of its unit normals — a single pattern covering all codimensions. **Typical use:** integrating a density (charge density, energy density, mass density) over a slice to get a total; the length element $\|\mathrm{d}\vec{\ell}\|_g$ of a worldline that gives proper time; the area element of a sphere through which a flux passes. Whenever you must integrate a scalar over a region of dimension less than four, this recipe gives you the correct measure with the correct orientation.

# Bridges

1. **Differential geometry — this is the affine-spacetime specialisation of the manifold Stokes theorem.** The general statement $\int_M \mathrm{d}\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega$ for a compactly supported $(n-1)$-form $\omega$ on an oriented smooth $n$-manifold with boundary is proved once and for all in [[Differential Geometry IX — Orientation, Integration, and Stokes' Theorem]], via partitions of unity and the half-space model, and the master theorem of this chapter, [[Thm - Stokes Theorem on Spacetime]], *is* that theorem with $M$ taken to be a region of flat $\mathbb{R}^4$. The specialisation buys two things. First, because Minkowski space is affine and globally covered by inertial coordinates, the partition-of-unity machinery is unnecessary for the regions physics cares about — one adapted coordinate chart usually suffices. Second, the constant metric $\eta$ lets every abstract object acquire an explicit Minkowski formula: the volume form is $\star\underline{n}$ with $\vec{n}$ a literal four-vector, the divergence theorem becomes $\Phi_{\partial\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v}) = \int\nabla\cdot\vec{v}\,\mathrm{d}U$ with $\mathrm{d}U = \sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm{d}^4x$, and the orientation is the [[Def - Spacetime Orientation|spacetime orientation]] fixed by the time and space orderings. The relationship is exactly that of [[Special Relativity III — Minkowski Spacetime and the Metric|Minkowski space]] to a general Lorentzian manifold: the curved theory is primary and general, the flat theory is the constant-metric chapter of it, and the bridge is "set $g_{\mu\nu}(x) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$".

2. **Vector calculus — div, grad, curl and the classical integral theorems are the three-dimensional shadows of $\mathrm{d}$ and $\star$.** The operators gradient, curl, and divergence of ordinary three-dimensional vector calculus are not three unrelated constructions: they are the exterior derivative $\mathrm{d}$ acting on $0$-, $1$-, and $2$-forms in three dimensions, dressed up with the Hodge star to turn forms back into vector fields. Correspondingly the fundamental theorem for gradients, the Kelvin–Stokes curl theorem, and the Gauss divergence theorem are the cases $p=1,2,3$ of the single identity $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$. This chapter makes the identification literal: Example 16.9 reads the Green–Riemann formula off the $p=2$ planar case, Example 16.10 reads Kelvin–Stokes off the $p=2$ surface case by recognising $\partial A_3/\partial x^2 - \partial A_2/\partial x^3$ as the $x$-component of $\mathrm{curl}\,\vec{A}$, and the 3D Gauss theorem $\int\nabla\cdot\vec{v}\,\mathrm{d}V = \oint\vec{v}\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec{S}$ falls out of the $p=3$ case applied to a 2-form built from $\vec{v}$. The payoff is that the seemingly miscellaneous "vector identities" of electromagnetism are organised by a single theorem, and that the four-dimensional generalisation — which ordinary vector calculus cannot even state, having no fourth dimension — is automatic in the language of forms.

3. **Electromagnetism — charge conservation is literally the four-dimensional divergence theorem.** The continuity equation $\nabla\cdot J = 0$, which expresses local conservation of electric charge, is in the language of this chapter the statement that the 3-form $\star\underline{J}$ is closed: $\mathrm{d}\star\underline{J} = (\nabla\cdot J)\epsilon = 0$. By Stokes' theorem the integral of $\star\underline{J}$ over the closed boundary of any four-region then vanishes, and by capping a slab between two spacelike slices with a tube at spatial infinity (where $J\to 0$), the flux through the future slice equals the flux through the past slice — which is to say the total charge $Q_\Sigma = \int_\Sigma\star\underline{J} = \int_\Sigma\rho\,\mathrm{d}V$ is the same on every slice. The total charge is conserved *and* the same for every observer's notion of "now", and both facts are the single 4D Gauss theorem applied to the current. This is the machinery that [[Special Relativity XXII — Maxwell's Equations|Maxwell's equations]] need to be rigorous: the homogeneous pair $\mathrm{d}F=0$ and the inhomogeneous pair $\mathrm{d}\star F = \mu_0\star J$ both live in this integration theory, and charge conservation is forced by $\mathrm{d}\circ\mathrm{d}=0$ applied to $\mathrm{d}\star F$.

4. **General relativity and energy-momentum — every conservation law is a vanishing boundary flux.** The pattern "$\nabla\cdot(\text{current}) = 0 \Rightarrow$ total charge conserved and slice-independent" is not special to electric charge. The local conservation of energy-momentum, $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, becomes by the identical flux argument the statement that the total four-momentum $P^\nu = \int_\Sigma T^{\nu\mu}\,\mathrm{d}\Sigma_\mu$ on a spacelike slice is conserved — see [[Special Relativity XXIII — The Energy-Momentum Tensor and Field Energy]] — and the same argument governs baryon-number conservation in [[Special Relativity XXIV — Relativistic Hydrodynamics|relativistic hydrodynamics]]. In general relativity the construction acquires a subtlety that is invisible here: in a curved spacetime there is generally no flat slice and no globally conserved energy, and the only conserved quantities are flux integrals of the gravitational field over a *sphere at spatial infinity* (the ADM and Komar masses), which are this chapter's flux integrals taken in the asymptotically-flat region. The flat-spacetime divergence theorem is thus the seed of the entire theory of conserved charges in gravitation, and the place where it fails — the absence of a metric-independent way to integrate a vector over a region in curved space — is exactly where the difficulty of "energy in general relativity" lives.

5. **Algebraic topology — Stokes' theorem is the pairing that defines de Rham cohomology.** Because $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}A = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} A$ holds for every form and every region, it sets up a duality between forms and regions: if $A$ is **closed** ($\mathrm{d}A = 0$) and $\mathscr{V}$ is a **cycle** ($\partial\mathscr{V}=0$), then the number $\int_{\mathscr{V}} A$ is unchanged when $A$ is shifted by an exact form (since $\int_{\mathscr{V}}\mathrm{d}B = \int_{\partial\mathscr{V}} B = 0$) and when $\mathscr{V}$ is shifted by a boundary (since $\int_{\partial W} A = \int_W \mathrm{d}A = 0$). So the integral depends only on the cohomology class of $A$ and the homology class of $\mathscr{V}$ — this bilinear pairing between de Rham cohomology and singular homology is the de Rham theorem, treated in [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]] and the algebraic-topology notes. The physical upshot is topological protection of fluxes: the integral of a closed-but-not-exact 2-form over a sphere that does not bound (a sphere enclosing a magnetic monopole) is a topological invariant, quantised and impossible to deform to zero, and this is the geometric origin of charge quantisation arguments and of the Aharonov–Bohm effect.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: integration is a pairing between forms and regions, and Stokes' theorem is its adjoint.** The deepest way to see this chapter is that there are two kinds of object — *differential forms* (degree $p$) and *oriented regions* (dimension $p$) — and integration $\int_{\mathscr{V}} A$ is a pairing between them, a bilinear map taking a $p$-form and a $p$-region to a number. In this picture the exterior derivative $\mathrm{d}$ (which raises a form's degree by one) and the boundary operator $\partial$ (which lowers a region's dimension by one) are *adjoint* with respect to the pairing: $\langle\mathrm{d}A, \mathscr{V}\rangle = \langle A, \partial\mathscr{V}\rangle$ is literally the statement of Stokes' theorem. This is why $\mathrm{d}\circ\mathrm{d}=0$ and $\partial\circ\partial=0$ are the same fact seen from the two sides (the boundary of a boundary is empty; the derivative of a derivative vanishes), and why closed forms pair trivially with boundaries and exact forms pair trivially with cycles. Once you hold this frame, every result in the chapter is predictable: there must be a volume form (the top-degree case), the integral of a form must be metric-free (the pairing does not mention the metric), and the metric must reappear only when you collapse a region to a number via its volume (which is the pairing of the *volume form* with the region). The metric-free pairing is the skeleton; the metric is the flesh you add to measure sizes.

**The true name of a flux is "the integral of a Hodge dual".** A flux is usually first met as "$\int\vec{v}\cdot\vec{n}\,\mathrm{d}S$", a vector dotted into a normal and integrated against an area measure — three separate ingredients (a vector, a normal, a measure) bolted together. This is the right thing to *compute* with but the wrong thing to *think*, because it hides the fact that a flux is a single geometric object: the integral of the 3-form $\star\underline{v}$. The operational characterisation $\Phi_{\mathscr{V}}(\vec{v}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}}\star\underline{v}$ is the true name, and it is more useful for three reasons. It makes the flux a form integral, hence immediately a candidate for Stokes' theorem — which is why every conservation law is one application of $\mathrm{d}$ away from a flux. It makes the flux manifestly independent of the parametrisation and the choice of normal pair (the Hodge dual carries the orientation intrinsically). And it generalises without effort: the flux of a 2-form through a 2-surface is $\int\star A$, the same recipe one degree down, where the "vector dotted into a normal" picture has no analogue because a 2-surface in four dimensions has a two-dimensional space of normals. When you see "flux", do not picture a vector piercing a surface — picture the Hodge dual being integrated.

**Forms are metric-free; volumes are not — and this is the whole organising principle.** The single most clarifying fact in the chapter is the sharp divide between two operations that look similar. Integrating a $p$-*form* over a $p$-region (operation 2) needs no metric: the proof of coordinate-independence uses only the antisymmetry of the form and the change-of-variables Jacobian, and Remark 16.1 of the source stresses that this integral does not involve $g$ at all. But integrating a *scalar* over a region, or computing a *volume*, is intrinsically metric-dependent, because "how big" is a metric question — you need $\sqrt{|g|}$ and the volume form $\epsilon_{\mathscr{V}}$, both built from $g$. This is why Stokes' theorem itself is metric-independent (it relates two form integrals) while the Gauss–Ostrogradsky theorems that follow from it are metric-dependent (they involve the divergence, the volume element, and fluxes, all of which use $g$). The practical consequence is a diagnostic you can apply to any integral in the chapter: if the integrand is an alternating form of the right degree, the metric is absent and the answer is a topological/differential quantity; if the integrand is a scalar density, the metric is present and the answer is a volume. Getting this distinction right tells you instantly whether $\sqrt{|g|}$ should appear, and it is the source of most sign and factor errors when it is got wrong.

**Local and global conservation are the same statement, related by one theorem.** It is worth isolating the conceptual heart of why this mathematical chapter is the prerequisite for all the field-theory chapters. A conservation law has two faces. The *local* face is a differential equation at every point — $\nabla\cdot J = 0$, the divergence of the current vanishes everywhere. The *global* face is an integral statement about totals — the total charge is the same at every time, and the same for every observer. These look like different kinds of claim, one pointwise and one about whole slices of spacetime, and a beginner can hold both without seeing that they are equivalent. The four-dimensional Gauss–Ostrogradsky theorem is exactly the bridge: it says the integral of the local divergence over a four-region equals the net flux out through the region's boundary, so a vanishing divergence everywhere is *the same fact* as a vanishing net boundary flux. Apply it to a slab between two spacelike slices and the equivalence becomes "charge in = charge out", i.e. the total is slice-independent. This single move — enclose, apply Gauss, use the vanishing divergence — is repeated verbatim for charge, for energy-momentum, for baryon number, for any current whatsoever, and it is the reason that "write the conservation law as a vanishing four-divergence" is the first thing one does with any conserved quantity in relativity. The local differential form and the global integral form are not two theorems to be separately remembered; they are one theorem and its restatement under Stokes.
