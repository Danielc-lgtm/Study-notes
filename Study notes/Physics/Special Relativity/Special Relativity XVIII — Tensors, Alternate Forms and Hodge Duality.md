# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we set $c = 1$** and use the **mostly-minus signature** $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, so a timelike vector has $X\cdot X > 0$. Where a formula is more recognisable with $c$ restored, both forms are given. This chapter is purely mathematical machinery — the algebra of tensors and forms on a *fixed* four-dimensional space — so the only physical input is the [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|metric]] and an [[Def - Spacetime Orientation|orientation]].

> [!warning] Convention: Gourgoulhon's signature and the robustness of these formulas
> Gourgoulhon (our rigour source for this chapter) uses the opposite signature $\mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$. For an ordinary vector this flips the sign of lowering ($X_i = -X^i$ here), as on [[Def - Metric Duality and Index Manipulation]]. But almost every *structural* identity in this chapter — the Levi-Civita components $\varepsilon_{0123} = +1$ and $\varepsilon^{0123} = -1$, the contraction identities $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = -24$, and the crucial $\star\star = (-1)^{p+1}$ — depends only on $\det g < 0$, which holds in **both** signatures. So these transcribe unchanged. The one genuinely signature-sensitive sign is the Lorentzian $\star^2 = -1$ on $2$-forms: it is $-1$ because the signature is Lorentzian ($\det g < 0$), and would be $+1$ in Euclidean signature.

- $E$ — the four-dimensional real vector space underlying [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|Minkowski space]]; $E^*$ — its dual, the space of linear forms
- $(e_\alpha)_{\alpha=0,1,2,3}$ — a basis of $E$, not necessarily orthonormal; $(e^\alpha)$ — its [[Def - Metric Duality and Index Manipulation|dual basis]], $\langle e^\alpha, e_\beta\rangle = \delta^\alpha{}_\beta$
- $\langle\omega, \vec v\rangle = \omega(\vec v) = \omega_\mu v^\mu$ — the action of a form $\omega$ on a vector $\vec v$
- $g$, $g_{\alpha\beta}$ — the [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|metric tensor]] and its components ($\eta_{\alpha\beta}$ in an orthonormal basis); $g^{\alpha\beta}$ — the inverse, $g^{\alpha\rho}g_{\rho\beta} = \delta^\alpha{}_\beta$; $\det g < 0$
- $\mathscr{T}_{(k,\ell)}(E)$ — the space of [[Def - Tensors on Minkowski Space|tensors of type (k,ℓ)]]: $k$ contravariant (form-slots), $\ell$ covariant (vector-slots); dimension $4^{k+\ell}$; valence $k+\ell$
- $T^{\alpha_1\dots\alpha_k}{}_{\beta_1\dots\beta_\ell}$ — components of a tensor; upper = contravariant, lower = covariant
- $\otimes$ — the [[Def - Tensor Operations|tensor product]]; $C^p_q$ — contraction on the $p$-th up and $q$-th down index; $T^\mu{}_\mu$ — the trace
- $P$ — change-of-basis matrix, $e'_\alpha = P^\beta{}_\alpha e_\beta$; for two inertial frames $P = \Lambda^{-1}$ with $\Lambda$ a [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz transformation]]
- $\mathscr{A}_p(E)$ — the space of [[Def - Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product|alternate p-forms]]; $\dim\mathscr{A}_p = \binom{4}{p} = (1,4,6,4,1)$; $\mathscr{A}_0 = \mathbb{R}$, $\mathscr{A}_1 = E^*$
- $\wedge$ — the [[Def - Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product|exterior (wedge) product]]; $B\wedge A = (-1)^{pq}A\wedge B$
- $[\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta]$ — the **Levi-Civita symbol** ($0, \pm1$, not a tensor); $\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$ — the [[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor|Levi-Civita tensor]] ($\varepsilon_{0123} = +1$ orthonormal right-handed)
- $\star$ — the [[Def - The Hodge Star|Hodge star]], $\star : \mathscr{A}_p \to \mathscr{A}_{4-p}$; $\star\star = (-1)^{p+1}$; on $2$-forms $\star^2 = -1$
- $u^\flat = g(\vec u, \cdot)$ — the metric-dual one-form of a vector $\vec u$; $\omega^\sharp$ — the metric-dual vector of a form $\omega$
- $\mathbf e \pm i\mathbf b$ — the self-dual / anti-self-dual (Riemann-Silberstein) combinations of the electric and magnetic parts of a $2$-form
- Summation convention: a repeated index, once up once down, is summed over $0,1,2,3$
- $\mathfrak{S}_p$ — the symmetric group on $p$ symbols; $k(\sigma)$ — transposition count of $\sigma$; $(-1)^{k(\sigma)} = \mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)$

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: it builds the algebra in which the electromagnetic field is one object instead of two, and that algebra is the algebra of **tensors and differential forms**. Everything relativity has constructed so far — [[Def - Four-Vector|four-vectors]], the [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|metric]], the angular-momentum bilinear form, the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz transformations]] — turns out to be instances of a single notion, the **tensor**, defined frame-independently as a multilinear map. And a special subfamily of tensors, the fully antisymmetric ones (the **alternate forms**), carries an extra operation, the **Hodge star**, whose behaviour on two-forms is the source of the most beautiful structural fact in classical field theory: that the electromagnetic field splits into a left-handed and a right-handed part, $\mathbf E + i\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf E - i\mathbf B$.

The right way to enter this chapter is to take seriously the demand that physics be **frame-independent**. A four-vector was already defined this way: not as a list of four numbers but as a geometric object whose components happen to transform by $\Lambda$. The tensor concept is the completion of that idea. A tensor *is* a multilinear map — it eats some forms and some vectors and returns a number, linearly in each slot — and its components in a basis are merely its values on the basis. The old, component-first definition ("an array that transforms a certain way") is inverted: the object is primary, the components secondary, and the transformation law is a theorem rather than a definition. This is not pedantry; it is what makes a *tensor equation* automatically true in every frame, which is the entire reason tensors are the language of relativistic physics. The discipline that enforces this — every free index matching, every summed index appearing once up and once down — is Tong's "indices up, indices down," and writing $X^\mu X^\mu$ (two upper indices) is, in his word, *illegal*, because it is not a frame-independent quantity.

The chapter's structural backbone is the chain of subspaces of the tensor algebra, narrowing to the part that integrates and dualises:
$$
\text{tensors } \mathscr{T}_{(k,\ell)}(E) \;\supset\; \text{alternate forms } \mathscr{A}_p(E) \;\xrightarrow{\ \star\ }\; \mathscr{A}_{4-p}(E),
\qquad \dim\mathscr{A}_p = \binom{4}{p} = (1,4,6,4,1).
$$
Section §18.1 defines tensors as multilinear maps and gives the three operations — [[Def - Tensor Operations|tensor product, contraction, and metric duality]] — that make them a closed algebra. This is where Tong's index calculus is made rigorous: raising and lowering with $g$, contracting an up against a down, and the rule that only such contractions are Lorentz scalars. Section §18.2 restricts to the fully antisymmetric tensors, the [[Def - Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product|p-forms]], and equips them with the [[Def - Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product|exterior product]] $\wedge$ — the antisymmetrised tensor product — turning them into the exterior algebra. The key numerical fact is the binomial dimension pattern $1,4,6,4,1$, symmetric about $p = 2$, because a $p$-form measures oriented $p$-volume and there are $\binom{4}{p}$ independent oriented $p$-planes. Section §18.3 introduces the two objects that need the metric *and* an orientation: the [[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor|Levi-Civita tensor]] $\varepsilon$ (the volume form, the four-dimensional determinant) and the [[Def - The Hodge Star|Hodge star]] $\star$ (which maps $p$-forms to $(4-p)$-forms by contracting into $\varepsilon$). The symmetry $\binom{4}{p} = \binom{4}{4-p}$ is exactly what lets $\star$ be an isomorphism, and on two-forms ($\binom{4}{2} = 6$, dual to itself) it becomes an automorphism with $\star^2 = -1$.

That last fact, $\star^2 = -1$ on two-forms, is the chapter's punchline, and it is worth saying why now. An operator squaring to $-1$ has no real eigenvalues, so over the reals it cannot be diagonalised — but over the complex numbers it has eigenvalues $\pm i$. The space of two-forms therefore complexifies and splits into a **self-dual** part ($\star F = iF$) and an **anti-self-dual** part ($\star F = -iF$), each of three complex dimensions. For the electromagnetic field $F$, with electric and magnetic parts $\mathbf E, \mathbf B$ relative to an observer, these are the combinations $\mathbf E + i\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf E - i\mathbf B$. And this is the *same* $\pm i$ splitting as the complexification of the [[Def - Lie Algebra of the Lorentz Group|Lorentz algebra]] $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_\mathbb{C} \cong \mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)$ from [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]]: the self-dual two-forms carry the $(1,0)$ representation, the anti-self-dual the $(0,1)$, and the projectors $\tfrac12(1\mp i\star)$ on forms are the image of the generators $\mathbf J \pm i\mathbf K$ on the algebra. The minus sign in $\star^2 = -1$ is there *because the signature is Lorentzian* (one timelike direction, $\det g < 0$); in Euclidean signature one would get $\star^2 = +1$ and a real decomposition (instantons). The chirality of the photon is the Lorentzian sign of the Hodge star.

The reader is assumed to be fluent in linear algebra and comfortable with [[Def - Metric Duality and Index Manipulation|raising and lowering indices]] from [[Special Relativity III — Minkowski Spacetime and the Metric]], and to know the rudiments of [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms|differential forms]] and the [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces|tensor product of vector spaces]] — this chapter is the special-relativistic, flat-space, single-fibre version of that machinery, and the [[Special Relativity XIX — Fields on Spacetime and the Covariant Derivative|next chapter]] promotes it to fields. The genuinely new conceptual ingredient is small but sharp: the Hodge star, and the willingness to let $\star^2 = -1$ push you into the complex numbers.

# Concept Map

## §18.1 Tensors and Their Operations

- **[[Def - Tensors on Minkowski Space]]**
	- A tensor of type $(k,\ell)$ is a multilinear map $E^* \times\cdots\times E^* \times E\times\cdots\times E \to \mathbb{R}$ eating $k$ forms and $\ell$ vectors — defined frame-independently, *not* as an array of numbers. Its components $T^{\alpha_1\dots}{}_{\beta_1\dots} = T(e^{\alpha_1},\dots, e_{\beta_1},\dots)$ are its values on basis forms and vectors, and the transformation law (one $P^{-1}$ per up-index, one $P$ per down-index, with $P = \Lambda^{-1}$ for inertial frames) is then a theorem. Four-vectors ($\mathscr{T}_{(1,0)} = E$), forms ($\mathscr{T}_{(0,1)} = E^*$), the metric (type $(0,2)$), Lorentz transformations (type $(1,1)$), and the Levi-Civita tensor (type $(0,4)$) are all instances. The valence $k+\ell$ is the robust invariant, since metric duality shuffles indices between up and down.

- **[[Def - Tensor Operations]]**
	- The three operations generating all of tensor algebra: the **tensor product** $\otimes$ (multiply components, raise valence), **contraction** $C^p_q$ (set one upper equal to one lower index and sum, lowering valence by two; the only-legal up–down pairing is forced by Lorentz invariance), and **metric duality** (lower with $g_{\alpha\beta}$, raise with $g^{\alpha\beta}$). The trace $T^\mu{}_\mu = C^1_1 T$ is the prototype contraction, and $C^1_1(\vec v\otimes\omega) = \langle\omega, \vec v\rangle$. Together they make $X\cdot Y = g_{\mu\nu}X^\mu Y^\nu = X_\mu Y^\mu$ ("lower then contract") and certify that an expression is a Lorentz scalar exactly when every summed index appears once up, once down.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Energy-Momentum Tensor *(from QFT and General Relativity)*
> With tensors and contraction in hand, the symmetric type-$(2,0)$ **energy-momentum tensor** $T^{\mu\nu}$ packages energy density, momentum density, and stress; its conservation $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is a tensor equation, hence frame-independent, and it is the source of gravity in general relativity; see [[Special Relativity XXIII — The Energy-Momentum Tensor and Field Energy]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Tensor Fields and the Covariant Derivative *(from General Relativity)*
> Letting a tensor vary from event to event gives a **tensor field**; differentiating it needs the [[Def - The Covariant Derivative|covariant derivative]] $\nabla$, because the partial derivative of components is not a tensor in curvilinear coordinates. The non-commutativity of $\nabla$ is the curvature tensor — gravitation; see [[Special Relativity XIX — Fields on Spacetime and the Covariant Derivative]].

- **[[Ex - Raising and lowering indices on a four-tensor]]** (⭐)
	- Lower the index on $X^\mu = (X^0, \mathbf X)$ to get $X_\mu = (X^0, -\mathbf X)$, build $F^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\alpha}\eta^{\nu\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}$ from the field-strength, and reproduce Tong's raised matrix $G^{\mu\nu}$ with the spatial signs flipped relative to $F_{\mu\nu}$.

- **[[Ex - Contracting a tensor and recovering the trace]]** (⭐)
	- Contract a type-$(1,1)$ tensor to its trace $T^\mu{}_\mu$, verify $\delta^\mu{}_\mu = 4$ and $g_{\mu\nu}g^{\mu\nu} = 4$, and show the field-strength is traceless ($F^\mu{}_\mu = 0$) because it is antisymmetric.

- **[[Ex - Change of basis and why X-mu X-mu is illegal]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Derive the component transformation laws $v'^\alpha = (P^{-1})^\alpha{}_\beta v^\beta$, $\omega'_\alpha = P^\beta{}_\alpha\omega_\beta$ from a change of basis, and show that $X^\mu X_\mu$ is invariant while $X^\mu X^\mu$ (two upper indices) is frame-dependent — Tong's "illegal" expression.

## §18.2 Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product

- **[[Def - Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product]]**
	- A $p$-form is a fully antisymmetric type-$(0,p)$ tensor — it changes sign under any swap of two arguments, equivalently vanishes when two coincide — and the $p$-forms make up the subspace $\mathscr{A}_p(E)$ with $\dim\mathscr{A}_p = \binom{4}{p} = (1,4,6,4,1)$, zero for $p > 4$. The **exterior product** $\wedge$ is the antisymmetrised tensor product, $a\wedge b = a\otimes b - b\otimes a$ for one-forms, associative and graded-commutative ($B\wedge A = (-1)^{pq}A\wedge B$, so $a\wedge a = 0$). The wedge powers $e^{\alpha_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{\alpha_p}$ ($\alpha_1<\cdots<\alpha_p$) form a basis, and a $2$-form expands as $A = \sum_{\alpha<\beta}A_{\alpha\beta}\,e^\alpha\wedge e^\beta$. $p$-forms are the objects that integrate over $p$-surfaces (flux, circulation, charge); the field strength is a $2$-form.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Differential Forms and the Exterior Derivative *(from Electromagnetism and Topology)*
> Promoting alternate forms to fields and adding the [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold|exterior derivative]] $d$ (raises degree by one, $d^2 = 0$) gives the de Rham complex; Maxwell's homogeneous equations are $dF = 0$ and the potential is $F = dA$. The cohomology of $d$ measures spacetime topology; see [[Special Relativity XX — Integration in Spacetime and Stokes' Theorem]] and [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Determinant and the Cross Product *(from Linear Algebra and Vector Calculus)*
> The top form $e^0\wedge e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3$ spans the one-dimensional $\mathscr{A}_4(E)$, and a linear map acts on it by $\det$ — the basis-free **determinant**. In three dimensions the wedge of one-forms plus metric duality is the **cross product**, and the exterior derivative is gradient, curl, and divergence in disguise.

- **[[Ex - The wedge of two one-forms]]** (⭐)
	- Compute $a\wedge b = a\otimes b - b\otimes a$ and its components $(a\wedge b)_{\mu\nu} = a_\mu b_\nu - a_\nu b_\mu$, verify antisymmetry and $a\wedge a = 0$, and interpret the six independent entries as the oriented plane (bivector) spanned by $a$ and $b$.

- **[[Ex - Counting the dimension of the space of p-forms]]** (⭐)
	- Show $\dim\mathscr{A}_p(E) = \binom{4}{p}$ by counting independent antisymmetric components, get $1,4,6,4,1$, and explain why $\mathscr{A}_p(E) = \{0\}$ for $p > 4$ (no five distinct indices in four dimensions) and why the symmetry $\binom{4}{p} = \binom{4}{4-p}$ permits Hodge duality.

- **[[Ex - Anticommutation and associativity of the wedge product]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove $B\wedge A = (-1)^{pq}A\wedge B$ from the definition, distinguishing the $1$-form $\wedge$ $1$-form case (anticommute) from $1$-form $\wedge$ $2$-form (commute), deduce that any odd-degree form squares to zero, and verify associativity on a triple wedge of one-forms.

- **[[Ex - Expanding a 2-form in the wedge basis]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Expand a $2$-form as $A = \tfrac12 A_{\alpha\beta}\,e^\alpha\wedge e^\beta = \sum_{\alpha<\beta}A_{\alpha\beta}\,e^\alpha\wedge e^\beta$, and write the electromagnetic field strength $F = -E_i\,e^0\wedge e^i + \tfrac12\epsilon_{ijk}B^k\,e^i\wedge e^j$ in this basis to read off its six components as $(\mathbf E, \mathbf B)$.

## §18.3 The Levi-Civita Tensor and Hodge Duality

- **[[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor]]**
	- The Levi-Civita tensor $\varepsilon$ is the unique alternate four-linear form (the volume form) taking value $+1$ on a right-handed orthonormal frame, with components $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = \pm\sqrt{-\det g}\,[\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta]$ — distinct from the Levi-Civita *symbol* $[\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta]$, which is not a tensor. Because $\det g < 0$ in both signatures, $\varepsilon_{0123} = +1$ but the raised $\varepsilon^{0123} = -1$. It needs an orientation (it flips sign under reflections — a pseudo-tensor) and obeys the contraction identities $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = -24$, $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\alpha}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\beta} = -6\delta^\alpha{}_\beta$, which are the algebraic engine of every Hodge computation.

- **[[Def - The Hodge Star]]**
	- The Hodge star $\star : \mathscr{A}_p \to \mathscr{A}_{4-p}$ maps a $p$-form to a $(4-p)$-form by contracting it (indices raised with $g^{-1}$) into $\varepsilon$: $(\star A)_{\alpha\dots} = \tfrac1{p!}\varepsilon_{\mu\dots\alpha\dots}g^{\mu\nu}\cdots A_{\nu\dots}$. It is an isomorphism (since $\dim\mathscr{A}_p = \dim\mathscr{A}_{4-p}$), with $\star\star = (-1)^{p+1}$; on $2$-forms it is an automorphism with $\star^2 = -1$. The minus sign is the Lorentzian signature ($\det g < 0$); it makes $\star$ a complex structure on $\mathscr{A}_2(E)$ ("multiplication by $i$"), forcing the self-dual/anti-self-dual decomposition. The star needs a metric (unlike $d$), which is why Maxwell's $d\star F = \mu_0\star J$ uses the metric but $dF = 0$ does not.

- **[[Thm - Hodge Star and the Exterior Product]]**
	- Two computational facts. First, the Hodge dual of a wedge of one-forms is a contraction with $\varepsilon$: $\star(a\wedge b) = \varepsilon(\vec a, \vec b, \cdot, \cdot)$, the four-dimensional parent of the cross product, because the $1/p!$ in $\star$ cancels the orderings of the wedge. Second, $\star\star A = (-1)^{p+1}A$, proved by colliding the two $\varepsilon$'s into the contraction identity $\varepsilon\varepsilon = -(4-p)!\,\delta\cdots\delta$, balancing factorials, and tracking the sign — $(-1)$ from $\det g < 0$, times $(-1)^p$ from reindexing. On $2$-forms this gives $\star^2 = -1$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Maxwell's Equations and Electric-Magnetic Duality *(from Electromagnetism)*
> The Hodge star is what closes Maxwell's equations: $dF = 0$ (metric-free) and $d\star F = \mu_0\star J$ (metric, via $\star$). The source-free equations are invariant under $F \mapsto \star F$, the duality $\mathbf E \to \mathbf B$, $\mathbf B \to -\mathbf E$, which $\star^2 = -1$ promotes to a continuous $U(1)$ rotation; see [[Special Relativity XXII — Maxwell's Equations]].

- **[[Thm - Orthogonal Decomposition of 2-Forms]]**
	- A $2$-form splits two ways. **Observer-relative:** for a unit timelike $\vec u$, $A = u^\flat\wedge q + \star(u^\flat\wedge b^\flat)$ with electric one-form $q = A(\cdot, \vec u)$ and magnetic vector $\vec b = \star A(\vec u, \cdot)^\sharp$ — exactly how the field $F$ presents to a lab as $(\mathbf E, \mathbf B)$. **Chiral and complex:** since $\star^2 = -1$, the complexified $\mathscr{A}_2(E)_\mathbb{C}$ splits into self-dual ($\star F = iF$) and anti-self-dual ($\star F = -iF$) eigenspaces, each three complex dimensions, built from $\mathbf e \pm i\mathbf b$. This is the same $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$ as the complexified Lorentz algebra of [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]] — the chirality of the field strength.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Self-Dual Fields, Helicity and the (A,B) Representations *(from QFT and Amplitudes)*
> The self-dual and anti-self-dual field strengths are the photon's two **helicities**, carrying the $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ representations of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_\mathbb{C} \cong \mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)$. In the spinor formalism they are written $F^+_{AB}$ and $F^-_{\dot A\dot B}$; this is the entry point to spinor-helicity and twistor methods for scattering amplitudes; see [[Special Relativity XI — SL(2,C), Spinors and the Spinor Map]] and [[Thm - The Complexification of so(1,3) and the (A,B) Decomposition]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Pauli-Lubanski Vector and Particle Spin *(from Particle Physics)*
> Contracting $\varepsilon$ with the angular-momentum tensor and four-momentum gives the **Pauli-Lubanski vector** $W^\mu = -\tfrac12\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}J_{\nu\rho}P_\sigma$, whose square $W^\mu W_\mu = -m^2 s(s+1)$ is a Casimir of the [[Def - The Poincaré Group|Poincaré group]] fixing the spin of a particle; see [[Special Relativity XII — Inertial Observers and the Poincaré Group]].

- **[[Ex - Components of the Levi-Civita tensor and the epsilon identities]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Establish $\varepsilon_{0123} = +1$ and $\varepsilon^{0123} = -1$ in an orthonormal frame (the sign flip from raising four indices through $\det\eta = -1$), and prove the contraction identity $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = -24$ by counting signed permutations.

- **[[Ex - Computing the Hodge dual of a 2-form]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute the Hodge duals of the basis $2$-forms — $\star(e^0\wedge e^1) = -e^2\wedge e^3$, $\star(e^2\wedge e^3) = e^0\wedge e^1$ — and show that for the field strength $\star F$ swaps $(\mathbf E, \mathbf B) \to (-\mathbf B, \mathbf E)$, a quarter-turn in the field plane.

- **[[Ex - The Hodge star squares to minus one on 2-forms]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Verify $\star^2 = -1$ on $2$-forms both from the general $\star\star = (-1)^{p+1}$ and by direct computation $\star\star(e^0\wedge e^1) = \star(-e^2\wedge e^3) = -e^0\wedge e^1$, and explain why the sign is $-1$ (Lorentzian, $\det g < 0$) rather than the Euclidean $+1$.

- **[[Ex - Self-dual and anti-self-dual decomposition of the field strength]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Complexify $\mathscr{A}_2(E)$, build the projectors $\tfrac12(1 \mp i\star)$ onto the $\pm i$ eigenspaces of $\star$, identify the self-dual and anti-self-dual parts with $\mathbf E \pm i\mathbf B$ (Riemann-Silberstein), and match them to the $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ representations of the complexified Lorentz algebra of [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]].

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The problems of this chapter, being algebraic, chase a small set of recognisable goals. The most common is to **compute a tensor or form operation in components**: raise or lower an index, contract two tensors, wedge two forms, take a Hodge dual. The entire skill is index bookkeeping — keeping every free index matched and every summed index once up, once down — and the payoff is always a quantity in a definite frame. A second target is to **establish an invariant or a covariant equation**: to show that a contraction is a Lorentz scalar (every index paired), or that an equation equates tensors of the same type (so it holds in all frames). This is the "tensor equations are frame-independent" principle put to work, and it is why one writes physics tensorially at all. A third target is **structural**: to prove $\star\star = (-1)^{p+1}$, to count $\dim\mathscr{A}_p = \binom{4}{p}$, to show the self-dual and anti-self-dual subspaces are Lorentz-invariant — facts about the algebra itself rather than any particular computation. A fourth, more conceptual, is to **decompose an object relative to an observer**: split a $2$-form into electric and magnetic parts, or a tensor into pieces parallel and perpendicular to a four-velocity, recognising that such splits are observer-dependent coordinates on an invariant object. A fifth is to **classify**: to use the field invariants (the real and imaginary parts of $(\mathbf e + i\mathbf b)^2$) to decide whether a field is electric-dominated, magnetic-dominated, or null. Across all five, the unifying remark is that one understands a relativistic object when one knows which of its features are frame-independent (its tensor type, its invariants, its self-dual/anti-self-dual split) and which are observer coordinates (its electric/magnetic parts, its components).

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A tensor's type is given** — its valence and index structure — which immediately fixes how it transforms and which operations apply; the type is the richest source, because it tells you what you are allowed to write. **An antisymmetry (or symmetry) is given** — a tensor is a $p$-form, or the metric is symmetric — which restricts the independent components ($\binom{4}{p}$ of them for a $p$-form) and unlocks the exterior algebra and the Hodge star. **The metric is available** — which is what permits raising and lowering, the inner product, and (with orientation) the Levi-Civita tensor and the Hodge star; without it only $\otimes$, contraction, and $d$ survive. **An orientation is fixed** — which is what makes $\varepsilon$ and $\star$ well-defined rather than ambiguous up to sign, and what distinguishes a vector from a pseudovector. **An observer (unit timelike vector) is named** — which supplies the splitting of any tensor into temporal and rest-space parts, the source of every electric/magnetic decomposition. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a given tensor type routes through the operations to a computed quantity; a given antisymmetry routes through the exterior algebra to a form computation; the metric routes through duality to an invariant; an orientation routes through $\varepsilon$ and $\star$ to a dual; an observer routes through the decomposition theorem to electric and magnetic parts. The [[Special Relativity XVIII — Tensors, Alternate Forms and Hodge Duality#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

These are the moves almost every problem in the chapter is assembled from. Because the difficulty here is index bookkeeping rather than physics, each operation states the index discipline it enforces. When stuck, scan the list and try each one.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Lower or raise an index with the metric.** Multiply by $g_{\alpha\beta}$ to lower, $g^{\alpha\beta}$ to raise; see [[Def - Metric Duality and Index Manipulation]] and [[Def - Tensor Operations]]. *Trigger:* an equation's free indices do not match up-and-down, or you want to contract two upper (or two lower) indices. *Pattern:* "lower one of them with $g$, then contract." In mostly-minus signature lowering flips the spatial signs, $X_i = -X^i$, and leaves the time component, $X_0 = X^0$.

2. **Contract an upper against a lower index.** Set one upper equal to one lower index and sum; this lowers valence by two and produces a frame-independent quantity; see [[Def - Tensor Operations]]. *Trigger:* you want a scalar, a trace, or to feed one tensor's slot from another. *Pattern:* the contracted index appears once up, once down — never two up or two down (Tong's "illegal" $X^\mu X^\mu$).

3. **Form a tensor product.** Multiply components of two tensors to build a higher-valence one, $(A\otimes B)^{\dots}{}_{\dots} = A^{\dots}{}_{\dots}B^{\dots}{}_{\dots}$; see [[Def - Tensor Operations]]. *Trigger:* you need a tensor of higher rank than the pieces in hand — the metric is $g = g_{\alpha\beta}e^\alpha\otimes e^\beta$, a field is built from potentials. *Pattern:* $\otimes$ concatenates slots and is associative but not commutative.

4. **Wedge two forms.** Antisymmetrise the tensor product, $a\wedge b = a\otimes b - b\otimes a$ for one-forms; see [[Def - Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product]]. *Trigger:* you want to stay inside the alternate forms, build a $2$-form from one-forms, or compute an oriented area/volume. *Pattern:* $B\wedge A = (-1)^{pq}A\wedge B$, so swapping two odd factors costs a sign and $a\wedge a = 0$.

5. **Expand a $p$-form in the wedge basis.** Write $A = \sum_{\alpha_1<\cdots<\alpha_p}A_{\alpha_1\dots\alpha_p}e^{\alpha_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{\alpha_p}$; see [[Def - Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product]]. *Trigger:* you want the independent components of a form, or to read off $(\mathbf E, \mathbf B)$ from $F$. *Pattern:* the strictly-increasing-index monomials are a basis of size $\binom{4}{p}$, and the coefficients are the same as the tensor components.

6. **Compute a Hodge dual by contracting into $\varepsilon$.** Apply $\star$: raise the form's indices and contract against the Levi-Civita tensor, $(\star A)_{\dots} = \tfrac1{p!}\varepsilon_{\mu\dots}g^{\mu\nu}\cdots A_{\nu\dots}$; see [[Def - The Hodge Star]]. *Trigger:* you need the $(4-p)$-form complementary to a $p$-form, the magnetic-from-electric swap, or to write Maxwell's $d\star F$. *Pattern:* for a wedge of one-forms use the shortcut $\star(a\wedge b) = \varepsilon(\vec a, \vec b, \cdot, \cdot)$ ([[Thm - Hodge Star and the Exterior Product]]).

7. **Invert the Hodge star with the sign rule.** Use $\star^{-1} = (-1)^{p+1}\star$, i.e. apply $\star$ again and fix the sign; see [[Thm - Hodge Star and the Exterior Product]]. *Trigger:* an equation reads $\star X = Y$ and you want $X$, or you are checking $\star^2$ on $2$-forms. *Pattern:* on $2$-forms $\star\star = -1$, so $X = -\star Y$.

8. **Reduce a product of two $\varepsilon$'s to Kronecker deltas.** Replace $\varepsilon^{\dots}\varepsilon_{\dots}$ by the signed $\delta$-combination with the Lorentzian factor, e.g. $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = -24$; see [[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor]]. *Trigger:* two Levi-Civita tensors appear in a product (a double Hodge star, a cross-product identity, a field-invariant computation). *Pattern:* $\varepsilon\varepsilon = -(4-p)!\,\delta\cdots\delta$ — the relativistic sibling of the $\epsilon$–$\delta$ identity.

9. **Decompose a $2$-form relative to an observer.** Split $A = u^\flat\wedge q + \star(u^\flat\wedge b^\flat)$ with $q = A(\cdot, \vec u)$ (electric) and $\vec b = \star A(\vec u, \cdot)^\sharp$ (magnetic); see [[Thm - Orthogonal Decomposition of 2-Forms]]. *Trigger:* a four-velocity is named and you want the electric/magnetic parts, or to derive the field transformation under a boost. *Pattern:* feed $\vec u$ into one slot for the electric part, take the Hodge dual for the magnetic part.

10. **Complexify and project onto self-dual / anti-self-dual parts.** Since $\star^2 = -1$ on $2$-forms, form $A^\pm = \tfrac12(A \mp i\star A)$, the $\pm i$ eigenforms; see [[Thm - Orthogonal Decomposition of 2-Forms]]. *Trigger:* you want the chiral pieces $\mathbf E \pm i\mathbf B$, the helicity decomposition, or to connect to the $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$ representations. *Pattern:* treat $\star$ as multiplication by $i$ and the natural variables become complex.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Contracting two indices in the same (up or down) position
> It is tempting, by analogy with the Euclidean dot product, to "sum over a repeated index" regardless of its position, writing $X^\mu X^\mu$ or $T_{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}$. This is **illegal**: such a sum carries an uncancelled $(P^{-1})(P^{-1})$ (or $PP$) under a change of basis and is therefore *not* Lorentz invariant. The concrete failure: $X^\mu X^\mu = (X^0)^2 + |\mathbf X|^2$ is the Euclidean norm, which differs between frames, whereas the invariant is $X^\mu X_\mu = (X^0)^2 - |\mathbf X|^2$. The operation becomes legal exactly when one index is first lowered (or raised) with the metric, so the contraction pairs one up with one down. This is the single most important rule of the chapter — Tong declares $X^\mu X^\mu$ "illegal to write."

> [!warning] 2. Treating the Levi-Civita symbol as a tensor
> The symbol $[\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta] \in \{-1,0,+1\}$ is the same array in every basis, so it is tempting to use it as if it were the components of the [[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor|Levi-Civita tensor]] $\varepsilon$. This is wrong in any non-orthonormal frame: the tensor components carry a factor $\sqrt{-\det g}$, $\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = \sqrt{-\det g}\,[\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta]$, and forgetting it gives incorrect answers in curvilinear coordinates (e.g. spherical coordinates, where $\sqrt{-\det g} = r^2\sin\theta$). The symbol equals the tensor components only in an orthonormal frame, where $\sqrt{-\det g} = 1$. The fix: always carry the $\sqrt{-\det g}$, or work exclusively in orthonormal frames where it is $1$.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming $\star^2 = +1$ on 2-forms
> Carrying Euclidean intuition, one expects the Hodge star to be an involution, $\star^2 = +1$, so that real self-dual and anti-self-dual forms exist. In Lorentzian signature this is **false**: $\star^2 = -1$ on $2$-forms (Gourgoulhon's $\star\star = (-1)^{p+1}$ with $p = 2$), because $\det g < 0$. The concrete consequence of getting this wrong: you would look for real eigenforms $\star F = \pm F$ and find none, or worse, construct a spurious real decomposition. The correct statement is that $\star$ has eigenvalues $\pm i$ over $\mathbb{C}$, so the self-dual/anti-self-dual split is *complex* ($\mathbf E \pm i\mathbf B$). The operation $\star^2 = +1$ becomes legal only in a positive-definite (Riemannian) four-space — the instanton setting — not in spacetime.

> [!warning] 4. Mixing up which factorial normalisation a formula uses
> The wedge-basis expansion has a $\tfrac1{p!}$ ($A = \tfrac1{p!}A_{\alpha\dots}e^\alpha\wedge\cdots$) but the strictly-ordered sum does not ($A = \sum_{\alpha_1<\cdots}A_{\alpha\dots}e^\alpha\wedge\cdots$); the Hodge star carries a $\tfrac1{p!}$; the exterior product of a $p$- and $q$-form carries $\tfrac1{p!q!}$. It is tempting to drop or double-count these. The failure shows up as answers off by integer factors (a Hodge dual twice too big, a field invariant off by $2$). The operation is performed correctly by deciding once whether indices are summed over *all* values (then divide by the symmetry factor) or only *ordered* values (then do not), and never mixing the two within one computation.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this chapter are won at the level of **index discipline**: the moment you decide what type of object you have and write down its index structure correctly, most of the work is done, because the legal operations then apply almost mechanically. So begin every problem by naming the type of every object — vector (one upper), form (one lower), $p$-form (antisymmetric lower indices), tensor of type $(k,\ell)$ — and checking that the expression you are asked about has all free indices matched and every summed index once up, once down. If it does not, the first move is always operation 1: raise or lower with the metric until it does.

If the problem **asks you to compute a tensor operation** — a contraction, a wedge, a Hodge dual — the strategy is to choose the most convenient basis (almost always an orthonormal one, where $g_{\alpha\beta} = \eta_{\alpha\beta}$ and $\sqrt{-\det g} = 1$, so the [[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor|Levi-Civita]] components are just the symbol) and grind the indices. The two labour-saving shortcuts are worth memorising: for a Hodge dual of a *wedge of one-forms*, use $\star(a\wedge b) = \varepsilon(\vec a, \vec b, \cdot, \cdot)$ (operation 6) rather than the raw definition; and for any product of *two* Levi-Civita tensors, use the contraction identity $\varepsilon\varepsilon = -(4-p)!\,\delta\cdots\delta$ (operation 8) to collapse to Kronecker deltas immediately. These two reduce most form computations to a few lines.

If the problem **asks you to establish an invariant** or to show an equation is frame-independent, the instrument is the recognition (operation 2) that a fully contracted expression — every index paired up-and-down — is a Lorentz scalar, and that an equation between tensors of the same type holds in every frame. The route is: write both sides with explicit indices, check the types match, and confirm every summed index is once up, once down; if so, the equation is covariant and you are done. The classic application is showing $X^\mu X_\mu$ is invariant while $X^\mu X^\mu$ is not, which is the same as showing the first is a legal contraction and the second is not.

If the problem **involves the Hodge star on $2$-forms**, the decisive fact is $\star^2 = -1$ (operation 7), and the decisive move is to *take it seriously as a complex structure*. Whenever you see $\star$ acting on $2$-forms and the problem smells of self-duality, helicity, or the $\mathbf E \pm i\mathbf B$ combination, complexify (operation 10): form the projectors $\tfrac12(1\mp i\star)$ and work with the self-dual and anti-self-dual parts, which are eigenforms of $\star$ with eigenvalue $\pm i$. The reason this is the right move is that $\star$ is diagonal on these parts, so any computation involving $\star$ becomes multiplication by $\pm i$. This is the route to the connection with the $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$ representations of [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]].

If the problem **names an observer** (a unit timelike four-velocity), the strategy is to decompose every $2$-form in sight into electric and magnetic parts relative to that observer (operation 9): the electric part is $A(\cdot, \vec u)$, the magnetic part is $\star A(\vec u, \cdot)^\sharp$. This is how the field strength becomes $(\mathbf E, \mathbf B)$, and decomposing the *same* $2$-form against a *different* observer (a boosted four-velocity) is exactly how one derives the transformation of electric and magnetic fields — the abstract source of "a moving charge produces a magnetic field."

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle: **the type is the strategy.** Once you know whether you hold a vector, a form, a $p$-form, a general tensor — and once you have written its indices in the right positions — the legal operations are forced, and the only remaining work is arithmetic. Every question in this chapter is the question "*what type is this object, and which index moves does that license?*" The physics chapters that follow (electromagnetism, energy-momentum, hydrodynamics) are then just this algebra applied to the field strength, the energy-momentum tensor, and the fluid four-velocity.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Def - Metric Duality and Index Manipulation|Raising and lowering with the metric]]**: $X_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}X^\nu$, $X^\mu = g^{\mu\nu}X_\nu$. This is the most-used operation in all of relativistic computation, because it is *free* — it costs nothing and applies to any index of any tensor. Reach for it whenever an equation's indices do not match up-and-down, whenever you want to contract two indices in the same position (lower one first), and whenever an inner product must be written as a contraction ($X\cdot Y = X_\mu Y^\mu$). Its disguised use is as the bridge between a four-vector and the one-form it represents — the gradient $\partial_\mu f$ is naturally a form, and $\partial^\mu f$ its raised version. Recognise its applicability the instant indices clash.

- **Contraction and the up–down rule**: a sum over one upper and one lower index is a Lorentz scalar; $T^\mu{}_\mu$ is the trace. The reusable content is the *legality test*: an expression is frame-independent exactly when every summed index appears once up, once down (operation 2). This single rule certifies, with no computation, that $X^\mu X_\mu$, $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$, and $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}$ are invariant (or covariant), and that $X^\mu X^\mu$ is not. Reach for it whenever you need to know whether a quantity is observer-independent, and use it to *write* invariants by pairing indices.

- **The exterior product and $B\wedge A = (-1)^{pq}A\wedge B$**: the antisymmetrised product that builds forms; see [[Def - Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product]]. Its typical use is to construct a $2$-form from one-forms (the field strength from potentials) and to compute oriented areas and volumes. The reusable facts are the sign rule (swapping two odd-degree factors costs a sign) and its consequence $a\wedge a = 0$ — which instantly kills many terms in a computation. Recognise its applicability whenever you must stay inside the alternate forms or compute something that integrates over a surface.

- **[[Def - The Hodge Star|The Hodge star]] and $\star\star = (-1)^{p+1}$**: the metric-and-orientation duality $\mathscr{A}_p \cong \mathscr{A}_{4-p}$. Its typical use is to produce the complementary form (the magnetic dual of the electric field, the integrand $\star 1 = \varepsilon$ of a volume integral) and to write the inhomogeneous Maxwell equation $d\star F = \mu_0\star J$. The single most reusable fact is $\star^2 = -1$ on $2$-forms: it makes $\star$ a complex structure, so the natural objects are $\mathbf E \pm i\mathbf B$, and any $\star$ on a $2$-form is "multiply by $i$." Reach for it whenever a $p$-form needs its $(4-p)$-form partner, and whenever self-duality or helicity appears.

- **The Levi-Civita contraction identities**: $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} = -24$, $\varepsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\alpha}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\beta} = -6\delta^\alpha{}_\beta$, and the general $\varepsilon\varepsilon = -(4-p)!\,\delta\cdots\delta$; see [[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor]]. The recognisable setup is any product of two [[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor|Levi-Civita tensors]] — a double Hodge star, a cross-product identity, a field-invariant computation. The reusable move is to replace $\varepsilon\varepsilon$ by the signed combination of Kronecker deltas (with the Lorentzian factor $-1$) and let the deltas collapse the sums. This is the relativistic $\epsilon$–$\delta$ identity, and it is what makes every Hodge-star proof finite.

# Bridges

1. **Linear algebra — the tensor algebra and the exterior power.** The space $\mathscr{T}_{(k,\ell)}(E)$ is the iterated [[Def - Tensor Product of Vector Spaces|tensor product]] $E^{\otimes k}\otimes(E^*)^{\otimes\ell}$, and the alternate forms $\mathscr{A}_p(E) = \Lambda^p E^*$ are the exterior powers, the antisymmetric part of $(E^*)^{\otimes p}$. The identification of a multilinear map with an element of a tensor-product space is the [[Thm - Universal Property of the Tensor Product|universal property]]: multilinear maps out of a product correspond to linear maps out of the tensor product, so a tensor is simultaneously "a multilinear gadget" and "a vector in a big space," and the two views are interchanged by that property. The whole chapter is multilinear algebra over $\mathbb{R}^4$ with the extra ingredient of a non-degenerate indefinite form $g$, which is what supplies metric duality, the Levi-Civita tensor, and the Hodge star — the three structures that distinguish a *geometry* from a bare vector space. A reader who has done the abstract tensor and exterior algebra of [[Linear Algebra IX — §9 Multilinear Algebra and Determinants|multilinear algebra]] will recognise every construction here as that algebra with the indefinite metric switched on.

2. **Differential geometry — forms on a manifold.** Everything on this page is the *fibrewise*, single-point version of the calculus of [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms|differential forms]] on a manifold. A [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold|differential p-form]] is a smooth field of alternate $p$-forms — a section of the bundle $\Lambda^p T^*M$ — and the wedge product, the [[Def - The Hodge Star|Hodge star]], and the [[Def - Riemannian Volume Form|volume form]] are the fibrewise operations made into operations on fields. What is missing here, and supplied in the [[Special Relativity XIX — Fields on Spacetime and the Covariant Derivative|next chapter]], is the *derivative*: the [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold|exterior derivative]] $d$ (which needs no metric, $d^2 = 0$) and the [[Def - The Covariant Derivative|covariant derivative]] $\nabla$ (which needs a connection). The reason special relativity can do all of this with a *fixed* space $E$ rather than a varying tangent space is that Minkowski space is flat: every tangent space is the same $E$, and the only changes of basis are global linear maps $P = \Lambda^{-1}$. The bridge to gravitation is exactly the step from this fixed $E$ to a tangent space that varies from event to event — the tensor algebra is unchanged, but its base point moves.

3. **Hodge theory — the star and harmonic forms.** The [[Def - The Hodge Star|Hodge star]] introduced here is the cornerstone of [[Hodge Theory I — Harmonic Forms and the Hodge Decomposition|Hodge theory]]. On a manifold, $\star$ defines the codifferential $\delta$ (the formal adjoint of $d$, up to sign $\delta = \pm\star d\star$) and the Hodge Laplacian $\Delta = d\delta + \delta d$, and the **Hodge decomposition theorem** writes every form uniquely as exact plus co-exact plus harmonic, with harmonic forms representing de Rham cohomology. The sign $\star\star = \pm1$ — $-1$ on $2$-forms in our Lorentzian signature, $+1$ in Riemannian signature — controls the structure of harmonic forms in middle degree, and on a Lorentzian manifold the elliptic Laplacian is replaced by the hyperbolic wave operator, which is why electromagnetic waves propagate. The flat case on this page is where the algebra of $\star$ is established; Hodge theory is what it becomes once you add a manifold, a Laplacian, and compactness.

4. **The Lorentz group and representation theory — the $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$ of the field strength.** The deepest bridge of the chapter runs from the [[Def - The Hodge Star|Hodge star]] on $2$-forms to the representation theory of the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]]. A $2$-form transforms in the six-dimensional adjoint representation of $SO(1,3)$, which is reducible: it is the direct sum $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$ of a left-handed and a right-handed three-dimensional representation. The mechanism that exhibits this split is precisely $\star^2 = -1$: complexifying, $\star$ has eigenvalues $\pm i$, and its $+i$ and $-i$ eigenspaces are the $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ summands — the **self-dual** and **anti-self-dual** $2$-forms. This is the *same* decomposition as the complexification of the [[Def - Lie Algebra of the Lorentz Group|Lorentz Lie algebra]], $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_\mathbb{C} \cong \mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)$, established in [[Thm - The Complexification of so(1,3) and the (A,B) Decomposition]]: the complex generators $\mathbf J \pm i\mathbf K$ that diagonalise the algebra are the algebraic shadow of the projectors $\tfrac12(1\mp i\star)$ that diagonalise $\star$ on forms. So the Hodge star is not merely a computational tool — it is the operator that *realises* the chiral decomposition of the Lorentz group on the space of fields, and the electromagnetic field's split into $\mathbf E + i\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf E - i\mathbf B$ is this representation theory made concrete.

5. **Electromagnetism — the field as a $2$-form and the cleanest Maxwell equations.** This entire chapter is the machinery that makes the next three clean. The electromagnetic field is a [[Def - Alternate Forms and the Exterior Product|2-form]] $F$, its potential a $1$-form $A$ with $F = dA$, and Maxwell's four equations collapse to two: $dF = 0$ (the homogeneous pair, automatic from $F = dA$ and $d^2 = 0$, **needing no metric**) and $d\star F = \mu_0\star J$ (the inhomogeneous pair, **needing the metric** through the [[Def - The Hodge Star|Hodge star]]). The split of $F$ into electric and magnetic fields relative to an observer is the observer-decomposition of [[Thm - Orthogonal Decomposition of 2-Forms]]; the boost transformation of $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ is the change of observer in that decomposition; the two field invariants $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} \propto \mathbf B^2 - \mathbf E^2$ and $\star F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} \propto \mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B$ are computed with the contraction identities; and the self-dual/anti-self-dual split is the helicity of the photon. The reason a relativity course detours through Hodge duality is that without it the electromagnetic chapters would be a mess of components, and with it they are two lines; see [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field]] and [[Special Relativity XXII — Maxwell's Equations]].

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a tensor is its components in any one basis, and the metric is what lets indices wander.** The single perspective that organises the chapter is that there is only *one* kind of object — the multilinear map — and everything else is bookkeeping. A four-vector, a form, the metric, a Lorentz transformation, the field strength, the Levi-Civita tensor: all are tensors, distinguished only by how many forms and vectors they eat. Once this is internalised, the index calculus stops being a collection of rules and becomes a single discipline: write the object's indices in the right positions, keep free indices matched and summed indices once up once down, and the legal operations are forced. The metric is the lubricant that lets an index slide between the up and the down position without changing the abstract object, so that $F_{\mu\nu}$, $F^\mu{}_\nu$, and $F^{\mu\nu}$ are the same field wearing three costumes. The whole of Tong's "indices up, indices down" is the statement that legal expressions are exactly those assembled from tensor product, contraction, and metric duality — and that anything else (a repeated upper index, the bare Levi-Civita symbol in a curvilinear frame) is not a tensor and not invariant.

**The true name of the Hodge star is "multiplication by $i$ on $2$-forms."** The official definition of $\star$ — raise indices, contract into $\varepsilon$, divide by $p!$ — is the right thing to *compute with* but the wrong thing to *think*. The operational essence, the thing to carry in working memory, is $\star^2 = -1$ on $2$-forms. An operator squaring to $-1$ is a complex structure: it is the algebraic incarnation of $i$. So on the space of $2$-forms, the natural variables are not the real form $F$ but the complex combinations $F \mp i\star F$, which are the eigenforms of $\star$ with eigenvalue $\pm i$ — and for the field strength these are $\mathbf E \pm i\mathbf B$. Every appearance of $\star$ on a $2$-form should trigger the thought "this is $i$, complexify," and every self-duality or helicity question is then a question about the $\pm i$ eigenspaces. The Euclidean instinct $\star^2 = +1$ is the trap: it would give real eigenvalues $\pm1$ and a real decomposition (instantons), but in spacetime the signature forces $-1$, and the field becomes chiral and complex.

**Inheritance: where does $\star^2 = -1$ come from? From the one timelike direction.** It is worth tracing the minus sign to its source, because it is the entire physical content. The Hodge star squares to $(-1)^{p(n-p)}\mathrm{sgn}(\det g)$; on $2$-forms in four dimensions the combinatorial part $(-1)^{2\cdot2}$ is $+1$, so the sign is entirely $\mathrm{sgn}(\det g)$. And $\det g < 0$ *because the metric has one timelike and three spacelike directions* — the Lorentzian signature. So the chirality of the electromagnetic field, the complexity of the photon's helicity, the $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$ split: all of it inherits from the single fact that time is one dimension with the opposite sign. In a world with two timelike directions, or with Euclidean signature, the sign would be $+1$ and the physics would be different. This is the same lesson as the [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|indefiniteness of the metric]] being "the physics, not a nuisance": the one minus sign in the signature propagates all the way up to the chirality of light.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: two epsilons in a product means Kronecker deltas.** The highest-density computational reflex in the chapter is this: whenever a product contains *two* [[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor|Levi-Civita tensors]] $\varepsilon\varepsilon$, immediately replace it by the signed sum of Kronecker deltas, $\varepsilon\varepsilon = -(4-p)!\,\delta\cdots\delta$, where $p$ is the number of free index pairs and the leading $-$ is the Lorentzian $\mathrm{sgn}(\det g)$. This single substitution is what makes the proof of $\star\star = (-1)^{p+1}$ finite, what reduces every cross-product identity to deltas, and what evaluates the field invariants. It is the four-dimensional, signature-dressed version of the vector-calculus identity $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ilm} = \delta_{jl}\delta_{km} - \delta_{jm}\delta_{kl}$. The reaction to "I see two $\varepsilon$'s" should be automatic: collapse to deltas, carry the minus sign, and let the Kronecker deltas contract the sums.

**Relativity makes the field one object — and the splitting is the observer.** The popular picture has an electric field and a magnetic field as two separate things that happen to interact. The tensor framework reveals the truth: there is *one* object, the $2$-form $F$, and "electric" and "magnetic" are the two pieces it falls into *when an observer is chosen*. Decompose $F$ against your four-velocity and you get your $\mathbf E$ and your $\mathbf B$; a moving observer decomposes the same $F$ differently and gets a different split — which is exactly why a charge at rest (pure $\mathbf E$) produces, for a moving observer, a magnetic field. The frame-independent content is $F$ itself, its tensor type, and its invariants; the $(\mathbf E, \mathbf B)$ split is an observer coordinate. This is the chapter's deepest lesson and the one that makes electromagnetism relativistic: the field is the invariant, the electric and magnetic parts are its frame-dependent shadows, and the Hodge star is the operator that rotates one shadow into the other.
