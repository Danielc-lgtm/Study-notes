# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we set $c = 1$**, and we use the **mostly-minus signature** $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)$, so a timelike vector $X$ has $X \cdot X > 0$, a spacelike vector has $X \cdot X < 0$, and a null vector has $X \cdot X = 0$. Where a formula is more recognisable with $c$ restored it is given both ways; to restore $c$, replace every velocity $v$ by $v/c$ and insert factors of $c$ by dimensional analysis.

> [!warning] Convention: opposite signature in Gourgoulhon
> This chapter follows Gourgoulhon's *Special Relativity in General Frames* Chapter 5, but **Gourgoulhon uses the mostly-plus signature** $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$, in which timelike vectors have $g(v,v) < 0$ and the Lorentz factor of one observer relative to another is $\Gamma_0 = -\vec u \cdot \vec u'$. Every scalar product is translated to mostly-minus here by flipping the overall sign of the metric: his $\Gamma_0 = -\vec u \cdot \vec u'$ becomes our $\Gamma_0 = u \cdot u'$, his orthogonal projector $\perp_u X = X + (u \cdot X)\,u$ becomes our $\perp_u X = X - (u \cdot X)\,u$ (because $u \cdot u = +1$ here), and his timelike condition $g(v,v) < 0$ becomes our $v \cdot v > 0$. Be vigilant: the sign of $u \cdot V$ inside the composition and Doppler formulas is exactly where the translation matters.

This chapter is about **two observers**, so almost every symbol comes in a primed and an unprimed copy.

- $\mathcal{O}, \mathcal{O}'$ — two observers, with worldlines $\mathcal{L}, \mathcal{L}'$ and unit future-directed four-velocities $u, u'$ (so $u \cdot u = u' \cdot u' = 1$)
- $E_u, E_{u'}$ — the **local rest spaces** of $\mathcal{O}, \mathcal{O}'$: the three-dimensional subspaces of vectors orthogonal to $u$, respectively $u'$
- $\perp_u$ — the **orthogonal projector** onto $E_u$, acting by $\perp_u X = X - (u \cdot X)\,u$ (mostly-minus form)
- $\Gamma_0$ — the **Lorentz factor between the two observers**, $\Gamma_0 = u \cdot u' = (1 - U^2)^{-1/2}$; symmetric in $\mathcal{O} \leftrightarrow \mathcal{O}'$
- $U$ — the **velocity of $\mathcal{O}'$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$**, a spacelike vector in $E_u$; $U = \lVert U \rVert_g$ its magnitude
- $U'$ — the **velocity of $\mathcal{O}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}'$**, a spacelike vector in $E_{u'}$; the central surprise is $U \ne -U'$ (they live in different spaces) yet $\lVert U \rVert_g = \lVert U' \rVert_g$
- $e, e'$ — unit spacelike vectors along $U, U'$: $e = U / U \in E_u$ and $e' = U' / U' \in E_{u'}$, each with $\lVert e \rVert_g = \lVert e' \rVert_g = 1$
- $\mathcal{P}$ — a third object, a massive particle (or a photon), with four-velocity $v$ (or null tangent $\ell$)
- $V, V'$ — the **velocities of $\mathcal{P}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$, respectively $\mathcal{O}'$**: $V \in E_u$, $V' \in E_{u'}$
- $\Gamma, \Gamma'$ — the Lorentz factors of $\mathcal{P}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}, \mathcal{O}'$: $\Gamma = u \cdot v$, $\Gamma' = u' \cdot v$
- $V_\parallel, \mathbf{V}_\perp$ — the **parallel** (along $e$) and **transverse** (orthogonal to $e$) parts of $V$; the transverse part lies in $E_u \cap E_{u'}$
- $n, n'$ — unit propagation directions of a photon relative to $\mathcal{O}, \mathcal{O}'$: $V = n$, $V' = n'$ (with $c=1$); $\theta, \theta'$ the corresponding incidence angles
- $\ell$ — proper length of a ruler (length in its own rest frame); $f_{\mathrm{em}}, f_{\mathrm{rec}}$ — emitted and received frequencies
- $\mathscr{S}$ — the **celestial sphere** of an observer: the set of light-ray directions, a unit sphere in the local rest space
- Spatial three-vectors are written in **bold** ($\mathbf{V}_\perp$); four-vectors are capital letters with no arrows ($U, V, X$); a repeated index, once up once down, is summed over $0,1,2,3$

# Motivation

The previous chapter ([[Special Relativity VII — Kinematics I, Motion Relative to an Observer]]) was about one observer watching one particle: it built the [[Def - Velocity Relative to an Observer|relative velocity]] $V$, the [[Def - Acceleration Relative to an Observer|relative acceleration]], and the [[Def - Lorentz Factor and Relative Velocity|Lorentz factor]] $\Gamma$. This chapter is about the *change of observer*: two observers $\mathcal{O}$ and $\mathcal{O}'$ both watch the same particle, and we ask how everything one of them measures is related to what the other measures. The single organising fact is that a relative velocity is a vector **in a particular observer's rest space**, and different observers have different rest spaces, so comparing measurements is never as simple as the Galilean intuition expects.

The cleanest way to see what is new is the very first result, reciprocity. In Newtonian physics, if $\mathcal{O}'$ moves at velocity $\mathbf{U}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$, then $\mathcal{O}$ moves at $-\mathbf{U}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}'$ — the two velocities are literally negatives. In relativity this is *false as a vector equation*: $U \in E_u$ and $U' \in E_{u'}$ live in different three-dimensional subspaces of spacetime, and there is no frame-independent way to say one is minus the other. What survives is weaker but still true — the two *speeds* are equal, $\lVert U \rVert_g = \lVert U' \rVert_g$. This is the template for the whole chapter: a Galilean identity between vectors degrades into a relation between their invariant magnitudes, and the geometry of which-vector-in-which-rest-space is what carries the new physics.

From there the chapter is a tour of transformation laws, each one the relativistic correction to a Galilean rule a reader already knows. Lengths transform by the **FitzGerald–Lorentz contraction** $\ell = \ell'/\Gamma_0$ (§8.1), and the derivation makes transparent that contraction is a consequence of the relativity of simultaneity, not a mechanical squeezing. Velocities compose by a law (§8.1) that, written four-dimensionally, is a single clean formula; written three-dimensionally it splits into a parallel piece and a transverse piece, and in the collinear special case it reduces to the velocity-addition rule

$$V' = \frac{V - U}{1 - UV}, \qquad \Gamma' = \Gamma\,\Gamma_0\,(1 - UV),$$

which is exactly the collinear [[Thm - Relativistic Velocity Addition|relativistic velocity addition]] of [[Special Relativity II — Simultaneity, Time Dilation and Length Contraction]], now derived from the four-velocity machinery rather than from the boost formulas. The non-collinear case hides a subtlety that becomes a major theme later: composing two boosts in different directions does not give a pure boost but a boost *times a rotation*, the **Wigner–Thomas rotation**, which [[Special Relativity IX — The Lorentz Group, Structure and Classification]] takes up in full.

Then the chapter turns to optics. Frequencies transform by the **Doppler effect** (§8.2), with a Doppler factor $1/[\Gamma(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V})]$ that contains a piece surviving even when the source moves transversely — the transverse Doppler effect, a pure time-dilation redshift with no Newtonian analogue, first measured by Ives and Stilwell. Directions transform by **aberration** (§8.2): a light ray that comes in at angle $\theta$ for $\mathcal{O}$ comes in at a smaller angle $\theta'$ for $\mathcal{O}'$, so a moving observer sees the sky bunch up ahead of their motion. Finally (§8.3), the chapter dismantles a naive expectation: that a fast-moving object simply *looks* FitzGerald-contracted. It does not. The image a camera records is built from photons that arrive simultaneously, having been *emitted* at different times, and the resulting picture of a moving sphere is a perfect circle (the **Penrose–Terrell** result), while a moving cube looks rotated rather than contracted. The same image-versus-position distinction explains why astrophysical jets appear to move faster than light.

The reader is assumed to have worked through [[Special Relativity VI — Observers, Local Rest Spaces and Local Frames]] and [[Special Relativity VII — Kinematics I, Motion Relative to an Observer]]: the [[Def - Observer and Local Rest Space|observer / local-rest-space]] formalism, the [[Def - The Orthogonal Projector onto the Local Rest Space|orthogonal projector]] $\perp_u$, and the relative-velocity decomposition $v = \Gamma(u + V)$ are the working tools here, used without re-derivation. The genuinely new ingredient is not any one formula but the discipline of tracking *which observer's rest space* each vector belongs to — that bookkeeping, and the invariants that survive it, is the entire chapter.

# Concept Map

## §8.1 Reciprocity and Velocity Composition

- **[[Thm - Reciprocity of Relative Velocity]]**
	- If $\mathcal{O}'$ has velocity $U$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$ and $\mathcal{O}$ has velocity $U'$ relative to $\mathcal{O}'$, then $U$ and $U'$ are *not* negatives of one another — they belong to the distinct rest spaces $E_u$ and $E_{u'}$ — but their magnitudes are equal: $\lVert U \rVert_g = \lVert U' \rVert_g$. Explicitly $U' = -(1/\Gamma_0)\perp_{u'}U$, the negative projection of $U$ onto $\mathcal{O}'$'s rest space, rescaled by the Lorentz factor $\Gamma_0 = u \cdot u'$. The Galilean identity $U' = -U$ is recovered only in the limit $\lVert U \rVert \to 0$, where all rest spaces collapse onto one common Newtonian space of simultaneity.

- **[[Thm - Length Contraction (General)]]**
	- A ruler carried by $\mathcal{O}'$ with proper length $\ell'$ (its length measured in $\mathcal{O}'$'s own rest space) is measured by $\mathcal{O}$ to have the shorter length $\ell = \ell'/\Gamma_0$, where $\Gamma_0 = (1 - U^2)^{-1/2} \ge 1$. The contraction occurs only along the direction of relative motion; transverse dimensions are unchanged because $E_u$ and $E_{u'}$ coincide in directions orthogonal to $U$. The effect is a direct consequence of the relativity of simultaneity: $\mathcal{O}$ and $\mathcal{O}'$ disagree about which events on the two ends of the ruler are simultaneous, and that disagreement *is* the contraction.

- **[[Thm - Law of Velocity Composition]]**
	- For a particle $\mathcal{P}$ with velocity $V$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$ and $V'$ relative to $\mathcal{O}'$, the four-dimensional law is $V' = \frac{1}{\Gamma_0 + U' \cdot \perp_{u'}V}\left[\perp_{u'}V + \Gamma_0 U'\right]$. Decomposed into parts parallel and transverse to the relative motion, with $U' = -U$, it reads $V'_\parallel = (V_\parallel - U)/(1 - UV_\parallel)$ and $\mathbf{V}'_\perp = \mathbf{V}_\perp/[\Gamma_0(1 - UV_\parallel)]$. In the collinear case $\mathbf{V}_\perp = 0$ it collapses to $V' = (V - U)/(1 - UV)$, the [[Thm - Relativistic Velocity Addition|velocity-addition law]] of §2. Composing two non-collinear boosts produces a boost *and* a rotation — the Wigner rotation, taken up in [[Special Relativity IX — The Lorentz Group, Structure and Classification]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Wigner Rotation and the Thomas Precession *(from the Lorentz Group)*
> The transverse part of the velocity-composition law is the seed of one of the deepest structural facts about the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]]: the boosts do **not** form a subgroup. Composing a boost in one direction with a boost in another gives a boost *times a spatial rotation*, the **Wigner rotation**. Accumulated continuously around a closed loop in velocity space it becomes the **Thomas precession**, the relativistic correction that halves the spin–orbit coupling in atomic physics. The angle is computed from the velocity-composition law worked out here; the group-theoretic statement is [[Special Relativity IX — The Lorentz Group, Structure and Classification]], and the Lie-algebraic version $[K_i, K_j] = -\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$ appears in [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Gyrogroups and the Algebra of Velocity Addition *(from Abstract Algebra)*
> Relativistic velocity addition $\oplus$ is **not associative and not commutative**, so the velocities of magnitude less than $c$ do not form a group under composition. They form a **gyrogroup**: a weakened group in which associativity and commutativity hold only up to the Wigner rotation, the "gyration" automorphism. This is Ungar's algebraic reformulation of the composition law worked out in §8.1, and it places the Thomas rotation at the centre of an entire algebraic theory.

- **[[Ex - The Fizeau experiment and the Fresnel drag]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Use the collinear composition law to find the speed of light in moving water and recover the Fresnel drag coefficient $\alpha = 1 - 1/n^2$, the first-order prediction Fizeau measured in 1850 — half a century before relativity.

- **[[Ex - Reciprocity of relative velocity from the four-velocities]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove directly from $u' = \Gamma_0(u + U)$ that $\lVert U' \rVert_g = \lVert U \rVert_g$ while $U' \ne -U$, and locate exactly where the Galilean intuition breaks.

- **[[Ex - Composing two perpendicular boosts and the Wigner rotation]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Compose a boost along $x$ with a boost along $y$ and show the result is a boost in a tilted direction *composed with a spatial rotation*, computing the rotation angle.

## §8.2 Doppler Effect and Aberration

- **[[Def - Relativistic Doppler Factor]]**
	- The Doppler factor is the ratio of received to emitted frequency for light, $\mathcal{D} = f_{\mathrm{rec}}/f_{\mathrm{em}} = 1/[\Gamma(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V})]$, where $\mathbf{V}$ is the source velocity relative to the receiver, $\mathbf{n}$ the unit vector from source to receiver, and $\Gamma = (1 - V^2)^{-1/2}$. It factors into a classical part $1/(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V})$, first order in $V$ and vanishing for transverse motion, and a relativistic part $1/\Gamma$, second order and surviving even transversely. For purely radial motion it simplifies to $\mathcal{D} = \sqrt{(1+V)/(1-V)}$ (approach) — the familiar blueshift/redshift factor.

- **[[Thm - The Doppler Effect]]**
	- An observer $\mathcal{O}'$ emitting light at proper-frequency $f_{\mathrm{em}}$ is received by $\mathcal{O}$ at frequency $f_{\mathrm{rec}} = f_{\mathrm{em}}/[\Gamma(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V})]$, with $\Gamma$ the Lorentz factor between the observers and $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V}$ the radial component of the source velocity. The non-relativistic limit is the first-order Doppler shift $f_{\mathrm{rec}} = (1 + \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V})f_{\mathrm{em}}$; the genuinely relativistic content is the surviving factor $1/\Gamma$ at $\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V}=0$, the **transverse Doppler effect**, which is a pure time-dilation redshift.

- **[[Thm - Aberration of Light]]**
	- A light ray arriving at incidence angle $\theta$ (to the direction of relative motion) for $\mathcal{O}$ arrives at angle $\theta'$ for $\mathcal{O}'$, with $\cos\theta' = (\cos\theta + U)/(1 + U\cos\theta)$, equivalently $\tan(\theta'/2) = \sqrt{(1-U)/(1+U)}\,\tan(\theta/2)$. One always has $\theta' \le \theta$: a moving observer sees light directions swept forward, so the stars bunch toward the direction of motion. For $\theta = \pi/2$ this gives $\cos\theta' = U$, the relativistic "headlight" and the pedestrian-in-the-rain effect.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Celestial Sphere as a Riemann Sphere and Möbius Action *(from Spinors and SL(2,C))*
> The half-angle aberration formula $\tan(\theta'/2) = \sqrt{(1-U)/(1+U)}\tan(\theta/2)$ is, under stereographic projection of the [[Def - Apparent Rotation and Images of Moving Objects|celestial sphere]], a **multiplication of the complex stereographic coordinate by a real constant** — a special **Möbius transformation**. The full statement is that the Lorentz group acts on the sky $\mathscr{S} \cong \mathbb{C}P^1$ by the Möbius transformations of $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$, a boost being a dilation, a rotation a rotation, and a general element a fractional-linear map. This is the geometric heart of the spinor map of [[Special Relativity XI — SL(2,C), Spinors and the Spinor Map]]: aberration is conformal, which is why it maps circles to circles and a sphere stays a sphere.

- **[[Ex - The Ives-Stilwell experiment and transverse Doppler]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Take the arithmetic mean of the forward and backward Doppler shifts of atoms moving at $\pm V$ and show the mean equals $\Gamma f_0$, isolating the time-dilation factor; this is the 1938 measurement that first confirmed transverse Doppler.

- **[[Ex - Stellar aberration and the celestial-sphere distortion]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Derive the stellar aberration $\sin\alpha = U/c$ giving the $20''$ yearly circle traced by a star at the ecliptic pole, and show how a uniform grid on the sky bunches toward the forward pole as $U \to c$.

- **[[Ex - The relativistic headlight effect]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Show that an isotropic emitter in its rest frame beams almost all its light into a forward cone of half-angle $\sim 1/\Gamma$ for a fast-moving observer, and compute the fraction of photons in the forward hemisphere.

## §8.3 Images of Moving Objects

- **[[Def - Apparent Rotation and Images of Moving Objects]]**
	- The *image* of an object perceived at an instant is the intersection of its worldtube with the observer's past light cone — the set of events whose emitted photons arrive together — and this differs from the *instantaneous position*, the intersection with the rest space, because the two are different slices of the worldtube. Consequently the FitzGerald contraction (a statement about position) is not what a camera records. A moving cube appears **rotated** by $\theta = \arcsin V$ rather than contracted (the Penrose–Terrell rotation); a moving sphere photographs as a **perfect circle** of unchanged shape; and an object moving toward the observer appears *elongated*, not shortened.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Penrose's Theorem and the Conformal Structure of the Sky *(from Twistor Theory and Conformal Geometry)*
> The fact that a moving sphere always photographs as a circle is **Penrose's theorem** (1959): the Lorentz group acts on the celestial sphere by conformal (angle-preserving) maps, and conformal maps of the 2-sphere send circles to circles. This is the observational shadow of the isomorphism between the restricted Lorentz group and the Möbius group $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ — the same fact that organises **twistor theory**, where the conformal structure of the sky, not the metric, is taken as fundamental. The "no visible contraction" result is the first physical hint that the natural object attached to an observer is not a metric sphere but a *conformal* sphere.

- **[[Ex - The Penrose-Terrell rotation of a moving sphere]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Use stereographic projection and the aberration formula to prove that the circular contour of a sphere maps to a circular contour for any observer, so a relativistically moving sphere is photographed as an undistorted disk.

- **[[Ex - The apparent superluminal motion of an astrophysical jet]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Derive $V_{\mathrm{app}} = V\sin\theta/(1 - V\cos\theta)$ for a blob moving at $V$ at angle $\theta$ to the line of sight, show it exceeds $c$ for $V$ near $c$ and small $\theta$, and apply it to the M87 jet's apparent $6c$.

- **[[Ex - Why a moving ruler is photographed elongated or rotated]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Reconstruct the image of a moving cube from the photon arrival-time condition, showing the back face has apparent width $V a$ and the front face $a/\Gamma$, and identify the result with a rotation by $\arcsin V$.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The problems of this chapter chase a recognisable handful of goals. The most common is to **transform a measured quantity between two observers**: given what $\mathcal{O}$ measures — a length, a velocity, a frequency, a direction — find what $\mathcal{O}'$ measures. These are direct applications of the four transformation laws (contraction, composition, Doppler, aberration), and the whole skill is in identifying which quantity is being transformed and which observer is which. A second, subtler target is to **establish a relativistic invariant or reciprocity**: to show that two observers agree on something (their relative speeds, the product of forward and backward Doppler frequencies) even when the Galilean intuition would have them agree on more (the relative velocity vectors). A third target, which dominates §8.3, is to **predict an image** — to compute not where an object *is* but how it *looks*, which requires the past-light-cone construction and almost always overturns the naive expectation. A fourth is to **extract a small relativistic correction** experimentally accessible at low speed: the Fresnel drag $1 - 1/n^2$, the transverse Doppler factor $\Gamma$, the stellar aberration $20''$ — each a first- or second-order effect that a precision experiment can isolate. A fifth, more conceptual, is to **resolve an apparent paradox of appearance**: why jets seem superluminal, why a sphere does not look squashed, why the pedestrian must tilt the umbrella. Across all five, the unifying remark is that you understand a change-of-observer problem when you know which vector lives in which rest space, and which invariant survives the change.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **Two observers and their relative velocity** are given, which immediately supplies $\Gamma_0$ and fixes the boost between the rest spaces; this is the richest source, because every transformation law is a function of $\Gamma_0$ and the direction of relative motion. **A particle's velocity in one observer's frame** is given — most usefully the rest frame of the object, where a ruler has its proper length and a source its proper frequency — and one transforms out. **The object is light** — a photon, with $V = c\mathbf{n}$ and a null tangent — which collapses the velocity-composition law into the aberration law and lets the Doppler factor be read off the same calculation. **The observer is inertial** ($\mathcal{O}'$ with zero four-acceleration), which is assumed throughout the acceleration-composition and image discussions to keep rest spaces globally defined and worldlines straight. **A photon arrival-time condition** is given — the requirement that the photons forming an image reach the observer simultaneously — which is the source unique to §8.3 and the one that distinguishes image from position. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a given relative velocity routes through a transformation law to a transformed measurement; a rest-frame description routes through a boost to a moving-frame description; a null tangent routes through aberration to an observed direction; an arrival-time condition routes through the past light cone to an image. The [[Special Relativity VIII — Kinematics II, Change of Observer#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves almost every problem in the chapter is assembled from. Because the recurring error is confusing which vector lives in which rest space, each operation states explicitly *which observer measures which quantity*. When stuck, scan the list and try each one.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Decompose a four-velocity relative to an observer.** Any four-velocity $v$ splits as $v = \Gamma(u + V)$, where $u$ is the observer's four-velocity, $\Gamma = u \cdot v$ is the Lorentz factor, and $V \in E_u$ is the relative velocity (with $u \cdot V = 0$); see [[Def - Velocity Relative to an Observer]]. *Trigger:* you have a particle's motion in spacetime and want its velocity as seen by a specific observer. This is the bridge between the frame-free four-velocity and the observer-dependent three-velocity, and it is the first move in almost every composition or Doppler problem.

2. **Project onto a rest space with $\perp_u$.** To extract the part of a vector $X$ seen by observer $\mathcal{O}$, apply the [[Def - The Orthogonal Projector onto the Local Rest Space|orthogonal projector]] $\perp_u X = X - (u \cdot X)\,u$; the result lies in $E_u$. *Trigger:* a vector is expressed in one observer's terms but you need its components in another observer's rest space. *Pattern:* "project $V$ onto $E_{u'}$ via $\perp_{u'}$, then read off the parallel and transverse pieces." This is the operation that turns the four-dimensional composition law into a three-dimensional one.

3. **Compute the Lorentz factor between observers as a scalar product.** The factor $\Gamma_0 = u \cdot u'$ is an invariant, equal to $(1 - U^2)^{-1/2}$; evaluate it in whichever description is easiest; see [[Def - Lorentz Factor and Relative Velocity]]. *Trigger:* any transformation law, since all of them are functions of $\Gamma_0$. Because $u \cdot u'$ is symmetric, $\Gamma_0$ is the same whether you call $\mathcal{O}'$ the moving one or $\mathcal{O}$ — the reciprocity of the Lorentz factor.

4. **Apply the velocity-composition law in the form fitted to the data.** Use the four-dimensional form $V' = [\perp_{u'}V + \Gamma_0 U']/(\Gamma_0 + U'\cdot\perp_{u'}V)$ when working abstractly, the parallel/transverse split $V'_\parallel = (V_\parallel - U)/(1 - UV_\parallel)$, $\mathbf{V}'_\perp = \mathbf{V}_\perp/[\Gamma_0(1 - UV_\parallel)]$ when components are given, and the collinear form $V' = (V - U)/(1 - UV)$ when all velocities are parallel; see [[Thm - Law of Velocity Composition]]. *Trigger:* a velocity is measured relative to a moving observer and you want it relative to another. The collinear form is the [[Thm - Relativistic Velocity Addition|velocity addition]] of §2.

5. **Specialise the composition law to light by setting $V = \mathbf{n}$.** A photon has relative velocity of magnitude $c = 1$, so $V = \mathbf{n}$ a unit vector; feeding this into composition gives the aberration law for directions and, from the same projection, the Doppler factor for frequencies; see [[Thm - Aberration of Light]]. *Trigger:* the moving object is a light ray. *Pattern:* "the photon's $V$ is a unit vector, so its transformation is pure direction-change (aberration) plus a magnitude bookkeeping that is the Doppler shift."

6. **Use length contraction in the direction of motion only.** A proper length $\ell'$ along the relative motion is measured as $\ell = \ell'/\Gamma_0$; transverse lengths are unchanged; see [[Thm - Length Contraction (General)]]. *Trigger:* an extended object moves relative to the measuring observer. The subtlety is that the measurement requires locating both ends *simultaneously in the measuring frame*, and that simultaneity is what differs — so always identify the measuring observer's rest space first.

7. **Build an image from the past light cone, not the rest space.** To find how a moving object *looks*, intersect its worldtube with the observer's past light cone $\mathcal{I}^-(O)$ — the locus of events whose photons arrive together — not with the rest space $E_u(O)$; see [[Def - Apparent Rotation and Images of Moving Objects]]. *Trigger:* the question asks what is *seen*, *photographed*, or *observed*, as opposed to where the object *is*. *Pattern:* "impose that the photons reach the observer simultaneously; solve for the emission events; that locus is the image." This is the operation that produces the apparent rotation and the apparent superluminal motion.

8. **Convert between angle and complex stereographic coordinate.** Map a sky direction at angle $\theta$ to the complex number $\zeta = e^{i\varphi}\tan(\theta/2)$ on the stereographically projected celestial sphere; aberration acts on $\zeta$ by multiplication by a real constant; see [[Def - Apparent Rotation and Images of Moving Objects]]. *Trigger:* you must show that a contour (a circle on the sky) keeps its shape under a change of observer. Because the map is conformal, circles go to circles — the engine of the Penrose–Terrell result.

9. **Take a Galilean limit to check a formula.** Every relativistic law in the chapter reduces, as $U \to 0$ (so $\Gamma_0 \to 1$ and all rest spaces coincide), to a familiar Newtonian rule: composition to $V' = V - U$, contraction to $\ell = \ell'$, Doppler to the first-order shift, aberration to the rain-umbrella tilt. *Trigger:* you want to verify a transcribed formula or fix a sign. The Galilean limit is the cheapest sanity check available.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Writing the reciprocal velocity as $U' = -U$
> The single most natural Galilean reflex — if $\mathcal{O}'$ moves at $U$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$, then $\mathcal{O}$ moves at $-U$ relative to $\mathcal{O}'$ — is **false as a vector equation** in relativity. The vectors $U$ and $U'$ live in *different* three-dimensional rest spaces, $E_u$ and $E_{u'}$ (see [[Thm - Reciprocity of Relative Velocity]]), and there is no frame-independent identification of one with minus the other; explicitly $U' = -(1/\Gamma_0)\perp_{u'}U$, which differs from $-U$ by the projection and the factor $\Gamma_0$. What *is* true is the equality of magnitudes, $\lVert U' \rVert_g = \lVert U \rVert_g$. The operation becomes legal exactly in the limit $\lVert U \rVert \to 0$, where the rest spaces merge into one Newtonian space and $\Gamma_0 \to 1$, so $U' = -U$ holds again.

> [!warning] 2. Adding velocities with the Galilean rule when they are non-collinear
> Even readers who know to use $V' = (V-U)/(1-UV)$ for collinear velocities are tempted to apply it component-by-component when $V$ has a transverse part — treating $V'_\perp = V_\perp - U_\perp$ or forgetting the $\Gamma_0$ in the transverse denominator. The transverse component does *not* simply subtract: $\mathbf{V}'_\perp = \mathbf{V}_\perp/[\Gamma_0(1 - UV_\parallel)]$ carries a Lorentz factor and a coupling to the *parallel* velocity ([[Thm - Law of Velocity Composition]]). A concrete failure: a particle moving purely transversely ($V_\parallel = 0$) for $\mathcal{O}$ has $\mathbf{V}'_\perp = \mathbf{V}_\perp/\Gamma_0$ for $\mathcal{O}'$ — its transverse speed is *reduced*, not unchanged, an effect with no Galilean analogue. The rule becomes legal only when $\mathbf{V}_\perp = 0$ (collinear case) or in the limit $U \to 0$.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming a fast object looks FitzGerald-contracted
> It is overwhelmingly tempting to say that since a moving rod *is* contracted by $1/\Gamma$, a photograph of it shows it contracted. This conflates the object's *position* (its rest-space slice) with its *image* (its past-light-cone slice), and the two are different ([[Def - Apparent Rotation and Images of Moving Objects]]). A moving sphere photographs as a *perfect circle*, not an ellipse (Penrose–Terrell); a moving cube looks *rotated* by $\arcsin V$, not flattened; an approaching rod looks *elongated*. The counterexample is the sphere: its contour is a circle on the sky for every observer, with no visible squashing. The contraction statement becomes a correct statement about appearance only for an object whose extent is *transverse* to the line of sight *and* at rest in the rest-space sense being measured — never for a freely photographed three-dimensional body.

> [!warning] 4. Forgetting the transverse Doppler factor when the source moves sideways
> The Newtonian Doppler formula $f_{\mathrm{rec}} = (1 + \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V})f_{\mathrm{em}}$ vanishes when the source moves transversely ($\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V} = 0$), so it is tempting to conclude there is no shift for transverse motion. There is: the relativistic factor $1/\Gamma$ survives, giving a pure redshift $f_{\mathrm{rec}} = f_{\mathrm{em}}/\Gamma$ ([[Thm - The Doppler Effect]]), the transverse Doppler effect, which is precisely the time dilation of the moving source's clock. The Ives–Stilwell experiment exists to measure exactly this surviving factor. The first-order formula becomes adequate only when $V^2 \ll V$, i.e. to first order in $V$; the transverse effect is the leading correction.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this chapter are won at the moment you set up the bookkeeping of *which observer's rest space* each vector belongs to. Almost every change-of-observer error is a rest-space error — confusing $U$ with $U'$, treating a transverse velocity as Galilean, mistaking position for image — so begin by naming the two observers, their four-velocities $u, u'$, and the rest spaces $E_u, E_{u'}$, and by deciding which one is "the measurer" for the quantity you want.

If the problem **asks to transform a velocity**, the decisive first move is to classify the geometry: are the velocities collinear, or is there a transverse part? For collinear velocities, reach straight for $V' = (V - U)/(1 - UV)$ — the [[Thm - Relativistic Velocity Addition|velocity-addition]] formula — and the companion Lorentz-factor law $\Gamma' = \Gamma\Gamma_0(1 - UV)$. For the general case, use the [[Thm - Law of Velocity Composition|parallel/transverse split]]: the parallel part obeys the same one-dimensional formula, while the transverse part picks up a factor $1/[\Gamma_0(1 - UV_\parallel)]$. The reason this works is that the only direction that gets boosted is the direction of relative motion; everything orthogonal merely inherits the time-dilation of the parallel motion. When the velocities are not collinear, remember that the composition is *not* a pure boost — it carries a hidden rotation (the Wigner rotation), which is harmless for computing $V'$ but essential the moment you compose three or more boosts or track an orientation.

If the problem **involves light** — a frequency, a colour, a direction of arrival — the first move is to recognise that a photon's relative velocity is a *unit* vector, $V = \mathbf{n}$. This collapses two laws into one: the *direction* transforms by [[Thm - Aberration of Light|aberration]], $\cos\theta' = (\cos\theta + U)/(1 + U\cos\theta)$, and the *frequency* transforms by the [[Thm - The Doppler Effect|Doppler factor]] $1/[\Gamma(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V})]$, both extracted from the same projection of the null tangent onto the two rest spaces. For frequency problems, decide whether the motion is radial (use $\sqrt{(1+V)/(1-V)}$), transverse (use $1/\Gamma$ — and remember the first-order formula would wrongly give no shift), or oblique (use the full factor). For direction problems, the half-angle form $\tan(\theta'/2) = \sqrt{(1-U)/(1+U)}\tan(\theta/2)$ is the one to use, because it is the form that becomes a clean stereographic statement.

If the problem **asks how something looks** — photographs, appears, is observed, as opposed to where it is — switch from the rest-space picture to the *past-light-cone* picture. The image is built from photons that arrive together but were emitted at different times, so impose the arrival-time condition and solve for the emission events; the resulting locus is the image. This is the strategy behind every result of §8.3: the apparent rotation of a cube (the back face becomes visible because its light, emitted earlier, arrives with the front face's), the circular image of a sphere (the contour is a circle on the sky for everyone), and the apparent superluminal motion of a jet (two images, taken at the observer's two reception times, are closer in time than the emissions because the source is chasing its own light). The diagnostic question is always: *is this about position or about image?* If image, never use FitzGerald contraction directly.

If the problem **asks to isolate a small relativistic correction** — the Fresnel drag, the transverse Doppler factor, the stellar aberration angle — the strategy is to expand the exact law to the relevant order and identify the term that distinguishes relativity from the Galilean prediction. Fizeau's $\alpha = 1 - 1/n^2$ is the first-order-in-$V/c$ expansion of the collinear composition law; Ives–Stilwell's $\Gamma$ is the second-order survivor of the averaged Doppler shift; stellar aberration's $20''$ is the first-order $\sin\alpha = U/c$. The reason these are the experimentally decisive quantities is that they are precisely where the Galilean and relativistic predictions *first differ*, so a precision measurement at low speed can discriminate.

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle: **track the rest spaces, and hunt for what is invariant across them.** Two observers disagree about lengths, velocities, frequencies, directions, and even about what an object looks like — but they agree about relative *speeds*, about the *product* of forward and backward Doppler frequencies, about the *shape* of a sphere's image, about causal order. Every question in this chapter is the question "what survives the change of observer, and what is merely the shadow it casts in one rest space?"

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Law of Velocity Composition|The velocity-composition law]]**: $V' = (V-U)/(1-UV)$ collinear, with the transverse correction $\mathbf{V}'_\perp = \mathbf{V}_\perp/[\Gamma_0(1-UV_\parallel)]$. This is the workhorse of the chapter and the most-reached-for formula in relativistic kinematics. Its recognisable setup is a velocity measured relative to a frame that is itself moving. The reusable content is threefold: it keeps $c$ as a fixed point and an unattainable ceiling, it reduces to the [[Thm - Relativistic Velocity Addition|collinear addition law]] of §2 in the parallel case, and — the part most often forgotten — its transverse piece carries a Lorentz factor, so a sideways velocity is *reduced*, not preserved, under a change of observer. Reach for it whenever speeds must be composed; combine it with the null condition $V = \mathbf{n}$ to get aberration for free.

- **The Lorentz factor between observers** $\Gamma_0 = u \cdot u' = (1 - U^2)^{-1/2}$: the universal currency of every transformation in the chapter. It is symmetric in the two observers (reciprocity of the factor), always $\ge 1$, and it is the multiplier in length contraction ($\ell = \ell'/\Gamma_0$), the divisor in transverse velocity transformation, and a factor in the Doppler shift and the Lorentz-factor composition $\Gamma' = \Gamma\Gamma_0(1 - UV)$. The reusable skill is to compute $\Gamma_0$ first, from whichever scalar product or speed is given, and pay every later step in that currency. Recognise its applicability the instant two observers appear and any quantity must cross between them.

- **[[Def - Relativistic Doppler Factor|The Doppler factor]]** $\mathcal{D} = 1/[\Gamma(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V})]$: the complete frequency-transformation law for light, packaging the classical first-order shift and the relativistic transverse shift in one expression. Its typical use is any problem about observed colour, frequency, or photon energy (since photon energy scales with frequency). The reusable content is the factorisation into $1/(1 - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{V})$, which is first order and direction-dependent, and $1/\Gamma$, which is second order and survives transverse motion. Reach for the radial form $\sqrt{(1+V)/(1-V)}$ for line-of-sight motion and the bare $1/\Gamma$ for transverse motion; the latter is the time-dilation redshift that the first-order formula misses.

- **[[Thm - Aberration of Light|The aberration law]]** $\tan(\theta'/2) = \sqrt{(1-U)/(1+U)}\tan(\theta/2)$: the direction-transformation law for light, and the one with the deepest downstream structure. Its recognisable setup is a light ray whose arrival direction differs between two observers. The reusable content is twofold: it always beams directions *forward* ($\theta' \le \theta$), so a fast observer sees the sky compressed ahead, and — the half-angle form especially — it is a *conformal* map of the celestial sphere, a multiplication of the stereographic coordinate by a real constant. The conformality is why a sphere's image stays circular and why the Lorentz group acts on the sky by Möbius transformations; reach for the half-angle form whenever a contour's shape must be tracked.

- **The image-versus-position distinction** (past light cone vs rest space): the principle that what an object *looks like* is its intersection with the observer's past light cone, not with the rest space. Its typical use is any "what is seen / photographed" problem, where it overturns the naive contraction expectation. The reusable move is to impose simultaneous *arrival* of photons (not simultaneous *emission*) and solve for the emission events. This single shift of viewpoint produces the Penrose–Terrell rotation, the circular image of a sphere, the elongation of an approaching rod, and the apparent superluminal motion of jets — four results that are all incomprehensible from the position picture and immediate from the image picture.

# Bridges

1. **The Lorentz group and the Wigner rotation — composition of boosts is not a boost.** The transverse part of the velocity-composition law is the kinematic face of a structural fact about the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]]: the set of pure boosts is *not* closed under composition. When you compose a boost taking $\mathcal{O}$ to $\mathcal{O}'$ with a boost taking $\mathcal{O}'$ to $\mathcal{O}''$, and the two boost directions differ, the resulting Lorentz transformation from $\mathcal{O}$ to $\mathcal{O}''$ is *not* a single boost but a boost followed by a spatial rotation — the **Wigner rotation**. The rotation angle is computed directly from the composition law derived in §8.1: write the two boosts as $4\times 4$ matrices, multiply, and polar-decompose the product into (positive-definite symmetric) $\times$ (orthogonal); the orthogonal factor is the rotation. The reason this matters physically is that a particle carried around a closed loop in velocity space — an electron orbiting a nucleus, say — accumulates these infinitesimal rotations into a net precession, the **Thomas precession**, $\boldsymbol{\Omega}_T = (\gamma^2/(\gamma+1))\,\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{v}$, which supplies the factor-of-two correction to the spin–orbit coupling that classical estimates miss. The full development is [[Special Relativity IX — The Lorentz Group, Structure and Classification]] (the polar decomposition and the Thomas-rotation angle) and [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]] (the Lie-algebra relation $[K_i, K_j] = -\varepsilon_{ijk}J_k$, which says that the commutator of two boost generators is a rotation generator — the infinitesimal Wigner rotation).

2. **Spinors and the Möbius action on the celestial sphere — aberration is conformal.** The half-angle aberration formula $\tan(\theta'/2) = \sqrt{(1-U)/(1+U)}\tan(\theta/2)$ becomes, under stereographic projection of an observer's celestial sphere onto the complex plane, the transformation $\zeta \mapsto k\zeta$ with $k = \sqrt{(1-U)/(1+U)}$ real — a dilation of the complex stereographic coordinate. A spatial rotation acts on the same coordinate by a rotation, and a general restricted Lorentz transformation acts by a **fractional-linear (Möbius) transformation** $\zeta \mapsto (a\zeta + b)/(c\zeta + d)$ with $ad - bc = 1$. This is the statement that the restricted Lorentz group $SO^+(1,3)$ is isomorphic to the Möbius group $\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$, acting on the sky $\mathscr{S} \cong \mathbb{C}P^1$. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix} \in SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ is exactly the spinor representation of the Lorentz transformation, and the two-to-one covering $SL(2,\mathbb{C}) \to SO^+(1,3)$ is visible in the sky because $\pm\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ give the same Möbius map. The whole construction is the subject of [[Special Relativity XI — SL(2,C), Spinors and the Spinor Map]]; the physical observation that anchors it is the aberration of starlight worked out here.

3. **Twistor theory and conformal geometry — the sky is a conformal sphere, not a metric one.** Penrose's theorem that a moving sphere always photographs as a circle (§8.3) is the observation that the Lorentz group acts on the celestial sphere by *conformal* (angle-preserving) transformations, and conformal automorphisms of the round 2-sphere send circles to circles. The deep reading is that the natural structure an observer carries on their sky is not a metric (which would single out a "true" angular size and could be squashed by a boost) but a **conformal structure** — only angles and the circle-family are observer-independent, while sizes are not. This is the entry point to **twistor theory**, in which Penrose takes the conformal structure of the light rays through a point, rather than the spacetime metric, as the primitive object: a twistor is essentially a point of the complexified celestial sphere together with its incidence data, and the conformal group of compactified Minkowski space is the structure group. The flat-spacetime fact that powers all of this — circles to circles under a change of observer — is precisely the image-of-a-sphere result of this chapter.

4. **Astrophysics and relativistic jets — superluminal motion as a projection effect.** The apparent-superluminal formula $V_{\mathrm{app}} = V\sin\theta/(1 - V\cos\theta)$ derived in §8.3 is the working tool of high-energy astrophysics, where jets of plasma ejected from active galactic nuclei and microquasars are routinely observed to move across the sky at apparent speeds of several times $c$. The resolution — that $V_{\mathrm{app}}$ is computed from two *images* and not two *positions*, so a source chasing its own emitted light compresses the apparent travel time — turns the apparent paradox into a *measurement*: from $V_{\mathrm{app}}$ and an independent constraint one bounds the true bulk Lorentz factor $\Gamma$ and the viewing angle $\theta$ of the jet. For the M87 jet, apparent speeds up to $\sim 6c$ imply $\Gamma \gtrsim 6$ and $\theta \lesssim 19^\circ$. The same Doppler factor that appears in §8.2 then governs the **relativistic beaming** that makes the approaching jet far brighter than the receding counter-jet — which is why one usually sees only one jet — closing the loop between the aberration, Doppler, and image results of this chapter and the observed morphology of these sources, taken up further in the discussion of radiating systems in [[Special Relativity XXIII — The Energy-Momentum Tensor and Field Energy]].

5. **Curved spacetime and gravitational lensing — the local sky is still a Lorentz sky.** In general relativity the change-of-observer laws of this chapter survive *locally* and unchanged: at any event, two observers with different four-velocities see the same null rays aberrated and Doppler-shifted exactly by the special-relativistic formulas, because the laws depend only on the tangent-space metric, which is Minkowski at every point (the [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|equivalence principle]]). What general relativity *adds* is that the null rays themselves bend between emission and reception, so the relation between the *source* direction and the *observed* direction acquires a gravitational piece on top of the kinematic aberration — this is **gravitational lensing**, and the observed-sky distortion near a black hole is the composition of the velocity aberration computed here with the gravitational deflection of [[General Relativity I — Einstein's Equations and Schwarzschild]]. The celestial-sphere distortion images of §8.2, computed for a fast inertial observer in flat space, are the special-relativistic kernel of the dramatic sky-distortion images near a Schwarzschild horizon, where an additional gravitational lensing is layered on top.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a relative velocity is a vector in someone's rest space, and the chapter is the algebra of changing whose.** The one idea that organises everything here is that there is no such thing as "the velocity" of an object — only its velocity *relative to a chosen observer*, which is a spacelike vector living in *that observer's* three-dimensional rest space $E_u$. Two observers have two different rest spaces, tilted relative to one another in spacetime, and every result in the chapter is the answer to "how does a vector in $E_u$ correspond to a vector in $E_{u'}$?" Reciprocity is the simplest instance: the velocity of $\mathcal{O}'$ in $E_u$ and the velocity of $\mathcal{O}$ in $E_{u'}$ are different vectors in different spaces, equal only in magnitude. The velocity-composition law is the general instance: project, rescale by $\Gamma_0$, re-decompose. Aberration is the instance for null vectors. Once you hold this frame, the formulas stop being a list to memorise and become inevitable — each is the unique way to carry a vector across the tilt between two rest spaces, and the recurring factors of $\Gamma_0$ are exactly the price of the tilt.

**The true name of the Doppler and aberration laws is "the Lorentz group acting on the celestial sphere."** The frequency shift and the direction change look like two separate optical effects, but they are two coordinates of a single object: the action of a Lorentz transformation on the bundle of light rays through an event. Each null ray carries a direction (a point of the sky $\mathscr{S} \cong \mathbb{C}P^1$) and a frequency (a positive scaling of the null tangent); a boost moves the direction by a Möbius transformation (aberration) and scales the frequency by the Doppler factor, and the two are linked because both come from the same action on the null tangent $\ell$. The operational payoff of this true name is enormous: it tells you in advance that aberration must be conformal (Möbius maps are conformal), hence that circles on the sky stay circles, hence that a sphere photographs as a disk — all without computing a single image, just from knowing what kind of map a Lorentz transformation induces on the sphere. When you see "Doppler" and "aberration", think not "two optical formulas" but "one group acting on the sky."

**Image is not position, and the difference is the whole of relativistic optics.** The most consequential conceptual move in the chapter is the refusal to identify what an object *is* with what it *looks like*. The position of an object is its slice by the observer's rest space — a "now" snapshot — and length contraction is a true statement about *that* slice. But a camera does not record the now-slice; it records the photons arriving now, which left the object at *different* past times, so the image is the slice by the past light cone. Because these are different slices of the same worldtube, the image can differ wildly from the contracted position: a sphere's image is a perfect circle, a cube looks rotated, an approaching rod looks stretched, and a jet looks superluminal. The reason this distinction is so easy to miss, and so important once grasped, is that in everyday life the two slices nearly coincide (light is fast, motions are slow), so we are trained to equate seeing with being. Relativistic optics is what happens when that training fails, and the corrective discipline is always the same: for "is", slice by the rest space; for "looks", slice by the past light cone.

**Relativity preserves speeds and shapes-up-to-conformality while demoting velocities and lengths.** As elsewhere in the subject, the popular reading of "relativity" as "everything is relative" is backwards. Yes, velocities, lengths, frequencies, and arrival directions are all observer-dependent — but the chapter installs firmer invariants in their place. Two observers agree on their relative *speed* even when they disagree on the relative *velocity vector* (reciprocity). They agree on the *product* of the forward and backward Doppler frequencies, $f_1 f_2 = f_0^2$, even when each frequency is shifted — the basis of the most precise modern test of relativity. They agree on the *shape* of a sphere's image (a circle) even when they disagree on its angular size — because the sky's conformal structure, not its metric, is observer-independent. The working instinct the chapter drills is to look past the frame-dependent quantity to the invariant it shadows: the speed behind the velocity, the frequency-product behind the frequencies, the conformal circle behind the apparent ellipse.
