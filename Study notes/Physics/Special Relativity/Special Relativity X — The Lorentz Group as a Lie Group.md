# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we set $c = 1$** and use the **mostly-minus signature** $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, so a timelike vector has $X\cdot X > 0$. To restore $c$, replace $t$ by $ct$, every velocity $v$ by $v/c$, and insert factors of $c$ by dimensional analysis. This chapter is the Lie-theoretic study of the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]] already defined in [[Special Relativity IV — The Invariant Interval, Rapidity and the Lorentz Group]] and classified in [[Special Relativity IX — The Lorentz Group, Structure and Classification]]; we now treat it as a smooth manifold and a Lie group, extract its Lie algebra, and read its representation theory.

- $O(1,3)$ — the **Lorentz group**, $4\times 4$ real $\Lambda$ with $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\,\Lambda = \eta$
- $SO^+(1,3)$ — the **restricted** (proper orthochronous) Lorentz group, the identity component
- $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ — the **Lie algebra** of the Lorentz group, $4\times 4$ real $\omega$ with $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\,\omega = 0$
- $\mathrm{Id}$ — the $4\times 4$ identity matrix; $\varepsilon, \delta$ — small real parameters
- $\omega, L, M$ — elements of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ (generators); $\omega_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\alpha}\omega^\alpha{}_\nu$ the index-lowered form (antisymmetric)
- $K_1, K_2, K_3$ — the **boost generators** (symmetric matrices, mix time with space)
- $J_1, J_2, J_3$ — the **rotation generators** (antisymmetric matrices, act as $\mathbf{e}_i\times$ on the spatial block)
- $G_a$, $a = 1,\dots,6$ — the six generators collectively, $(G_1,\dots,G_6) = (K_1,K_2,K_3,J_1,J_2,J_3)$
- $[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]$ — the **Lie bracket**, here the matrix commutator $[L_1,L_2] = L_1L_2 - L_2L_1$
- $C_{ab}{}^c$ — the **structure constants**, $[G_a,G_b] = \sum_c C_{ab}{}^c G_c$
- $\epsilon_{ijk}$ — the fully antisymmetric symbol on $\{1,2,3\}$ ($+1$ even, $-1$ odd, $0$ repeated)
- $\exp$ — the matrix exponential $\exp(\omega) = \sum_{n\ge0}\omega^n/n!$; $\exp : \mathfrak{so}(1,3) \to SO^+(1,3)$
- $\psi$ — rapidity (boost parameter); $\varphi, \theta$ — rotation angle; $\mathbf{n} = n^i\mathbf{e}_i$ — a unit spatial direction
- $\pi_1(X)$ — the fundamental group of $X$; $\mathbb{Z}/2$ — the two-element group; $\cong$ — diffeomorphism or isomorphism
- $SO(3)$ — the Euclidean rotation group; $\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^3$ — real projective $3$-space; $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ — the double cover
- $A_i = \tfrac12(J_i + iK_i)$, $B_i = \tfrac12(J_i - iK_i)$ — the complexified generators of the $(A,B)$ decomposition
- $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})$ — the rotation algebra's compact real form and its complexification
- $(j_A, j_B)$ — the pair of half-integers labelling an irreducible Lorentz representation

> [!warning] Convention: Gourgoulhon's opposite signature and generator-sign
> Gourgoulhon (*Special Relativity in General Frames*, Ch. 7), the principal source for this chapter, uses the mostly-**plus** signature $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$, writes the group $O(3,1)$, and sets the boost generators to $K_i = -\mathscr{J}_{0i}$. The combination produces his commutators (Eq. 7.44) $[K_i,K_j] = -\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$, $[K_i,J_j] = -\epsilon_{ijk}K_k$, $[J_i,J_j] = \epsilon_{ijk}J_k$. We follow the mostly-**minus** convention of Giulini (Eq. 51), with $K_i = +\mathscr{J}_{i0}$, giving the $J,K$ relations stated throughout — the form standard in the physics literature and used by the existing vault pages. Same algebra; the boost-generator sign and one minus differ. We also use the **mathematics convention** $\Lambda = \exp(\omega)$ (real generators, no factor of $i$), converting to the physicists' Hermitian generators $\Lambda = \exp(i\theta^a T_a)$ by $T_a = -iG_a$ when the $(A,B)$ relations are quoted in the $i\epsilon$ form.

# Motivation

Here is the entire chapter in one sentence: the Lorentz group is a six-dimensional Lie group, and once you know its Lie algebra you know which particles can exist. Everything that follows unpacks that claim.

So far the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]] has been a set of matrices — the linear maps preserving the interval, sorted into four components, parametrised by rapidities and angles. That is the algebra of the group. But the group is also a *manifold*: its elements depend smoothly on six continuous parameters, and the multiplication is a smooth map. This dual nature, group and manifold at once, is what makes it a **Lie group**, and Lie groups have a magical property discovered by Sophus Lie. Almost everything about a Lie group is already determined by its behaviour infinitesimally close to the identity — by the tangent space at the identity, made into an algebra by a bracket that records the group's non-commutativity. This tangent algebra is the **Lie algebra** $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$, and the bulk of this chapter is its construction and its consequences.

The strategy is to linearise. A transformation infinitely close to the identity is $\Lambda = \mathrm{Id} + \varepsilon\,\omega$, and substituting into the defining equation $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\,\Lambda = \eta$ and keeping first order gives the condition on the generator $\omega$:
$$
\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\,\omega = 0
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\omega_{\mu\nu} = -\omega_{\nu\mu}\ \ (\text{antisymmetric once an index is lowered}).
$$
The generators form a six-dimensional vector space, with a basis of three boost generators $K_i$ and three rotation generators $J_i$. The whole content of the algebra is then how these six bracket together — the commutators
$$
[J_i, J_j] = \epsilon_{ijk}J_k, \qquad [J_i, K_j] = \epsilon_{ijk}K_k, \qquad [K_i, K_j] = -\epsilon_{ijk}J_k,
$$
which say: rotations close into rotations, boosts transform as a vector under rotations, and — crucially — two boosts bracket into *minus a rotation*. That last minus sign is the most consequential symbol in the chapter. It is the algebraic seed of the Thomas rotation, the reason the boosts do not form a subgroup, and (squared to $-1$) the reason the algebra splits over the complex numbers into two copies of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$.

The finite transformations are recovered by **exponentiation**: $\exp : \mathfrak{so}(1,3) \to SO^+(1,3)$ sends a boost generator to a boost (hyperbolic functions) and a rotation generator to a rotation (circular functions), and — remarkably for a non-compact group — it is *surjective*, so the restricted Lorentz group is exactly the exponentials of its Lie algebra. Then comes the global structure: as a manifold $SO^+(1,3) \cong \mathbb{R}^3 \times SO(3)$, which is connected but not compact, and — the deep fact — **not simply connected**, with fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}/2$. That single $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is the topological reason spinors exist: the group has a double cover $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, and the representations living only on the cover are the half-integer-spin fields.

The chapter culminates in the representation theory, and this is where the deepest material is framed. Complexifying the algebra and forming $A_i = \tfrac12(J_i + iK_i)$, $B_i = \tfrac12(J_i - iK_i)$ splits $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_{\mathbb{C}}$ into two commuting copies of the angular-momentum algebra:
$$
\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_{\mathbb{C}} \;\cong\; \mathfrak{su}(2)_{\mathbb{C}} \oplus \mathfrak{su}(2)_{\mathbb{C}}.
$$
Because the representation theory of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ is just the theory of spin, the irreducible representations of the Lorentz group are labelled by a *pair* of spins $(j_A, j_B)$ — and this pair is the complete classification of relativistic fields. The scalar is $(0,0)$, the Weyl spinors are $(\tfrac12,0)$ and $(0,\tfrac12)$, the four-vector is $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, the electromagnetic field strength is $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$. So the modest question "what is the tangent space of the Lorentz group at the identity?" turns out to be the gateway to the entire field content of physics and to Wigner's classification of particles by mass and spin.

The reader is assumed to have worked through the basic theory of [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|Lie groups, Lie algebras, and the exponential map]] — the definitions of [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]], [[Def - Lie Algebra|Lie algebra]], [[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group|the Lie algebra of a Lie group]], and [[Def - Exponential Map of a Lie Group|the exponential map]] — and to know the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]], [[Def - Rapidity|rapidity]], and the [[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group|polar decomposition]] from the earlier special-relativity chapters. The genuinely new ingredient is small: it is the willingness to treat the group as a manifold and to take its complexification seriously. Some familiarity with the representation theory of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ (spin multiplets, Clebsch–Gordan) makes the final section land harder, but it is recalled where needed.

# Concept Map

## §10.1 Lie Group Structure and Topology

- **[[Def - Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations]]**
	- An infinitesimal Lorentz transformation is $\Lambda = \mathrm{Id} + \varepsilon\,\omega + O(\varepsilon^2)$ that satisfies $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\,\Lambda = \eta$ to first order in $\varepsilon$. Substituting and collecting the order-$\varepsilon$ term gives the generator condition $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\,\omega = 0$, equivalently that $\omega_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\alpha}\omega^\alpha{}_\nu$ is antisymmetric — "$\omega$ is antisymmetric once an index is lowered". This is the indefinite-signature version of "infinitesimal rotations are antisymmetric matrices"; the price of the metric is that three of the generators (the boosts) become *symmetric* matrices. The generators form a six-dimensional vector space, the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$.

- **[[Thm - Topology of the Lorentz Group]]**
	- As a manifold $SO^+(1,3) \cong \mathbb{R}^3 \times SO(3)$, the $\mathbb{R}^3$ being the boosts (rapidity vectors) and the $SO(3)$ the rotations. Hence the group is connected but non-compact (the boost factor is unbounded), and its fundamental group is $\pi_1 = \pi_1(\mathbb{R}^3)\times\pi_1(SO(3)) = \{1\}\times\mathbb{Z}/2 = \mathbb{Z}/2$ — it is *not* simply connected. The non-trivial $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is exactly the existence of spinors: the group has a connected double cover $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, and the belt/plate trick is the visual demonstration that a $2\pi$ rotation is a non-contractible loop. The full $O(1,3)$ has four components, each diffeomorphic to $SO^+(1,3)$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Spinors and the Double Cover *(from Spinors / Quantum Field Theory)*
> The fundamental group $\pi_1(SO^+(1,3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2$ is the topological existence theorem for **spinors**. A non-simply-connected group has a universal cover — here $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, mapping $2{:}1$ onto the Lorentz group — and the representations that live only on the cover are the half-integer-spin fields, on which a $2\pi$ rotation acts as $-1$. The electron is the physical embodiment of this $\mathbb{Z}/2$. See [[Special Relativity XI — SL(2,C), Spinors and the Spinor Map]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: No Finite-Dimensional Unitary Representations *(from Quantum Field Theory)*
> Non-compactness forces the only finite-dimensional *unitary* representation of $SO^+(1,3)$ to be trivial. So the finite-dimensional field representations $(j_A,j_B)$ are non-unitary, and the unitary representations that carry quantum states are infinite-dimensional — the structural reason a relativistic quantum theory needs a **field** rather than a finite-component wavefunction.

## §10.2 Generators and the Lie Algebra

- **[[Def - Lie Algebra of the Lorentz Group]]**
	- The Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is the tangent space to $SO^+(1,3)$ at the identity with the matrix commutator as bracket: the $4\times 4$ real matrices $\omega$ with $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\,\omega = 0$, of dimension $6$. A basis is the three boost generators $K_i$ (symmetric, mixing $t$ with $x^i$) and three rotation generators $J_i$ (antisymmetric, acting as $\mathbf{e}_i\times$ on the spatial block). The commutators $[J_i,J_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$, $[J_i,K_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}K_k$, $[K_i,K_j]=-\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$ are the entire algebraic content; the minus sign in the last is the source of the Thomas rotation and of the complex split. The algebra is simple, and isomorphic to $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})$ as a real Lie algebra.

- **[[Def - Generators and Structure Constants]]**
	- The generators $G_a = (K_1,K_2,K_3,J_1,J_2,J_3)$ are a basis of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$; the structure constants $C_{ab}{}^c$ are the coefficients in $[G_a,G_b] = \sum_c C_{ab}{}^c G_c$, defined because the algebra is closed under the bracket. For the Lorentz group every structure constant is $0$, $+1$, or $-1$ (each a $\pm\epsilon_{ijk}$), so the $216$ coefficients are mostly zero. They obey antisymmetry $C_{ab}{}^c = -C_{ba}{}^c$ and a Jacobi constraint, and they determine the entire group law through Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff. The sign pattern $(+,+,-)$ on $([J,J],[J,K],[K,K])$ distinguishes the non-compact $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ from the compact $\mathfrak{so}(4)$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Angular-Momentum Algebra *(from Quantum Mechanics)*
> The rotation block $[J_i,J_j] = \epsilon_{ijk}J_k$ is exactly the **angular-momentum algebra**, which on the quantum side reads $[\hat L_i, \hat L_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}\hat L_k$, and under the Poisson bracket in [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics|Hamiltonian mechanics]] reads $\{L_i, L_j\} = \epsilon_{ijk}L_k$. The rotation generators *are* angular momentum; the boost generators are the new, genuinely relativistic addition. The structure constants $\epsilon_{ijk}$ are the same that govern the cross product on $\mathbb{R}^3$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Four-Rotation of a Local Frame is an so(1,3) Element *(from Accelerated Observers)*
> The antisymmetric **four-rotation** $\Omega$ that governs how an observer's [[Def - Local Frame and Four-Rotation|local frame]] turns along its worldline, $\mathrm{d}e_\alpha/\mathrm{d}\tau = \Omega(e_\alpha)$, is literally an element of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$: decomposing $\Omega = a^i K_i + \omega^i J_i$, the four-acceleration $a^i$ sits in the boost generators and the spatial rotation $\omega^i$ in the rotation generators. So a frame's evolution over a proper-time step is the Lorentz transformation $\exp(\mathrm{d}\tau\,\Omega)$. See [[Special Relativity XVI — Accelerated Observers]].

## §10.3 The Exponential Map and Structure Constants

- **[[Thm - The Exponential Map Generates the Restricted Lorentz Group]]**
	- The matrix exponential restricts to a map $\exp : \mathfrak{so}(1,3) \to SO^+(1,3)$, sending each generator to a restricted Lorentz transformation: $\exp(\psi\,\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{K})$ is the boost of rapidity $\psi$ (built from $\cosh,\sinh$ because $K^2$ is a positive projector), and $\exp(\varphi\,\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{J})$ is the rotation by $\varphi$ (built from $\cos,\sin$ because $J^2$ is negative). The map is *surjective* — special to $SO^+(1,3)$ among non-compact groups, and false for $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ — so the restricted group is exactly the exponentials of its algebra. The composition law $\exp(L_1)\exp(L_2) = \exp(L_1+L_2+\tfrac12[L_1,L_2]+\cdots)$ is Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff, so the structure constants determine the whole group multiplication.

- **[[Thm - The Complexification of so(1,3) and the (A,B) Decomposition]]**
	- Over $\mathbb{C}$, the generators $A_i = \tfrac12(J_i+iK_i)$ and $B_i = \tfrac12(J_i-iK_i)$ satisfy the decoupled relations $[A_i,A_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}A_k$, $[B_i,B_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$, $[A_i,B_j]=0$, so $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_{\mathbb{C}} \cong \mathfrak{su}(2)_{\mathbb{C}}\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)_{\mathbb{C}} = \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\oplus\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})$. The factor $i^2 = -1$ exactly cancels the minus sign in $[K,K]=-\epsilon J$, repairing the split that holds over $\mathbb{R}$ for $\mathfrak{so}(4)$. Irreducible representations are then labelled by a pair $(j_A,j_B)$ of half-integers, of dimension $(2j_A+1)(2j_B+1)$ — the scalar $(0,0)$, Weyl spinors $(\tfrac12,0)/(0,\tfrac12)$, four-vector $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, field strength $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$ — and the physical spin under rotations is $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The (A,B) Classification of All Fields *(from Quantum Field Theory)*
> The pair $(j_A,j_B)$ is the **complete classification of relativistic fields**, and this decomposition is its origin. Building a Lorentz-invariant Lagrangian means combining fields' $(j_A,j_B)$ labels down to the singlet $(0,0)$ by Clebsch–Gordan in each factor independently. The two factors $A, B$ are **left and right chirality**, exchanged by parity — and the weak interaction's coupling to left-handed fermions only is the statement that nature uses one factor and not the other. This is the gateway to the entire **Wigner programme**.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Thomas Rotation from Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff *(from Accelerated Observers)*
> Composing two boosts in different planes, the BCH series produces a term $\tfrac12[K_a,K_b] = -\tfrac12\epsilon_{abc}J_c$, a *rotation* generator manufactured purely by the non-commutativity of boosts. So the product of two non-collinear boosts is a boost times a rotation — the **Thomas rotation** ([[Def - Thomas Rotation]]) — and the $[K,K]=-J$ relation is its exact algebraic origin. This drives Thomas precession of a spinning particle on a curved trajectory; see [[Special Relativity XVI — Accelerated Observers]].

- **[[Ex - The Thomas rotation from the boost-boost commutator]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Use Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff on two boosts of small rapidity in perpendicular planes to show the product is a boost times a rotation, with rotation angle $\approx \tfrac12\psi_1\psi_2$ to leading order, and trace the rotation to the $[K_1,K_2] = -J_3$ structure relation.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of a Lie-theoretic chapter on the Lorentz group chase a recognisable set of goals. The most common is to **compute a Lie algebra or its brackets**: identify which matrices are generators (the condition $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\,\omega = 0$), find the explicit generator matrices, and evaluate commutators to confirm the structure relations. A second target is to **exponentiate a generator** — to recover a finite boost or rotation from its infinitesimal generator, watching the hyperbolic or circular functions emerge from the sign of the generator's square. A third is **structural / topological**: to establish that the group is connected, non-compact, has four components, or has fundamental group $\mathbb{Z}/2$, almost always by the polar decomposition into a contractible boost factor and the rotation group. A fourth is to **change basis to reveal hidden structure** — most importantly the $(A,B)$ decomposition that splits the complexified algebra into two copies of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, and the reading of fields as $(j_A,j_B)$ representations. A fifth, more physical, is to **extract a composition law**: to compose two boosts and find the Thomas rotation, or to show collinear boosts add rapidities, via Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff. Across all five the unifying remark is that you understand the Lorentz group, in this chapter, when you can pass freely between three pictures of it — the matrices, the Lie algebra with its commutators, and the global manifold — and recognise which picture makes a given question trivial.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A transformation is near the identity**, which immediately supplies a generator $\omega$ via $\Lambda = \mathrm{Id} + \varepsilon\,\omega$ and routes the problem into the Lie algebra. **A generator is given by its matrix or its bracket relations**, which supplies the structure constants and unlocks both exponentiation (to the finite group) and Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (to compositions). **The polar decomposition is available** — every restricted transformation is a boost times a rotation — which is the master key to all topological questions, since it presents the group as a product manifold. **A representation is built from angular momenta**, which signals the $(A,B)$ decomposition: two commuting copies of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$, hence a $(j_A,j_B)$ label, hence the full apparatus of spin and Clebsch–Gordan. **The group is connected and has a double cover**, which by covering-space theory pins the fundamental group to $\mathbb{Z}/2$ and produces the spinor representations. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a near-identity transformation routes through the generator condition to the Lie algebra; a generator routes through the exponential to a finite transformation, or through BCH to a composition law; the polar decomposition routes through the product-manifold structure to the topology; a pair of angular momenta routes through the $(A,B)$ split to the field classification. The [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves almost every problem in the chapter is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Linearise the defining equation to find generators.** Substitute $\Lambda = \mathrm{Id} + \varepsilon\,\omega$ into $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\,\Lambda = \eta$ and keep the order-$\varepsilon$ term to obtain $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\,\omega = 0$; see [[Def - Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations]]. *Trigger:* you need to know which matrices are Lorentz generators, or you are given a transformation close to the identity. *Pattern:* "expand to first order, set the linear term to zero, read off the generator condition." The same procedure works for any matrix group from its defining equation — it is $\ker$ of the differential of the constraint at the identity.

2. **Check the generator condition by lowering an index.** A matrix $\omega$ is a generator if and only if $\eta\,\omega$ is antisymmetric, equivalently $\omega_{\mu\nu} = -\omega_{\nu\mu}$; see [[Def - Lie Algebra of the Lorentz Group]]. *Trigger:* you have a candidate matrix and want to know whether it lies in $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$. *Pattern:* multiply by $\eta$ and test antisymmetry — note that the *boosts* are symmetric before lowering, so the test is on $\eta\,\omega$, not $\omega$.

3. **Compute a commutator from the structure relations.** Use $[J_i,J_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$, $[J_i,K_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}K_k$, $[K_i,K_j]=-\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$, extended bilinearly, rather than multiplying $4\times 4$ matrices; see [[Def - Generators and Structure Constants]]. *Trigger:* you need $[L_1,L_2]$ for generators $L_1, L_2$ expressed in the $K,J$ basis. *Pattern:* expand each in the basis, apply the three relations term by term, collect — the $\epsilon$ does the bookkeeping.

4. **Exponentiate a generator to a finite transformation.** Compute $\exp(t\,G)$ by resumming the power series, using $G^2$ to find the closed form: $\exp(\psi K) = \mathrm{Id} + \sinh\psi\,K + (\cosh\psi-1)K^2$, $\exp(\varphi J) = \mathrm{Id} + \sin\varphi\,J + (1-\cos\varphi)J^2$; see [[Thm - The Exponential Map Generates the Restricted Lorentz Group]]. *Trigger:* you have a generator and want the finite boost or rotation. *Pattern:* find $G^2$, recognise whether it is a positive ($\to\cosh,\sinh$) or negative ($\to\cos,\sin$) projector, resum.

5. **Add parameters when generators commute.** If $[L_1,L_2]=0$ then $\exp(L_1)\exp(L_2)=\exp(L_1+L_2)$, so collinear boosts add rapidities and coaxial rotations add angles; see [[Thm - Boosts Compose by Adding Rapidities]]. *Trigger:* composing two boosts in the same plane or two rotations about the same axis. *Pattern:* check the generators commute, then add the parameters — the nonlinear group law collapses to scalar addition.

6. **Apply Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff when generators do not commute.** Use $\exp(L_1)\exp(L_2) = \exp(L_1 + L_2 + \tfrac12[L_1,L_2] + \cdots)$ to compose non-commuting transformations; see [[Thm - The Exponential Map Generates the Restricted Lorentz Group]]. *Trigger:* composing two boosts in different planes, or a boost with a rotation. *Pattern:* the leading correction $\tfrac12[L_1,L_2]$ is a single commutator from the structure constants — for two boosts it is a rotation, the Thomas rotation.

7. **Factor a transformation by the polar decomposition.** Write any $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ as a boost times a rotation, $\Lambda = BR$; see [[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group]]. *Trigger:* any structural or topological question, or any need to separate the "genuinely relativistic" part of a transformation from its rotation. *Pattern:* the factorisation is unique and continuous, presenting the group as the product manifold $\mathbb{R}^3 \times SO(3)$ — the source of all topology.

8. **Read topology off the product manifold.** Use $SO^+(1,3) \cong \mathbb{R}^3 \times SO(3)$ and the product formula $\pi_1(X\times Y) = \pi_1(X)\times\pi_1(Y)$; see [[Thm - Topology of the Lorentz Group]]. *Trigger:* questions about connectedness, compactness, fundamental group, or covers. *Pattern:* the boost factor $\mathbb{R}^3$ is contractible and contributes nothing; *all* the topology is $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2$.

9. **Change basis to $(A,B)$ to decouple the algebra.** Form $A_i = \tfrac12(J_i+iK_i)$, $B_i = \tfrac12(J_i-iK_i)$ to split $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_{\mathbb{C}}$ into two commuting $\mathfrak{su}(2)$'s; see [[Thm - The Complexification of so(1,3) and the (A,B) Decomposition]]. *Trigger:* any representation-theory question, or any field labelled by spin and chirality. *Pattern:* compute in each $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ factor independently, label by $(j_A,j_B)$, recover the rotation spin as $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}$.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating the matrix product of generators as a generator
> It is tempting to think that since $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is a set of matrices, it is closed under matrix multiplication — so that $K_1 K_2$ is again a generator. It is not: $\eta(K_1 K_2)$ is *not* antisymmetric, so $K_1 K_2 \notin \mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ ([[Def - Lie Algebra of the Lorentz Group]]). The algebra is closed only under the *commutator* $[K_1,K_2] = K_1 K_2 - K_2 K_1 = -J_3$, which is in the algebra. The repair condition: the natural operation on a matrix Lie algebra is always the commutator, never the product — the product lands you in the enveloping algebra, not the Lie algebra. This is exactly why a Lie algebra has a *bracket* and not a multiplication.

> [!warning] 2. Splitting the real algebra into two copies of su(2)
> The $(A,B)$ decomposition $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_{\mathbb{C}} = \mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)$ tempts one to write $SO^+(1,3) = SU(2)\times SU(2)$ or $\mathfrak{so}(1,3) = \mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)$ over the reals. Both are false. The real algebra $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is *simple* — it has no proper ideal — so it cannot split over $\mathbb{R}$ at all; the split requires *complexifying*, and the two factors $A, B$ are exchanged by complex conjugation (they are not separately real). The counterexample is that $SU(2)\times SU(2)$ is compact while $SO^+(1,3)$ is not. The correct real statement is $\mathfrak{so}(1,3) \cong \mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})$ as a real Lie algebra (and $\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})$ complexifies to the double). The repair: keep "complexified" in front of the decomposition at all times.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming the exponential of a sum is the product of exponentials
> Writing $\exp(\psi K_1 + \varphi J_3) = \exp(\psi K_1)\exp(\varphi J_3)$ is correct *only when the generators commute*. $K_1$ and $J_3$ do not ($[J_3, K_1] = K_2 \ne 0$), so the equality fails, and the discrepancy is governed by Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff — for two boosts, this discrepancy is precisely the [[Def - Thomas Rotation|Thomas rotation]]. The counterexample is that $\exp(\psi K_1)\exp(\psi K_2)$ (two boosts) is a boost times a rotation, not a pure boost, whereas naive exponentiation of the sum would suggest otherwise. The repair condition: the factorisation $\exp(L_1+L_2) = \exp(L_1)\exp(L_2)$ is legal exactly when $[L_1,L_2] = 0$; otherwise use BCH.

> [!warning] 4. Assuming the exponential map's surjectivity is automatic
> Because $\exp$ is surjective onto $SO(3)$ (every rotation has an axis and angle), one is tempted to assume it is automatically surjective onto $SO^+(1,3)$. Surjectivity onto $SO(3)$ used *compactness*, which the Lorentz group lacks. For the closely related non-compact group $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ the exponential is genuinely *not* surjective. The surjectivity of $\exp$ onto $SO^+(1,3)$ ([[Thm - The Exponential Map Generates the Restricted Lorentz Group]]) is a special, non-automatic fact requiring the polar decomposition plus an eigenvalue argument. The repair: never assume surjectivity for a non-compact group without a dedicated proof; the product-of-two-exponentials form is always safe, the single-exponential form is not.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this chapter are won by deciding, at the outset, *which of the three pictures of the Lorentz group* — the matrices, the Lie algebra, or the global manifold — makes the question trivial, and translating into that picture.

If the problem **asks for a Lie algebra computation** — find the generators, evaluate a commutator, identify a structure constant — work in the algebra and never touch the group. The decisive first move is the generator condition: a matrix $\omega$ lies in $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ exactly when $\eta\,\omega$ is antisymmetric ([[Def - Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations]]), which both tells you the generators and gives the dimension count (six independent antisymmetric $4\times 4$ matrices). For commutators, *do not multiply $4\times 4$ matrices* — expand in the $K,J$ basis and apply the three structure relations $[J,J]=J$, $[J,K]=K$, $[K,K]=-J$ ([[Def - Generators and Structure Constants]]); the antisymmetric symbol $\epsilon_{ijk}$ does all the bookkeeping, and the only place to slip is the sign in $[K_i,J_j] = -[J_j,K_i]$. The single fact worth carrying is the sign pattern $(+,+,-)$: rotations close with a plus, boosts close with a minus, and that minus is where all the interesting structure (Thomas rotation, complex split) comes from.

If the problem **asks to recover a finite transformation** — exponentiate a generator, compose two boosts — move from the algebra to the group via the exponential. The mechanical step is to find $G^2$ and recognise its sign: a boost generator has $K^2$ a *positive* projector, so the series resums to $\cosh,\sinh$; a rotation generator has $J^2$ a *negative* projector, so it resums to $\cos,\sin$ ([[Thm - The Exponential Map Generates the Restricted Lorentz Group]]). For composing transformations, the question is always "do the generators commute?": if yes, the parameters simply add ([[Thm - Boosts Compose by Adding Rapidities]]); if no, the leading correction is the single commutator $\tfrac12[L_1,L_2]$ from Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff, and for two boosts that commutator is a rotation — the Thomas rotation. The reason this works is that the structure constants contain the entire group law, so any composition is computable from the brackets without ever multiplying group elements.

If the problem **is structural or topological** — connectedness, compactness, components, fundamental group, covers — reach immediately for the [[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group|polar decomposition]], which presents the group as the product manifold $\mathbb{R}^3 \times SO(3)$ ([[Thm - Topology of the Lorentz Group]]). The boost factor $\mathbb{R}^3$ is contractible and topologically inert; *all* the interesting topology lives in the rotation factor $SO(3) \cong \mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^3$, whose fundamental group is $\mathbb{Z}/2$. So "is the Lorentz group simply connected?" reduces to "is $SO(3)$ simply connected?" — and the answer, no, with $\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}/2$, is the belt trick. For the *number of components*, count locally constant sign invariants: $\det = \pm 1$ and $\mathrm{sign}\,\Lambda^0{}_0 = \pm 1$ give $2\times 2 = 4$. The general lesson is that the global shape of the Lorentz group is the shape of its rotation subgroup, padded with contractible boosts.

If the problem **is about representations or fields** — what are the irreducible representations, how does a field transform, what is its spin and chirality — change basis to $(A,B)$ and decouple. The combinations $A_i = \tfrac12(J_i+iK_i)$, $B_i = \tfrac12(J_i-iK_i)$ split the complexified algebra into two commuting $\mathfrak{su}(2)$'s ([[Thm - The Complexification of so(1,3) and the (A,B) Decomposition]]), so a representation is a *pair* of spins $(j_A,j_B)$ and the entire machinery of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ — multiplets, raising/lowering operators, Clebsch–Gordan — applies to each factor independently. To find the physical spin under rotations, add the two angular momenta, $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}$; to find the chirality, ask which factor the field is charged under. Building a Lorentz-invariant means combining labels down to $(0,0)$. This is the move that turns the hard-looking representation theory of a non-compact group into two copies of the spin theory every physicist already knows.

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle: **the Lie algebra is the Lorentz group, linearised, and almost every question is easier in the algebra than in the group.** A finite boost is a clumsy hyperbolic matrix; its generator $K$ is a single symmetric matrix with two nonzero entries. The non-commutativity of boosts is invisible in the matrices but is the single bracket $[K,K]=-J$ in the algebra. The existence of spinors is a subtle global fact about the group but is the elementary statement $\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}/2$ about its rotation subgroup. The classification of all fields is an intractable question about the group but is two copies of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ after the $(A,B)$ change of basis. When confused about the Lorentz group, linearise: pass to $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$, where the structure is finite, linear, and transparent.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **The generator condition $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\,\omega = 0$** ([[Def - Infinitesimal Lorentz Transformations]]): the membership test for the Lie algebra, and the most-used single fact in the chapter. It is *free* — it costs one matrix multiplication by $\eta$ followed by an antisymmetry check — and it applies to any candidate matrix. Reach for it whenever you need to know if something is a generator, whenever you need the dimension of the algebra (count the antisymmetric $4\times 4$ matrices: six), and whenever a transformation is given near the identity. Its most powerful disguised use is as the *definition* of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$: the algebra is "the matrices that pass this test", and the boost-symmetric / rotation-antisymmetric split is read directly off it. Recognise its applicability the instant a matrix is proposed as an infinitesimal Lorentz transformation.

- **The three structure relations $[J,J]=J$, $[J,K]=K$, $[K,K]=-J$** ([[Def - Generators and Structure Constants]]): the entire algebraic content of the Lorentz group, packaged as a sign pattern. The reusable skill is to compute *any* commutator by expanding in the $K,J$ basis and applying these three, never multiplying $4\times 4$ matrices. Combine them with the exponential and they give every composition law; combine them with the $(A,B)$ change of basis and they decouple into two copies of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$; combine them with Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff and the $[K,K]=-J$ relation produces the Thomas rotation. The single most reusable consequence is that the minus sign in $[K,K]=-J$ is the fingerprint of non-compactness and the seed of the complex split. Reach for these relations whenever brackets, compositions, or the algebra's structure are in play.

- **The exponential resummation $\exp(\psi K) = \mathrm{Id} + \sinh\psi\,K + (\cosh\psi-1)K^2$** ([[Thm - The Exponential Map Generates the Restricted Lorentz Group]]): the bridge from algebra to group, with the sign of $G^2$ choosing hyperbolic versus circular. Its typical use is to recover a finite boost or rotation from its generator in one step, and its reusable content is the dichotomy: $K^2$ positive gives $\cosh,\sinh$ (boost), $J^2$ negative gives $\cos,\sin$ (rotation). It is also the engine behind surjectivity — every restricted transformation is an exponential — and behind the addition of rapidities when generators commute. Recognise its applicability whenever a generator must be turned into a finite transformation, or whenever you want to see *why* boosts are hyperbolic.

- **The polar decomposition $\Lambda = BR$ and the product manifold $\mathbb{R}^3 \times SO(3)$** ([[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group]], [[Thm - Topology of the Lorentz Group]]): the master key to every structural and topological question. Its recognisable setup is any question about the global shape of the group — connectedness, compactness, components, fundamental group. The reusable move is to present the group as a product, after which the topology is the product of the factors' topologies: the boost factor $\mathbb{R}^3$ is contractible and inert, so *all* the topology is $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2$. Combine it with covering-space theory and it produces the double cover $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ and the existence of spinors. Reach for it the instant a topological or "global structure" question appears.

- **The $(A,B)$ decomposition $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_{\mathbb{C}} = \mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)$** ([[Thm - The Complexification of so(1,3) and the (A,B) Decomposition]]): the organising principle of all Lorentz representations, and the chapter's deepest single tool. Its setup is any representation-theory or field-classification question. The reusable content is that a representation is a *pair* of spins $(j_A,j_B)$, so the fully-solved theory of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ — multiplets, Clebsch–Gordan, raising/lowering — applies twice, independently. Combine it with the requirement of Lorentz invariance and it constructs all interaction terms (combine labels to $(0,0)$); combine it with reality and chirality constraints and it classifies physical fields (Dirac, Weyl, Majorana). The single most reusable fact is the dictionary: scalar $(0,0)$, Weyl $(\tfrac12,0)/(0,\tfrac12)$, vector $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, field strength $(1,0)\oplus(0,1)$. Reach for it whenever spin, chirality, or field content is in question.

# Bridges

1. **Lie theory — the Lorentz group is the worked example of the general machinery.** The vault's [[Differential Geometry XI — Lie Groups, Lie Algebras, and the Exponential Map|Lie-theory chapter]] defines a [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] as a manifold with smooth group operations, its [[Def - The Lie Algebra of a Lie Group|Lie algebra]] as the tangent space at the identity with the commutator bracket, and the [[Def - Exponential Map of a Lie Group|exponential map]] as the time-one flow of the generator's left-invariant vector field. This chapter instantiates every piece of that machinery for $G = SO^+(1,3)$: the abstract tangent space becomes the concrete $\eta$-skew matrices $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$, the abstract bracket becomes the matrix commutator (as [[Ex - The Lie Bracket on a Matrix Lie Algebra is the Commutator|the general theorem]] guarantees for matrix groups), and the abstract exponential becomes the matrix power series. The Lorentz group is the example on which the general theory is most physically motivated, and conversely the general theory is what tells us the construction is canonical — that the bracket and the exponential are not ad hoc but forced.

2. **The rotation subgroup — $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ sits inside $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$, and the cross product is its bracket.** The three rotation generators $J_i$ span a copy of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ (see [[Ex - The Lie Algebra of SO(3) is Antisymmetric Matrices]]), the antisymmetric $3\times 3$ matrices, embedded in $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ as the block-diagonal $\mathrm{diag}(0, \hat{\mathbf{n}})$. The bracket $[J_i,J_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$ is identical to the cross-product structure constants under the hat map $\mathfrak{so}(3)\cong(\mathbb{R}^3,\times)$, so the rotation block of the Lorentz algebra is literally the algebra of angular momentum and of the cross product. What the Lorentz algebra adds is the three boost generators $K_i$ and the way they attach: as a vector under rotations ($[J,K]=K$), with mutual commutators that are rotations ($[K,K]=-J$). The reader who knows $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ already knows half of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$; the genuinely new content is the boosts and their minus sign.

3. **Spinors — the topology $\pi_1 = \mathbb{Z}/2$ is the existence theorem for half-integer spin.** Because $SO^+(1,3)$ is not simply connected ([[Thm - Topology of the Lorentz Group]]), it has a connected double cover, the universal cover, realised as $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ via the [[Def - The Spinor Map and SL(2,C)|spinor map]] of [[Special Relativity XI — SL(2,C), Spinors and the Spinor Map]]. The representations that live only on the cover — the ones that assign *two* matrices $\pm A$ to each Lorentz transformation — are the spinors, on which the non-contractible $2\pi$-rotation loop acts as $-1$. This is the precise mechanism by which the geometry of the group (a $\mathbb{Z}/2$ in its fundamental group) produces the physics of fermions (a sign change under $2\pi$ rotation). The chapter's topology section and the spinor chapter are two halves of one statement: the group's $\mathbb{Z}/2$ and the spinor's $\pm$ are the same $\mathbb{Z}/2$, seen once as a loop and once as a representation.

4. **Quantum field theory — the $(A,B)$ decomposition classifies all fields, and the Poincaré Casimirs classify all particles.** The split $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)_{\mathbb{C}} = \mathfrak{su}(2)\oplus\mathfrak{su}(2)$ ([[Thm - The Complexification of so(1,3) and the (A,B) Decomposition]]) labels every relativistic field by a pair of spins $(j_A,j_B)$, and building a Lorentz-invariant Lagrangian is the combinatorics of reducing tensor products of these labels to the singlet $(0,0)$. Adjoining the four translation generators promotes the Lorentz algebra to the **Poincaré algebra** ([[Def - The Poincaré Group]]), whose two Casimir invariants — the mass $P^2 = m^2$ and the spin (the square of the Pauli–Lubanski vector, $= -m^2 s(s+1)$) — are what Wigner showed label the unitary irreducible representations, i.e. the elementary particles. So this chapter's homogeneous Lorentz algebra is the seed of the full programme: $(A,B)$ classifies the *fields* (finite-dimensional, non-unitary, the objects in a Lagrangian), and the Poincaré Casimirs classify the *particles* (infinite-dimensional unitary, the states in a Hilbert space). The bridge from fields to particles is the field equations, and the whole structure is set up in [[Special Relativity XII — Inertial Observers and the Poincaré Group]].

5. **Accelerated and rotating observers — the four-rotation of a local frame is an $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ element.** An observer carrying an orthonormal [[Def - Local Frame and Four-Rotation|local frame]] along a worldline sees the frame turn according to an antisymmetric four-rotation $\Omega$, with $\mathrm{d}e_\alpha/\mathrm{d}\tau = \Omega(e_\alpha)$. This $\Omega$ is literally a generator in $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$, and decomposing it $\Omega = a^i K_i + \omega^i J_i$ puts the observer's four-acceleration $a^i$ into the boost generators and the spatial rotation rate $\omega^i$ into the rotation generators — so the frame's evolution over a proper-time step $\mathrm{d}\tau$ is the infinitesimal Lorentz transformation $\exp(\mathrm{d}\tau\,\Omega)$. This is the bridge from the abstract Lie algebra to the concrete kinematics of [[Special Relativity XVI — Accelerated Observers]] and [[Special Relativity XVII — Rotating Observers]]: the Fermi–Walker transport law, the Thomas precession, and the desynchronisation of clocks on a rotating disk are all statements about a one-parameter family of Lorentz transformations $\exp(\tau\,\Omega(\tau))$ generated by an $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$-valued four-rotation, and the Thomas precession in particular is the same $[K,K]=-J$ rotation that this chapter extracts from the boost-boost commutator.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: linearise, and the Lorentz group becomes finite, linear data.** The single idea organising the chapter is that a Lie group is most transparent at its identity, where it degenerates to a vector space with a bracket — its Lie algebra. Every difficult global or nonlinear feature of the Lorentz group has an easy infinitesimal counterpart. The clumsy hyperbolic matrices of a boost collapse to a single symmetric generator $K$. The invisible non-commutativity of two boosts becomes the one-line bracket $[K,K]=-J$. The subtle topological fact that spinors exist becomes the elementary $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}/2$. The intractable classification of all relativistic fields becomes two copies of the undergraduate theory of spin, after one complex change of basis. The lesson, transferable to every Lie group, is: when a question about the group is hard, ask the corresponding question about the algebra, where the structure is linear and finite-dimensional. The exponential map and Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff guarantee that the answer in the algebra controls the answer in the group, at least near the identity — and for $SO^+(1,3)$, where the exponential is surjective, the control is global.

**The true name of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ is "rotations plus boosts, with $[K,K]=-J$".** The nine commutators look like a lot to remember, but they compress to one sentence: the rotation generators close among themselves ($[J,J]=J$), the boost generators transform as a vector under rotations ($[J,K]=K$), and two boosts bracket to *minus* a rotation ($[K,K]=-J$). Every structural fact in the chapter is a reading of these. The boosts fail to close (no boost subgroup) because $[K,K]$ is a rotation, not a boost. The Thomas rotation appears in composing boosts because $[K,K]$ is a rotation. The algebra splits over $\mathbb{C}$ because the minus sign in $[K,K]=-J$ is $i^2$, repairable by the factor of $i$ in $A = \tfrac12(J+iK)$. The algebra is non-compact, distinguished from the compact $\mathfrak{so}(4)$, precisely by that minus sign. When you need the Lorentz algebra, do not memorise a table — remember "boosts almost-close, into minus a rotation", and reconstruct the rest from the subalgebra-and-vector structure.

**The minus sign is the whole of relativity, again.** A recurring theme of the special-relativity chapters is that the single sign difference between the Euclidean metric $\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1,1)$ and the Minkowski metric $\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ is the source of every relativistic effect. At the level of the Lie algebra this reappears sharply. The Euclidean rotation algebra $\mathfrak{so}(4)$ has $[K,K]=+J$ and splits into two copies of $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ *over the reals* — four-dimensional rotations factor into two independent three-dimensional ones, and this is a real, compact, simply-decoupled structure. Flip the one sign and you get $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ with $[K,K]=-J$, which is *simple* over the reals and splits only after complexifying, with the two factors conjugate rather than independent. That single flipped sign is what makes the Lorentz group non-compact rather than compact, what makes its finite-dimensional representations non-unitary, what makes left and right chirality complex-conjugate rather than independent, and ultimately what makes spinors and antiparticles a feature of relativistic physics. The chapter is, at bottom, the study of how one minus sign in a $4\times 4$ matrix propagates into the structure of all of matter.

**Representation theory is the deep content, and it was hiding in the brackets all along.** It is tempting to view the Lie algebra as bookkeeping — a tidy way to organise boosts and rotations. The deeper truth, and the reason this chapter frames the Lorentz group through representation theory, is that the bracket structure *is* the classification of fields. The question "what kinds of objects can transform consistently under the Lorentz group?" is the question "what are the irreducible representations of $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$?", and the $(A,B)$ decomposition answers it completely: a relativistic object is a pair of spins $(j_A,j_B)$. Scalars, the four kinds of spinor, vectors, the electromagnetic field, the graviton — every field in physics is a point in this two-dimensional lattice of half-integers, and every Lorentz-invariant law is a recipe for combining these labels to the singlet. The commutators $[K,K]=-J$ and the factor of $i$ that decouples them are not technical lemmas; they are the statement that the symmetry group of spacetime is, in disguise, two copies of the rotation group, and that is why "spin" is the universal label of matter. The chapter's modest starting question about a tangent space ends at the organising principle of quantum field theory.
