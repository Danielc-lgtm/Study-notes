# Problem Statement

Wigner's classification reduces the representation theory of the Poincaré group to the representation theory of the **little group** — the subgroup of Lorentz transformations fixing a standard momentum. Determine the little group and the representation content in the two physical cases. Working with $c = 1$, $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$:

1. **Massive case.** Take the standard momentum $P = (m, 0, 0, 0)$, $m > 0$. Identify the subgroup of $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ fixing $P$ as the rotation group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, and conclude that the irreducible representations are labelled by spin $s \in \{0, \tfrac{1}{2}, 1, \ldots\}$, each of dimension $2s+1$.
2. **Massless case.** Take the standard momentum $P = (E, 0, 0, E)$, $E > 0$ (so $P^2 = 0$). Identify the little group as $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$, the Euclidean group of the plane, generated by one rotation $J_3$ and two "translations" $T_1, T_2$.
3. Show that for $P$ null, $W^2 = 0$ and $W^\mu = h P^\mu$ for a scalar **helicity** $h$; argue that demanding finite-dimensionality of the physical representation forces the two $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ "translations" to act trivially, leaving the single discrete label $h = \pm s$.
4. Conclude that a massive spin-$s$ particle has $2s+1$ polarisations while a massless particle of spin $s \ge \tfrac{1}{2}$ has exactly $2$ (helicities $\pm s$); apply to the photon ($s = 1 \Rightarrow h = \pm 1$, two polarisations) and the massive $W$ boson ($s = 1 \Rightarrow$ three polarisations).

**Recall:**

![[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group#The Definition]]

The **little group** of a momentum $P$ is the stabiliser $\{\Lambda \in \mathrm{SO}^+(1,3) : \Lambda P = P\}$. Wigner's induction theorem: an irreducible unitary representation of the [[Def - The Poincaré Group|Poincaré group]] is determined by an irreducible representation of the little group of any one standard momentum. The four-momentum $P^\mu$ has $P^2 = m^2$ (massive, timelike) or $P^2 = 0$ (massless, null); see [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass]]. The spin Casimir is $W^2 = -m^2 s(s+1)$ (massive).

# Convergent Strategy

**Problem class.** A *classify-a-representation* problem: find the little group of a standard momentum and read off the multiplet structure. The [[Special Relativity XII — Inertial Observers and the Poincaré Group#Problem-Solving Strategy|topic strategy]] for representation problems says exactly this — fix a standard momentum, find its stabiliser, decompose.

**Assumption pattern.** The decisive datum is the *type* of the standard momentum: timelike (massive) gives the compact little group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, null (massless) gives the non-compact $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$. The signpost is "massive or massless?": the causal character of $P$ dictates the little group, hence the polarisation count.

**Theorem routing.** The route is Wigner's induction: (i) for $P = (m, \mathbf{0})$, the transformations fixing it are exactly spatial rotations $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, whose irreducibles are spin-$s$ multiplets of dimension $2s+1$; (ii) for null $P$, the stabiliser is $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$; (iii) $W = hP$ from $W^2 = 0$, $W\cdot P = 0$, $P^2 = 0$, and finite-dimensionality kills the $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ translations, leaving helicity $\pm s$; (iv) count polarisations.

**Key decision point.** The crux is that the little group's *compactness* decides everything. The massive little group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is compact, so its finite-dimensional unitary representations are the familiar spin multiplets ($2s+1$ states). The massless little group $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ is non-compact, and its faithful unitary representations are infinite-dimensional (continuous spin); demanding finitely many states forces the non-compact "translation" generators to act trivially, collapsing to a single helicity. Recognising that "massless has fewer polarisations" is a statement about the *non-compactness* of $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ is the whole insight.

# Legal Operations Used

1. **Classify a representation via its little group** (operation 9 from the topic page). Both standard momenta are stabilised, the little groups identified, and the multiplets read off.

2. **Forgetting $W\cdot P = 0$ is fatal** (illegal operation 4 of the topic page): the massless analysis *uses* $W\cdot P = 0$ together with $W^2 = 0$ and $P^2 = 0$ to force $W = hP$, so the orthogonality is load-bearing here.

# Hints

> [!note]- Hint 1
> A Lorentz transformation fixing $P = (m, 0, 0, 0)$ must leave the time direction invariant. The transformations of $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ fixing the timelike vector $e_0$ are exactly the rotations of the orthogonal spatial $\mathbb{R}^3$ — the group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$.

> [!note]- Hint 2
> $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is compact; its irreducible unitary representations are the spin-$s$ representations, $s = 0, \tfrac12, 1, \ldots$, of dimension $2s+1$ (the angular-momentum multiplets). By Wigner's induction these label the massive Poincaré representations.

> [!note]- Hint 3
> For null $P = (E, 0, 0, E)$, the stabiliser is generated by $J_3$ (rotation about the $z$-axis, the direction of motion) and two combinations $T_1 = K_1 + J_2$, $T_2 = K_2 - J_1$ that fix $P$. These satisfy $[T_1, T_2] = 0$ and $[J_3, T_a] = \epsilon_{ab}T_b$ — exactly the algebra of $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$, the Euclidean group of the plane (one rotation, two translations).

> [!note]- Hint 4
> $W$ is orthogonal to $P$ and (for null $P$) $W^2 = 0$. A null vector orthogonal to a null vector must be proportional to it: $W^\mu = hP^\mu$. The scalar $h$ is the helicity. The $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ "translations" $T_1, T_2$ would, if acting non-trivially, give a continuous label (continuous spin); demanding finitely many states sets them to zero, leaving $h$ as the only label, quantised to $\pm s$.

# Solution

The little group decides the polarisation count. Step 1 finds $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ for the massive case, giving $2s+1$ states. Step 2 finds $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ for the massless case. Step 3 reduces the massless content to a single helicity $\pm s$. Step 4 contrasts the polarisation counts and applies to the photon and the $W$ boson.

**Step 1: Massive little group is $\mathrm{SO}(3)$; spin-$s$ multiplets.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Take the standard massive momentum $P = (m, 0, 0, 0)$, the rest-frame four-momentum. A Lorentz transformation $\Lambda \in \mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ in the little group must satisfy $\Lambda P = P$, i.e. it must fix the timelike vector $m\,e_0$, hence fix the time direction $e_0$. A proper orthochronous Lorentz transformation fixing $e_0$ acts trivially on the time axis and must preserve the orthogonal complement $e_0^\perp = \mathrm{span}(e_1, e_2, e_3)$, the spatial $\mathbb{R}^3$, as an isometry of its (negative-definite, hence after a sign Euclidean) metric. The orientation-preserving isometries of $\mathbb{R}^3$ fixing the origin are exactly the rotations:
> $$\text{little group of } (m, \mathbf{0}) = \mathrm{SO}(3).$$
> $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is **compact**, and its irreducible unitary representations are the familiar **spin-$s$** representations, labelled by $s \in \{0, \tfrac{1}{2}, 1, \tfrac{3}{2}, \ldots\}$ (half-integers included, via the double cover $\mathrm{SU}(2)$), each of dimension $2s+1$ — the states $s_z = -s, -s+1, \ldots, +s$. By Wigner's induction theorem, these label the massive irreducible representations of the Poincaré group: a massive particle of mass $m$ and spin $s$ is the representation induced from the spin-$s$ representation of its rest-frame rotation group, with $W^2 = -m^2 s(s+1)$.

**Step 2: Massless little group is $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> For a massless particle there is no rest frame ($P$ is null, $P^2 = 0$). Take the standard null momentum $P = (E, 0, 0, E)$ — a particle moving in the $+z$ direction. The little group is $\{\Lambda \in \mathrm{SO}^+(1,3) : \Lambda P = P\}$. Three generators fix $P$:
> - $J_3$, rotation about the $z$-axis (the direction of motion): rotating about the momentum direction leaves $P$ unchanged.
> - $T_1 = K_1 + J_2$ and $T_2 = K_2 - J_1$, two specific boost–rotation combinations. One checks directly that each annihilates $P$: $T_1 P = (K_1 + J_2)(E, 0, 0, E) = 0$, and similarly $T_2 P = 0$ (the boost and rotation pieces cancel on the null vector).
>
> Their commutators are
> $$[T_1, T_2] = 0, \qquad [J_3, T_1] = T_2, \qquad [J_3, T_2] = -T_1.$$
> This is precisely the Lie algebra of $\mathrm{ISO}(2) = \mathbb{R}^2 \rtimes \mathrm{SO}(2)$, the **Euclidean group of the plane**: $J_3$ is the rotation of the plane, and $T_1, T_2$ are the two commuting translations on which it acts. So
> $$\text{little group of } (E, 0, 0, E) = \mathrm{ISO}(2).$$
> Unlike $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, this group is **non-compact** (the translations $T_1, T_2$ generate non-compact directions), which is the source of the qualitative difference in the massless case.

**Step 3: $W = hP$ and the collapse to helicity.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> For the null standard momentum, the Pauli–Lubanski vector satisfies three constraints: $W\cdot P = 0$ (always, [[Ex - Computing the Pauli-Lubanski vector]]), $W^2 = 0$ (since $W^2$ is a scalar and the massless representations have vanishing spin Casimir for the physical, finite-dimensional case), and $P^2 = 0$. A null vector $W$ orthogonal to a null vector $P$, in Lorentzian signature, must be **proportional** to it (the only vectors orthogonal to a null vector $P$ and themselves null are multiples of $P$). Hence
> $$W^\mu = h\,P^\mu$$
> for a scalar $h$, the **helicity** — the component of angular momentum along the direction of motion. (Indeed $h = \boldsymbol{J}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}$, the projection of spin onto momentum.)
>
> Why is $h$ the only label, and why discrete? The $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ little group has two "translation" generators $T_1, T_2$. If they acted non-trivially on the physical states, their eigenvalues would form a *continuous* parameter (since they generate non-compact directions), giving a particle with a continuous internal degree of freedom — the "continuous-spin" or "infinite-spin" representations, with infinitely many polarisations. No such particle is observed, and demanding a *finite-dimensional* (in fact, finite-polarisation) physical representation forces $T_1, T_2$ to act *trivially* (as zero) on the states. Then only $J_3$ acts non-trivially, and being the generator of the compact $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ rotation about the momentum, its eigenvalue — the helicity $h$ — is quantised. For a particle of spin $s$, the allowed helicities are $h = \pm s$ (the two extreme projections; a massless particle has *only* these, not the intermediate values). So the massless representation is labelled by the single discrete helicity $\pm s$.

**Step 4: Polarisation counts — photon versus $W$ boson.**

> [!note]- Derivation
> Assembling the two cases:
> - **Massive spin $s$:** little group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, representation of dimension $2s+1$ — so $2s+1$ polarisation states.
> - **Massless spin $s \ge \tfrac{1}{2}$:** little group $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ with translations acting trivially, representation labelled by helicity $\pm s$ — so exactly $2$ polarisation states.
>
> *Photon* ($s = 1$, massless): helicities $h = \pm 1$, giving **two** polarisation states (left- and right-circular polarisation). It does *not* have a third, longitudinal polarisation — the would-be $s_z = 0$ state is absent, which in field language is the statement that the longitudinal photon is pure gauge. This is *why* light has two polarisations, not three.
>
> *Massive $W$ boson* ($s = 1$, massive): little group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, dimension $2(1)+1 = 3$ — **three** polarisation states ($s_z = -1, 0, +1$). The massive vector boson *does* have the longitudinal polarisation that the photon lacks. This is the sharp, observable difference between a massive and a massless spin-1 particle, and it is dictated entirely by the change of little group from $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ to $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$.
>
> (The graviton, $s = 2$ massless, has helicities $\pm 2$, two polarisations; a massive spin-2 particle would have $2(2)+1 = 5$. The discontinuity between these counts as $m \to 0$ is the van Dam–Veltman–Zakharov discontinuity.)

> [!note]- Complete formal solution
> *Massive:* the little group of $P = (m, \mathbf{0})$ is the set of $\mathrm{SO}^+(1,3)$ elements fixing $e_0$, namely the spatial rotations $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ — compact, with irreducible unitary representations the spin-$s$ multiplets ($s = 0, \tfrac12, 1, \ldots$) of dimension $2s+1$. *Massless:* the little group of null $P = (E, 0, 0, E)$ is generated by $J_3$ and $T_1 = K_1 + J_2$, $T_2 = K_2 - J_1$ with $[T_1, T_2] = 0$, $[J_3, T_a] = \epsilon_{ab}T_b$ — the algebra of $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$, non-compact. For null $P$, $W\cdot P = 0$, $W^2 = 0$, $P^2 = 0$ force $W = hP$ (a null vector orthogonal to a null vector is parallel to it), with $h$ the helicity; finite-dimensionality forces the non-compact $T_1, T_2$ to act trivially, leaving the single discrete label $h = \pm s$. Hence a massive spin-$s$ particle has $2s+1$ polarisations, a massless one exactly $2$: the photon ($s=1$) has $h = \pm 1$, two polarisations, while the massive $W$ boson ($s=1$) has three ($s_z = -1, 0, +1$). The difference is the change of little group $\mathrm{SO}(3) \to \mathrm{ISO}(2)$. $\blacksquare$

# Key Takeaways

**The little group's compactness decides the polarisation count: compact $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ gives $2s+1$, non-compact $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ gives $2$.** The entire massive-versus-massless distinction is a statement about the stabiliser of the standard momentum. A massive particle has a rest frame, its little group is the compact rotation group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, and compact groups have finite-dimensional unitary representations — the spin-$s$ multiplets with $2s+1$ states. A massless particle has no rest frame, its little group is the non-compact $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$, and the non-compact directions (the "translations" $T_1, T_2$) would give continuous, infinite-dimensional representations unless forced to act trivially — leaving just the single helicity. The transferable insight: to find the states of a particle, find the little group of its momentum and ask whether it is compact (finite multiplet) or non-compact (helicity, after killing the non-compact part). This reduces an intractable infinite-dimensional problem to the finite representation theory of a three-dimensional group.

**A null vector orthogonal to a null vector is parallel to it — this geometric fact is what produces helicity.** The reduction $W = hP$ in the massless case rests on a simple Lorentzian-geometry fact: in signature $(1,3)$, if $W$ is null and orthogonal to the null vector $P$, then $W \parallel P$. (The orthogonal complement of a null vector contains the null vector itself, and the only null directions in it are multiples of $P$.) This collapses the spin information of a massless particle to a single scalar, the helicity $h = \boldsymbol{J}\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{P}}$, the projection of angular momentum onto the direction of motion. The diagnostic to carry: whenever you have a four-vector built to be orthogonal to a null momentum and itself null, conclude it is proportional to the momentum — this is the structural origin of helicity, and it is why massless particles are labelled by a single number rather than a multiplet. The orthogonality $W\cdot P = 0$ is load-bearing; dropping it loses the argument.

**The photon's two polarisations and the $W$ boson's three are the same physics — the change of little group as mass turns on.** The most memorable payoff is the sharp, observable contrast: a massless spin-1 particle (photon) has two polarisations, a massive one ($W$, $Z$) has three, and the difference is *entirely* the change of little group from $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ to $\mathrm{SO}(3)$. The missing third state of the photon is the longitudinal polarisation, which exists for the massive boson but is "pure gauge" for the photon — and in the Standard Model this is exactly the polarisation a massless gauge boson *acquires* when it eats a Goldstone boson via the Higgs mechanism, becoming massive. So the polarisation count is not a detail but a window onto mass generation. The reusable principle: whenever you compare a massive and a massless particle of the same spin, expect the massive one to have *more* polarisations ($2s+1$ versus $2$), and recognise the extra states as the longitudinal modes that the little group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ admits but $\mathrm{ISO}(2)$ forbids. This single observation organises the polarisation content of every gauge theory and every massive field.
