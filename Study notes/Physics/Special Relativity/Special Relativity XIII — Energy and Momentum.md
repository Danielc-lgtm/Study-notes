# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we set $c = 1$**, and we use the **mostly-minus** signature $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)$, so a timelike vector has $X \cdot X > 0$. With $c = 1$ a velocity is a pure number with $|\mathbf{v}| < 1$, energy and mass and momentum all share one unit, and a four-momentum has $P \cdot P = m^2$ rather than the dimension-laden $-m^2c^2$. To restore $c$, reinstate it by dimensional analysis: every energy $E$ becomes $E$, every momentum $\mathbf{p}$ becomes $\mathbf{p}c$, the mass term $m^2$ becomes $m^2c^4$, and the famous identity reads $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2c^2 + m^2c^4$. Where the contrast with the Newtonian limit is the point, $c$ is restored explicitly and flagged.

> [!warning] Convention
> Gourgoulhon, the rigour source for this chapter, works in the opposite **mostly-plus** signature $g = \operatorname{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$, in which the four-momentum is fundamentally a *linear form* $\boldsymbol{p}$ and the mass is $\vec{p}\cdot\vec{p} = -m^2c^2$ (timelike means $\vec p\cdot\vec p \le 0$), and the energy is $E = -c\,\langle\boldsymbol{p}, \vec{u}_0\rangle$. Every formula transcribed here flips the overall sign of the metric: his $\vec p\cdot\vec p = -m^2c^2$ becomes our $P\cdot P = +m^2$, his $E = -c\langle\boldsymbol p,\vec u_0\rangle$ becomes our $E = P\cdot U_0$, and his Mandelstam $s = -(\vec p_1+\vec p_2)^2$ becomes our $s = (P_1+P_2)^2$. We treat the four-momentum throughout as the *vector* $P^\mu$, which is the standard physics convention (and Tong's).

- $\mu, \nu, \rho, \sigma \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ — spacetime indices; $i, j, k \in \{1,2,3\}$ — purely spatial indices
- $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)$ — the Minkowski metric; $A_\mu = \eta_{\mu\nu}A^\nu = (A^0, -\mathbf{A})$ is $A$ with its index lowered
- $A \cdot B = \eta_{\mu\nu}A^\mu B^\nu = A^0 B^0 - \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ — the Minkowski inner product
- $X, U, P, F, A$ — four-vectors (capital letters, no arrows); $\mathbf{v}, \mathbf{p}, \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{f}$ — spatial three-vectors (bold)
- $\tau$ — **proper time**, the invariant clock-reading along a worldline; $U^\mu = dX^\mu/d\tau = \gamma(1,\mathbf{u})$ — the **four-velocity**, normalised $U\cdot U = 1$
- $\mathbf{u} = d\mathbf{x}/dt$ — the ordinary three-velocity in a frame, $u = |\mathbf{u}|$; $\gamma = (1-u^2)^{-1/2}$ — the **Lorentz factor**, with $dt/d\tau = \gamma$
- $m$ — the **rest mass** (invariant mass), a Lorentz scalar; the modern meaning of "mass"
- $P^\mu = m U^\mu = (E, \mathbf{p})$ — the **four-momentum**; satisfies $P\cdot P = m^2$
- $E = \gamma m$ — the **total energy**; $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{u}$ — the **relativistic three-momentum**; $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 + m^2$ — the **dispersion relation**
- $E_{\text{kin}} = (\gamma - 1)m$ — the **kinetic energy** relative to an observer
- $U_0$ — the four-velocity of an **observer** $\mathcal{O}$; for a particle of four-momentum $P$, $E = P\cdot U_0$ and $\mathbf{p} = $ the orthogonal projection of $P$ onto $\mathcal{O}$'s rest space
- $\hbar$ — the reduced Planck constant; for a photon $E = \hbar\omega$, $\mathbf{p} = \hbar\mathbf{k}$, $P^\mu = \hbar K^\mu$ with $K^\mu$ the wave four-vector
- $F^\mu = dP^\mu/d\tau$ — the **four-force**; satisfies $F\cdot U = dm/d\tau$, so a mass-preserving (**pure**) force has $F\cdot U = 0$
- $s, t, u$ — the **Mandelstam variables** of a two-body process; $s = (P_1+P_2)^2$ is the invariant mass squared of the system
- $\mathcal{P}_1, \mathcal{P}_2, \ldots$ — particles; primes denote post-collision states; $\Sigma$ — a spacelike hypersurface (a "slice of now")

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one equation: $P = mU$. Take the rest mass of a particle — a single Lorentz-invariant number — and multiply it by the [[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration|four-velocity]], the four-vector that the previous chapters built out of proper time. The result is the **four-momentum**, and it is the object on which all of relativistic dynamics turns. Its time component is the energy, its spatial components the relativistic momentum, its Minkowski length the rest mass, and its conservation is the master law from which every collision, decay, and creation of particles is computed.

The reason this single object carries so much is that it dissolves distinctions Newtonian mechanics kept separate. Newton had energy and he had momentum, and they were different things obeying different conservation laws; he had mass, conserved by a third, independent law. Special relativity fuses all three. The energy and the momentum become the time and space parts of one four-vector, so that "conservation of energy" and "conservation of momentum" are the time and space components of the *single* statement that $P^\mu$ is conserved. And mass is no longer separately conserved at all: it is merely the length of that four-vector, and length is not additive, so mass can be converted into kinetic energy and back. This is the content of

$$P^\mu = (E, \mathbf{p}), \qquad P\cdot P = E^2 - \mathbf{p}^2 = m^2,$$

read three ways: $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 + m^2$ is the energy–momentum relation; $E = m$ at rest is $E = mc^2$; and $P\cdot P = m^2$ is the invariant that every calculation in the chapter exploits. The whole subject is the working-out of consequences of these readings.

There is one technique that runs through everything, and it is worth naming at the outset because once you have it the chapter has almost no further surprises. It is the **invariant-mass technique**: whenever a particle appears in a problem whose energy and direction you do not actually want to know, isolate its four-momentum on one side of the conservation equation and take the Minkowski square of both sides. Squaring converts the unwanted four-momentum into the *known scalar* $m^2$ (or $0$ for a photon), eliminating it from the problem, while the inner products on the other side can be evaluated in whichever frame is most convenient. This one move — rearrange to $P = \ldots$, then square — solves Compton scattering, particle decay, scattering kinematics, and every reaction threshold. It is the relativistic analogue of completing the square, and it works because the square of a four-vector is frame-independent: it is the same number to every observer, so you may compute it where the algebra is easiest and trust the answer everywhere.

The chapter then divides into three movements. Section §13.1 builds the four-momentum of a single particle and reads off its mass and energy, the energy and momentum a given observer measures (these are *relative* to the observer, like velocity), the special case of the photon (massless, null four-momentum, no rest frame), and the equivalence of mass and energy. Section §13.2 states the one fundamental principle — conservation of four-momentum for an isolated system — and unleashes it on collisions: elastic scattering and the Compton effect, the inverse Compton effect that powers high-energy astrophysics, and inelastic collisions with their reaction thresholds. Section §13.3 returns to non-isolated particles, defines the four-force as the proper-time derivative of four-momentum, recovers Newton's second law in Lorentz-covariant form, and shows how energy evolves under a force.

The reader is assumed to have worked through [[Special Relativity V — Worldlines, Proper Time and Four-Velocity|four-velocity and proper time]] thoroughly — the four-momentum is built directly on them — and to be comfortable with the indefinite [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|Minkowski metric]] and the [[Def - Classification of Four-Vectors|timelike/spacelike/null trichotomy]]. The genuinely new physics is small and concentrated in two facts: $E = mc^2$, and the replacement of three Newtonian conservation laws by the single conservation of the four-momentum vector. Everything else is the disciplined transfer of mechanics into Minkowski space, with the invariant-mass technique as the recurring computational engine.

# Concept Map

## §13.1 Four-Momentum, Mass and Energy

- **[[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass]]**
	- The **four-momentum** of a particle is $P^\mu = m U^\mu = (E, \mathbf{p})$, the rest mass times the [[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration|four-velocity]]. The **rest mass** $m$ is a Lorentz invariant — the mass in the particle's own rest frame — and is the modern meaning of "mass" (the older "relativistic mass" $\gamma m$ is just the energy $E$). The time component is the energy $E = \gamma m$, the spatial part the relativistic momentum $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{u}$. The four-velocity normalisation $U\cdot U = 1$ becomes the **mass-shell relation** $P\cdot P = m^2$, equivalently $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 + m^2$. As $u\to c$ both $E$ and $|\mathbf{p}|$ diverge, which is why no massive particle reaches the speed of light. For a single isolated particle, conservation of four-momentum forces the worldline straight, the mass constant, and the motion uniform — the law of inertia.

- **[[Thm - Energy and Momentum Relative to an Observer]]**
	- An observer $\mathcal{O}$ with four-velocity $U_0$ measures the energy of a particle of four-momentum $P$ as the invariant contraction $E = P\cdot U_0$, and measures its three-momentum as the orthogonal projection $\mathbf{p}$ of $P$ onto $\mathcal{O}$'s [[Def - Observer and Local Rest Space|local rest space]] ($U_0^\perp$). This is the orthogonal decomposition $P = E\,U_0 + \mathbf{p}$ with $\mathbf{p}\cdot U_0 = 0$ and $\mathbf{p}$ spacelike, and taking its Minkowski square reproduces the Einstein relation $E^2 = m^2 + \mathbf{p}^2$. Energy and momentum are *relative* to the observer — different observers measure different values for the same particle, exactly as they measure different velocities — while the rest mass $m$, the length of $P$, is absolute.

- **[[Thm - Mass-Energy Equivalence]]**
	- Expanding $E = \gamma m c^2 = mc^2 + \tfrac12 m u^2 + \cdots$ shows the energy of a particle is its **rest energy** $mc^2$ plus the Newtonian kinetic energy plus relativistic corrections; the kinetic energy is $E_{\text{kin}} = (\gamma-1)mc^2$, *not* $\tfrac12 mu^2$. A particle at rest still has energy $E = mc^2$: mass *is* a form of energy. The invariant relation $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2c^2 + m^2c^4$ follows cleanly by evaluating $P\cdot P$ in the rest frame. The practical consequence is that the rest mass of a composite system is *not* the sum of its parts' masses — binding energy lowers it, internal kinetic energy raises it — and mass can be converted into kinetic energy and back, which is what makes nuclear energy and particle physics possible.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group *(from Representation Theory)*
> The mass-shell relation $P\cdot P = m^2$ is the first **Casimir invariant** of the Poincaré group — the quantity that commutes with every boost, rotation, and translation and so labels an entire irreducible representation. In the Wigner classification a *particle* is precisely an irreducible unitary representation of the Poincaré group, labelled by two invariants: this mass $m^2$, and a spin (the second Casimir, built from the **Pauli–Lubanski vector**). This is the deep reason rest mass is frame-independent — it is a Casimir — and it is developed in [[Special Relativity XII — Inertial Observers and the Poincaré Group|Special Relativity XII]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: The de Broglie Relation and Quantum Fields *(from Quantum Field Theory)*
> Promoting the four-momentum to the operator $P_\mu \to i\hbar\partial_\mu$ — the covariant completion of $E\to i\hbar\partial_t$, $\mathbf{p}\to -i\hbar\nabla$ — turns the algebraic mass-shell relation $P\cdot P = m^2$ into the **Klein–Gordon equation** $(\Box + m^2)\phi = 0$, whose plane-wave solutions $e^{-iK\cdot X}$ carry the dispersion relation $K\cdot K = m^2$. The bridge is de Broglie's $P^\mu = \hbar K^\mu$. Because $E = mc^2$ makes particle number unstable (concentrate enough energy and pairs are created), the single-particle wavefunction must give way to an operator-valued **quantum field** — the birth of quantum field theory.

- **[[Ex - The invariant mass of a system of particles]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show the invariant mass $M^2 = (\sum P_i)\cdot(\sum P_i)$ of a system's total four-momentum is Lorentz invariant, equals the total energy in the centre-of-momentum frame, and exceeds $\sum m_i$ whenever the parts move relative to one another; apply it to two photons and to two massive particles.

- **[[Ex - Rest energy, kinetic energy and the Newtonian limit]]** (⭐)
	- Verify $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 + m^2$ by squaring $P$ in the rest frame, expand $E = \gamma m$ to recover $mc^2 + \tfrac12 mu^2$, and compute the Lorentz factor and kinetic energy of an LHC proton ($E = 7$ TeV) and an ultra-high-energy cosmic ray ($E = 10^{20}$ eV).

- **[[Ex - Energy and momentum of a system and the comoving observer]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show that the energy and momentum of a system are additive, $E = \sum E_a$ and $\mathbf{p} = \sum\mathbf{p}_a$, that a comoving (centre-of-momentum) observer measures zero total momentum and energy equal to the system mass, and that for an isolated system measured by an inertial observer both are constant.

## §13.2 Conservation and Collisions

- **[[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum]]**
	- In any process with no external forces, the **total four-momentum is conserved**: $\sum_{\text{in}} P_i^\mu = \sum_{\text{out}} P_j^\mu$. Stated frame-independently, the total four-momentum of an isolated system on any closed hypersurface vanishes, equivalently it takes the same value on any two spacelike slices — "nothing leaves the slice but what entered it". This single four-vector equation comprises conservation of energy (the $\mu=0$ component) and conservation of three-momentum (the spatial components), and it holds in every inertial frame because both sides transform identically. It *replaces* the separate Newtonian laws of mass and energy conservation, and it is the master tool of every collision and decay problem.

- **[[Def - The Four-Momentum of a Photon]]**
	- A **photon** is a massless particle, $m = 0$, on a null worldline at speed $c$ in every frame; proper time does not advance along it, so it has no four-velocity. It still has a four-momentum, the **null** four-vector $P^\mu = (E, \mathbf{p})$ with $P\cdot P = 0$, equivalently $E = |\mathbf{p}|$. Relative to an observer $U_0$ with photon direction $\mathbf{n}$, $P = E(U_0 + \mathbf{n})$. Quantum mechanics supplies the magnitude: $E = \hbar\omega$ and $P^\mu = \hbar K^\mu$, tying the photon to the wave four-vector. A photon enters [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|conservation of four-momentum]] on the same footing as a massive particle, which is what makes pair production, Compton scattering, and the Doppler effect computable.

- **[[Def - Massless Particles]]**
	- A particle is **massless** exactly when its four-momentum is null, $P\cdot P = 0$: the $m\to 0$ limit of the mass-shell hyperboloid is the light cone. Massless particles travel at the universal speed limit $c$ in every frame (so the constancy of light is really the statement that the photon is massless), have $E = |\mathbf{p}|$, and possess no rest frame. The known massless particles are the photon and the graviton. The mass-shell sign trichotomy classifies all particles: $P\cdot P > 0$ timelike (massive, $u<c$), $P\cdot P = 0$ null (massless, $u=c$), $P\cdot P < 0$ spacelike (a **tachyon**, $u>c$) — and tachyons are excluded, signalling not superluminal travel but a vacuum instability in any field theory.

- **[[Thm - Elastic Collisions and the Compton Effect]]**
	- In an **elastic** collision $\mathcal{P}_1 + \mathcal{P}_2 \to \mathcal{P}_1 + \mathcal{P}_2$ the natures and masses are unchanged, which forces $P_1'\cdot P_2' = P_1\cdot P_2$ and hence a conserved relative speed. For two identical particles the post-collision opening angle is $\pi/2$ in the Newtonian limit (the billiard-ball right angle) but strictly less than $\pi/2$ relativistically. The **Compton effect** is the elastic scattering $\gamma + e^- \to \gamma + e^-$ off a stationary electron; isolating and squaring the recoil-electron four-momentum gives $\tfrac{1}{E_1'} - \tfrac{1}{E_1} = \tfrac{1}{m_e}(1-\cos\theta)$, equivalently the wavelength shift $\lambda' - \lambda = \tfrac{h}{m_e c}(1-\cos\theta)$ with $h/m_ec$ the electron Compton wavelength.

- **[[Thm - Inelastic Collisions and Particle Production]]**
	- An **inelastic** collision changes the particle content or masses; the system can produce new particles. The reaction $\mathcal{P}_1 + \mathcal{P}_2 \to \mathcal{P}_1' + \cdots + \mathcal{P}_N'$ is dynamically possible if and only if the invariant mass of the incoming system reaches the sum of the product masses, $m = \sqrt{(P_1+P_2)^2} \ge \sum_a m_a'$ — at **threshold** all products are at rest in the centre-of-momentum frame. For a fixed target ($\mathcal{P}_2$ at rest) this becomes $E_1 \ge \tfrac{1}{2m_2}(m_{\text{thres}}^2 - m_1^2 - m_2^2)$, which grows as the *square* of $m_{\text{thres}}$, against the *linear* growth in a collider — the reason colliders exist.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Mandelstam Variables and Crossing Symmetry *(from Particle Physics)*
> The kinematics of any $2\to 2$ process is captured by three Lorentz invariants, the **Mandelstam variables** $s = (P_1+P_2)^2$, $t = (P_1-P_3)^2$, $u = (P_1-P_4)^2$, satisfying the constraint $s+t+u = \sum_i m_i^2$. Here $s$ is the invariant mass squared of the system, equal to the centre-of-mass energy squared $E_{\text{cm}}^2$ — the "$\sqrt{s} = 13\text{ TeV}$" of the LHC. A single analytic amplitude $\mathcal{M}(s,t,u)$ describes several physical processes by **crossing symmetry**, continued into different regions of the $(s,t,u)$ plane. This invariant language is the indispensable preliminary to scattering theory; see [[Ex - Mandelstam variables for two-body scattering]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Energy–Momentum Tensor and Perfect Fluids *(from General Relativity and Continuum Mechanics)*
> A single particle carries a four-momentum $P^\mu$; a continuous distribution carries an **energy–momentum tensor** $T^{\mu\nu}$, the flux of the $\mu$-component of four-momentum across a surface of constant $x^\nu$. For a **perfect fluid** it takes the form $T^{\mu\nu} = (\rho + p)U^\mu U^\nu - p\,\eta^{\mu\nu}$ with $\rho$ the energy density and $p$ the pressure. Its conservation $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is the local, field-theoretic version of [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|four-momentum conservation]], and $T^{\mu\nu}$ is the source term in Einstein's field equations — developed in [[Special Relativity XXIII — The Energy-Momentum Tensor and Field Energy|Special Relativity XXIII]].

- **[[Ex - Compton scattering]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Derive the Compton wavelength shift $\lambda' - \lambda = \tfrac{h}{m_e c}(1-\cos\theta)$ for $\gamma + e^- \to \gamma + e^-$ by rewriting conservation as $P_e' = P_\gamma + P_e - P_\gamma'$ and squaring to eliminate the recoil electron via $P_e'\cdot P_e' = m_e^2$.

- **[[Ex - Two-body decay kinematics]]** (⭐⭐)
	- A particle of mass $m_1$ at rest decays into two of masses $m_2, m_3$; conservation fixes the product energies and Lorentz factors entirely, $\gamma_2 = (m_1^2+m_2^2-m_3^2)/(2m_1m_2)$, and forbids the decay unless $m_1 \ge m_2 + m_3$; apply to Higgs $\to\gamma\gamma$ and to the boosted-frame photon angle.

- **[[Ex - Mandelstam variables for two-body scattering]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- For $1+2\to 3+4$ define $s,t,u$ as Minkowski squares of four-momentum sums and differences, prove they are Lorentz invariant and obey $s+t+u = \sum m_i^2$, identify $\sqrt{s}$ as the centre-of-mass energy, and compute $s$ for a collider versus a fixed target.

- **[[Ex - Threshold energy for particle production]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Find the minimum energy to create new particles in a collision via the invariant $(\sum P)^2$, evaluated in the lab frame and again in the centre-of-momentum frame where at threshold all products are at rest; derive the antiproton-production threshold $E_1 \ge 7m_p \simeq 6.57$ GeV and the pair-production threshold.

- **[[Ex - Whether a particle reaction is kinematically allowed]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Use conservation of four-momentum as a filter: show a free photon cannot decay to $e^+e^-$ (null cannot equal timelike), find the two-photon pair-production threshold $E_1 E_2(1-\cos\theta) \ge 2m_e^2$, and explain why $\gamma + p \to e^+ + e^- + p$ needs a third body.

- **[[Ex - Inverse Compton scattering and the GZK cutoff]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Show that a moving electron can *give* energy to a photon, with maximum gain $\max E_\gamma'/E_\gamma \simeq 4\Gamma_e^2$ for an ultra-relativistic electron, and derive the GZK cutoff: a cosmic-ray proton above $\sim 6\times10^{19}$ eV photoproduces pions on the cosmic microwave background and so cannot reach us from far away.

- **[[Ex - The relativistic rocket]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- A rocket propelled by emitting photons backwards; conservation of four-momentum integrates to a relativistic Tsiolkovsky equation $\gamma(1+v) = M_0/M$, relating final rapidity to the fraction of rest mass converted to radiation.

## §13.3 Four-Force and the Equation of Motion

- **[[Def - Four-Force]]**
	- The **four-force** acting on a particle is the proper-time derivative of its four-momentum, $F^\mu := dP^\mu/d\tau$; it vanishes exactly when the particle is isolated. Writing $P = mU$ gives $F = mA + \tfrac{dm}{d\tau}U$ with $A$ the [[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration|four-acceleration]], and contracting with $U$ gives $F\cdot U = dm/d\tau$. A **pure** (or Minkowski) four-force is one orthogonal to the four-velocity, $F\cdot U = 0$; by the previous identity a pure force *preserves the rest mass*. The single physically important pure force is the electromagnetic Lorentz force $F^\mu = q\,F^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu$ (antisymmetry of the field tensor guarantees $F\cdot U = 0$). The relation $F^\mu = dP^\mu/d\tau$ is empty until the form of $F$ is supplied by the interaction.

- **[[Thm - Relativistic Newton's Second Law]]**
	- Newton's second law made Lorentz-covariant is $F^\mu = dP^\mu/d\tau$, whose spatial part for an inertial observer is $\mathbf{F} = d\mathbf{p}/dt = \tfrac{d}{dt}(\gamma m\mathbf{u})$ — the ordinary three-force equals the rate of change of relativistic momentum — and whose time part is the **work–energy theorem** $dE/dt = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{u}$, the power delivered. Crucially the acceleration is *not* parallel to the force: differentiating $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{u}$ produces a longitudinal $\gamma^3$ and transverse $\gamma$ response, so a force along the motion is "harder" than a force across it. For a pure force ($dm/d\tau = 0$) the work–energy theorem governs the kinetic energy directly; in the Newtonian limit $\gamma\to 1$ it reduces to $m\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{F}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Lorentz Four-Force and the Field Tensor *(from Electromagnetism)*
> The one fundamental pure four-force is the **Lorentz force** $F^\mu = q\,F^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu$, where $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ is the antisymmetric **field-strength tensor** built from the four-potential $A^\mu$, and $q$ is the charge. Its spatial part reproduces $\mathbf{f} = q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B})$ and its time part the rate of working $q\,\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{u}$; the antisymmetry of $F_{\mu\nu}$ is exactly what makes the force pure and so conserves the charge's rest mass. This four-force drives cyclotrons and synchrotrons and is developed in [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field|Special Relativity XXI]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Action Principle and the Free-Particle Lagrangian *(from Analytical Mechanics)*
> The equation $dP^\mu/d\tau = 0$ for a free particle is the Euler–Lagrange equation of the **relativistic action** $S = -m\int d\tau$ — the proper time along the worldline, weighted by $-m$. The conjugate momentum $\partial L/\partial\dot X^\mu$ is exactly the four-momentum $P_\mu$, the mass-shell $P\cdot P = m^2$ is a primary constraint, and the [[Thm - Inertial Worldlines Maximise Proper Time|reversed triangle inequality]] makes the straight worldline a *maximum* of proper time. This is the cleanest entry to the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulation of relativistic mechanics, developed in [[Special Relativity XV — The Principle of Least Action|Special Relativity XV]].

- **[[Ex - The acceleration is not parallel to the force]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Differentiate $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{u}$ to show $\mathbf{F} = \gamma m\mathbf{a} + \gamma^3 m(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}$, deduce the longitudinal mass $\gamma^3 m$ and transverse mass $\gamma m$, and explain why a constant force never accelerates a particle past $c$.

- **[[Ex - The relativistic work-energy theorem]]** (⭐)
	- Derive $dE/dt = \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{u}$ from $\tfrac{d}{d\tau}(P\cdot P) = 0$ for a pure force, confirm it is the time component of $F^\mu = dP^\mu/d\tau$, and integrate it to find the work needed to accelerate a particle from rest to speed $u$.

- **Charged particle in a uniform electric field** (forward to [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field|Special Relativity XXI]])
	- The cleanest worked application of the equation of motion to a concrete force solves $d\mathbf{p}/dt = q\mathbf{E}$ for a charge released from rest, obtaining $\mathbf{p} = q\mathbf{E}t$ and the hyperbolic worldline $x(t) = \tfrac{1}{a}(\sqrt{1+a^2t^2}-1)$ with $a = qE/m$ — the speed approaches but never reaches $c$, and the rest mass is preserved because the Lorentz force is pure. Because the electromagnetic field is owned by [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field|Special Relativity XXI]], this exercise is developed there; the present chapter supplies the force-free and pure-force machinery it relies on.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of relativistic dynamics chase a small, recognisable set of goals, and naming the goal is the first step of every solution. The most common is **finding an energy or a momentum**: the energy of a decay product, the threshold energy to create a particle, the recoil of an atom, the frequency of a scattered photon. A second is **finding a speed, an angle, or a direction**: the speed of the fragments of a decay, the opening angle between two scattered particles, the direction of a recoiling electron. A third is **finding an invariant mass** — the rest mass of a composite system, which is emphatically *not* the sum of its parts' masses, and which is the single cleanest number characterising a collection of particles. A fourth is **establishing a kinematic inequality or a possibility statement**: a particle can decay only if heavier than its products; a single photon cannot pair-produce in vacuum; a reaction needs at least a certain energy. A fifth, more structural, is **showing a law is Lorentz-covariant** — that it is built from genuine four-vectors and tensors and so holds in every frame. The unifying remark is that all five are questions about a *conserved four-vector* or a *Lorentz invariant*: you understand a relativistic process when you know the total four-momentum (conserved) and the invariant masses (frame-independent), and every target above is read off from those two.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **No external forces act** — then four-momentum is conserved, $\sum P_{\text{in}} = \sum P_{\text{out}}$, and this single four-vector equation is the richest source in the subject. **A particle is massive with known rest mass** — then $P\cdot P = m^2$ constrains its four-momentum to a hyperboloid, and squaring any expression for $P$ produces the scalar $m^2$. **A particle is massless** — then $P\cdot P = 0$, even more powerful as a simplifier because the term vanishes outright. **A convenient frame is available** — the rest frame of a decaying particle, or the centre-of-momentum frame of a collision, in which the total spatial momentum vanishes and the algebra collapses (especially at threshold, where all products are at rest there). **A quantity is known to be a Lorentz scalar** — rest mass, an inner product $P\cdot Q$, the Mandelstam $s$ — then it may be computed in whichever frame is easiest and the answer transported unchanged. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: "no external forces" routes through conservation to an energy or angle; "known rest mass" routes through $P\cdot P = m^2$ to an invariant; "convenient frame" routes a hard vector equation into a trivial scalar one. The [[Special Relativity XIII — Energy and Momentum#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves almost every problem in the topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list and try each one. Everything is self-contained: a reader who knows only that a four-vector is a four-component object whose Minkowski inner product $A\cdot B = A^0 B^0 - \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ is the one combination every observer agrees on should be able to follow each operation from its description alone.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Write down the total four-momentum and set it equal before and after.** In any process without external forces, [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|conservation of four-momentum]] gives $\sum_{\text{in}}P^\mu = \sum_{\text{out}}P^\mu$ — four scalar equations in one. This is the single most-used operation; the first line of almost every solution is "conservation of four-momentum reads $P_1 + P_2 = P_3 + P_4$". It already contains conservation of energy (the $\mu=0$ component) and conservation of three-momentum (the spatial components), so you never invoke those separately. *Trigger:* any collision, decay, absorption, or emission. *Pattern:* "by four-momentum conservation, $\sum P_{\text{in}} = \sum P_{\text{out}}$", then manipulate into invariants.

2. **Square a four-momentum to extract its invariant mass.** For any four-momentum $P\cdot P = m^2$ — a Lorentz scalar — and for a photon $P\cdot P = 0$; see [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass]]. So whenever an equation can be brought to the form $P = (\text{stuff})$, taking the Minkowski square of both sides converts the left side into the *known number* $m^2$ and the right side into inner products you can evaluate. *Trigger:* a particle whose four-momentum you do not want to know in detail — isolate it on one side and square. *Pattern:* the engine of [[Ex - Compton scattering|Compton scattering]] and [[Ex - Threshold energy for particle production|threshold]] problems.

3. **Go to the centre-of-momentum frame.** Choose the inertial frame in which the total spatial momentum vanishes, $\sum\mathbf{p} = 0$. There the total four-momentum is purely temporal, $(E_{\text{tot}}, \mathbf{0})$, and its square is simply $E_{\text{tot}}^2$ — the *invariant mass squared* of the whole system. *Trigger:* any multi-particle process, especially threshold and decay problems. *Pattern:* the centre-of-momentum frame is "nearly always the smart frame" because it kills the spatial components and turns vector bookkeeping into scalar arithmetic; at threshold all products are at rest there.

4. **Go to the rest frame of a chosen massive particle.** The frame in which a massive particle is momentarily at rest gives it four-momentum $(m,\mathbf{0})$ and four-velocity $(1,\mathbf{0})$ — the simplest possible components. *Trigger:* one distinguished massive particle (a decaying nucleus, the target electron in Compton scattering). *Pattern:* compute in its rest frame, then, because the quantities of interest are invariants, transport the answer to any frame for free; this is how [[Ex - Two-body decay kinematics|two-body decay]] is solved.

5. **Use $E = P\cdot U_0$ to read the energy an observer measures.** The energy of a particle of four-momentum $P$ relative to an observer of four-velocity $U_0$ is the invariant contraction $E = P\cdot U_0$, and its three-momentum is the orthogonal projection of $P$ onto the observer's rest space; see [[Thm - Energy and Momentum Relative to an Observer]]. *Trigger:* "what energy/frequency does *this* observer measure?". *Pattern:* contract the four-momentum with the observer's four-velocity — this gives the relativistic Doppler effect for a photon, $E_{\text{obs}} = \hbar\,U_0\cdot K$, in one line.

6. **Use a Lorentz invariant to switch frames.** Any Minkowski inner product $P\cdot Q$, any rest mass $m$, the Mandelstam $s$ — all are Lorentz scalars, the *same number in every frame*. *Trigger:* a quantity awkward in the frame the problem is posed in. *Pattern:* recognise it as an invariant, compute it in whatever frame makes it trivial, and equate the two expressions — the labour-saving heart of threshold problems and of [[Ex - Mandelstam variables for two-body scattering|Mandelstam]] kinematics.

7. **Lorentz-transform a four-vector when a component is wanted in another frame.** If a four-vector's components are known in $S$ and a component is wanted in $S'$, apply the boost: $A'^\mu = \Lambda^\mu{}_\nu A^\nu$. *Trigger:* "what energy/angle does the *other* observer see?". *Pattern:* the transformation of $P^\mu$ gives how energy and momentum mix between frames; the inverse Compton effect and aberration are this operation on the photon four-momentum.

8. **Use the mass-shell to trade energy for momentum.** From $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 + m^2$ a known energy gives $|\mathbf{p}| = \sqrt{E^2 - m^2}$ and vice versa; for a photon $E = |\mathbf{p}|$. *Trigger:* a problem gives one of energy or momentum and asks for the other. *Pattern:* the mass-shell is the bridge, and its photon form $E = |\mathbf{p}|$ is what lets photon energies and momenta be used interchangeably.

9. **Differentiate four-momentum with respect to proper time to get the four-force.** To relativise a force, write $F^\mu = dP^\mu/d\tau$ — a four-vector because $\tau$ is a scalar; see [[Def - Four-Force]]. *Trigger:* a non-isolated particle, a force, an acceleration. *Pattern:* convert to coordinate time with $d/d\tau = \gamma\,d/dt$, giving the three-force $d\mathbf{p}/dt = \mathbf{f}$ and the power $dE/dt = \mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{u}$; the orthogonality $F\cdot U = dm/d\tau$ shows a pure force preserves mass.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating rest mass as additive, $m_{\text{total}} = \sum m_i$
> The mass of a composite system is the most over-trusted quantity in the subject. Rest mass is *not* additive: the invariant mass is $M^2 = (\sum P_i)\cdot(\sum P_i)$, and expanding produces cross terms $2P_i\cdot P_j$ that depend on the relative motion of the parts. The concrete counterexample: two photons each of zero mass, moving in different directions, form a system of nonzero invariant mass $M^2 = 2P_1\cdot P_2 = 2E_1 E_2(1-\cos\theta) > 0$ ([[Ex - The invariant mass of a system of particles]]); a bound nucleus weighs *less* than its free constituents by the binding energy; a hot gas weighs more than a cold one. What is conserved and additive is the four-momentum $\sum P_i^\mu$; the mass is the length of that vector sum, and the length of a sum is not the sum of lengths. The operation becomes legal exactly when all the parts are at rest relative to one another (all $P_i$ parallel), so that there is no relative motion to contribute.

> [!warning] 2. Using the Newtonian momentum $\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{u}$ and kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mu^2$
> Every instinct trained on Newtonian mechanics writes momentum as mass times velocity. Relativistically the conserved momentum is $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{u}$, with the extra $\gamma$, and the kinetic energy is $(\gamma-1)mc^2$, not $\tfrac12 mu^2$. The Newtonian forms are only the leading Taylor terms in $u/c$ and fail badly as $u\to c$: $\mathbf{p} = m\mathbf{u}$ stays finite while the true momentum diverges, and a calculation using it would "prove" a particle can be pushed past $c$ ([[Ex - The acceleration is not parallel to the force]]). The diverging $\gamma$ is precisely what enforces the speed limit. The Newtonian expressions become legal only in the strict limit $\gamma \to 1$ — that is, $u \ll c$ — never as an identity.

> [!warning] 3. Confusing total energy with rest energy, $E = mc^2$ versus $E = \gamma mc^2$
> The two slogans are constantly conflated. $E = mc^2$ is the energy of a particle *at rest*; $E = \gamma mc^2$ is its total energy *in motion*. A particle's energy is $E = mc^2 + (\text{kinetic energy})$, and only the rest part is $mc^2$ ([[Thm - Mass-Energy Equivalence]]). The error of writing $E = mc^2$ for a moving particle drops its entire kinetic energy. The older language rescued the famous formula by calling $\gamma m$ the "relativistic mass" and writing $E = (\text{relativistic mass})c^2$ — but relativistic mass is just energy over $c^2$, carries no new information, and obscures that the invariant mass is the rest mass $m$. The slogan $E = mc^2$ is legal only when applied to the particle in its own rest frame.

> [!warning] 4. Assuming the acceleration points along the force
> In Newtonian mechanics $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{F}/m$, so acceleration and force are parallel. Relativistically they are not: differentiating $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{u}$ gives $\mathbf{F} = \gamma m\mathbf{a} + \gamma^3 m(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{u})\mathbf{u}$, so a force at an angle to the velocity produces an acceleration at a *different* angle ([[Ex - The acceleration is not parallel to the force]]). The response is $\gamma^3 m$ along the motion (the "longitudinal mass") and $\gamma m$ across it (the "transverse mass") — the same force produces a smaller acceleration along the direction of travel. The naive $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{F}/m$ is recovered only as $\gamma\to 1$; at relativistic speeds you must invert the full tensor relating $\mathbf{a}$ to $\mathbf{F}$.

> [!warning] 5. Letting a single photon decay or pair-produce in vacuum
> It is tempting to write $\gamma \to e^+ + e^-$: a photon has plenty of energy, $511$ keV each is enough. But the reaction is kinematically forbidden, and the obstruction is purely a statement about four-vectors. The photon's four-momentum is **null**, $P_\gamma\cdot P_\gamma = 0$, while the sum $P_{e^+} + P_{e^-}$ of two future-pointing *timelike* four-momenta is itself timelike, $(P_{e^+}+P_{e^-})^2 > 0$ ([[Ex - Whether a particle reaction is kinematically allowed]]). A null four-vector can never equal a timelike one, in any frame, so conservation of four-momentum cannot hold — equivalently, in the would-be centre-of-momentum frame of the pair the photon would have to be at rest, which no photon can. The reaction becomes legal only when a third body (a nucleus) is present to absorb recoil: $\gamma + p \to p + e^+ + e^-$, where the proton's four-momentum makes the books balance.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this topic are won the moment you classify what is being asked and recognise the conserved or invariant object that answers it. Begin there.

If the problem **describes a collision, decay, or creation process and asks for an energy, momentum, speed, or angle**, you are in the central problem class, and the first line of the solution is fixed: write [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|conservation of four-momentum]], $\sum_{\text{in}} P^\mu = \sum_{\text{out}} P^\mu$. This is one four-vector equation, hence four scalar equations, and it is almost always enough. The skill is in what comes next, and it is governed by a single question: *is there a four-momentum in the problem you do not care about?* If a decay produces a particle whose energy and direction you are not asked for, or Compton scattering recoils an electron you only need to eliminate, then **isolate that unwanted four-momentum on one side of the equation and square it**. Squaring converts it into the scalar $P\cdot P = m^2$ (or $0$ for a photon), the unwanted vector vanishes from the problem, and what remains is a scalar equation in the quantities you do want. This "rearrange to $P = \ldots$, then square" move is the single most important technique in the chapter; it is how [[Ex - Compton scattering|Compton scattering]], [[Ex - Two-body decay kinematics|two-body decay]], and [[Ex - Threshold energy for particle production|threshold]] problems are all solved.

If the problem **asks for a threshold — the minimum energy for a reaction to happen** — the governing idea is that *at threshold the products are all at rest in the centre-of-momentum frame*. There is no kinetic energy to spare: every bit of available energy goes into making rest mass. So evaluate the invariant $(\sum P)\cdot(\sum P)$ — the total four-momentum squared, the invariant mass squared of the whole system, the *same in every frame*. Compute it in the lab frame, where the kinematics are given, and again in the centre-of-momentum frame, where at threshold $\sum P = (\sum m_{\text{products}}, \mathbf{0})$ so the square is just $(\sum m_{\text{products}})^2$. Equating the two frame computations of the same invariant delivers the threshold condition in one line. This is operations 3 and 6 combined: the centre-of-momentum frame trivialises one side, and the invariance of $P\cdot P$ imports it into the lab.

If the problem **involves a photon or any massless particle**, the simplification is sharper still. A photon has no rest frame and no four-velocity, but it has a [[Def - The Four-Momentum of a Photon|four-momentum]] that is *null*: $P\cdot P = 0$. So whenever you square an expression containing a photon's four-momentum, that term simply drops out. In a problem with several photons, the photon–photon inner products $P_\gamma\cdot P_\gamma' = E_\gamma E_\gamma'(1-\cos\theta)$ survive but each photon's self-square vanishes. The photon also couples to the wave four-vector through $P^\mu = \hbar K^\mu$, so any question about the *colour* of light — the Doppler effect, Compton scattering — is answered by tracking the energy via $E_{\text{obs}} = P\cdot U_0 = \hbar\,U_0\cdot K$.

If the problem **asks you to relate measurements made by two different observers** — what energy or angle the other observer sees — the route is to identify the relevant four-vector, write its components where they are known, and either Lorentz-transform it (operation 7) or contract it with the second observer's four-velocity (operation 5). The transformation of $P^\mu$ gives how energy and momentum mix between frames; the inverse Compton effect is exactly this applied to the photon four-momentum, and it is why a moving electron can pump a photon's energy up by a factor $4\Gamma_e^2$ ([[Ex - Inverse Compton scattering and the GZK cutoff]]). Do not transform coordinates and recompute — transform the four-vector directly, exactly as the dot product eliminates coordinates from Euclidean geometry.

If the problem **involves a force, an acceleration, or a non-isolated particle**, switch from conservation to the equation of motion $F^\mu = dP^\mu/d\tau$ ([[Thm - Relativistic Newton's Second Law]]). Project it onto an inertial observer: the spatial part is $d\mathbf{p}/dt = \mathbf{f}$ with $\mathbf{p} = \gamma m\mathbf{u}$, the time part the work–energy theorem $dE/dt = \mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{u}$. The recurring subtlety is that *acceleration is not parallel to force* — you must differentiate $\gamma m\mathbf{u}$ honestly, keeping the $\gamma^3$ longitudinal term. For the one important force, the electromagnetic Lorentz force, orthogonality $F\cdot U = 0$ guarantees the rest mass is preserved, so $E = \gamma m$ tracks the speed and the worldline is found by integrating $d\mathbf{p}/dt = q\mathbf{f}$ (worked out in [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field|Special Relativity XXI]], which owns the field).

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle. **The two things you can always trust are the conserved total four-momentum and the Lorentz-invariant scalars.** A specific component of a four-vector — an energy, a single momentum component — is frame-dependent and treacherous; the *vector* $\sum P^\mu$ is conserved, and the *scalars* $P\cdot Q$ and $m^2$ are frame-independent. So the disciplined approach is: write the conserved four-vector equation, then manipulate it only into statements about invariants — by squaring, by contracting with a chosen four-velocity, by going to a frame that trivialises an invariant. Solve the problem in the world of invariants, where every observer agrees, and translate back to components only at the very end. A solution that lives in component-land frame-hops and accumulates $\gamma$ factors; a solution that lives in invariant-land is two or three lines long.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|Conservation of four-momentum]]**: $\sum_{\text{in}} P^\mu = \sum_{\text{out}} P^\mu$. This is the most-used single fact in the topic because it is the *only* dynamical input most problems need: in the absence of external forces it holds, it is exact (not an approximation), and it bundles conservation of energy and three-momentum into one frame-independent statement. The recognisable setup is any process — collision, decay, absorption, emission — and the first line of the solution is always to write it down. **Typical use:** combine it with squaring — conservation gives a four-vector equation, squaring turns it into a scalar equation about invariants. Recognise its applicability whenever particles interact and nothing external pushes on them.

- **The mass-shell relation $P\cdot P = m^2$** (equivalently $E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 + m^2$): every particle's four-momentum has Minkowski-length-squared equal to its rest mass squared, and *zero* for a photon. Its reusable content is that it converts a four-vector into a known scalar: whenever you can isolate a four-momentum on one side of an equation, squaring both sides replaces it by $m^2$. **Typical use:** the engine of elimination — it removes uninteresting particles from a calculation — and the relation connecting energy and momentum, so any problem giving one and asking for the other routes through it. The photon's version $P\cdot P = 0$ is even more useful because the term vanishes outright.

- **The invariant-mass technique** — rearrange to $P = (\text{stuff})$, then square. This is the chapter's signature move and deserves to be listed as a property in its own right because it appears in nearly every collision problem. Whenever a particle's four-momentum is unwanted, isolating it and squaring trades the four-vector $P$ for the scalar $m^2$, collapsing a vector equation into a scalar one. **Typical use:** Compton scattering (eliminate the recoil electron), two-body decay (eliminate one product), threshold problems (square the total four-momentum). The reason it always works is the frame-independence of $P\cdot P$: the resulting scalar equation holds in every frame, so you evaluate the surviving inner products wherever the geometry is simplest.

- **$E = P\cdot U_0$ and the orthogonal decomposition $P = E\,U_0 + \mathbf{p}$** ([[Thm - Energy and Momentum Relative to an Observer]]): the energy an observer measures is the contraction of the four-momentum with the observer's four-velocity, and the momentum is the orthogonal projection onto the observer's rest space. **Typical use:** any question about what a specific observer measures — the Doppler effect ($E_{\text{obs}} = \hbar\,U_0\cdot K$), the energy of a particle in a new frame — is answered by contracting with the appropriate $U_0$. It is the bridge between the absolute four-momentum and the relative quantities (energy, momentum) that depend on the observer, and squaring the decomposition reproduces $E^2 = m^2 + \mathbf{p}^2$ for free.

- **The Lorentz invariance of inner products**: any contraction $P\cdot Q$ is the same number in every inertial frame. This is the most reusable structural fact, the licence behind "compute in the convenient frame". **Typical use:** a quantity awkward in the lab is recognised as an inner product, evaluated in a friendlier frame (rest frame, centre-of-momentum frame), and equated across frames. Threshold problems, the Mandelstam variables, the Compton shift, and the inverse Compton energy gain are all this one move, and the discipline it enforces — keep contractions one-index-up-one-index-down — is also what guarantees a written law is Lorentz-covariant.

# Bridges

1. **Particle physics and collider kinematics — the Mandelstam invariants.** [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|Conservation of four-momentum]] is not merely one tool among many in particle physics; it is the computational backbone of the entire subject, the input to every cross-section calculation, every decay-rate prediction, every event reconstruction at a detector. The structure that makes it tractable is that the kinematics of a two-body process $1+2\to 3+4$ is captured, up to overall rotations, by just three Lorentz-invariant numbers — the **Mandelstam variables** $s = (P_1+P_2)^2$, $t = (P_1-P_3)^2$, $u = (P_1-P_4)^2$, each the Minkowski square of a sum or difference of external [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momenta]] and so a scalar every observer agrees on. The first, $s$, is the square of the *total* four-momentum, hence the invariant mass squared of the whole system; evaluated in the centre-of-momentum frame, where the total four-momentum is purely temporal, it equals the centre-of-mass energy squared $s = E_{\text{cm}}^2$ — the "$\sqrt{s} = 13$ TeV" that headlines the LHC. The other two, $t$ and $u$, are momentum transfers carrying scattering-angle information, and they are not independent: combining the four mass-shell conditions $P_i\cdot P_i = m_i^2$ with conservation $P_1+P_2 = P_3+P_4$ forces the constraint $s+t+u = \sum_i m_i^2$, so two of the three fix the kinematics completely. Their deeper role is **crossing symmetry**: a single analytic amplitude $\mathcal{M}(s,t,u)$ describes several physical processes at once — the $s$-channel reaction $1+2\to 3+4$ and the $t$- and $u$-channel reactions obtained by continuing the same function into a different region of the $(s,t,u)$ plane, an incoming particle "crossed" to an outgoing antiparticle. Computing one channel delivers the others for free. This invariant language — $s$, $t$, $u$, and the conservation law relating them — is the one in which all of scattering theory is written; see [[Ex - Mandelstam variables for two-body scattering]].

2. **Quantum field theory — from the mass-shell to fields and antimatter.** The mass-shell relation $P\cdot P = m^2$, which here is just the geometric statement that the [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momentum]] has Minkowski length the rest mass, is the seed from which all of relativistic quantum theory grows. The bridge is the substitution $P_\mu \to i\hbar\partial_\mu$ — the covariant completion of the Schrödinger prescription $E\to i\hbar\partial_t$ and $\mathbf{p}\to -i\hbar\nabla$, now packaged into a single four-vector of operators. Feeding this operator into the algebraic constraint $P\cdot P = m^2$ converts it into a differential equation, $(\Box + m^2)\phi = 0$, the **Klein–Gordon equation**, with $\Box = \partial_\mu\partial^\mu$ the d'Alembertian; its plane-wave solutions $e^{-iK\cdot X}$ carry the dispersion relation $K\cdot K = m^2$, exactly the wave four-vector of the photon. But the Klein–Gordon equation is second order in time, which spoils the conserved positive probability of single-particle quantum mechanics, and **Dirac** therefore sought a *first*-order equation whose square reproduces it; the only factorisation forces the wavefunction to carry several components and the coefficients to be matrices satisfying a Clifford algebra, and out of this come, with no extra input, the **Dirac equation**, spin-$\tfrac12$, and a doubling of solutions Dirac was forced to read as **antimatter**. Finally $E = mc^2$ makes particle number unstable — concentrate enough energy and pair creation makes new particles — so a fixed-particle-number wavefunction cannot describe the physics, and the wavefunction must be promoted to an operator-valued **quantum field** whose excitations are particles. Single-particle relativistic quantum mechanics gives way to **quantum field theory**, with creation and annihilation built in.

3. **Geometric mechanics — the relativistic action is proper time.** A free relativistic particle extremises the functional $S = -m\int d\tau$, the proper time along its worldline weighted by $-m$; this is the relativistic counterpart of the principle of least action, with integrand $-m\sqrt{\eta_{\mu\nu}\dot X^\mu\dot X^\nu}$ playing the role of the Lagrangian. The Euler–Lagrange equation is precisely $dU^\mu/d\tau = 0$, a straight worldline, the [[Def - Four-Force|force-free]] equation of motion. A reader who knows geometric mechanics will recognise that the conjugate momentum $\partial L/\partial\dot X^\mu$ is exactly the [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momentum]] $P_\mu$, and that the mass-shell $P\cdot P = m^2$ is a *primary constraint* in the Hamiltonian sense — the relativistic free particle is a constrained system whose constraint generates reparametrisation of the worldline. The [[Thm - Inertial Worldlines Maximise Proper Time|reversed triangle inequality]] then makes the straight worldline a *maximum*, not a minimum, of proper time, because the Minkowski metric is indefinite. This is the cleanest entry to the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian formulation of relativistic mechanics, taken up in [[Special Relativity XV — The Principle of Least Action|Special Relativity XV]].

4. **General relativity — from free particles to geodesics, and from conservation to $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$.** A free relativistic particle obeys $dU^\mu/d\tau = 0$: its [[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration|four-velocity]] is constant, its worldline a straight line in flat Minkowski space — Newton's first law, rewritten covariantly. General relativity keeps the principle that a free-falling particle moves "as straight as possible" but transfers it to a *curved* spacetime, where "straight" is defined intrinsically. The flat-space proper-time derivative $d/d\tau$ acquires correction terms encoding how the coordinate basis twists from point to point, and the equation of motion becomes the **geodesic equation** $dU^\mu/d\tau + \Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}U^\nu U^\rho = 0$, with the **Christoffel symbols** $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}$ built from first derivatives of the metric; in flat Cartesian coordinates every $\Gamma$ vanishes and the geodesic equation collapses back to $dU^\mu/d\tau = 0$. What curves spacetime is matter: the **energy–momentum tensor** $T^{\mu\nu}$ — the field-theoretic carrier of the four-momentum of bridge 1's continuum — is the source of curvature through Einstein's equations $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$, and the flat-space conservation $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$, the local form of [[Thm - Conservation of Four-Momentum|four-momentum conservation]], upgrades to the covariant $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$; the contracted Bianchi identity $\nabla_\mu G^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is precisely what makes the field equations consistent with it. Special relativity is the tangent-space approximation to general relativity — every freely-falling observer carries a local Minkowski frame, and the whole of this topic is what they read off in it. This is the road to [[General Relativity I — Einstein's Equations and Schwarzschild|general relativity]].

5. **Electromagnetism and accelerators — the Lorentz four-force.** The one relativistic force that survives from the Newtonian world is the electromagnetic one, and [[Thm - Relativistic Newton's Second Law|the relativistic equation of motion]] for a charge is $dP^\mu/d\tau = q\,F^\mu{}_\nu U^\nu$, where $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$ is the antisymmetric **field-strength tensor** packaging the electric and magnetic fields and $A^\mu$ is the **four-potential**. The antisymmetry of $F_{\mu\nu}$ makes the four-force *pure*, $F\cdot U = q\,F_{\mu\nu}U^\mu U^\nu = 0$, so it conserves the charge's rest mass — the mathematical reason a charged particle in a field keeps its identity. Projected onto an inertial observer the spatial part is the familiar $\mathbf{f} = q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B})$ and the time part the rate of working $q\,\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{u}$. This is the force that drives every particle accelerator: a uniform $\mathbf{E}$ produces the hyperbolic motion of a linear accelerator, a uniform $\mathbf{B}$ the circular motion of a cyclotron at frequency $\omega = qB/\gamma m$. The full development, including how $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are slices of one Lorentz tensor, is [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field|Special Relativity XXI]].

6. **Astrophysics — inverse Compton scattering and the high-energy sky.** The same Compton algebra that makes an X-ray lose energy to a stationary electron, run in reverse, makes a low-energy photon *gain* energy from a fast-moving electron — the **inverse Compton effect**, with maximum energy gain $\max E_\gamma'/E_\gamma \simeq 4\Gamma_e^2$ for an ultra-relativistic electron ([[Ex - Inverse Compton scattering and the GZK cutoff]]). This single mechanism organises much of high-energy astrophysics. The cosmic microwave background — the thermal radiation at $2.725$ K filling the universe — is up-scattered by the hot electrons of the plasma in galaxy clusters, distorting the spectrum in the **Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect** now used to catalogue clusters. The relativistic electrons in the jets of **blazars** (active galactic nuclei whose jets point at Earth) inverse-Compton-scatter their own synchrotron photons up into the TeV gamma-ray range, producing the characteristic double-humped spectrum seen by Cherenkov telescopes. And the reaction has a dark side for cosmic rays: a proton above $E_{\text{crit}} \sim 6\times10^{19}$ eV photoproduces pions on the microwave background, $\gamma_{\text{CMB}} + p \to \pi^0 + p$, losing energy over $\sim 100$ Mpc — the **GZK cutoff** that should truncate the cosmic-ray spectrum, observed in 2008. The relativistic kinematics of this chapter is the working language of the field.

7. **Nuclear physics and the source of stellar energy — $E = mc^2$ as a balance sheet.** The non-additivity of mass that [[Thm - Mass-Energy Equivalence|mass–energy equivalence]] forces is not an abstraction; it is the energy source of the sun and of every nuclear reactor and weapon. A bound nucleus has rest mass *less* than the sum of its free nucleons' masses, by the binding energy divided by $c^2$ — the **mass defect** — and that missing mass is released as kinetic energy and radiation when the nucleus forms. In fusion, four protons combine (through a chain) into a helium-4 nucleus whose mass is about $0.7\%$ less than the four protons', and that $0.7\%$ of $mc^2$ is what the sun radiates; in fission, a heavy nucleus splits into fragments of smaller total mass, releasing the difference. The bookkeeping is exactly the invariant-mass calculation of [[Ex - The invariant mass of a system of particles]]: the total four-momentum is conserved, the rest mass of the products is the length of the total four-momentum, and the kinetic energy liberated is the deficit between the initial rest mass and the products' rest masses. Einstein's relation is the conversion rate, and the smallness of the percentage hides the enormousness of $c^2$: a gram of mass is $9\times10^{13}$ joules.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: the four-momentum is the one object, and everything else is a way of looking at it.** It is tempting to treat energy, momentum, and mass as three separate quantities with three separate conservation laws, as Newton did. The relativistic reorganisation is that there is *one* object, the four-momentum $P^\mu$, and energy, momentum, and mass are three different readings of it. Energy is its time component (relative to an observer); momentum is its spatial part (relative to an observer); mass is its Minkowski length (absolute). Conservation of energy and conservation of momentum are the time and space components of the single statement that $P^\mu$ is conserved; conservation of mass is *not* a separate law at all, because the length of a vector sum is not the sum of lengths. Once this is internalised the chapter has no surprises: every quantity is a reading of $P$, every conservation law is the conservation of $P$, every paradox about mass dissolves into the elementary fact that $|P_1 + P_2| \ne |P_1| + |P_2|$. Stop thinking of energy, momentum, and mass as three things; think of one four-vector and three ways of measuring it.

**The true name of mass is "the length of the four-momentum vector".** The textbook definition of rest mass — the mass measured in the rest frame — is correct but operationally clumsy, because it forces you into a particular frame. The useful characterisation is geometric: $P^\mu$ is a four-vector, and its Minkowski length is the rest mass, $P\cdot P = m^2$. This reframing explains at once every counterintuitive fact about mass. Mass is not additive because the length of a sum of vectors is not the sum of lengths. The invariant mass of a system of two photons can be nonzero even though each has zero length, because two null vectors can sum to a timelike one. A bound system is lighter than its parts because the binding energy is a negative contribution to the length of the total four-momentum. And mass is frame-independent for the same reason a vector's length is basis-independent. The slogan to carry away: mass is not "amount of stuff", it is "the Minkowski norm of the energy–momentum vector", and the entire chapter's worth of paradoxes evaporates the moment you read it that way.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: see an unwanted four-momentum, isolate it and square.** This is the highest-density procedural insight in the chapter, and it is worth stating as a reflex. Whenever a problem contains a particle whose energy and direction you are not asked for — the recoil electron in Compton scattering, one product of a decay, the entire set of products at threshold — do not try to track it. Move its four-momentum to one side of the conservation equation, $P_{\text{unwanted}} = (\text{the rest})$, and take the Minkowski square. The left side becomes the *known number* $m^2$ (or $0$ for a photon); the unwanted vector has vanished. What remains is a scalar equation, frame-independent, in exactly the quantities you wanted. The reason it works is that squaring a four-vector is a Lorentz invariant, so the equation it produces holds everywhere and the surviving inner products can be evaluated in the most convenient frame. This single reflex, applied mechanically, solves the majority of the exercises in the chapter; the art is only in recognising *which* four-momentum is the unwanted one — and it is always the one you are not asked about.

**Why colliders exist: the centre-of-momentum frame is where energy is not wasted.** A fixed-target experiment and a collider both aim to concentrate energy into rest mass, but they differ by a square. To create a particle of mass $M$ by colliding two particles of mass $m$, the required beam energy in the centre-of-momentum frame grows *linearly* with $M$ — each beam needs roughly $\tfrac12 M$ of kinetic energy, after which all products sit at rest. But in the lab frame, with one target at rest, the required energy grows as $M^2$, because conservation of momentum forbids the products from being at rest: a moving incoming particle must leave moving products, and that unavoidable kinetic energy in the final state is energy not available for creating mass. The ratio is dramatic — to reach the LHC's $14$ TeV of available energy with a fixed target one would need to accelerate a single proton to about $10^5$ TeV. The deep statement is that the invariant $s = (\sum P)^2$ measures the energy that is *actually available* for the reaction, and it equals the lab energy only when the momenta cancel — that is, in a collider, where the laboratory *is* the centre-of-momentum frame. The whole engineering of two counter-rotating beams is the physical realisation of "make the lab frame the centre-of-momentum frame so that no energy is wasted on motion of the products".
