# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we set $c = 1$** and use the **mostly-minus signature** $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, so a timelike vector has $X \cdot X > 0$. Where a divergence or a metric is more recognisable with $c$ restored, both forms are given. The new and important standing convention of *this* chapter is that **coordinates are arbitrary**: until now every coordinate system on spacetime has been an inertial (affine, pseudo-orthonormal) one, but here we allow any smooth invertible relabelling of events — spherical, rotating, accelerated (Rindler), null. In such coordinates the components $g_{\alpha\beta}$ of the metric are *position-dependent* and the coordinate basis is no longer orthonormal, yet the spacetime is still flat. This is the entire point: it is the machinery of general relativity, run on a spacetime that happens to have no curvature.

> [!warning] Convention: Gourgoulhon uses the opposite signature
> Gourgoulhon's *Special Relativity in General Frames* (the source for this chapter) uses **mostly-plus** $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$. Every metric-component array below has been translated to mostly-minus by flipping the overall sign of $g$. Two formulas are *signature-independent in four dimensions* and are reproduced unchanged: the Christoffel formula $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12 g^{\gamma\mu}(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\beta}+\partial_\beta g_{\alpha\mu}-\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta})$ (the sign flips cancel in pairs $g^{\gamma\mu}g_{\cdots}$), and the determinant factor $\sqrt{-\det g}$ (flipping all four signs multiplies a $4\times 4$ determinant by $(-1)^4=+1$, so $\det g$ is unchanged and remains negative since the signature is still $(1,3)$).

- $\mathscr{E}$ — Minkowski spacetime as an affine space; $E$ — the underlying vector space of displacements; points $M \in \mathscr{E}$ are **events**
- $(x^\alpha) = (x^0,x^1,x^2,x^3)$ — an **arbitrary coordinate system** on $\mathscr{E}$, i.e. a diffeomorphism $\Phi : \mathscr{E} \to \mathbb{R}^4$, $M \mapsto (x^0,x^1,x^2,x^3)$; primed $(x'^\alpha)$ is a second one
- Greek indices $\alpha,\beta,\mu,\nu = 0,1,2,3$; Latin $i,j,k = 1,2,3$; Einstein summation (repeated index once up once down is summed)
- $\vec{e}_\alpha(M)$ — the **coordinate basis** (natural basis) at $M$ associated with $(x^\alpha)$; defined by $\overrightarrow{MM'} = \mathrm{d}x^\alpha\,\vec{e}_\alpha(M)$ for $M'$ at $(x^\alpha + \mathrm{d}x^\alpha)$. In differential-geometry notation $\vec{e}_\alpha = \partial/\partial x^\alpha$
- $e^\alpha$ — the dual basis of $(\vec{e}_\alpha)$; equals the gradient of the coordinate, $e^\alpha = \boldsymbol{\nabla} x^\alpha = \mathrm{d}x^\alpha$
- $g$, $g_{\alpha\beta}$ — the **metric tensor** and its components in the coordinate basis, $g_{\alpha\beta} = \vec{e}_\alpha\cdot\vec{e}_\beta$; in an inertial frame $g_{\alpha\beta} = \eta_{\alpha\beta}$, but in arbitrary coordinates $g_{\alpha\beta} = g_{\alpha\beta}(M)$
- $g^{\alpha\beta}$ — the inverse matrix, $g^{\alpha\mu}g_{\mu\beta} = \delta^\alpha{}_\beta$
- $\det g$ — the determinant of the matrix $(g_{\alpha\beta})$; with signature $(1,3)$ it is negative, and $\sqrt{-\det g}$ is the metric volume factor
- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$ — the **covariant derivative** (the connection); for a scalar it is the **gradient**; $\boldsymbol{\nabla}f$, $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\vec{v}$, $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{T}$
- $\nabla_\alpha$ — components of the covariant derivative; $\nabla_\beta v^\alpha$, $\nabla_\beta \omega_\alpha$, $\nabla_\rho T^{\cdots}{}_{\cdots}$
- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\vec v}\,\boldsymbol{T} = \boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{T}(\dots,\vec v)$ — covariant derivative of $\boldsymbol{T}$ along the vector $\vec v$ ($\vec v$ in the last slot)
- $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}$ — the **connection coefficients** of the basis $(\vec{e}_\alpha)$, $\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\vec{e}_\beta}\vec{e}_\alpha = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,\vec{e}_\mu$; in a coordinate basis built from the metric they are the **Christoffel symbols**
- $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{v} = \nabla_\mu v^\mu$ — the **divergence** of a vector field; $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\boldsymbol{T}$ the divergence of a tensor field
- $\mathbf{d}$ — the **exterior derivative**; on a $p$-form $A$ it gives a $(p+1)$-form $\mathbf{d}A$, with $(\mathbf{d}A)_{\alpha\beta} = \partial_\alpha A_\beta - \partial_\beta A_\alpha$ for a $1$-form, and $\mathbf{d}\circ\mathbf{d} = 0$
- $\wedge$ — the exterior (wedge) product; $\star$ — the **Hodge star**; $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, $\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}$ — the Levi-Civita tensor, $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} = \pm\sqrt{-\det g}\,\mathbf{d}x^0\wedge\mathbf{d}x^1\wedge\mathbf{d}x^2\wedge\mathbf{d}x^3$
- $\partial_\alpha \equiv \partial/\partial x^\alpha$ — partial derivative with respect to the coordinate $x^\alpha$

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: this is general relativity's tensor calculus — the covariant derivative, the connection coefficients, the Christoffel symbols — installed on *flat* spacetime, and the only thing flat spacetime is missing is curvature.

Up to this point every coordinate system on spacetime has been inertial: an origin event plus a pseudo-orthonormal basis, with the metric the constant array $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$ at every event. That is a luxury. The moment you describe spacetime the way a rotating observer does, or a uniformly accelerated one, or in spherical coordinates, the coordinate basis stops being orthonormal and the components of the metric become functions of position. The rotating-disk metric, for instance, carries off-diagonal terms and a position-dependent $g_{tt}$, even though it is nothing but ordinary Minkowski spacetime seen through rotating axes. Once $g_{\alpha\beta}$ varies from event to event, the naive derivative $\partial v^\alpha/\partial x^\beta$ of a vector field is no longer a tensor — it picks up garbage terms from the position-dependence of the basis vectors themselves — and we need a corrected derivative that *is* a tensor. That corrected derivative is the covariant derivative $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$, and the correction is bookkept by the connection coefficients $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}$.

The structural backbone of the chapter is a single chain of definitions, each forced by the previous:

$$\text{arbitrary coordinates} \;\Rightarrow\; g_{\alpha\beta}(M) \;\Rightarrow\; \boldsymbol{\nabla} \;\Rightarrow\; \Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12 g^{\gamma\mu}\!\left(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\beta}+\partial_\beta g_{\alpha\mu}-\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta}\right) \;\Rightarrow\; \boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot,\ \mathbf{d}.$$

Read it left to right: allowing arbitrary coordinates forces a position-dependent metric; a position-dependent metric forces a derivative that knows how the basis turns, the covariant derivative; that derivative is encoded in the Christoffel symbols, which are computed from the first derivatives of the metric; and from $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$ flow the two operators that every later physical chapter needs — the divergence (conservation laws) and the exterior derivative (Maxwell's equations).

Two facts make this chapter the cleanest possible bridge to gravitation, and they are worth stating now because they are the whole reason the chapter exists. First, the metric of flat spacetime is a *constant tensor* — the same bilinear form at every event — so its covariant derivative vanishes identically, $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$. This is the metric-compatibility condition; together with the symmetry $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\gamma{}_{\beta\alpha}$ (torsion-freeness) it makes $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$ the unique Levi-Civita connection of $g$. The very same equation $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$ defines the connection in general relativity; the *only* difference is that there $g$ is a genuinely position-dependent field, not a constant in disguise. Second, in flat spacetime the Christoffel symbols can be made to vanish *everywhere at once* by passing to inertial coordinates — they are an artefact of the coordinate choice, not of the geometry. The obstruction to doing this in a curved spacetime is exactly the Riemann curvature tensor, which is built from the *derivatives* of the Christoffel symbols. So flat spacetime is the special case in which $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}$ is nonzero but its curvature is zero, and the step to general relativity is the one-line change "let the metric curve, and the Christoffels can no longer all be transformed away." Everything you build here — the index gymnastics, the divergence formula, $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$ — survives that change unaltered.

The exterior derivative $\mathbf{d}$ is the chapter's second protagonist, and it is independent of the first. Where $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$ needs a metric to exist, $\mathbf{d}$ needs nothing but the differentiable structure: applied to a $p$-form it antisymmetrises the covariant derivative, and in any coordinate basis the Christoffel terms cancel by symmetry, leaving an operator built from partial derivatives alone. It is nilpotent ($\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$), it obeys a graded Leibniz rule, and it is the right generalisation of gradient, curl, and divergence to spacetime. It is the language in which $F = \mathbf{d}A$ and the homogeneous Maxwell equations $\mathbf{d}F = 0$ are written, and the chapter closes by showing that the divergence of a vector field is, up to Hodge stars, an exterior derivative in disguise.

The reader is assumed to have just worked through [[Special Relativity XVIII — Tensors, Alternate Forms and Hodge Duality|tensors, alternate forms and the Hodge star]] — tensors as multilinear maps, components and the change-of-basis law, the wedge product, and the Levi-Civita tensor — and to be fluent with multivariate differentiation and the chain rule. The differential-geometry background (the abstract notions of connection, covariant derivative, and exterior derivative on a manifold) is assumed available and is bridged to throughout; the point of the chapter is to see that the flat-spacetime versions defined here are exactly those manifold notions specialised to a spacetime with zero curvature.

# Concept Map

## §19.1 Arbitrary Coordinates and Tensor Fields

- **[[Def - Arbitrary Coordinates and the Coordinate Basis]]**
	- An arbitrary coordinate system on spacetime is a diffeomorphism $\Phi : \mathscr{E} \to \mathbb{R}^4$, $M \mapsto (x^0,x^1,x^2,x^3)$ — any smooth invertible relabelling of events, not necessarily affine. At each event it induces the **coordinate basis** $\vec{e}_\alpha(M)$ through $\overrightarrow{MM'} = \mathrm{d}x^\alpha\,\vec{e}_\alpha(M)$, the vector measuring the displacement produced by incrementing $x^\alpha$; in differential geometry $\vec{e}_\alpha = \partial/\partial x^\alpha$. Under a change of coordinates the basis transforms by the Jacobian, $\vec{e}_\alpha = (\partial x'^\beta/\partial x^\alpha)\,\vec{e}'_\beta$, and the metric components by $g_{\alpha\beta} = (\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\alpha)(\partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\beta)\,g'_{\mu\nu}$. In spherical coordinates the coordinate basis is orthogonal but not orthonormal; in rotating coordinates the metric even acquires off-diagonal terms — yet the spacetime is still flat Minkowski space.

- **[[Def - The Covariant Derivative]]**
	- For a tensor field $\boldsymbol{T}$ of type $(k,\ell)$, the covariant derivative $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\boldsymbol{T}$ is the type $(k,\ell+1)$ tensor field whose value on a displacement $\mathrm{d}\vec{x}$ reproduces the first-order variation of $\boldsymbol{T}$: $\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{T} = \boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\mathrm{d}\vec{x}}\boldsymbol{T}$. For a scalar it is the gradient $\boldsymbol{\nabla}f$, a linear form with components $\nabla_\alpha f = \partial f/\partial x^\alpha$ (metric-free). For a vector field its components in a *fixed* basis are just $\nabla_\beta v^\alpha = \partial v^\alpha/\partial x^\beta$, but in a position-dependent basis a correction is needed. It obeys the Leibniz rule for $\otimes$ and commutes with contraction; on the flat metric it satisfies $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$ because $g$ is a constant tensor, which makes it the Levi-Civita connection.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Connection on a Manifold *(from Differential Geometry and General Relativity)*
> The covariant derivative defined here is, in the language of [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation|Riemannian geometry]], an **affine connection** — a rule for differentiating tensor fields that produces tensors. On flat spacetime it is forced to be the trivial one (partial derivatives in inertial coordinates), but the *definition* makes no use of flatness. Promoting the constant $\eta$ to a position-dependent metric field $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ keeps every formula on this page word-for-word; what changes is that the connection acquires curvature. This is the single most important continuity in physics: the derivative that organises relativistic field theory *is* the derivative that organises gravitation.

- **[[Ex - The metric and coordinate basis in rotating coordinates]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Transform the inertial metric through a co-rotating coordinate change to the Langevin metric, exhibiting the frame-dragging cross term $g_{(ct)\varphi} = -\omega r^2\sin^2\theta$ and the position-dependent $g_{(ct)(ct)} = 1-\omega^2 r^2\sin^2\theta$ that vanishes at the light cylinder; then confirm the spacetime is flat by the vanishing of the Riemann tensor — a complicated metric need not mean curvature.

- **[[Ex - The coordinate basis of spherical coordinates is orthogonal but not orthonormal]]** (⭐)
	- Compute the spherical coordinate basis and metric $\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-r^2,-r^2\sin^2\theta)$, show the basis is orthogonal but not orthonormal (the $g_{\theta\theta}=-r^2$ scale factor), and prove the orthonormal frame $\hat r,\hat\theta,\hat\varphi$ is not a coordinate basis because it has a nonzero Lie bracket $[\hat r,\hat\theta] = -r^{-1}\hat\theta$.

- **[[Ex - The gradient of a coordinate is the dual basis vector]]** (⭐)
	- Show $\langle\boldsymbol{\nabla}x^\alpha,\vec{e}_\beta\rangle = \delta^\alpha{}_\beta$, so the coordinate gradients are the dual basis $e^\alpha = \mathbf{d}x^\alpha$; deduce a scalar gradient has components $\nabla_\alpha f = \partial f/\partial x^\alpha$ with no Christoffel correction, and that the gradient is intrinsically a covector requiring the metric to become a vector.

## §19.2 The Covariant Derivative and Christoffel Symbols

- **[[Special Relativity XIX/Def - Christoffel Symbols|Def - Christoffel Symbols]]**
	- The connection coefficients $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}$ of a basis are defined by $\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\vec{e}_\beta}\vec{e}_\alpha = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\,\vec{e}_\mu$ — they record how each basis vector turns as you move along another. They constitute $4^3 = 64$ scalar fields. When the basis is the coordinate basis of a metric they are symmetric in their lower indices and computed from the first derivatives of the metric, $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12 g^{\gamma\mu}(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\beta}+\partial_\beta g_{\alpha\mu}-\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta})$; these are the **Christoffel symbols**. In inertial coordinates they all vanish, and the covariant derivative reduces to partial differentiation; in spherical coordinates the nonzero ones are $\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-r$, $\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}=1/r$, and their relatives — exactly the symbols of three-dimensional polar geometry, since the time part is flat.

- **[[Thm - Divergence of a Vector and Tensor Field]]**
	- The divergence of a vector field is the contraction of its covariant derivative, $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{v} = \nabla_\mu v^\mu = \partial_\mu v^\mu + \Gamma^\nu{}_{\mu\nu}v^\mu$, and the trace of the Christoffel symbols collapses to a logarithmic derivative of the metric determinant, giving the compact and coordinate-robust formula $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{v} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-\det g}}\,\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-\det g}\,v^\mu)$. For an antisymmetric tensor of type $(2,0)$ the same trick yields $\nabla_\mu T^{\alpha\mu} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-\det g}}\,\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-\det g}\,T^{\alpha\mu})$, the form used for the electromagnetic field and the energy–momentum tensor. The formula reproduces the familiar spherical divergence and is the seed of every relativistic conservation law $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot J = 0$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Geodesic Equation *(from General Relativity)*
> The Christoffel symbols are exactly the coefficients in the **geodesic equation** $\ddot{x}^\gamma + \Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}\dot{x}^\alpha\dot{x}^\beta = 0$, the equation of a freely-falling worldline. On flat spacetime in inertial coordinates $\Gamma = 0$ and the geodesics are straight lines; in rotating or accelerated coordinates the nonzero Christoffels reproduce the centrifugal and Coriolis "forces" as pure coordinate artefacts. In general relativity the *same* equation, with the *same* symbols computed from a curved $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$, is the law of gravitational free fall — gravity is the Christoffel symbols of a curved metric. See [[Riemannian Geometry II — Geodesics, the Exponential Map, and Variational Principles|Riemannian geodesics]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Curvature as the Obstruction to Flatness *(from General Relativity)*
> On this flat spacetime the Christoffels are nonzero in curvilinear coordinates but can be transformed away *globally* by returning to inertial coordinates. The tensor that measures the failure of this — built from the *derivatives and squares* of the Christoffel symbols, $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma}$ — is the **Riemann curvature tensor**, and it vanishes identically here. Its non-vanishing is the invariant signature of a true gravitational field; see [[Riemannian Geometry III — Riemann Curvature and Topology|Riemann curvature]] and [[General Relativity I — Einstein's Equations and Schwarzschild]].

- **[[Ex - Christoffel symbols of spherical coordinates]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Invert the diagonal spherical metric and compute all nonzero Christoffel symbols from $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12 g^{\gamma\mu}(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\beta}+\partial_\beta g_{\alpha\mu}-\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta})$: $\Gamma^r{}_{\theta\theta}=-r$, $\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}=1/r$, $\Gamma^\varphi{}_{\theta\varphi}=\cot\theta$, and relatives. They are purely spatial (the time part is flat) and identical to Euclidean $\mathbb{R}^3$, and the result is signature-independent.

- **[[Ex - The covariant derivative of the metric is zero]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$ both conceptually (the flat metric is a constant tensor) and by computation (the Christoffel terms reassemble $\partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta}$), check $\nabla_r g_{\theta\theta}=0$ in spherical coordinates, and deduce that index-raising commutes with $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$ — trivial here, but the defining condition of the connection in general relativity.

- **[[Ex - Divergence in curvilinear coordinates via the metric determinant]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Derive the spherical divergence from $\sqrt{-\det g}=r^2\sin\theta$, compute $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{e}_r = 2/r$ both ways (determinant formula and Christoffel trace), and verify $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{e}_x = 0$ for the constant Cartesian field — constant components can have nonzero divergence and varying components zero.

- **[[Ex - Christoffel symbols and fictitious forces in rotating coordinates]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Compute the rotating-frame Christoffels, identify the centrifugal ($\Gamma^i{}_{tt}=-\omega^2 x^i$) and Coriolis ($\Gamma^i{}_{tj}=\mp\omega$) symbols, recover the Newtonian accelerations $\ddot x = \omega^2 x + 2\omega\dot y$ from the slow-motion geodesic equation, and confirm via zero Riemann tensor that these forces are fictitious — the flat-space rehearsal for gravity.

## §19.3 Differential Forms and the Exterior Derivative

- **[[Special Relativity XIX/Def - The Exterior Derivative|Def - The Exterior Derivative]]**
	- The exterior derivative of a differential $p$-form $A$ is the $(p+1)$-form $\mathbf{d}A$ obtained by antisymmetrising the covariant derivative $\boldsymbol{\nabla}A$. On a $0$-form it is the gradient, $\mathbf{d}f = \boldsymbol{\nabla}f$; on a $1$-form, $(\mathbf{d}A)_{\alpha\beta} = \nabla_\alpha A_\beta - \nabla_\beta A_\alpha$. In any coordinate basis the symmetric Christoffel terms cancel between the antisymmetrised slots, so the formula reduces to partial derivatives alone — $(\mathbf{d}A)_{\alpha\beta} = \partial_\alpha A_\beta - \partial_\beta A_\alpha$ — and $\mathbf{d}$ needs no metric: it is defined by the differentiable structure of spacetime. It is the right generalisation of gradient, curl, and divergence at once, and the field strength of electromagnetism is $F = \mathbf{d}A$.

- **[[Thm - Properties of the Exterior Derivative]]**
	- The exterior derivative is nilpotent, $\mathbf{d}\circ\mathbf{d} = 0$ (because mixed partial derivatives commute), so every exact form ($A = \mathbf{d}B$) is closed ($\mathbf{d}A = 0$); the Poincaré lemma supplies the converse on the whole spacetime or any star-shaped region. It satisfies the graded Leibniz rule $\mathbf{d}(A\wedge B) = \mathbf{d}A\wedge B + (-1)^p A\wedge\mathbf{d}B$ with $p = \deg A$. In a coordinate basis a $p$-form expands as $A = \sum_{\alpha_1<\cdots<\alpha_p} A_{\alpha_1\cdots\alpha_p}\,\mathbf{d}x^{\alpha_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{d}x^{\alpha_p}$, the volume form is $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} = \pm\sqrt{-\det g}\,\mathbf{d}x^0\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathbf{d}x^3$, and the divergence of a vector is the codifferential $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{v} = -\star\mathbf{d}\star\underline{v}$, equivalently $\mathbf{d}\star\underline{v} = (\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{v})\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: De Rham Cohomology and Maxwell's Equations *(from Differential Geometry and Electromagnetism)*
> The pair "$\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$, closed-versus-exact" is the engine of **de Rham cohomology**: the quotient of closed forms by exact forms measures the topology of the underlying space, and on a star-shaped domain it is trivial (Poincaré lemma). Physically, $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$ is why the homogeneous Maxwell equations $\mathbf{d}F = 0$ hold *automatically* once $F = \mathbf{d}A$, and why magnetic monopoles are forbidden in this formulation. The flat-spacetime exterior derivative defined here is the same operator as the manifold [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold|exterior derivative]] of [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Charge Conservation as a Closed Form *(from Electromagnetism)*
> The identity $\mathbf{d}\star\underline{v} = (\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{v})\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ turns a divergence into an exterior derivative, so a divergence-free current $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot J = 0$ becomes the statement that the $3$-form $\star\underline{J}$ is **closed**. Combined with Stokes' theorem in the next chapter, this is the geometric content of electric-charge conservation: the flux of charge through any closed hypersurface vanishes. The codifferential $-\star\mathbf{d}\star$ introduced here is the adjoint of $\mathbf{d}$ and the operator whose kernel-and-image structure underlies the **Hodge decomposition**.

- **[[Ex - The exterior derivative of a 1-form is the curl]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute $(\mathbf{d}\underline{v})_{\alpha\beta} = \partial_\alpha v_\beta - \partial_\beta v_\alpha$ (Christoffels cancelling by symmetry), show its Hodge dual is the Cartesian curl $\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_j v_k$, and conclude that $\mathrm{curl}\,\mathrm{grad}=0$ and $\mathrm{div}\,\mathrm{curl}=0$ are both the single identity $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$.

- **[[Ex - The exterior derivative is nilpotent]]** (⭐)
	- Prove $\mathbf{d}\mathbf{d}f = 0$ and $\mathbf{d}\mathbf{d}A = 0$ directly, the second-derivative terms cancelling in pairs by the equality of mixed partials; explain why this is connection-independent (unlike $[\nabla,\nabla]$ = curvature), and deduce exact $\Rightarrow$ closed, with the converse the Poincaré lemma.

- **[[Ex - The graded Leibniz rule for the exterior derivative]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove $\mathbf{d}(A\wedge B) = \mathbf{d}A\wedge B + (-1)^{\deg A}A\wedge\mathbf{d}B$ by expanding in a coordinate basis, and identify the sign $(-1)^{\deg A}$ as the transposition cost of sliding the degree-$1$ operator past the factors of $A$ — making $\mathbf{d}$ a graded derivation of the exterior algebra.

- **[[Ex - The field strength as an exterior derivative and the homogeneous Maxwell equations]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Define $F = \mathbf{d}A$, derive the homogeneous Maxwell equations $\mathbf{d}F = 0$ and gauge invariance $A\to A+\mathbf{d}\chi$ from nilpotency alone, and show the inhomogeneous equation $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$ forces charge conservation $\nabla_\nu J^\nu = 0$ via the determinant divergence formula.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of this chapter pursue a recognisable handful of goals. The most common is to **compute a connection or its symbols in a given coordinate system** — to write down the $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}$ of spherical, rotating, or Rindler coordinates from the metric, the way one computes a Jacobian. The skill is purely organisational: read off $g_{\alpha\beta}$, plug into the Christoffel formula, and keep track of which symbols survive. A second target is to **verify a tensor identity that the naive partial derivative would get wrong**: that $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$, that $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\vec{e}_r \neq 0$ even though the components of $\vec{e}_r$ are constant, that a divergence computed two ways agrees. These exercises are calibration checks on the central claim that $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$, not $\partial$, is the derivative that respects the geometry. A third target is to **establish a structural property of an operator** — that $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$, that the Christoffels are symmetric, that the exterior derivative is independent of the connection — typically by an antisymmetrisation argument or by the equality of mixed partial derivatives. A fourth is to **recover a familiar vector-calculus formula** as a special case: the spherical divergence, the curl as a component of $\star\mathbf{d}$, the gradient as $\mathbf{d}f$. A fifth, forward-looking, is to **express a physical law in coordinate-free form** — charge conservation as $\mathbf{d}\star\underline{J} = 0$, the field strength as $F = \mathbf{d}A$ — in preparation for electromagnetism. Across all five the unifying remark is that you understand a field-on-spacetime computation when you can separate what is geometry (the tensor, the form, the invariant) from what is coordinate artefact (the position-dependence of the basis, the nonzero Christoffels of curvilinear coordinates).

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A coordinate system is given**, which immediately supplies the metric components $g_{\alpha\beta}(M)$ by the change-of-basis law, and hence the Christoffel symbols and every derived operator; this is the richest source, because it converts a verbal description ("rotating observer", "spherical coordinates") into a definite array of functions. **The metric is recognised as flat** — as Minkowski's $\eta$ in disguise — which guarantees $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$, the symmetry of the Christoffels, and the global existence of inertial coordinates in which $\Gamma$ vanishes; this is what lets you sanity-check any curvilinear computation against a flat answer. **A field is built from the coordinate basis itself** — $\vec{v} = \vec{e}_r$, say — whose components are constant by construction, so that any nonzero covariant derivative is *entirely* the connection term, isolating $\Gamma$. **An object is an antisymmetric tensor or a differential form**, which is the trigger for the $\sqrt{-\det g}$ divergence formula and for the cancellation of Christoffel terms in the exterior derivative — antisymmetry is what makes $\mathbf{d}$ metric-free. **A quantity is recognised as a gradient, a curl, or a divergence of ordinary vector calculus**, which is the bridge to its coordinate-free counterpart $\mathbf{d}f$, $\star\mathbf{d}\underline{v}$, or $-\star\mathbf{d}\star\underline{v}$. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a given coordinate system routes through the metric to the Christoffels to a divergence; an antisymmetric tensor routes through the determinant formula to a conservation law; a closed form routes through the Poincaré lemma to a potential. The [[Special Relativity XIX — Fields on Spacetime and the Covariant Derivative#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves almost every computation in the chapter is assembled from. The reader's fluency is in analysis and geometry, so each operation states *what kind of object it produces* — a tensor, a form, a scalar — because the entire discipline of the subject is keeping covariant objects covariant. When stuck, scan the list and try each one.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Read off the metric from a coordinate change.** Given a coordinate system $(x^\alpha)$ related to inertial coordinates $(x'^\beta)$, compute the Jacobian $\partial x'^\beta/\partial x^\alpha$ and apply $g_{\alpha\beta} = (\partial x'^\mu/\partial x^\alpha)(\partial x'^\nu/\partial x^\beta)\,\eta_{\mu\nu}$; see [[Def - Arbitrary Coordinates and the Coordinate Basis]]. *Trigger:* a problem names a non-inertial coordinate system (spherical, rotating, Rindler, null) and you need its geometry. *Pattern:* "the metric in these coordinates is $g_{\alpha\beta} = J^{\mathsf T}\eta J$ with $J$ the Jacobian", after which everything else is mechanical.

2. **Compute Christoffel symbols from the metric.** Plug the metric components into $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12 g^{\gamma\mu}(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\beta}+\partial_\beta g_{\alpha\mu}-\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta})$; see [[Special Relativity XIX/Def - Christoffel Symbols|Def - Christoffel Symbols]]. *Trigger:* you need a covariant derivative, a divergence, or a geodesic equation in curvilinear coordinates. *Pattern:* compute $g^{\gamma\mu}$ first (it is diagonal for orthogonal coordinates), then only the surviving derivatives of $g_{\alpha\beta}$ contribute; use the symmetry $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\gamma{}_{\beta\alpha}$ to halve the work.

3. **Covariantly differentiate a vector or a one-form.** Use $\nabla_\beta v^\alpha = \partial_\beta v^\alpha + \Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta}v^\mu$ for an upper index and $\nabla_\beta \omega_\alpha = \partial_\beta\omega_\alpha - \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\omega_\mu$ for a lower one; see [[Def - The Covariant Derivative]]. *Trigger:* you want the rate of change of a field that is a genuine tensor, not a coordinate-dependent partial. *Pattern:* "$+\,\Gamma$ for each upper index, $-\,\Gamma$ for each lower index, with the derivation index $\beta$ always on the right of $\Gamma$." The sign rule is the most error-prone single thing in the chapter.

4. **Use $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$ to commute the metric past the derivative.** Because the flat metric is a constant tensor, the covariant derivative annihilates it, so $g_{\alpha\beta}$ and $g^{\alpha\beta}$ pass freely in and out of $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$; in particular raising and lowering indices commutes with covariant differentiation; see [[Def - The Covariant Derivative]]. *Trigger:* an index needs raising or lowering inside a covariant derivative. *Pattern:* "$\nabla_\mu(g_{\alpha\beta}T^\beta) = g_{\alpha\beta}\nabla_\mu T^\beta$", which is exactly what lets you treat $\nabla_\mu v_\alpha$ and $\nabla_\mu v^\alpha$ as the same operation up to an index move.

5. **Compute a divergence by the determinant formula.** Replace the connection-coefficient form by $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{v} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-\det g}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-\det g}\,v^\mu)$, and similarly for an antisymmetric tensor; see [[Thm - Divergence of a Vector and Tensor Field]]. *Trigger:* you need a divergence in curvilinear coordinates, or a conservation law. *Pattern:* "compute $\det g$, take $\sqrt{-\det g}$, and the divergence is one over it times the partial of (it times the component)" — this avoids computing $\Gamma$ entirely.

6. **Antisymmetrise to get the exterior derivative, then drop the Christoffels.** For a form, $(\mathbf{d}A)_{\alpha\beta\cdots} = \partial_\alpha A_{\beta\cdots} \mp \cdots$ — the alternating sum of partial derivatives, with no connection terms; see [[Special Relativity XIX/Def - The Exterior Derivative|Def - The Exterior Derivative]]. *Trigger:* the object is a differential form and you want its exterior derivative. *Pattern:* "$\mathbf{d}$ of a $p$-form is the alternating sum of $\partial_\alpha A_{\cdots}$ over the $p+1$ slots; the Christoffels cancel by symmetry, so use partials." This is legal *only* because $\Gamma$ is symmetric in a coordinate basis.

7. **Use $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$ to kill an exterior derivative of an exterior derivative.** Any $\mathbf{d}\mathbf{d}A$ is zero, so $\mathbf{d}A = 0$ whenever $A = \mathbf{d}B$; see [[Thm - Properties of the Exterior Derivative]]. *Trigger:* you have a form that is itself an exterior derivative (a field strength $F = \mathbf{d}A$, a gradient $\mathbf{d}f$) and want to show another exterior derivative vanishes. *Pattern:* "$F = \mathbf{d}A \Rightarrow \mathbf{d}F = \mathbf{d}\mathbf{d}A = 0$ for free" — this is the homogeneous Maxwell pair without any computation.

8. **Apply the graded Leibniz rule to a wedge product.** Differentiate $A\wedge B$ by $\mathbf{d}(A\wedge B) = \mathbf{d}A\wedge B + (-1)^{\deg A}A\wedge\mathbf{d}B$; see [[Thm - Properties of the Exterior Derivative]]. *Trigger:* the object is a wedge of two forms. *Pattern:* "differentiate the left factor, then the right with a sign $(-1)^{\deg A}$." The sign is the whole content; forgetting it is the standard error.

9. **Recognise a vector-calculus operator in disguise and convert.** A gradient is $\mathbf{d}f$, a curl is the Hodge dual of $\mathbf{d}$ of the metric-dual $1$-form, a divergence is $-\star\mathbf{d}\star$ of it; see [[Special Relativity XIX/Def - The Exterior Derivative|Def - The Exterior Derivative]] and [[Thm - Divergence of a Vector and Tensor Field]]. *Trigger:* a three-dimensional vector-calculus identity needs lifting to spacetime, or a spacetime identity needs interpreting in familiar terms. *Pattern:* "$\mathrm{grad} = \mathbf{d}$, $\mathrm{curl} = \star\mathbf{d}$ (on $1$-forms via the metric), $\mathrm{div} = -\star\mathbf{d}\star$", which is why $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$ contains both $\mathrm{curl}\,\mathrm{grad} = 0$ and $\mathrm{div}\,\mathrm{curl} = 0$.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Differentiating a vector field component by component with $\partial$ instead of $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$
> The seductive error is to write the "rate of change" of a vector field $\vec{v}$ as the array of partial derivatives $\partial_\beta v^\alpha$ and call it a tensor. It is not: under a change of coordinates $\partial_\beta v^\alpha$ picks up a second-derivative term $\partial_\beta\partial_\alpha x'^\gamma$ from the position-dependence of the basis, and so does not transform as a $(1,1)$ tensor. The concrete counterexample is the field $\vec{v} = \vec{e}_r$ in spherical coordinates: its components $v^\alpha = (0,1,0,0)$ are *constant*, so $\partial_\beta v^\alpha = 0$ everywhere, yet $\vec{e}_r$ visibly rotates as you move around the origin and its true derivative is nonzero — indeed $\nabla_\theta v^\theta = 1/r$. The operation becomes legal exactly when you add the connection term: $\nabla_\beta v^\alpha = \partial_\beta v^\alpha + \Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta}v^\mu$ is a tensor. (In a *fixed* basis — inertial coordinates — the two coincide, which is why one never notices the distinction in elementary special relativity.)

> [!warning] 2. Using the Christoffel formula $\Gamma = \tfrac12 g^{-1}(\partial g + \partial g - \partial g)$ in a non-coordinate basis
> The clean formula $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12 g^{\gamma\mu}(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\beta}+\partial_\beta g_{\alpha\mu}-\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta})$ is valid *only* when $(\vec{e}_\alpha)$ is the coordinate basis of the metric, because its derivation uses the symmetry $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\gamma{}_{\beta\alpha}$, which holds only for coordinate bases. The counterexample is the *orthonormal* spherical frame $\vec{e}'_1 = \vec{e}_r$, $\vec{e}'_2 = r^{-1}\vec{e}_\theta$, $\vec{e}'_3 = (r\sin\theta)^{-1}\vec{e}_\varphi$: it is not a coordinate basis (no coordinate system has it as its natural basis), its connection coefficients are *not* symmetric, and the metric-formula gives the wrong answer. In such a frame the connection coefficients (Ricci rotation coefficients) must be computed from $\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\vec{e}'_\beta}\vec{e}'_\alpha$ directly, and there is an extra antisymmetric piece. The repair condition is simply: use a coordinate basis, or compute the coefficients from the definition.

> [!warning] 3. Keeping the Christoffel terms in an exterior derivative
> Having learned that the covariant derivative of a form is $\nabla_\alpha A_\beta = \partial_\alpha A_\beta - \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}A_\mu$, it is tempting to keep those $\Gamma$ terms when writing the exterior derivative $(\mathbf{d}A)_{\alpha\beta} = \nabla_\alpha A_\beta - \nabla_\beta A_\alpha$. They cancel: the term $-\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}A_\mu$ from the first piece and $+\Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\alpha}A_\mu$ from the second are equal by the symmetry $\Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\alpha}$, so $(\mathbf{d}A)_{\alpha\beta} = \partial_\alpha A_\beta - \partial_\beta A_\alpha$ exactly. Writing $(\mathbf{d}A)_{\alpha\beta} = \nabla_\alpha A_\beta - \nabla_\beta A_\alpha$ and then *not* cancelling — or worse, computing the Christoffels and getting a nonzero contribution — is double-counting that produces a non-tensorial mess. The deeper point is that $\mathbf{d}$ is independent of the connection altogether: it would give the same answer for any connection, because antisymmetrisation kills the symmetric part. The "repair" is to trust the cancellation and use partial derivatives.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The computations of this chapter are won by deciding, at the outset, whether the object you are handling is a *tensor field that needs the covariant derivative* or a *differential form that needs the exterior derivative* — because the two derivatives behave completely differently, and conflating them is the source of nearly every error.

If the problem **asks for a covariant derivative or a divergence in curvilinear coordinates**, the decisive first move is to obtain the metric and then the Christoffel symbols. Write the coordinate change to inertial coordinates, form the Jacobian, and read off $g_{\alpha\beta} = J^{\mathsf T}\eta J$; this is mechanical and converts the verbal description into an array of functions. Then either compute the [[Special Relativity XIX/Def - Christoffel Symbols|Christoffel symbols]] from the metric and assemble $\nabla_\beta v^\alpha = \partial_\beta v^\alpha + \Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta}v^\mu$ — remembering $+\Gamma$ for upper indices, $-\Gamma$ for lower — or, if you only need a divergence, bypass the Christoffels entirely with the determinant formula $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{v} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-\det g}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-\det g}\,v^\mu)$ from [[Thm - Divergence of a Vector and Tensor Field]]. The reason the determinant formula is worth reaching for is that it packages the entire trace $\Gamma^\nu{}_{\mu\nu}$ into a single logarithmic derivative of $\det g$, so you never compute a connection coefficient at all. Always sanity-check the curvilinear answer against the flat one: if the field is a constant (inertial) vector dressed in curvilinear clothing, its divergence must come out to whatever it is in inertial coordinates.

If the problem **asks you to verify a tensor identity that the partial derivative would botch**, the strategy is to isolate the connection term. The cleanest such problems use a field built from the coordinate basis itself — $\vec{v} = \vec{e}_r$, whose components are constant — so that $\partial_\beta v^\alpha = 0$ and the *entire* covariant derivative is the Christoffel term $\Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta}v^\mu$. This makes vivid the central claim of the chapter, that $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\vec{e}_r \neq 0$ even though "$\vec{e}_r$ has constant components", and it is the canonical way to see *why* the naive derivative fails. For $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$, the slick route is conceptual — the flat metric is a constant tensor, so its covariant derivative vanishes by definition — but the instructive route is to plug $g_{\alpha\beta}$ and the Christoffels into $\nabla_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta} = \partial_\gamma g_{\alpha\beta} - \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\gamma}g_{\mu\beta} - \Gamma^\mu{}_{\beta\gamma}g_{\alpha\mu}$ and watch it cancel; doing so once, in spherical coordinates, permanently demystifies metric compatibility.

If the problem **involves a differential form**, switch to the exterior derivative and *forget the metric*. The exterior derivative of a form is the alternating sum of partial derivatives of its components — $(\mathbf{d}A)_{\alpha\beta} = \partial_\alpha A_\beta - \partial_\beta A_\alpha$ for a $1$-form, and the pattern of [[Special Relativity XIX/Def - The Exterior Derivative|Def - The Exterior Derivative]] for higher degrees — with no Christoffel symbols, because antisymmetrisation cancels the symmetric connection. The two structural facts that win these problems are $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$ and the graded Leibniz rule. If you must show some exterior derivative vanishes, look for whether the form is itself exact: $F = \mathbf{d}A$ instantly gives $\mathbf{d}F = 0$. If you must differentiate a wedge, apply Leibniz with the sign $(-1)^{\deg A}$ and never forget that sign. And if a three-dimensional vector-calculus identity is lurking, translate it: gradient is $\mathbf{d}$, curl is $\star\mathbf{d}$ on the metric-dual $1$-form, divergence is $-\star\mathbf{d}\star$, and then $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$ delivers both $\mathrm{curl}\,\mathrm{grad} = 0$ and $\mathrm{div}\,\mathrm{curl} = 0$ in one stroke.

The single unifying question of the chapter is this: **which part of what I have written is geometry, and which part is an artefact of my coordinates?** The metric, the Christoffel-corrected derivative, the forms, and the invariants are geometry; the nonzero Christoffel symbols of spherical or rotating coordinates, the position-dependence of the basis, the centrifugal terms in the rotating metric are coordinate artefacts that vanish in inertial coordinates. Every exercise is, at bottom, a drill in keeping these two apart — which is exactly the discipline that makes the transition to a genuinely curved spacetime, where the geometry no longer admits any inertial coordinates at all, a single conceptual step rather than a leap.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Def - The Covariant Derivative|The covariant derivative]]** $\nabla_\beta v^\alpha = \partial_\beta v^\alpha + \Gamma^\alpha{}_{\mu\beta}v^\mu$: the derivative that turns the rate-of-change of a tensor field into another tensor. This is the workhorse of the chapter and of all of relativistic field theory and gravitation. Its recognisable setup is any rate-of-change of a vector or tensor field in coordinates where the basis is not constant; the reusable content is the sign rule ($+\Gamma$ per upper index, $-\Gamma$ per lower, derivation index on the right) and the fact that on the flat metric it satisfies $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$, so raising and lowering indices commutes with it. Reach for it the instant a "$\partial$" of a field needs to be a tensor, and remember that in inertial coordinates it collapses to the ordinary partial derivative — which is both the sanity check and the reason it is the unique sensible generalisation.

- **The Christoffel formula** $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12 g^{\gamma\mu}(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\beta}+\partial_\beta g_{\alpha\mu}-\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta})$ (see [[Special Relativity XIX/Def - Christoffel Symbols|Def - Christoffel Symbols]]): the bridge from "I know the metric" to "I can covariantly differentiate". Its typical use is the first step of any curvilinear computation — feed it $g_{\alpha\beta}$ and it returns the connection. The reusable skills are that $g^{\gamma\mu}$ is diagonal for orthogonal coordinates (so only matching indices survive), that the symmetry $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\gamma{}_{\beta\alpha}$ halves the bookkeeping, and that in flat spacetime the result can always be transformed to zero — so a nonzero $\Gamma$ is never, by itself, a sign of gravity. It is also the literal definition of the gravitational field in general relativity, computed there from a curved metric.

- **The determinant divergence formula** $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{v} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-\det g}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-\det g}\,v^\mu)$ (see [[Thm - Divergence of a Vector and Tensor Field]]): the labour-saving device that computes a divergence without ever computing a Christoffel symbol. Its recognisable setup is any divergence or conservation law in curvilinear coordinates, and its antisymmetric-tensor version $\nabla_\mu T^{\alpha\mu} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{-\det g}}\partial_\mu(\sqrt{-\det g}\,T^{\alpha\mu})$ is the form in which the source side of Maxwell's equations and the conservation of energy–momentum are written. The reusable move is "trace the Christoffels into a log-derivative of $\det g$", which is why this single formula reproduces the spherical, cylindrical, and every other classical divergence as special cases.

- **The exterior derivative** $\mathbf{d}$, with $(\mathbf{d}A)_{\alpha\beta} = \partial_\alpha A_\beta - \partial_\beta A_\alpha$ (see [[Special Relativity XIX/Def - The Exterior Derivative|Def - The Exterior Derivative]]): the metric-free derivative of forms, the unification of gradient, curl, and divergence. Its recognisable setup is any differential form whose rate-of-change is wanted, and its reusable content is that it needs *no* connection — antisymmetrisation cancels the Christoffels — so it is the same in every coordinate system and on every (even curved) spacetime. Reach for it whenever the object is a form rather than a general tensor; it is the operator in which electromagnetism is cleanest ($F = \mathbf{d}A$), and its independence from the metric is what makes the homogeneous Maxwell equations manifestly coordinate-invariant.

- **Nilpotency and Leibniz** $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$ and $\mathbf{d}(A\wedge B) = \mathbf{d}A\wedge B + (-1)^{\deg A}A\wedge\mathbf{d}B$ (see [[Thm - Properties of the Exterior Derivative]]): the two algebraic laws that make the exterior calculus a calculus. The recognisable setup for nilpotency is "$F$ is itself an exterior derivative", which instantly gives $\mathbf{d}F = 0$ and is the reason exact implies closed; the recognisable setup for Leibniz is a wedge product whose derivative is wanted. The reusable content is that $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$ packages the two classical identities $\mathrm{curl}\,\mathrm{grad} = 0$ and $\mathrm{div}\,\mathrm{curl} = 0$, and that the sign $(-1)^{\deg A}$ in Leibniz is the one piece you must never drop. Together with the Poincaré lemma (closed implies exact on star-shaped domains) these laws are the entire engine of de Rham cohomology and of the potential-existence theorems used throughout electromagnetism.

# Bridges

1. **Riemannian geometry — the covariant derivative *is* a connection, and flat spacetime is the zero-curvature case.** A [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation|connection]] on a manifold is a rule $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$ for differentiating tensor fields so that the result is again a tensor; an [[Riemannian Geometry I/Def - Affine Connection on a Vector Bundle|affine connection]] is specified in coordinates by its connection coefficients, and a [[Riemannian Geometry I/Def - Metric-Compatible Connection|metric-compatible]], torsion-free one is the [[Riemannian Geometry I/Def - Levi-Civita Connection|Levi-Civita connection]], unique by the [[Riemannian Geometry I/Thm - Koszul Formula|Koszul formula]] and the [[Riemannian Geometry I/Thm - Fundamental Theorem of Riemannian Geometry (Full Proof)|fundamental theorem of Riemannian geometry]]. The covariant derivative defined in this chapter is precisely that connection, specialised to a spacetime whose metric happens to be the constant $\eta$ in disguise. The condition that pins it down — $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$ together with $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \Gamma^\gamma{}_{\beta\alpha}$ — is the metric-compatibility-plus-torsion-free condition of the manifold theory, word for word; and the Christoffel formula here is identical to the manifold [[Riemannian Geometry I/Def - Christoffel Symbols|Christoffel symbols]]. The *only* thing that distinguishes the present flat case is that the connection has zero curvature, so the Christoffels can be transformed away everywhere at once. The exercise [[Riemannian Geometry I/Ex - The Levi-Civita Connection of Polar Coordinates|computing the Levi-Civita connection of polar coordinates]] is the same calculation as our spherical-Christoffel exercise, performed on Euclidean space.

2. **General relativity — gravity is the Christoffel symbols of a curved metric, and this is the one-line step.** The single most important continuity in physics is that the machinery built here transfers to gravitation by changing exactly one thing: the metric. In [[General Relativity I — Einstein's Equations and Schwarzschild|general relativity]] the constant $\eta_{\mu\nu}$ is promoted to a position-dependent field $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ determined by the matter distribution through the Einstein equations, but the covariant derivative is *still* defined by $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$, the Christoffels are *still* computed by $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12 g^{\gamma\mu}(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\beta}+\partial_\beta g_{\alpha\mu}-\partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta})$, and a freely-falling worldline *still* obeys the geodesic equation $\ddot{x}^\gamma + \Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}\dot{x}^\alpha\dot{x}^\beta = 0$ with the same symbols. What changes is that the curvature, built from the derivatives of the Christoffels via $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho{}_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho{}_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\sigma}$ (see [[Riemannian Geometry III — Riemann Curvature and Topology|Riemann curvature]]), is no longer zero, and *that* non-vanishing is the invariant, coordinate-free fact of a gravitational field. In flat spacetime the centrifugal and Coriolis terms felt by a rotating observer are nonzero Christoffels with zero curvature — fictitious forces; in a real gravitational field they are nonzero Christoffels with nonzero curvature — and no change of coordinates removes them globally. This chapter is therefore literally a dress rehearsal for general relativity on a stage with the curvature switched off.

3. **Exterior calculus on manifolds — the flat-spacetime $\mathbf{d}$ is the manifold exterior derivative.** The exterior derivative defined here by antisymmetrising the covariant derivative is the same operator as the [[Def - Exterior Derivative on a Manifold|exterior derivative on a manifold]] developed in [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms|differential forms]], where it is defined intrinsically without any connection at all. Remark 15.7 of the source makes the agreement explicit: although the present definition uses $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$, the connection terms cancel by the symmetry of the Christoffels, so $\mathbf{d}$ depends on nothing but the differentiable structure — exactly the manifold definition. The consequence is that everything proved here about $\mathbf{d}$ (nilpotency, Leibniz, the Poincaré lemma, the coordinate expansion) is inherited unchanged from the manifold theory, and conversely the flat-spacetime computations are a concrete model in which to practise the abstract identities. The codifferential $-\star\mathbf{d}\star$ introduced at the end of the chapter is the formal adjoint of $\mathbf{d}$ and the operator whose interaction with $\mathbf{d}$ governs the Hodge decomposition of forms.

4. **Vector calculus — $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$ contains $\mathrm{curl}\,\mathrm{grad} = 0$ and $\mathrm{div}\,\mathrm{curl} = 0$.** The three differential operators of classical vector calculus are special cases of the exterior derivative dressed with Hodge stars: on functions $\mathbf{d}$ is the gradient, on the metric-dual $1$-form of a vector $\star\mathbf{d}$ is the curl (worked out explicitly in the source as $(\nabla\times\vec{v})^i = [i,j,k]\partial v^k/\partial x^j$), and $-\star\mathbf{d}\star$ is the divergence. The two famous vanishing identities of vector calculus are then a single statement, $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$: applying it to a $0$-form gives $\mathrm{curl}\,\mathrm{grad} = 0$, and applying it after a Hodge star to a $1$-form gives $\mathrm{div}\,\mathrm{curl} = 0$. This is why those two identities always felt like the same fact wearing two hats — they are. The chapter therefore both generalises the gradient/curl/divergence of three-dimensional space to four-dimensional spacetime and explains, by a single nilpotency, the coincidences that elementary vector calculus presents as unmotivated.

5. **Electromagnetism — the field strength is $\mathbf{d}A$ and charge conservation is a closed form.** Special relativity reads most cleanly on the electromagnetic field, and this chapter supplies the two operators that make it so. The electromagnetic potential is a $1$-form $A$, and the field strength is the $2$-form $F = \mathbf{d}A$ (the source identifies it as exactly the $2$-form of the Lagrangian-coupling chapter); because $F$ is an exterior derivative, the homogeneous Maxwell equations $\mathbf{d}F = \mathbf{d}\mathbf{d}A = 0$ hold automatically, with no computation and no choice of metric — they are pure $\mathbf{d}^2 = 0$. The inhomogeneous pair will use the divergence formula and the Hodge star. Meanwhile the identity $\mathbf{d}\star\underline{v} = (\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot\vec{v})\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ turns the conservation of electric charge, $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\!\cdot J = 0$, into the statement that the $3$-form $\star\underline{J}$ is closed, which — combined with Stokes' theorem in the next chapter — is the geometric content of charge conservation as a vanishing flux through any closed hypersurface. The whole apparatus of [[Special Relativity XXI — The Electromagnetic Field|the electromagnetic field]] and [[Special Relativity XXII — Maxwell's Equations|Maxwell's equations]] is assembled from the covariant derivative and the exterior derivative defined here.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: a position-dependent basis demands a corrected derivative, and the correction is the connection.** The entire chapter follows from one observation. As long as the basis vectors are constant — as in inertial coordinates — the derivative of a vector field is just the array of derivatives of its components, and nothing more needs to be said. The moment the basis vectors themselves vary from event to event — as in spherical or rotating coordinates — the components can be constant while the vector field genuinely changes, because the *basis* is turning underneath. The naive derivative $\partial_\beta v^\alpha$ sees only the components and is therefore blind to this turning; it is not a tensor. The covariant derivative repairs the blindness by adding a term that accounts for how the basis turns, and that term is exactly the connection coefficient: $\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{\vec{e}_\beta}\vec{e}_\alpha = \Gamma^\mu{}_{\alpha\beta}\vec{e}_\mu$ *is* the rate at which $\vec{e}_\alpha$ turns when you move along $\vec{e}_\beta$. Once you see the Christoffel symbol as "the turning of the basis", every formula in the chapter becomes inevitable: the $+\Gamma$ for upper indices and $-\Gamma$ for lower indices is just the product rule applied to $v^\alpha\vec{e}_\alpha$ and to $\omega_\alpha e^\alpha$, and $\boldsymbol{\nabla}g = 0$ says that the metric — which measures lengths and angles between basis vectors — does not change even as the basis turns, because turning preserves lengths and angles.

**The true name of the Christoffel symbol is "fictitious force", and curvature is the part that is not fictitious.** The official definition of $\Gamma^\gamma{}_{\alpha\beta}$ is a combination of metric derivatives, but the operationally useful characterisation is physical: the Christoffel symbols are the centrifugal, Coriolis, and inertial "forces" that appear when you insist on using non-inertial coordinates. A rotating observer attributes the outward drift of a free particle to a centrifugal force; that force is a Christoffel symbol of the rotating metric, and it is fictitious precisely because a single change of coordinates — to the inertial frame — makes it vanish everywhere. This is the deepest lesson the flat case teaches, and it is the one that makes general relativity comprehensible: gravity *looks* like a Christoffel symbol (the equivalence principle says a freely-falling observer feels no gravity, just as an inertial observer feels no centrifugal force), but it differs in one decisive respect — its Christoffels cannot all be transformed away at once. The obstruction is the curvature, the part of the connection that survives every change of coordinates. So the chapter draws the exact line between what gravitation shares with a rotating frame (the Christoffel symbols, the geodesic equation, the apparent forces) and what makes it irreducibly different (the curvature), and it does so on a spacetime where you can verify, by transforming to inertial coordinates, that the curvature is zero.

**The exterior derivative is the half of the connection that needs no metric — and that is why it carries the topology.** It is striking that two derivatives live in this chapter, and that one of them, the exterior derivative, is independent of the metric while the other, the covariant derivative, is not. The reason is antisymmetrisation. The covariant derivative of a form has a symmetric Christoffel correction; antisymmetrising over all slots cancels that correction completely, leaving an operator built from partial derivatives alone. Because $\mathbf{d}$ needs no metric, it is insensitive to the geometry and sees only the differentiable and topological structure of spacetime — which is exactly why the closed-modulo-exact quotient it defines (de Rham cohomology) measures topology, not geometry. On a star-shaped region every closed form is exact (the Poincaré lemma), so the cohomology is trivial and potentials always exist; on a region with holes, closed-but-not-exact forms detect the holes. This is the conceptual payoff of the chapter's final third: the metric-dependent covariant derivative is the right tool for the local, geometric content of physics (proper times, curvatures, divergences), while the metric-free exterior derivative is the right tool for the global, topological content (the existence of potentials, the conservation laws, the quantisation of flux). Maxwell's equations split along exactly this seam — the homogeneous pair $\mathbf{d}F = 0$ is topological and automatic, the inhomogeneous pair is geometric and dynamical — and recognising the seam is recognising why electromagnetism takes the shape it does.
