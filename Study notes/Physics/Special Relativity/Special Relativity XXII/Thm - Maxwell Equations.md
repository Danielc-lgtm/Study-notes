# Notation

We set $c = 1$ and use the signature $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$. The electromagnetic field is the antisymmetric $2$-form $F$, components $F_{\mu\nu}$; $F^\sharp$ is the type-$(2,0)$ tensor $F^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\alpha}\eta^{\nu\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}$. The [[Def - The Electric Four-Current|four-current]] is $J$, with metric-dual $1$-form $\underline J$; $\star$ is the [[Def - The Hodge Star|Hodge star]], $d$ the [[Def - The Exterior Derivative|exterior derivative]], $\nabla$ the [[Def - The Covariant Derivative|covariant derivative]]. The constants satisfy $\varepsilon_0\mu_0 = c^{-2} = 1$. Relative to an observer of four-velocity $U_0$, the field gives the electric and magnetic fields $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf B$ and the current gives $\rho$, $\mathbf J$. Brackets on indices denote antisymmetrisation, $T_{[\alpha\beta\gamma]} = \tfrac{1}{3!}(T_{\alpha\beta\gamma} \pm \cdots)$. Full registry on [[Special Relativity XXII — Maxwell's Equations]].

# Statement

> **Theorem (Maxwell's equations).** The electromagnetic field $F$ generated by an electric four-current $J$ on Minkowski spacetime satisfies the two equations
> $$dF = 0 \qquad\text{(homogeneous)}, \qquad d{\star}F = \mu_0\,{\star}J \qquad\text{(inhomogeneous)}.$$
> The homogeneous equation is equivalent, in components, to the **Bianchi identity**
> $$\partial_\alpha F_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta F_{\gamma\alpha} + \partial_\gamma F_{\alpha\beta} = 0, \qquad \text{i.e.}\qquad \partial_{[\alpha}F_{\beta\gamma]} = 0,$$
> and the inhomogeneous equation is equivalent, via $\star d{\star}F^\sharp = \nabla\cdot F^\sharp$, to the **divergence form**
> $$\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0\,J^\nu.$$

> **Companion form (three-dimensional).** Relative to a rigid array of inertial observers of four-velocity $U_0$, with $F$ decomposed into the electric field $\mathbf E$ and magnetic field $\mathbf B$ and $J$ into the charge density $\rho$ and current density $\mathbf J$, the two tensor equations are equivalent to the four three-dimensional Maxwell equations:
> $$\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf E = -\frac{\partial\mathbf B}{\partial t} \qquad (\text{from } dF = 0),$$
> $$\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla\times\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf J + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} \qquad (\text{from } d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J).$$

# Motivation

These are the equations the whole tensor-calculus arc has been building toward. The previous topic gave the electromagnetic field $F$ as a single antisymmetric tensor and showed how it acts on charge; this theorem is the law that says how $F$ is *generated* by charge. Without it, $F$ is a free-floating object with no dynamics; with it, electromagnetism is a complete theory — given the charges, the field is determined.

The reason to state Maxwell's equations as $dF = 0$ and $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$, rather than as the four three-dimensional equations of the textbook, is that this form makes the structure visible and the invariance manifest. The four three-dimensional equations look like four unrelated facts; the two tensor equations reveal that they are two, and that the two have very different characters. The homogeneous equation is geometry — it holds automatically once a potential exists, it needs no metric, it is invariant under all coordinate changes. The inhomogeneous equation is dynamics — it carries the source, it uses the metric through the Hodge star, and it is invariant precisely under the metric-preserving transformations, the Lorentz group. Seeing this split is the point of the covariant formulation, and it is what makes the generalisation to gauge theory possible.

There is also a profound historical inversion buried here. Maxwell wrote his equations in the 1860s, in a preferred frame (the aether), and they already predicted a frame-independent wave speed $c$ — which is impossible under the Galilean transformation. The equations were relativistic two decades before relativity. The entire labour of Lorentz, Poincaré, and Einstein was to find the transformations under which Maxwell's equations are invariant, and that search produced the Lorentz transformation. So Maxwell's equations are not an application of special relativity; they are its parent. The covariant form $dF = 0$, $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$ is the statement of that parentage: it exhibits the equations as manifestly Lorentz-invariant, the property whose discovery created the subject.

# Sources and Targets

**Sources (Input Broadening)**

The theorem's precondition is "$F$ is the electromagnetic field generated by the four-current $J$". Recognising the disguises this wears is most of the skill.

The first disguised source is **"$F = dA$ for some potential $A$"**. Whenever the field is expressed through a potential, the homogeneous half of the theorem is automatic — $dF = d(dA) = 0$ by $d^2 = 0$ — and you need only impose the inhomogeneous half. The bridge is the Poincaré lemma run backwards: a potential's existence *is* the homogeneous equation. This is nonobvious because one usually thinks of $dF = 0$ as a law to verify, when in fact it is a consequence of having a potential. *Example problem:* given $A_\mu$, show that the resulting $F$ automatically obeys Faraday's law and the no-monopole law without any further computation.

The second disguised source is **"the charge and current densities $\rho$, $\mathbf J$ relative to some observer are given"**. A problem stated in three-dimensional language — "a wire carries current $I$", "a region has charge density $\rho$" — is a problem about the four-current $J^\mu = (\rho, \mathbf J)$ in that observer's frame, and the theorem applies once you assemble $J$. The bridge is the [[Def - The Electric Four-Current|four-current's]] observer-decomposition $J = \rho U_0 + \mathbf J$. The nonobviousness is that the four-dimensional theorem governs every three-dimensional electromagnetics problem; the three-dimensional equations are its shadows. *Example problem:* derive the magnetic field of a current-carrying wire by assembling its four-current and applying $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$.

The third disguised source is **"the field is a known solution of the wave equation"**. In vacuum $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$ and $dF = 0$ together force $\Box F = 0$; conversely a divergence-free, closed $2$-form satisfying the wave equation is a vacuum Maxwell field. So any object built as a transverse, null wave automatically satisfies Maxwell in vacuum. The bridge is that $\Box F = 0$ with $dF = 0$ reconstructs both equations. *Example problem:* verify that a plane wave $F = F_1(x - ct)$ with transverse, null structure solves the source-free Maxwell equations.

**Targets (Output Amplification)**

The conclusion is "$F$ obeys $dF = 0$ and $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$".

Combine the conclusion with **applying $d$ to the inhomogeneous equation**. Since $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$, applying $d$ gives $0 = d(d{\star}F) = \mu_0\,d{\star}J$, so $d{\star}J = 0$ — charge conservation. The further result is the [[Thm - Electric Charge Conservation and the Gauss Theorem|continuity equation]] $\nabla\cdot J = 0$, obtained for free. The combination is nonobvious because conservation looks like independent physics but is forced by the equations' structure; it is among the most useful facts in the topic. *Example:* prove that no consistent solution of Maxwell's equations can have charge appearing without a compensating current.

Combine the conclusion with **a potential and the Lorenz gauge**. Writing $F = dA$ and imposing $\nabla\cdot A = 0$, the inhomogeneous equation $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$ becomes $\Box A^\nu = \mu_0 J^\nu$. The further result is that solving Maxwell reduces to solving four scalar wave equations, the gateway to the retarded potential and [[Thm - Electromagnetic Waves|electromagnetic waves]]. The combination is the foundation of every solution method. *Example:* solve for the field of an arbitrary source via the retarded Green function.

Combine the conclusion with **integration over a closed hypersurface**. Since $d{\star}J = 0$, [[Thm - Stokes Theorem on Spacetime|Stokes' theorem]] gives $\oint\star J = 0$ over any closed hypersurface, hence the total charge is conserved in time; and $\oint\star F = \mu_0 Q$ over a closed $2$-surface gives Gauss's law. The further result is the integral form of electromagnetism. The combination converts the local (differential) equations into global (integral) conservation and flux laws. *Example:* derive Gauss's law $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S = Q/\varepsilon_0$ from $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$.

# Why Is It True

The deepest way to see why Maxwell's equations have this two-part shape is to recognise that the two parts answer two different questions, and that one of them is not really a physical law at all.

**The homogeneous equation $dF = 0$ is true because $F = dA$, and "the field has a potential" is half of what Maxwell's equations *mean*.** The bolded mechanism: **once the field is the exterior derivative of a potential, $dF = d(dA) = 0$ is forced by the single algebraic fact $d^2 = 0$, so the homogeneous equation is an identity of the calculus of forms, not a contingent law of nature.** It could not have been otherwise; any field expressible through a potential satisfies it. Physically, $dF = 0$ packages two statements — that there are no magnetic monopoles ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$) and that a changing magnetic field induces an electric field (Faraday, $\nabla\times\mathbf E = -\partial_t\mathbf B$) — and both follow from the existence of $A$.

The inhomogeneous equation $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$ is the genuine dynamical law, and its shape is dictated by three demands. First, it must relate the field to its source $J$, so the source must appear; the natural source object is the $3$-form $\star J$, whose integral over a spatial slice is the charge. Second, the left side must be a $3$-form built from $F$ that can equal $\star J$; the field $F$ is a $2$-form, its Hodge dual $\star F$ a $2$-form, and the only first-order $3$-form built from it is $d{\star}F$. Third, the equation must be Lorentz-invariant; the Hodge star $\star$, which uses the metric, is exactly the ingredient that makes $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$ invariant under the metric-preserving transformations and no others. So the form of the inhomogeneous equation is nearly forced: it is the simplest Lorentz-invariant first-order relation between the field $2$-form and the source $3$-form.

The component translation is mechanical but illuminating. The homogeneous $dF = 0$ unpacks to the cyclic sum $\partial_\alpha F_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta F_{\gamma\alpha} + \partial_\gamma F_{\alpha\beta} = 0$, the Bianchi identity; choosing the indices to include or exclude the time direction sorts these into Faraday's law (one time index) and no-monopole (all spatial). The inhomogeneous $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$, after taking the Hodge star and metric dual, becomes $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$; the $\nu = 0$ component is Gauss's law ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$) and the spatial components are Ampère's law with Maxwell's displacement-current correction. The four three-dimensional equations are thus the time-and-space components of two four-dimensional equations, sorted by how many of their indices point along the observer's time direction.

The displacement current — the term $\tfrac{1}{c^2}\partial_t\mathbf E$ in Ampère's law that Maxwell added by hand and that makes light possible — is not added by hand here. It is the inevitable companion of the spatial current $\mathbf J$ in the four-current $J^\mu = (\rho, \mathbf J)$, forced by the requirement that $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$ be consistent with $\nabla\cdot J = 0$. In the covariant form there is simply no Ampère's law without the displacement current; they are one equation.

# What Makes This Hard

The conceptual hurdle is believing that the homogeneous equation is *free* — that $dF = 0$ requires no physics beyond the existence of a potential — while the inhomogeneous one is the entire dynamical content; people often treat all four three-dimensional equations as equally fundamental. The technical hurdle is the index bookkeeping of $\star d{\star}F^\sharp = \nabla\cdot F^\sharp$, where one must carefully track the Hodge star's action on a $2$-form (it returns a $2$-form in four dimensions), the double-dual sign $\star\star = -1$ on $2$-forms in Lorentzian signature, and the antisymmetry of $F$; a dropped sign there inverts the source term. The most common error is to mis-sort the three-dimensional equations — putting Faraday with Gauss — by not tracking which four-dimensional equation (homogeneous or inhomogeneous) and which index components each comes from.

# Rederivation Scaffold

**This section is self-sufficient: reading only what follows should let you reconstruct the entire derivation.**

**High-level strategy:**
The two equations $dF = 0$ and $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$ are postulated (or derived from the action, see [[Thm - Maxwell Equations from a Principle of Least Action]]). Everything else is translation: unpack $dF = 0$ into the Bianchi identity by writing out the exterior derivative of a $2$-form; convert $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$ to $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$ using the Hodge-star/divergence identity; then project both onto an observer to sort into the four three-dimensional equations.

**Subgoal decomposition:**

1. **Unpack $dF = 0$ into components.** Write $(dF)_{\alpha\beta\gamma} = \partial_\alpha F_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta F_{\gamma\alpha} + \partial_\gamma F_{\alpha\beta}$ and set it to zero.
   - *Hint:* The exterior derivative of a $2$-form is the antisymmetrised gradient; the three cyclic terms come from the three ways to single out a derivative index.
   - *Why needed:* It produces the Bianchi identity, the component form of the homogeneous equation.

2. **Convert the inhomogeneous equation to divergence form.** Use $\star d{\star}F^\sharp = \nabla\cdot F^\sharp$ (valid because $F$ is antisymmetric) to turn $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$ into $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$.
   - *Hint:* Take the Hodge star of both sides; on the left, $\star d{\star}(\cdot)$ acting on $F^\sharp$ is the divergence; on the right, $\star{\star}J = J$ up to sign (use $\star\star = -1$ on the appropriate degree).
   - *Why needed:* The divergence form is what couples to the potential and what projects cleanly onto an observer.

3. **Project the homogeneous equation onto an observer.** Set the Bianchi index $\gamma = 0$ (one time index) to get Faraday's law; set all indices spatial to get the no-monopole law.
   - *Hint:* $F_{0i} \leftrightarrow E_i$ and $F_{ij} \leftrightarrow \epsilon_{ijk}B_k$; one time index in the cyclic sum produces $\partial_t\mathbf B + \nabla\times\mathbf E$.
   - *Why needed:* It recovers $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf E = -\partial_t\mathbf B$ from $dF = 0$.

4. **Project the inhomogeneous equation onto an observer.** Set $\nu = 0$ in $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$ for Gauss's law; set $\nu$ spatial for Ampère's law.
   - *Hint:* $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu 0} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf E$ and $J^0 = \rho$; the spatial components give $\nabla\times\mathbf B$ with the displacement current $\partial_t\mathbf E/c^2$ from the $\mu = 0$ term.
   - *Why needed:* It recovers $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ and Ampère's law from $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$.

# Lemma Decomposition

> [!note]- Lemma 1: The homogeneous equation is the Bianchi identity
> **Statement:** $dF = 0$ holds if and only if $\partial_\alpha F_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta F_{\gamma\alpha} + \partial_\gamma F_{\alpha\beta} = 0$ for all $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$.
>
> **Hint:** Write out the components of the exterior derivative of a $2$-form.
>
> **Why needed:** It gives the component (index) form of the homogeneous equation, from which the three-dimensional Faraday and no-monopole laws are read off.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > The exterior derivative of a $2$-form $F = \tfrac12 F_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu\wedge dx^\nu$ is the $3$-form $dF = \tfrac12 \partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\lambda\wedge dx^\mu\wedge dx^\nu$. Its components, fully antisymmetrised, are $(dF)_{\lambda\mu\nu} = \partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu}$ (the three cyclic permutations, each with $+$ sign because $F$ is already antisymmetric and the wedge supplies the alternation; see [[Def - The Exterior Derivative]]). Setting $dF = 0$ is equivalent to the vanishing of this cyclic sum for every triple of indices, which is the Bianchi identity $\partial_{[\alpha}F_{\beta\gamma]} = 0$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 2: The inhomogeneous equation is the divergence form
> **Statement:** $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$ is equivalent to $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$.
>
> **Hint:** Use the identity $\star d{\star}F^\sharp = \nabla\cdot F^\sharp$ for the antisymmetric tensor $F^\sharp$, and $\star\star J = J$ up to sign.
>
> **Why needed:** The divergence form is the equation that couples to the potential and projects to Gauss's and Ampère's laws.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > For a $2$-form $F$ in four-dimensional Lorentzian spacetime, the double Hodge dual satisfies $\star\star F = -F$. Apply $\star$ to both sides of $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$. On the right, $\star{\star}J = J$ for the $3$-form $\star J$ (the $1$-form $\underline J$ has $\star\star\underline J = -\underline J$ on $1$-forms, and tracking the degree gives $\star(\star J) = -\underline J$ up to the convention; with our signs the metric-dual reproduces $J^\nu$). On the left, the combination $\star d\star$ applied to a $2$-form is (minus) the codifferential, and raising indices gives the divergence: explicitly, computing $(\star d{\star}F)^\nu = \tfrac{1}{3!}\epsilon^{\mu\rho\sigma\nu}(d{\star}F)_{\mu\rho\sigma}$ and using $\nabla_\mu\epsilon^{\mu\rho\sigma\nu} = 0$ (the Levi-Civita tensor is covariantly constant) reduces it to $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu}$. Hence $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$. In inertial coordinates this is $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$, and the Christoffel terms vanish. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 3: The homogeneous equation gives Faraday and no-monopole
> **Statement:** With $F_{0i} = E_i$ and $F_{ij} = -\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$ in an observer's frame, the Bianchi identity gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf E = -\partial_t\mathbf B$.
>
> **Hint:** Take all three indices spatial for no-monopole; take one index $= 0$ for Faraday.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the three-dimensional content of $dF = 0$, the half of Maxwell that is automatic from the potential.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Take $\alpha,\beta,\gamma = 1,2,3$ (all spatial) in the Bianchi identity: $\partial_1 F_{23} + \partial_2 F_{31} + \partial_3 F_{12} = 0$. With $F_{ij} = -\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$ this reads $-(\partial_1 B_1 + \partial_2 B_2 + \partial_3 B_3) = 0$, i.e. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$. Now take one index equal to $0$, say $\alpha = 0$, $\beta = i$, $\gamma = j$ spatial: $\partial_0 F_{ij} + \partial_i F_{j0} + \partial_j F_{0i} = 0$. With $F_{ij} = -\epsilon_{ijk}B_k$ and $F_{0i} = E_i$, this is $-\partial_t(\epsilon_{ijk}B_k) - \partial_i E_j + \partial_j E_i = 0$, which after contracting with $\epsilon_{ij\ell}$ gives $\partial_t B_\ell + (\nabla\times\mathbf E)_\ell = 0$, i.e. $\nabla\times\mathbf E = -\partial_t\mathbf B$. $\blacksquare$

> [!note]- Lemma 4: The inhomogeneous equation gives Gauss and Ampère
> **Statement:** With $F^{0i} = -E^i$ and $F^{ij} = -\epsilon^{ijk}B_k$, the divergence form $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$ gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf J + \tfrac{1}{c^2}\partial_t\mathbf E$.
>
> **Hint:** Set $\nu = 0$ for Gauss; $\nu = i$ for Ampère, where the $\mu = 0$ term supplies the displacement current.
>
> **Why needed:** It is the three-dimensional content of the dynamical equation, including the displacement current that makes waves possible.
>
> > [!note]- Full proof
> > Set $\nu = 0$: $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu 0} = \partial_i F^{i0} = \partial_i E^i = \nabla\cdot\mathbf E$ (using $F^{00} = 0$ and $F^{i0} = E^i$), equal to $\mu_0 J^0 = \mu_0\rho = \rho/(\varepsilon_0 c^2) \cdot c^2$; with $c = 1$, $\mu_0 = 1/\varepsilon_0$, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$. Set $\nu = i$ spatial: $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu i} = \partial_0 F^{0i} + \partial_j F^{ji} = -\partial_t E^i + (\nabla\times\mathbf B)^i$ (using $F^{0i} = -E^i$ and $F^{ji} = -\epsilon^{jik}B_k$ giving $\partial_j F^{ji} = (\nabla\times\mathbf B)^i$), equal to $\mu_0 J^i$. Rearranging, $(\nabla\times\mathbf B)^i = \mu_0 J^i + \partial_t E^i$; restoring $c$, the time term is $\tfrac{1}{c^2}\partial_t E^i$, the **displacement current**, which arose entirely from the $\mu = 0$ (time-derivative) part of the divergence. $\blacksquare$

# Formal Proof

> [!note]- Complete formal proof
> We take the covariant equations $dF = 0$ and $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$ as the statement of the law (they are derived from a variational principle in [[Thm - Maxwell Equations from a Principle of Least Action]]) and establish their equivalence to the Bianchi/divergence and three-dimensional forms.
>
> **Homogeneous equation.** By Lemma 1, $dF = 0$ is equivalent in components to the Bianchi identity $\partial_\alpha F_{\beta\gamma} + \partial_\beta F_{\gamma\alpha} + \partial_\gamma F_{\alpha\beta} = 0$. By Lemma 3, projecting this onto an inertial observer — taking all indices spatial, then one index temporal — yields $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf E = -\partial_t\mathbf B$.
>
> **Inhomogeneous equation.** By Lemma 2, $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$ is equivalent to the divergence form $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$, using the identity $\star d{\star}F^\sharp = \nabla\cdot F^\sharp$ and the covariant constancy of the Levi-Civita tensor. By Lemma 4, projecting this onto the observer — $\nu = 0$, then $\nu$ spatial — yields $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf J + \tfrac{1}{c^2}\partial_t\mathbf E$, the displacement current arising from the time-derivative part of the divergence.
>
> **Consistency.** Applying $\nabla_\nu$ to the divergence form gives $\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0\nabla_\nu J^\nu$; the left side vanishes because $\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu$ is symmetric in $\mu,\nu$ while $F^{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric, so $\nabla\cdot J = 0$ — charge conservation is a necessary consequence (proved fully in [[Thm - Electric Charge Conservation and the Gauss Theorem]]). The component counts match: the two covariant equations are identities between $3$-forms (or vectors), each with $4$ independent components, totalling $8$; the three-dimensional equations are $1 + 3 + 1 + 3 = 8$ scalar/vector components. $\blacksquare$

# Cross-Field Exercise Suggestions

**Magnetostatics from the covariant equation.** The Biot–Savart law for the magnetic field of a steady current is the spatial part of $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$ in the static limit, solved via the vector potential. Deriving it from the covariant equation, rather than postulating it, is the divergence-form theorem applied to a static current; the application is nonobvious because Biot–Savart is usually presented as an independent empirical law.

**Waveguides and boundary conditions.** The behaviour of electromagnetic fields in a waveguide is governed by the source-free Maxwell equations plus boundary conditions on the conductor; the allowed modes are solutions of $\Box F = 0$ subject to $\mathbf E_\parallel = 0$ at the walls. Recognising the mode equation as the Maxwell wave equation with constraints is the theorem applied in a confined geometry; the application is out-of-distribution because waveguide modes are usually treated by separation of variables without reference to the covariant origin.

**Magnetohydrodynamics.** In a conducting fluid, Maxwell's equations couple to the fluid four-current $J = \rho_0 U + \sigma F\cdot U$ (Ohm's law in covariant form), and the resulting MHD equations govern plasmas, stellar interiors, and accretion disks. Recognising the field dynamics as Maxwell with a constitutive current is the theorem applied to continuous matter; the application is surprising because MHD is usually presented as a fluid theory, with electromagnetism as an add-on rather than the covariant Maxwell system.

# Bridges

- **[[Def - The Four-Potential]]** — the homogeneous equation $dF = 0$ is precisely the statement that $F$ has a potential, $F = dA$; this theorem's first half *is* the existence of the four-potential, by the Poincaré lemma. Introducing $A$ makes $dF = 0$ automatic and reduces the inhomogeneous equation to a wave equation, so the potential is the computational gateway opened by the homogeneous half of this theorem.

- **[[Thm - Electric Charge Conservation and the Gauss Theorem]]** — applying $\nabla_\nu$ to the divergence form $\nabla_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu$ and using the antisymmetry of $F^{\mu\nu}$ forces $\nabla\cdot J = 0$; charge conservation is a corollary of this theorem, not an independent postulate. The same structure ($\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu(\text{antisymmetric}) = 0$) recurs in gravitation, where it forces $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$.

- **[[Thm - Hodge Star and the Exterior Product]]** — the inhomogeneous equation's Hodge star is what carries the metric and makes the equation Lorentz-invariant; converting $d{\star}F$ to the divergence $\nabla\cdot F^\sharp$ relies on the double-dual identity $\star\star = -1$ on $2$-forms and the covariant constancy of the Levi-Civita tensor, both established in the Hodge-duality machinery of topic XVIII.

- **[[Gauge Theory I — Connections on Vector Bundles and the Electromagnetic Connection]]** — the two-part structure of Maxwell generalises to Yang–Mills: the homogeneous equation $dF = 0$ becomes the Bianchi identity $DF = 0$ that every curvature satisfies, and the inhomogeneous equation becomes the Yang–Mills equation $D{\star}F = {\star}J$, now nonlinear because $F = dA + A\wedge A$ carries the self-interaction $A\wedge A$. Electromagnetism is the abelian case where the bracket vanishes.

> [!tip] The Wave Equation and Electromagnetic Radiation *(from §22.2)*
> Combining the two equations in vacuum forces $\Box F = 0$, the [[Thm - Electromagnetic Waves|wave equation]] whose solutions are electromagnetic waves travelling at $c$ — the discovery that light is electromagnetic. With a source, the inhomogeneous equation plus the Lorenz gauge gives $\Box A = \mu_0 J$, solved by the retarded potential.

> [!tip] Yang–Mills Theory and the Standard Model *(from Gauge Theory and QFT)*
> The covariant form $dF = 0$, $d{\star}F = \mu_0{\star}J$ is the abelian template for the **Yang–Mills equations** governing the strong and weak interactions. Promoting $A$ to a Lie-algebra-valued connection and $F$ to its curvature $F = dA + A\wedge A$, the homogeneous equation becomes the nonabelian Bianchi identity and the inhomogeneous one becomes a nonlinear field equation in which the field sources itself. The Standard Model is the gauge theory of $\mathrm{SU}(3)\times\mathrm{SU}(2)\times\mathrm{U}(1)$; Maxwell is its $\mathrm{U}(1)$ factor.
