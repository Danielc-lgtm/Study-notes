# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we set $c = 1$** and use the **mostly-minus signature** $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, so a timelike vector has $X\cdot X > 0$, a spacelike one $X\cdot X < 0$, and a null one $X\cdot X = 0$. Where a formula is more recognisable with $c$ restored, it is given both ways. The primary source for this chapter, Gourgoulhon's *Special Relativity in General Frames*, uses the opposite "mostly-plus" signature $\mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$; every scalar product, norm, and Lorentz-factor relation transcribed from it has had its overall sign flipped to match ours. The coordinate matrices $\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta$ of a linear map in a given basis are **signature-independent** — the signature governs only how scalar products are computed, not the entries of a matrix in a fixed basis — so the classification matrices below are reproduced verbatim.

- $E$ — the vector space underlying Minkowski spacetime $\mathbb{M}$; four-dimensional, carrying the metric $\eta$
- $\Lambda$ — a Lorentz transformation, an element of the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]] $O(1,3)$; $\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta$ its matrix in an orthonormal basis
- $\eta$, $\eta_{\alpha\beta}$ — the [[Def - Minkowski Space and the Metric|Minkowski metric]], $\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$
- $(e_0, e_1, e_2, e_3)$ — a right-handed orthonormal (pseudo-orthonormal) basis: $e_0\cdot e_0 = +1$, $e_i\cdot e_i = -1$ for $i = 1,2,3$, $e_0$ future-pointing
- $X\cdot Y$, $g(X,Y)$ — the Minkowski inner product $\eta_{\alpha\beta}X^\alpha Y^\beta = X^0Y^0 - X^1Y^1 - X^2Y^2 - X^3Y^3$
- $O(1,3)$ — the **Lorentz group**; $SO(1,3)$ the proper ($\det = +1$) subgroup; $O^+(1,3)$ the orthochronous ($\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$) subgroup; $SO^+(1,3)$ the **restricted** (proper orthochronous) Lorentz group, the connected component of the identity
- $I, P, T$ — spacetime inversion $-\mathrm{Id}$, parity $\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, time reversal $\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$ (the discrete reflections)
- $\ell, k$ — future-directed null vectors, normalised by $\ell\cdot k = 2$ (mostly-minus; Gourgoulhon's $\ell\cdot k = -2$)
- $\psi$ — the **rapidity** of a boost, $\Gamma = \cosh\psi$, $V = \tanh\psi$; the same quantity that [[Special Relativity IV — The Invariant Interval, Rapidity and the Lorentz Group|Special Relativity IV]] writes $\varphi$ (see [[Def - Rapidity]])
- $\Gamma$ — the **Lorentz factor** of a boost, $\Gamma = \cosh\psi = (1 - V^2)^{-1/2}$ (with $c$: $(1 - V^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$)
- $V$, $\mathbf{V}$ — the **velocity parameter** $V = \tanh\psi$ and the **velocity vector** $\mathbf{V} = V\,e_1$ of a boost relative to a 4-velocity $u$
- $\theta$ — the angle of a **spatial rotation** (the angle in the invariant timelike plane's orthogonal complement)
- $\alpha$ — the parameter $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^+$ of a **null rotation**
- $\chi$ — the angle between the two velocity vectors $\mathbf{V}_1, \mathbf{V}_2$ of a pair of boosts being composed (Gourgoulhon's $\theta$ in §6.7)
- $\varphi_T$ — the **Thomas rotation angle** (also called the **Wigner angle**)
- $\Pi$, $\Pi^\perp$ — a two-dimensional plane and its orthogonal complement; $E = \Pi \oplus^\perp \Pi^\perp$ is a $2+2$ decomposition
- $\mathrm{tr}\,\Lambda$ — the trace of $\Lambda$, basis-independent
- Summation convention: a repeated index, once up once down, is summed over $0,1,2,3$; Latin $i,j$ run over $1,2,3$

# Motivation

Here is the chapter in one sentence: the [[Def - The Lorentz Group|Lorentz group]] $O(1,3)$ has a precise anatomy — four connected pieces, a single connected core, and inside that core exactly two kinds of building block — and once you know that anatomy every Lorentz transformation becomes a short list of moves. [[Special Relativity IV — The Invariant Interval, Rapidity and the Lorentz Group|Special Relativity IV]] defined $O(1,3)$ as the linear maps preserving the interval and split it into four components by the two signs $\det\Lambda = \pm 1$ and $\Lambda^0{}_0 \gtrless 0$. That was a coarse classification. This chapter is the fine one: it dissects the connected core $SO^+(1,3)$ — the *restricted* Lorentz group, the only piece physics uses, because it is the only piece that connects continuously to the identity and so can be reached by a real physical process.

The first surprise is that there are not three kinds of restricted transformation but two. Naively one expects rotations (mixing space with space), boosts (mixing time with space), and perhaps some hybrid. The algebra says otherwise. Every restricted Lorentz transformation admits at least one **invariant null direction** — a light ray it maps to itself — and the entire classification flows from counting these. If there are two distinct invariant null directions the transformation is a **four-screw**, a boost and a rotation acting in two orthogonal planes simultaneously (rotations and boosts are the degenerate cases where one of the two is trivial). If there is exactly one, the transformation is a **null rotation**, a parabolic motion sliding along the light cone that has no Euclidean analogue at all. There is nothing else. This is the structural backbone of the chapter, the dichotomy that organises §9.2:

$$
\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Lambda \ \text{is a}\
\begin{cases}
\textbf{four-screw} & \text{(two invariant null directions: boost} \times \text{rotation)},\\[2pt]
\textbf{null rotation} & \text{(one invariant null direction: parabolic)}.
\end{cases}
$$

The proof is genuinely algebraic. One picks a null eigenvector $\ell$, builds a partner null vector $k$ with $\ell\cdot k = 2$, and from the pair $(\ell, k)$ manufactures an adapted orthonormal frame in which the matrix of $\Lambda$ depends on exactly three parameters — a rapidity $\psi$, a rotation angle $\theta$, and a null-rotation parameter $\alpha$. Reading off which parameters survive gives the whole taxonomy, and the eigenvalues fall out of a factored characteristic polynomial. No spacetime diagram is consulted; the figures are mnemonics for an argument that stands entirely on the bilinear form.

The centrepiece is §9.3's **polar decomposition**: relative to any chosen observer's 4-velocity $e_0$, every restricted $\Lambda$ factors *uniquely* as a boost times a rotation, $\Lambda = S \circ R$, with $S$ a boost whose plane contains $e_0$ and $R$ a spatial rotation of $e_0$'s rest space. This is the relativistic shadow of the linear-algebra theorem that any invertible matrix is a positive-definite-symmetric matrix times an orthogonal one — the boost is the symmetric positive part, the rotation is the orthogonal part. It is the single most useful structural fact about the group, because it lets you separate the "change of velocity" content of a transformation from its "change of spatial orientation" content.

And it has a sting in its tail. The decomposition is unique, but it does *not* make $SO^+(1,3)$ a product of a boost-group and a rotation-group, because the boosts do not form a subgroup. Compose two boosts whose planes are not coplanar and the result is *not* a boost: it is a boost times a leftover rotation, the **Thomas rotation**. Derived algebraically in §9.3 — compose two non-collinear boosts, polar-decompose the product, and the rotation factor is the Thomas rotation — this residual twist is the kinematic seed of **Thomas precession**, the relativistic correction to the precession of a spinning body, which [[Special Relativity XVI — Accelerated Observers|Special Relativity XVI]] takes up. The deepest statement of the chapter is the reason this all hangs together: $SO^+(1,3)$ is a *simple* group, with no normal subgroups at all, so no clean factorisation into smaller groups can exist — the Thomas rotation is the obstruction made visible.

The reader is assumed to know the contents of [[Special Relativity IV — The Invariant Interval, Rapidity and the Lorentz Group|Special Relativity IV]] thoroughly — the group $O(1,3)$, its four components, rapidity, and boosts as hyperbolic rotations — and to be comfortable with basic group theory (subgroups, normal subgroups, quotient groups, the Klein four-group) and with eigenvalue/eigenvector computation over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$. The genuinely new ingredient is not heavy: it is the willingness to take the *null* structure of the metric as the organising principle, building frames from null vectors rather than from the timelike $e_0$. That single change of viewpoint is what turns the Lorentz group from a list of matrices into a structure with a small, knowable shape.

# Concept Map

## §9.1 Subgroups and Components of O(1,3)

- **[[Def - Subgroups and Components of the Lorentz Group]]**
	- The Lorentz group $O(1,3)$ has four connected components, cut out by the two locally-constant signs $\det\Lambda = \pm 1$ (proper/improper) and $\Lambda^0{}_0 \gtrless 0$ (orthochronous/antichronous). The subgroups are the **proper** $SO(1,3)$ ($\det = +1$), the **orthochronous** $O^+(1,3)$ ($\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$), and their intersection the **restricted** $SO^+(1,3)$, the component of the identity. The three reflections — spacetime inversion $I = -\mathrm{Id}$, parity $P = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, time reversal $T = \mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$ — reduce any $\Lambda$ to a restricted one by left-multiplication, and $\{\mathrm{Id}, I, P, T\}$ is a copy of the Klein four-group $\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$. Only $SO^+(1,3)$ is connected to $\mathrm{Id}$, hence only it is reachable by a continuous physical process.

- **[[Thm - The Restricted Lorentz Group is a Normal Subgroup]]**
	- $SO^+(1,3)$ is a normal subgroup of $O(1,3)$: for every $\Lambda \in O(1,3)$ and $\Lambda_0 \in SO^+(1,3)$, the conjugate $\Lambda\Lambda_0\Lambda^{-1}$ is again restricted, because conjugation preserves both the determinant and (a short argument shows) the sign of the time-component. The quotient $O(1,3)/SO^+(1,3)$ is the four-element group $\{\mathrm{Id}, I, P, T\} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$, recording which of the four components $\Lambda$ lives in. The antichronous and improper *sets* are not subgroups — none contains the identity — but their cosets are exactly the non-identity classes of the quotient.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Lie Algebra of the Lorentz Group *(from Special Relativity X)*
> The connected component $SO^+(1,3)$ is a six-dimensional Lie group, and its tangent space at the identity is the **Lie algebra** $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ — three rotation generators and three boost generators. The component structure of this chapter is the global, discrete shadow of that local, continuous structure; the exponential map fills out $SO^+(1,3)$ from $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$, and the discrete reflections $P, T$ are precisely what the exponential map cannot reach. See [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]] and [[Def - Lie Algebra of the Lorentz Group]].

- **[[Ex - Proving the restricted Lorentz group is a subgroup]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Verify directly from $\det$ and $\Lambda^0{}_0$ that $SO^+(1,3)$ is closed under products and inverses and contains the identity, using $(\det\Lambda)^2 = 1$ and the inequality $\Lambda^0{}_0\Lambda'^0{}_0 \le (\Lambda\Lambda')^0{}_0$ that the reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality supplies for the time-components.

- **[[Ex - Reducing any Lorentz transformation to a restricted one]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Given an arbitrary $\Lambda \in O(1,3)$, decide its component from $\mathrm{sgn}(\det\Lambda)$ and $\mathrm{sgn}(\Lambda^0{}_0)$, then write it as one of $\Lambda_0$, $I\Lambda_0$, $P\Lambda_0$, $T\Lambda_0$ with $\Lambda_0 \in SO^+(1,3)$, exhibiting the reduction explicitly.

- **[[Ex - The discrete reflections form the Klein four-group]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Show $\{\mathrm{Id}, I, P, T\}$ is closed under composition with $I = PT = TP$, each element its own inverse, hence isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$, and identify it with the quotient $O(1,3)/SO^+(1,3)$.

## §9.2 Classification of Restricted Lorentz Transformations

- **[[Thm - Invariant Null Direction of a Restricted Lorentz Transformation]]**
	- Every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ leaves invariant at least one null direction: there is a nonzero null vector $\ell$ with $\Lambda(\ell) = \lambda\ell$, $\lambda > 0$. The clean proof is topological — $\Lambda$ permutes the null directions, which form the celestial sphere $S^2$ (the intersection of the past light cone with a spacelike hyperplane), inducing an orientation-preserving continuous self-map of $S^2$, and every such map has a fixed point (Brouwer/Lefschetz). The existence of this null eigenvector is the seed of the whole classification: it provides the first vector of an adapted frame.

- **[[Def - Classification of Restricted Lorentz Transformations]]**
	- Starting from an invariant null direction $\mathrm{Span}(\ell)$, one builds a partner null vector $k$ with $\ell\cdot k = 2$ and an adapted orthonormal frame $(e_0, e_1, e_2, e_3)$ with $e_0 = \tfrac12(\ell + k)$, $e_1 = \tfrac12(\ell - k)$, in which $\Lambda$'s matrix depends on three parameters: a rapidity $\psi$, a rotation angle $\theta$, and a null-rotation parameter $\alpha \ge 0$. The four named special cases are the **spatial rotation** ($\psi = 0$, $\alpha = 0$: a Euclidean rotation of a spacelike plane), the **Lorentz boost** ($\alpha = 0$, $\theta = 0$: a hyperbolic rotation of a timelike plane), the **null rotation** ($\psi = 0$, $\theta = 0$: a parabolic slide along the light cone), and the general **four-screw** (a commuting boost and rotation in orthogonal planes). Every restricted transformation is a four-screw or a null rotation.

- **[[Def - Null Rotations and Four-Screws]]**
	- A **four-screw** of timelike plane $\Pi$ is the composition of a boost of $\Pi$ and a spatial rotation of $\Pi^\perp$; the two factors commute and the transformation has exactly two invariant null directions (the two null lines of $\Pi$). A **null rotation** leaves invariant a *null plane* — a degenerate two-plane $\mathrm{Span}(\ell, e_3)$ tangent to the light cone, whose induced metric has signature $(0,-)$ — and has exactly one invariant null direction. The null rotation is the genuinely non-Euclidean element: its matrix is $\mathrm{Id}$ plus a nilpotent, so $(\Lambda - \mathrm{Id})^2 \ne 0$ but $(\Lambda - \mathrm{Id})^3 = 0$, a Jordan block that no rotation or boost exhibits.

- **[[Thm - Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a Lorentz Boost]]**
	- A Lorentz boost of rapidity $\psi$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_+ = e^{\psi}$, $\lambda_- = e^{-\psi}$, and $\lambda_0 = 1$ (multiplicity two). The eigenvectors of $\lambda_\pm$ are the two *null* vectors $\ell_\pm = e_0 \pm e_1$ spanning the light-cone lines of the boost plane; the eigenvalue $1$ belongs to the entire spacelike plane $\mathrm{Span}(e_2, e_3)$ fixed pointwise. In velocity variables $\lambda_\pm = \sqrt{(1\pm V)/(1\mp V)}$ are exactly the relativistic Doppler factors, so the boost stretches one light-cone generator by the redshift factor and shrinks the other by the blueshift factor.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Double Cover SL(2,ℂ) and the Algebraic Proof *(from Special Relativity XI)*
> The topological existence proof for an invariant null direction has a purely algebraic counterpart through the **spinor map**: a restricted Lorentz transformation lifts to a $2\times 2$ complex matrix $A \in SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, whose action on the celestial sphere $\mathbb{C}P^1$ is a Möbius transformation, and a Möbius transformation always has a fixed point because the characteristic polynomial of $A$ always has a root over $\mathbb{C}$. The four-screw/null-rotation dichotomy is then the loxodromic/parabolic dichotomy of Möbius maps. See [[Special Relativity XI — SL(2,C), Spinors and the Spinor Map]] and [[Thm - Lorentz Group Covered by SL(2,C)]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: Wigner's Classification and the Little Group *(from Special Relativity XII)*
> The set of transformations fixing a chosen null or timelike direction is a **little group**, and Wigner's classification of relativistic particles is exactly the representation theory of these little groups: $SO(3)$ for a massive particle (whose rest 4-momentum is fixed by spatial rotations) and the null-rotation group $ISO(2)$ for a massless particle (whose null 4-momentum is fixed by null rotations together with rotations about it). The null rotation, the odd element of this chapter's classification, is precisely the generator of helicity for massless particles. See [[Special Relativity XII — Inertial Observers and the Poincaré Group]] and [[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group]].

- **[[Ex - Classifying a given Lorentz transformation by its invariant null directions]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Given an explicit restricted $\Lambda$, find its invariant null directions by solving $\Lambda(\ell) = \lambda\ell$ for null $\ell$, count them, and conclude whether $\Lambda$ is a boost, a rotation, a four-screw, or a null rotation; cross-check against the trace formulas $\cosh\psi = \tfrac12\mathrm{tr}\,\Lambda - 1$ and $\cos\theta = \tfrac12\mathrm{tr}\,\Lambda - 1$.

- **[[Ex - A null rotation fixes a unique null direction and is parabolic]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Write the null-rotation matrix in an adapted frame, verify it fixes $\mathrm{Span}(\ell)$ and the null plane $\mathrm{Span}(\ell, e_3)$, show $N := \Lambda - \mathrm{Id}$ satisfies $N^3 = 0$ but $N^2 \ne 0$ (a single Jordan block of size three), and confirm the eigenvalue $1$ has algebraic multiplicity four but geometric multiplicity two.

- **[[Ex - The eigenvalues of a boost are the Doppler factors]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Diagonalise a boost in the null basis $(\ell_+, \ell_-, e_2, e_3)$, obtain eigenvalues $e^{\pm\psi}$ on the null directions, identify $e^{\pm\psi} = \sqrt{(1\pm V)/(1\mp V)}$ with the longitudinal Doppler factors, and explain why a boost has real eigenvalues while a rotation has complex ones.

- **[[Ex - A four-screw is characterised by two invariant null directions]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Prove the equivalence "two distinct invariant null directions $\iff$ four-screw" both ways: a four-screw fixes both null lines of its boost plane, and conversely two invariant null directions $\ell, k$ force the partner construction to terminate with $\alpha = 0$, yielding the commuting boost-times-rotation form.

## §9.3 Polar Decomposition, Boosts and the Thomas Rotation

- **[[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group]]**
	- Relative to any unit future-timelike vector $e_0$, every restricted $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ factors uniquely as $\Lambda = S \circ R$, where $S$ is a boost whose plane contains $e_0$ (with Lorentz factor $\Gamma = e_0\cdot\Lambda(e_0)$ and plane $\mathrm{Span}(e_0, \Lambda(e_0))$) and $R$ is a spatial rotation of the rest space $e_0^\perp$. The proof builds $S$ from the image $\Lambda(e_0)$ — the unique boost carrying $e_0$ to $\Lambda(e_0)$ — and sets $R = S^{-1}\Lambda$, which fixes $e_0$ and is therefore a pure spatial rotation. This is the special-relativistic instance of the linear-algebra polar decomposition $M = (\text{symmetric positive-definite}) \times (\text{orthogonal})$: the boost is the symmetric positive part, the rotation the orthogonal part.

- **[[Def - Thomas Rotation]]**
	- The **Thomas rotation** (or **Wigner rotation**) is the spatial rotation $R$ left over when the composition of two non-collinear Lorentz boosts is polar-decomposed: $\Lambda_2 \circ \Lambda_1 = S \circ R$, where $S$ is a boost and $R \ne \mathrm{Id}$ a rotation in the plane spanned by the two boost velocities. It exists precisely because the boosts do not form a subgroup: the product of two non-coplanar boosts is a four-screw, not a boost. The Thomas rotation is the kinematic origin of **Thomas precession**, the relativistic correction to the precession rate of a spinning particle following a curved worldline.

- **[[Thm - Composition of Coplanar Boosts gives a Boost times Thomas Rotation]]**
	- If two boosts share a plane (collinear velocities), their product is again a boost, with rapidities adding, $\psi = \psi_1 + \psi_2$, and Lorentz factor $\Gamma = \Gamma_1\Gamma_2(1 + V_1V_2)$; coplanar boosts form an abelian subgroup. If the planes differ (velocities at angle $\chi \in (0,\pi)$), the product $\Lambda_2 \circ \Lambda_1$ is *not* a boost: its matrix is not symmetric, so by the polar decomposition it equals a boost $S$ times a nontrivial Thomas rotation $R$. The composite boost has $\Gamma = \Gamma_1\Gamma_2(1 + V_1V_2\cos\chi)$, recovering the relativistic velocity-addition law, and the residual $R$ is the Thomas rotation.

- **[[Thm - The Thomas Rotation Angle]]**
	- The Thomas rotation angle $\varphi_T$ for two boosts of Lorentz factors $\Gamma_1, \Gamma_2$, velocity moduli $V_1, V_2$, at angle $\chi$, satisfies $\cos\varphi_T = 1 - \dfrac{(\Gamma_1-1)(\Gamma_2-1)}{1+\Gamma}\sin^2\chi$ with $\Gamma = \Gamma_1\Gamma_2(1 + V_1V_2\cos\chi)$, and $-\pi \le \varphi_T \le 0$ (a clockwise rotation). For perpendicular boosts ($\chi = \pi/2$) it reduces to $\cos\varphi_T = (\Gamma_1+\Gamma_2)/(1+\Gamma_1\Gamma_2)$. The remarkably symmetric Macfarlane form $\cos(\varphi_T/2) = (1+\Gamma+\Gamma_1+\Gamma_2)/\sqrt{2(1+\Gamma)(1+\Gamma_1)(1+\Gamma_2)}$ and the Stapp form for $\sin\varphi_T$ express it through the three Lorentz factors alone.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Thomas Precession *(from Special Relativity XVI)*
> A gyroscope carried around a closed loop in velocity space — for instance an electron orbiting a nucleus, continuously accelerated and so continuously boosted in changing directions — accumulates a net spatial rotation equal to the sum of the infinitesimal Thomas rotations along the loop. In the limit this becomes the **Thomas precession** rate $\boldsymbol{\Omega}_T = \dfrac{\gamma^2}{\gamma+1}\,\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{v}$, a purely kinematic precession with no force behind it, which famously supplies the missing factor of $\tfrac12$ in the spin–orbit coupling of atomic physics. See [[Special Relativity XVI — Accelerated Observers]] and [[Def - Thomas Precession]].

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Gyrogroup Structure of Velocity Space *(from the algebra of velocity addition)*
> Relativistic velocity addition $\mathbf{V}_1 \oplus \mathbf{V}_2$ is neither commutative nor associative, and the Thomas rotation is the exact obstruction to both: $\mathbf{V}_1 \oplus \mathbf{V}_2 = R[\mathbf{V}_1,\mathbf{V}_2]\,(\mathbf{V}_2 \oplus \mathbf{V}_1)$, and a parallel identity repairs associativity. The open ball of velocities with $\oplus$ is the prototype **gyrogroup** (Ungar's term), a group-like structure in which the Thomas rotation plays the role of an automorphism-valued cocycle. This is the precise algebraic content of "boosts do not form a subgroup," and it is why $SO^+(1,3)$, being **simple**, admits no factorisation respecting the boost/rotation split.

- **[[Ex - Proving the polar decomposition of a restricted Lorentz transformation]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Carry out the polar decomposition for a concrete $\Lambda$ — compute $\Lambda(e_0)$, build the boost $S$ carrying $e_0$ to it, form $R = S^{-1}\Lambda$, verify $R$ fixes $e_0$ and is a rotation — then prove uniqueness in general by showing the boost factor is forced by $\Lambda(e_0)$ alone.

- **[[Ex - The Thomas rotation angle for two perpendicular boosts]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compose two perpendicular boosts of equal speed, extract the Thomas rotation from the polar decomposition of the product matrix, and verify the angle $\cos\varphi_T = (\Gamma_1+\Gamma_2)/(1+\Gamma_1\Gamma_2)$, checking the non-relativistic limit $\varphi_T \approx -\tfrac12 V_1 V_2$.

- **[[Ex - Boosts do not form a subgroup and the Wigner rotation]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Show that the product of two non-coplanar boosts is not symmetric (hence not a boost), identify the residual Wigner rotation, and use the gyrogroup identity $\mathbf{V}_1 \oplus \mathbf{V}_2 = R\,(\mathbf{V}_2 \oplus \mathbf{V}_1)$ to demonstrate that velocity addition fails to commute by exactly the Thomas angle.

- **[[Ex - The KAK decomposition and the six parameters]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Establish the rotation–boost–rotation factorisation $\Lambda = R(H)\,B[V]\,R(H')$ of an arbitrary restricted $\Lambda$ (a Cartan $KAK$ decomposition), and reconcile the apparent seven parameters (two $SO(3)$ matrices plus one rapidity) with the group being six-dimensional by locating the one-parameter redundancy.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The problems of this chapter chase a recognisable handful of goals, all structural rather than numerical. The most common is to **classify a transformation**: given a Lorentz matrix, or a description of how it acts, decide which of the four components it lives in and — if restricted — which of the two intrinsic types (four-screw or null rotation) it is, and find its invariant directions, its rapidity, its rotation angle. A second target is to **establish a subgroup or normal-subgroup claim**: that $SO^+(1,3)$ is a subgroup, that it is normal, that the coplanar boosts form an abelian subgroup, that the boosts as a whole do *not*. These are verified by closure arguments on the defining inequalities ($\det$, $\Lambda^0{}_0$) and on symmetry of matrices. A third is to **factor a transformation** — to produce its polar decomposition $S \circ R$, or its $KAK$ rotation–boost–rotation form — and to prove the factorisation unique. A fourth, the most physical, is to **extract the Thomas rotation**: to compose two boosts, recognise that the product is not a boost, and compute the leftover rotation angle, usually as a step toward Thomas precession. A fifth is to **compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors** and read their meaning: that a boost's eigenvalues are Doppler factors on null eigenvectors, that a rotation's are unimodular complex numbers, that a null rotation is a nontrivial Jordan block. Across all five the unifying remark is that you understand a Lorentz transformation when you know its invariant subspaces — which null directions, planes, and vectors it fixes — because the invariant structure *is* the classification.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **A restricted transformation is given** — explicitly as a matrix, or implicitly as "a Lorentz transformation continuously connected to the identity," or as "the transformation relating the frames of two observers" (which is automatically restricted, since both frames are right-handed and future-pointing). This immediately licenses the existence of an invariant null direction and hence the whole adapted-frame machinery. **A null vector or null eigenvector is in hand** — then it serves as the first vector $\ell$ of the partner construction, and a second null vector $k$ with $\ell\cdot k = 2$ completes a timelike plane. **A boost is recognised** — its plane is timelike and spanned by a 4-velocity, its matrix in a semi-adapted basis is symmetric, its eigenvalues are real, and it is fully determined by its plane and its rapidity. **A pair of boosts with given velocities is given** — then their composition is governed by the velocity-addition law for the boost factor and the Thomas-angle formula for the rotation factor, and the angle $\chi$ between the velocities is the controlling parameter. **A 4-velocity $e_0$ is chosen** — this is the input to the polar decomposition, splitting the transformation relative to that observer into a boost (change of velocity) and a rotation (change of orientation). The recurring move is to route a source to a target: a given restricted matrix routes through the invariant-null-direction theorem to a classification; a chosen $e_0$ routes through the polar decomposition to a boost-rotation split; two given boost velocities route through velocity addition and the Thomas-angle formula to a composite four-screw. The [[Special Relativity IX — The Lorentz Group, Structure and Classification#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves nearly every problem in the chapter is assembled from. The reader's difficulty here is algebraic bookkeeping, not physics, so each operation states exactly which invariant object it produces or consumes. When stuck, scan the list and try each one.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Read the component of $\Lambda$ from its two signs.** Compute $\det\Lambda$ and $\Lambda^0{}_0$: the pair of signs $(\mathrm{sgn}\det\Lambda, \mathrm{sgn}\Lambda^0{}_0)$ names one of the four components, and $SO^+(1,3)$ is the $(+,+)$ piece; see [[Def - Subgroups and Components of the Lorentz Group]]. *Trigger:* any classification question, or any "is this transformation physical" question. *Pattern:* $(\det\Lambda)^2 = 1$ and $(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 = 1 + \sum_i(\Lambda^i{}_0)^2 \ge 1$ are free from $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$, so the two signs are always available.

2. **Reduce to a restricted transformation by a reflection.** Left-multiply $\Lambda$ by whichever of $I, P, T$ flips its signs to $(+,+)$: $\Lambda = I\Lambda_0$, $P\Lambda_0$, or $T\Lambda_0$ with $\Lambda_0 \in SO^+(1,3)$; see [[Thm - The Restricted Lorentz Group is a Normal Subgroup]]. *Trigger:* the transformation is improper or antichronous and you want to apply restricted-group theory. The reflections are involutions, so the reduction is its own inverse.

3. **Find an invariant null direction.** Solve $\Lambda(\ell) = \lambda\ell$ for a *null* vector $\ell$ (impose $\ell\cdot\ell = 0$ on a real eigenvector); the existence is guaranteed for restricted $\Lambda$ by [[Thm - Invariant Null Direction of a Restricted Lorentz Transformation]]. *Trigger:* you want to classify $\Lambda$ or build an adapted frame. *Pattern:* the null eigenvector is the first vector $\ell$ of the partner construction; its eigenvalue $\lambda = e^\psi$ gives the rapidity.

4. **Build the adapted frame from a null pair.** Given a future null eigenvector $\ell$, pick a second future null vector $k$ not collinear with $\ell$, rescale so $\ell\cdot k = 2$, and set $e_0 = \tfrac12(\ell+k)$, $e_1 = \tfrac12(\ell-k)$, completing an orthonormal tetrad with $\Pi = \mathrm{Span}(e_0,e_1)$ timelike and $\Pi^\perp = \mathrm{Span}(e_2,e_3)$ spacelike; see [[Def - Classification of Restricted Lorentz Transformations]]. *Trigger:* you have one invariant null direction and want the three-parameter normal form. This is the central labour-saving device of §9.2.

5. **Count invariant null directions to name the type.** Two distinct invariant null directions $\Rightarrow$ four-screw; exactly one $\Rightarrow$ null rotation; three or more $\Rightarrow$ identity; see [[Def - Null Rotations and Four-Screws]]. *Trigger:* you have computed the null eigenvectors and want the classification. *Pattern:* the boost and the rotation are the four-screws with, respectively, $\theta = 0$ and $\psi = 0$.

6. **Use the trace to extract the parameter.** For a boost, $\cosh\psi = \tfrac12\mathrm{tr}\,\Lambda - 1$; for a spatial rotation, $\cos\theta = \tfrac12\mathrm{tr}\,\Lambda - 1$ (the trace being basis-independent); see [[Thm - Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a Lorentz Boost]] and [[Def - Classification of Restricted Lorentz Transformations]]. *Trigger:* you know the type and want the rapidity or angle without diagonalising. *Pattern:* $\mathrm{tr}\,\Lambda = 2\cosh\psi + 2$ for a boost, $2\cos\theta + 2$ for a rotation.

7. **Polar-decompose relative to a chosen 4-velocity.** Pick $e_0$; build the boost $S$ with plane $\mathrm{Span}(e_0,\Lambda(e_0))$ and Lorentz factor $\Gamma = e_0\cdot\Lambda(e_0)$ carrying $e_0 \mapsto \Lambda(e_0)$; set $R = S^{-1}\Lambda$, a spatial rotation fixing $e_0$; then $\Lambda = S\circ R$; see [[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group]]. *Trigger:* you want to separate the velocity-change content from the orientation-change content, or to extract a Thomas rotation. *Pattern:* the boost is determined entirely by the image $\Lambda(e_0)$; everything else is rotation.

8. **Compose boosts by velocity addition plus a Thomas rotation.** For two boosts of velocities $\mathbf{V}_1, \mathbf{V}_2$ at angle $\chi$, the product is a boost $S$ (with $\Gamma = \Gamma_1\Gamma_2(1+V_1V_2\cos\chi)$, velocity the relativistic sum) times a Thomas rotation $R$ in $\mathrm{Span}(\mathbf{V}_1,\mathbf{V}_2)$; see [[Thm - Composition of Coplanar Boosts gives a Boost times Thomas Rotation]]. *Trigger:* you must compose two boosts. If they are collinear ($\chi = 0$ or $\pi$) the rotation vanishes and rapidities simply add.

9. **Diagonalise in the null basis.** To find a boost's eigenstructure, work in $(\ell_+, \ell_-, e_2, e_3)$ with $\ell_\pm = e_0\pm e_1$: the boost is diagonal there, $\mathrm{diag}(e^\psi, e^{-\psi}, 1, 1)$; see [[Thm - Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a Lorentz Boost]]. *Trigger:* any eigenvalue/eigenvector question about a boost, or a check that a transformation is a boost. *Pattern:* real eigenvalues on null directions signal a boost; unimodular complex eigenvalues signal a rotation; a single eigenvalue $1$ with a size-three Jordan block signals a null rotation.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating the boosts as a subgroup
> It is overwhelmingly tempting to think the boosts form a subgroup of $SO^+(1,3)$, by analogy with the way they form a one-parameter subgroup along a fixed direction. They do *not*. The composition of two boosts whose planes are not coplanar is a **four-screw**, not a boost: its matrix in a semi-adapted basis fails to be symmetric ([[Thm - Composition of Coplanar Boosts gives a Boost times Thomas Rotation]]), and the leftover is the Thomas rotation. Concretely, compose a boost along $x$ with a boost along $y$: the product rotates the $z$-fixed plane through a nonzero angle, as the explicit matrix shows. The operation becomes legal exactly when the two boosts are **collinear** (coplanar planes, $\chi = 0$ or $\pi$) — only then do they share a plane, commute, and compose to a boost with rapidities adding. The set of *all* boosts is not a group because $SO^+(1,3)$ is simple and has no proper normal subgroup for them to be.

> [!warning] 2. Assuming the spatial part of a Lorentz matrix is a rotation
> Given $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$, one is tempted to read off the lower-right $3\times 3$ block $\Lambda^i{}_j$ as "the rotation part." This block is generally *not* orthogonal: for a pure boost along $x$ it is $\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1)$ on $(y,z)$ but $\Lambda^1{}_1 = \cosh\psi \ne 1$, so the block is not in $SO(3)$ at all. The actual rotation content is recovered only through the **polar decomposition** ([[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group]]): the rotation $R = S^{-1}\Lambda$, after the boost $S$ is divided out, is what is genuinely orthogonal. The naive reading is legal only when $\Lambda$ is *already* a pure rotation (block form $\mathrm{diag}(1, H)$ with $H \in SO(3)$), i.e. when $\Lambda(e_0) = e_0$.

> [!warning] 3. Expecting every restricted transformation to be diagonalisable
> Carrying over the intuition that rotations and boosts diagonalise (over $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ respectively), one is tempted to conclude that every restricted Lorentz transformation is diagonalisable. The **null rotation** is the counterexample: its only eigenvalue is $1$, with algebraic multiplicity four but geometric multiplicity two, so it has a nontrivial Jordan block of size three and cannot be diagonalised over any field ([[Def - Null Rotations and Four-Screws]]). The matrix $\Lambda - \mathrm{Id}$ is nilpotent of index three. Diagonalisability is restored only for four-screws — transformations with two distinct invariant null directions — which is exactly the condition that the parameter $\alpha$ vanish.

> [!warning] 4. Forgetting the scale factor when a map preserves the null cone
> When proving the invariant-null-direction theorem or relating the null cone to the metric, one is tempted to argue that a linear map preserving the null cone $\{X\cdot X = 0\}$ must preserve the metric outright. It need only preserve the metric *up to a positive scalar*: a map sending the cone to itself satisfies $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \kappa\,\eta$ for some $\kappa > 0$, because two quadratic forms with the same zero locus are proportional, not equal. The scale $\kappa$ is killed only by an extra input — the relativity-principle symmetry $\kappa(V)\kappa(-V) = 1$ in the interval-invariance proof. Dropping the scale factor turns the conformal group (which preserves the cone) into the Lorentz group (which preserves the metric), and they differ by exactly the dilations.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this chapter are won the moment you stop thinking of a Lorentz transformation as a matrix and start thinking of it as a set of *invariant subspaces*. Almost every question is "what does this transformation leave fixed?", and the answer — which null directions, which planes, which vectors — determines everything else.

If the problem **asks to classify a transformation**, the decisive first move is to find its invariant null directions. Solve $\Lambda(\ell) = \lambda\ell$ subject to $\ell\cdot\ell = 0$. For a restricted $\Lambda$ at least one solution exists ([[Thm - Invariant Null Direction of a Restricted Lorentz Transformation]]); count them. Two distinct null directions mean a four-screw, and the boost and rotation parts read off from the two null eigenvalues $e^{\pm\psi}$ and the rotation acting on the orthogonal spacelike plane. Exactly one null direction means a null rotation, and you can confirm it by checking that $\Lambda - \mathrm{Id}$ is nilpotent. If you would rather not diagonalise, the trace shortcuts give the parameters directly: $\tfrac12\mathrm{tr}\,\Lambda - 1$ equals $\cosh\psi$ for a boost and $\cos\theta$ for a rotation ([[Thm - Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a Lorentz Boost]]). The reason the null-direction count works is structural: the classification *is* the count, because the three-parameter normal form is built from a null pair, and the parameters that survive are dictated by how many null directions are fixed.

If the problem **is a subgroup question** — is this set a subgroup, is it normal, is it abelian — the instrument is a closure check on the defining data. For $SO^+(1,3)$, verify that products and inverses preserve $\det = +1$ and $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$; the determinant is multiplicative and the time-component inequality follows from the reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for future-timelike vectors ([[Ex - Proving the restricted Lorentz group is a subgroup]]). For normality, check that conjugation preserves the component, which it does because conjugation preserves both signs ([[Thm - The Restricted Lorentz Group is a Normal Subgroup]]). For the boosts, the closure check *fails*: the product of non-coplanar boosts is not symmetric, hence not a boost, and the obstruction is the Thomas rotation. The meta-lesson is that "is this a subgroup" reduces to "is the defining property closed under composition," and the answer is most informative when it is *no*.

If the problem **asks to factor a transformation**, choose the observer first. The polar decomposition is relative to a 4-velocity $e_0$, and the boost factor is determined entirely by the image $\Lambda(e_0)$: build the unique boost carrying $e_0$ to $\Lambda(e_0)$, then the rotation is whatever is left, $R = S^{-1}\Lambda$, which fixes $e_0$ ([[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group]]). The route is always the same — compute $\Lambda(e_0)$, read the boost off it, divide it out, identify the rotation — and uniqueness follows because the boost factor has no freedom once $\Lambda(e_0)$ is fixed. The same philosophy gives the $KAK$ form: peel off a rotation on each side to standardise the boost along a coordinate axis.

If the problem **involves composing boosts**, do not multiply the matrices blindly. Decompose the computation into its two physical outputs: the velocity-addition law gives the boost factor's velocity and Lorentz factor, $\Gamma = \Gamma_1\Gamma_2(1 + V_1V_2\cos\chi)$, and the Thomas-angle formula gives the rotation factor ([[Thm - The Thomas Rotation Angle]]). The controlling parameter is the angle $\chi$ between the two boost velocities: at $\chi = 0$ or $\pi$ the boosts are collinear, the rotation vanishes, and rapidities add; at intermediate $\chi$ a genuine Thomas rotation appears, maximal near $\chi = \pi/2$. When the problem wants Thomas precession, recognise that an accelerating particle is being boosted in continuously changing directions, and the precession is the accumulated infinitesimal Thomas rotation around the velocity-space loop.

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle: **build your frame from null vectors, not from the timelike axis.** The Euclidean instinct is to start with $e_0$ and rotate the spatial axes; the Lorentzian classification is cleanest when you start with a null eigenvector $\ell$, manufacture its partner $k$, and let the timelike $e_0 = \tfrac12(\ell+k)$ emerge. The null cone is the invariant geometry of the Lorentz group — it is what every Lorentz transformation preserves and what distinguishes the three types of plane — so the frame adapted to the null structure is the frame in which the transformation is simplest. Every question in this chapter is, at bottom, the question "how does this transformation move the light cone?"

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - Invariant Null Direction of a Restricted Lorentz Transformation|Existence of an invariant null direction]]**: every $\Lambda \in SO^+(1,3)$ fixes at least one null direction. This is the most-used single fact in the classification, because it is *free* — it costs nothing and applies to any restricted transformation — and it bootstraps everything: the null eigenvector it supplies is the first vector of the adapted frame, from which the three-parameter normal form and the entire taxonomy follow. Reach for it the instant you must classify a transformation or build a frame adapted to it. Its deepest disguised use is as the bridge to $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$: the null direction is a fixed point of the induced Möbius map on the celestial sphere, which always exists because a complex polynomial always has a root. Recognise its applicability whenever a restricted transformation appears and you need a distinguished direction to anchor a computation.

- **[[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group|The polar decomposition]]** $\Lambda = S \circ R$: relative to a chosen 4-velocity, boost times rotation, uniquely. This is the workhorse of all structural calculation in the chapter. Its recognisable setup is any situation where you must separate "how the velocity changes" from "how the spatial orientation changes" — a change of observer, a sequence of boosts, an extraction of a Thomas rotation. The reusable move is always the same: the boost is read off the image $\Lambda(e_0)$, and the rotation is the remainder $S^{-1}\Lambda$. It is the relativistic instance of the matrix polar decomposition $M = (\text{positive symmetric})(\text{orthogonal})$, so all the intuition from linear algebra transfers. Combine it with the composition of boosts and it *produces* the Thomas rotation; combine it with a basis choice and it *gives* the $KAK$ form.

- **The trace formulas** $\tfrac12\mathrm{tr}\,\Lambda - 1 = \cosh\psi$ (boost) or $\cos\theta$ (rotation): the parameter without diagonalising. Because the trace is basis-independent, these let you extract the rapidity of a boost or the angle of a rotation from the raw matrix, in any frame, in one line. The typical use is a quick classification check or a parameter readout once the type is known. The reusable skill is reading the trace as a single dial: a trace exceeding $4$ signals a boost (since $\cosh\psi \ge 1$), a trace below $4$ signals a rotation (since $\cos\theta \le 1$), and the value pins down the parameter. Reach for it whenever a problem hands you a matrix and asks "what boost is this" or "what angle is this."

- **The boost is symmetric, the rotation is orthogonal**: the matrix criteria that separate the two building blocks. A boost in a semi-adapted basis has $\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta = \Lambda^\beta{}_\alpha$ (symmetric), while a spatial rotation has the block form $\mathrm{diag}(1, H)$ with $H^{\mathsf T}H = I$ (orthogonal). The reusable content is a fast test: to decide whether a given transformation is a boost, check symmetry; to extract the rotation content, polar-decompose. This is exactly why the product of two non-coplanar boosts is *not* a boost — its matrix is not symmetric — and the asymmetry *is* the Thomas rotation. Recognise its applicability whenever you need to tell a boost from a four-screw, or to verify a claimed factorisation.

- **The eigenvalues of a boost are Doppler factors** $\lambda_\pm = e^{\pm\psi} = \sqrt{(1\pm V)/(1\mp V)}$ on null eigenvectors: the spectral signature of a boost. Its recognisable setup is any eigenvalue computation, or any need to connect the abstract rapidity to a measurable quantity. The reusable insight is twofold: the eigenvectors are *null* (the boost stretches one light-cone generator and shrinks the other), and the eigenvalues are the longitudinal redshift and blueshift factors, so the rapidity is the logarithm of the Doppler shift. Combine this with the classification and it distinguishes a boost (real eigenvalues, null eigenvectors) from a rotation (complex eigenvalues) from a null rotation (a single Jordan block). It is the calculation that makes the rapidity physically tangible.

# Bridges

1. **Linear algebra — the Lorentz polar decomposition is the matrix polar decomposition specialised.** The matrix polar decomposition theorem states that any invertible real matrix $M$ factors uniquely as $M = SR$ with $S$ symmetric positive-definite and $R$ orthogonal ($R^{\mathsf T}R = I$); $S = (MM^{\mathsf T})^{1/2}$ and $R = S^{-1}M$. The polar decomposition of a restricted Lorentz transformation ([[Thm - Polar Decomposition of the Lorentz Group]]) is exactly this theorem applied inside $SO^+(1,3)$: the boost $S$ is the symmetric positive-definite factor — symmetric because a boost's matrix in a semi-adapted basis satisfies $\Lambda^\alpha{}_\beta = \Lambda^\beta{}_\alpha$, positive-definite because its eigenvalues $e^{\psi}, e^{-\psi}, 1$ are all strictly positive — and the rotation $R$ is the orthogonal factor, with $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ in the ordinary (not metric) sense, because a spatial rotation has block form $\mathrm{diag}(1, H)$ with $H \in SO(3)$. The one subtlety is that "orthogonal" here means $R^{\mathsf T}R = I_4$, the identity in the literal transpose sense, *not* $R^{\mathsf T}\eta R = \eta$; both happen to hold for a spatial rotation, but it is the former that makes the analogy with the linear-algebra theorem exact. The proof structure is identical — read off the symmetric part from the image, divide it out, the remainder is orthogonal — and the uniqueness has the same source: the positive symmetric factor is forced.

2. **Group theory — the four components and the Klein four-group quotient.** A [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]]'s set of connected components is itself a group, the quotient by the identity component, and for the Lorentz group this quotient is the Klein four-group. The two sign functions $\det\Lambda \in \{\pm 1\}$ and $\mathrm{sgn}\,\Lambda^0{}_0 \in \{\pm 1\}$ are homomorphisms $O(1,3) \to \mathbb{Z}/2$, and together they give a surjection $O(1,3) \to \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$ whose kernel is the restricted group $SO^+(1,3)$ ([[Thm - The Restricted Lorentz Group is a Normal Subgroup]]). The first isomorphism theorem then identifies the quotient $O(1,3)/SO^+(1,3) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$, realised concretely by the coset representatives $\{\mathrm{Id}, P, T, PT = I\}$ — parity, time reversal, their product the total inversion. This is the same mechanism by which $O(n)/SO(n) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$ records orientation; the Lorentz group simply has *two* independent reflections, in time and in space, instead of one. The fact that $I, P, T$ are involutions and commute is precisely the structure of $\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$.

3. **The Lie algebra and the exponential map — the discrete versus the continuous classification.** The connected component $SO^+(1,3)$ is a six-dimensional [[Def - Lie Group|Lie group]] whose [[Def - Lie Algebra|Lie algebra]] $\mathfrak{so}(1,3)$ — defined in [[Special Relativity X — The Lorentz Group as a Lie Group]] — consists of the matrices $\omega$ with $\omega^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta\omega = 0$, antisymmetric when one index is lowered, spanned by three rotation generators $J_i$ and three boost generators $K_i$. The classification of this chapter is the *integrated* picture: a one-parameter subgroup $\exp(t\,\omega)$ is a boost if $\omega$ is a boost generator (eigenvalues $\pm 1$ on the relevant plane), a rotation if $\omega$ is a rotation generator (eigenvalues $\pm i$), and a null rotation if $\omega$ is a particular nilpotent combination $K_x + J_y$ (whose exponential has the Jordan-block form). The reason the exponential map is surjective onto $SO^+(1,3)$ — every restricted transformation is some $\exp(\omega)$ — is exactly the classification: each of the two types lies on a one-parameter subgroup, and the identity component is the union of these. The discrete reflections $P, T$ are what the exponential map *cannot* reach, because they live in other components, and that is the global obstruction to writing $O(1,3) = \exp(\mathfrak{so}(1,3))$.

4. **The double cover $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ — the classification as Möbius dynamics.** The restricted Lorentz group is doubly covered by $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$, the group of $2\times 2$ complex matrices of determinant one ([[Thm - Lorentz Group Covered by SL(2,C)]] in [[Special Relativity XI — SL(2,C), Spinors and the Spinor Map]]), via the spinor map that sends a four-vector to a Hermitian $2\times 2$ matrix and a Lorentz transformation to conjugation $X \mapsto AXA^\dagger$. Under this cover the celestial sphere of null directions becomes the Riemann sphere $\mathbb{C}P^1$, and a Lorentz transformation acts on it as the Möbius transformation $z \mapsto (az+b)/(cz+d)$ determined by $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$. The invariant-null-direction theorem of this chapter ([[Thm - Invariant Null Direction of a Restricted Lorentz Transformation]]) becomes the elementary fact that a Möbius transformation always has a fixed point, because the characteristic equation of $A$ always has a root over $\mathbb{C}$. The four-screw/null-rotation dichotomy maps onto the loxodromic/parabolic dichotomy of Möbius transformations: a four-screw, with two fixed points on the sphere and eigenvalues $e^{\pm(\psi+i\theta)/2}$ for $A$, is loxodromic; a null rotation, with a single fixed point and $A$ a parabolic (unipotent) matrix with trace $\pm 2$, is parabolic. The algebraic proof of the classification that Gourgoulhon defers is precisely this Möbius analysis, and it is the cleanest possible derivation.

5. **Hyperbolic geometry and the gyrogroup — velocity space as a non-Euclidean manifold.** The physical velocities, normalised by $c$, fill the open unit ball $\mathcal{B} = \{\mathbf{V} : |\mathbf{V}| < 1\}$, and relativistic velocity addition makes this ball into a **gyrogroup** — a group-like structure that fails commutativity and associativity by exactly the Thomas rotation ([[Def - Thomas Rotation]]). The defect is precise: $\mathbf{V}_1 \oplus \mathbf{V}_2 = R[\mathbf{V}_1,\mathbf{V}_2]\,(\mathbf{V}_2 \oplus \mathbf{V}_1)$, where $R$ is the Thomas rotation, so the two orders of addition differ by a rotation rather than being equal. Geometrically, the ball with the Lorentz-invariant metric is a model of three-dimensional **hyperbolic space**, the boosts act as its translations (hyperbolic isometries moving the origin), and the Thomas rotation is the holonomy of parallel transport around a geodesic triangle: carry a vector around a triangle in hyperbolic space and it returns rotated by an angle equal to the area deficit, which is the Thomas angle. This is why composing three boosts that return the velocity to its start leaves a net rotation — the hyperbolic triangle they trace has nonzero area. Rapidity is the hyperbolic distance from the origin, which is exactly why rapidities add for collinear boosts (translations along a single geodesic compose by adding arc length) but not for non-collinear ones.

6. **Thomas precession and atomic physics — the kinematic factor of one-half.** The Thomas rotation is not a mathematical curiosity; it is measured in the fine structure of atomic spectra. An electron bound in an atom is continuously accelerated by the nuclear Coulomb field, hence continuously boosted in changing directions, and the sequence of infinitesimal boosts around its orbit accumulates a net rotation of its spin — the **Thomas precession**, with rate $\boldsymbol{\Omega}_T = \frac{\gamma^2}{\gamma+1}\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{v}$ ([[Special Relativity XVI — Accelerated Observers]], [[Def - Thomas Precession]]). When Thomas computed this in 1926 he resolved a factor-of-two discrepancy that had plagued the theory of spin–orbit coupling: the naive spin–orbit interaction overpredicts the fine-structure splitting by a factor of two, and the Thomas precession, precessing the spin in the opposite sense, supplies exactly the compensating $-\tfrac12$. The chain from this chapter is direct: two non-coplanar boosts compose to a boost times a Thomas rotation ([[Thm - Composition of Coplanar Boosts gives a Boost times Thomas Rotation]]), the infinitesimal version of that rotation integrated around the orbit is the precession, and the precession is the missing $\tfrac12$. A purely kinematic effect — no torque, no force — visible in the spectrum of hydrogen.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: classify by the light cone, not by the time axis.** The single conceptual key to this chapter is to organise everything around the null cone rather than around the timelike direction. The Euclidean reflex — inherited from the rotation group, where one fixes an axis and rotates the perpendicular plane — is to start a Lorentz transformation from $e_0$ and ask how it tilts the spatial axes. That reflex makes the classification look like a mess of cases. The Lorentzian truth is that the invariant geometry of $O(1,3)$ *is* the null cone: it is the one structure every Lorentz transformation preserves, it is what separates timelike from spacelike planes, and it is what the three types of plane (timelike, spacelike, null) are defined against. Once you build frames from null vectors — take an invariant null direction $\ell$, manufacture its partner $k$, let $e_0 = \tfrac12(\ell+k)$ emerge — the whole taxonomy collapses to a count of how many null directions are fixed: two gives a four-screw, one gives a null rotation, three or more gives the identity. The null rotation, which looks bizarre from the timelike viewpoint (a parabolic motion with a single Jordan block), is the most natural object of all from the null viewpoint: it is the transformation that slides the cone along itself, fixing one generator. The entire difficulty of the subject is the unfamiliarity of taking null directions as primary; the entire simplicity of it appears the moment you do.

**The true name of a boost is "symmetric positive-definite," and of a rotation is "orthogonal."** The operational characterisation that organises §9.3 is not the geometric one (a boost mixes time and space, a rotation mixes space and space) but the matrix one: in a semi-adapted basis a boost is a symmetric positive-definite matrix and a rotation is an orthogonal matrix. This is the true name because it is what you actually compute with. It tells you instantly that the product of two boosts need not be a boost — the product of two symmetric matrices is symmetric only when they commute — and that the failure of symmetry *is* the rotation content, extracted by polar decomposition. It tells you that a boost's eigenvalues are real and positive (a symmetric positive-definite matrix has real positive eigenvalues), namely the Doppler factors $e^{\pm\psi}$, while a rotation's are unimodular complex. And it makes the polar decomposition not a relativistic accident but the bog-standard linear-algebra theorem $M = SR$ read inside the Lorentz group. When you see "boost," think "symmetric positive part"; when you see "rotation," think "orthogonal part"; and the whole of §9.3 becomes the statement that every Lorentz transformation has a unique such splitting once an observer is chosen.

**Simplicity is why the Thomas rotation is unavoidable.** A trigger-reaction pattern worth internalising: whenever you are tempted to factor $SO^+(1,3)$ into a product of a boost-group and a rotation-group, remember that it is a *simple* group — it has no proper normal subgroup at all — and stop. The boosts cannot form a subgroup, and there can be no clean "boost part times rotation part" factorisation of the *group*, precisely because such a factorisation would exhibit a normal subgroup, and there is none. The polar decomposition is a factorisation of each *element* relative to a chosen observer, not of the group, and the price of that relativity is the Thomas rotation: change the observer, and the boost-rotation split changes. The Thomas rotation is the obstruction to making the element-wise splitting into a group-wise one, made visible. This is the deepest "why" in the chapter, and it reframes a string of facts — boosts not closing under composition, velocity addition not commuting, three boosts leaving a residual rotation, the gyrogroup defect — as a single phenomenon: the simplicity of the Lorentz group forbids the splitting you want, and the Thomas rotation is what you get instead. The kinematic effect measured in atomic spectra is, at bottom, a theorem about the absence of normal subgroups.

**The classification is a fixed-point theorem in two guises.** It is worth seeing that the existence of an invariant null direction — the seed of the entire classification — is a fixed-point theorem, and that it has two faces. Topologically, a restricted Lorentz transformation permutes the null directions, which form a two-sphere (the celestial sphere), inducing an orientation-preserving continuous self-map, and Brouwer's theorem (or the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem, since the sphere has Euler characteristic two) forces a fixed point. Algebraically, the same transformation lifts to $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ acting on the Riemann sphere by Möbius transformations, and a Möbius map has a fixed point because the characteristic polynomial of its $2\times 2$ matrix has a root over $\mathbb{C}$ — the fundamental theorem of algebra in disguise. These are the same fact seen through topology and through algebra, and their agreement is not a coincidence: the celestial sphere and the Riemann sphere are the same sphere, and the orientation-preserving self-map is the Möbius transformation. The chapter's preference for the algebraic proof reflects a general principle of these notes — coordinates over pictures — but the topological proof is the one that reveals *why* there is always a fixed point: a vector field on a two-sphere must vanish somewhere, and so must the displacement of null directions under a restricted Lorentz transformation.
