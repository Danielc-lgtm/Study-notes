# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we set $c = 1$.** The speed of light is a conversion factor between units of time and units of space, and once you measure time in metres it disappears from every formula; where a result is more recognisable with $c$, it is given both ways. To restore $c$, replace $t$ by $ct$, every velocity $v$ by $v/c$, and insert factors of $c$ by dimensional analysis.

A second standing convention, in force across the whole Special Relativity rewrite: the **signature is mostly-minus**, $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)$, so a timelike vector has $X \cdot X > 0$, a spacelike vector has $X \cdot X < 0$, and a null vector has $X \cdot X = 0$.

> [!warning] Convention: Gourgoulhon uses the opposite signature
> This chapter follows Gourgoulhon's *Special Relativity in General Frames* (Chapter 4), which uses the **mostly-plus** signature $\eta = \mathrm{diag}(-1,+1,+1,+1)$, where timelike means $X \cdot X < 0$. Every scalar product on his pages carries the opposite overall sign to ours. The translation rule is mechanical: flip the sign of every dot product. His Lorentz factor $\Gamma = -\vec u \cdot \vec u'$ becomes our $\Gamma = U_0 \cdot U$; his normalisation $\vec u' \cdot \vec u' = -1$ becomes our $U \cdot U = +1$; his velocity decomposition $\vec u' = \Gamma(\vec u + \vec V/c)$ keeps its form because $\vec V$ is spacelike and its *physical* norm $|\mathbf v|$ is unchanged. Be vigilant: a sign dropped in translation inverts a timelike/spacelike classification.

- $\mathcal{O}$ — an **observer**: a future-directed timelike unit worldline carrying a frame; its four-velocity is $U_0$
- $\mathcal{P}$ — a **particle** whose motion relative to $\mathcal{O}$ we describe; its worldline is $\mathcal{L}'$, four-velocity $U$
- $U_0$, $U$ — four-velocities of the observer and the particle; both future-directed timelike unit vectors, $U_0 \cdot U_0 = U \cdot U = 1$ (Gourgoulhon writes these $\vec u$ and $\vec u'$)
- $\tau$ — proper time of the observer $\mathcal{O}$, sometimes written $t$ (it is $\mathcal{O}$'s clock reading); $\tau'$ — proper time of the particle $\mathcal{P}$
- $\Gamma$ — the **Lorentz factor** of $\mathcal{P}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$, defined by $\mathrm{d}\tau = \Gamma\,\mathrm{d}\tau'$
- $\mathcal{E}_{U_0}(\tau)$, $E_{U_0}$ — the **local rest space** of $\mathcal{O}$ at proper time $\tau$, the spacelike hyperplane $U_0^{\perp}$ orthogonal to $U_0$
- $\perp_{U_0}$ — the **orthogonal projector** onto the local rest space, $\perp_{U_0} = \mathrm{Id} - (\,\cdot\, U_0)\,U_0$
- $\mathbf{V}$, $V$ — the **velocity of $\mathcal{P}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$**; a vector in the local rest space, $V \cdot U_0 = 0$ and $V$ spacelike. Bold $\mathbf{V}$ when stressing it is a spatial three-vector; $|\mathbf{V}|$ its Euclidean norm, the **speed**
- $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$, $\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{\!\mathrm{acc}}$ — the **acceleration of $\mathcal{P}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$**, $\boldsymbol{\gamma} := \mathrm{d}^2\mathbf{x}/\mathrm{d}\tau^2$, a spatial vector in the rest space (Gourgoulhon's $\vec\gamma$; written $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ here to avoid collision with the Lorentz factor $\Gamma$)
- $A$, $A_0$ — the **four-acceleration** of the particle ($A = \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}\tau'$) and of the observer ($A_0 = \mathrm{d}U_0/\mathrm{d}\tau$); spacelike, with $A \cdot U = 0$, $A_0 \cdot U_0 = 0$
- $\overrightarrow{OM}$ — the **position vector** of $\mathcal{P}$ in $\mathcal{O}$'s local rest space: $\overrightarrow{OM} = x^i\, e_i$, where $O$ is the event on $\mathcal{O}$'s worldline at the relevant instant and $M$ the perceived position of $\mathcal{P}$
- $(e_\alpha) = (e_0 = U_0, e_1, e_2, e_3)$ — the observer's orthonormal **local frame**; $e_0$ is $U_0$, the $e_i$ span the rest space
- $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ — the spatial **angular velocity (four-rotation)** of $\mathcal{O}$'s local frame; $\times_{U_0}$ the rest-space cross product
- $N$ — the **propagation direction** of a photon relative to $\mathcal{O}$: a unit spatial vector in the rest space, $|N| = 1$, $N \cdot U_0 = 0$
- $\ell$ — the null vector along a photon's worldline adapted to $\mathcal{O}$ by $\ell \cdot U_0 = 1$; then $\ell = U_0 + N$
- $u^0, u^i$ — components of a four-velocity in the observer's frame; $V^i$ — components of the relative velocity; $\mu,\nu = 0\ldots3$; $i,j = 1,2,3$
- $\mu^{-}$ — the muon; $\tau_0 = 2.2 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{s}$ its proper mean lifetime; $V_{\mathrm{light}}$ — the norm of the velocity of light relative to a terrestrial observer

# Motivation

Here is the chapter in one sentence: it is the rigorous, frame-independent rebuild of every effect that **Special Relativity II** ([[Special Relativity II — Simultaneity, Time Dilation and Length Contraction]]) discovered by inspecting two inertial frames — time dilation, the speed ceiling, the constancy of light — done now for a *single arbitrary observer*, possibly accelerated, possibly rotating, with the particle's motion expressed in terms of geometric invariants instead of coordinate transformations.

The reorganisation is worth dwelling on, because it is the central conceptual move of the entire general-frames programme. In **Special Relativity II** the protagonist was a *coordinate transformation*: you wrote down two inertial frames $S$ and $S'$, related them by a [[Def - The Lorentz Transformation|Lorentz boost]], and read off how a clock or a rod transformed. That is fine for inertial observers, but it says nothing about what an accelerated observer measures, because there is no global frame attached to an accelerating worldline. The fix, set up in the previous chapter on observers ([[Special Relativity VI — Observers, Local Rest Spaces and Local Frames]]), is to attach to each observer a *local* structure — a four-velocity $U_0$, a [[Def - Observer and Local Rest Space|local rest space]] $U_0^\perp$, an orthonormal frame — and to express the motion of a particle entirely in terms of scalar products and projections involving $U_0$ and the particle's four-velocity $U$. The result is that every kinematic quantity becomes a *geometric object*: the Lorentz factor is a scalar product, the relative velocity is an orthogonal projection, the propagation direction of light is a unit vector in a hyperplane. None of these depends on a choice of coordinates; they depend only on the two worldlines and the metric.

The structural backbone of the whole chapter is a single decomposition. Given the observer's four-velocity $U_0$ and the particle's four-velocity $U$, the latter splits, *orthogonally*, into a part along $U_0$ and a part in the local rest space:
$$U = \Gamma\,(U_0 + V), \qquad V \cdot U_0 = 0, \qquad \Gamma = U \cdot U_0.$$
Everything else is a corollary of this one equation. The coefficient $\Gamma$ of $U_0$ is the Lorentz factor — and reading it as a scalar product, $\Gamma = U \cdot U_0$, makes time dilation a one-line consequence of the reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. The rest-space part $\Gamma V$ contains the relative velocity $V$, whose norm $|\mathbf V| = \sqrt{-V \cdot V}$ is the speed; the normalisation $U \cdot U = 1$ forces $|\mathbf V| < 1$, which is the speed-of-light ceiling, now a statement about the geometry of the unit hyperboloid rather than about velocity addition. Differentiate the decomposition once more and you get the four-acceleration in terms of the rest-space acceleration; specialise to a photon, where $U$ degenerates to a null vector $\ell = U_0 + N$, and you get the propagation direction $N$ and the result that light moves at speed $|N| = 1 = c$ for *every* observer at a point of their own worldline — the constancy of the speed of light recovered as a *theorem*, not assumed as a postulate.

The chapter closes with the experiments, and they are not decoration. Time dilation is confirmed every time a cosmic-ray muon reaches the ground: created high in the atmosphere with a proper lifetime of $\tau_0 = 2.2\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, a muon should travel only $c\tau_0 \approx 660\,\mathrm{m}$ before decaying, yet a copious flux survives the $\sim 10\,\mathrm{km}$ descent because, to the terrestrial observer, its clock runs slow by $\Gamma \gg 1$. The Frisch–Smith experiment measured exactly this and found $\Gamma = 8.4 \pm 2.0$ at $V \approx 0.995c$. The invariance of the speed of light is confirmed by the Michelson–Morley experiment (the speed of light does not depend on the *direction* of the observer's motion) and the Kennedy–Thorndike experiment (it does not depend on the *magnitude* either) — the two together establishing the full content of the constancy postulate experimentally.

The reader is assumed to have worked through [[Special Relativity V — Worldlines, Proper Time and Four-Velocity]] (the [[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration|four-velocity]] $U = \mathrm{d}X/\mathrm{d}\tau$, its unit norm, [[Def - Proper Time|proper time]]) and [[Special Relativity VI — Observers, Local Rest Spaces and Local Frames]] (the observer, the local rest space $U_0^\perp$, the [[Def - The Orthogonal Projector onto the Local Rest Space|orthogonal projector]]). The new ingredient here is not a new postulate but a habit: read every kinematic quantity as a scalar product or a projection involving the two four-velocities, and the relativity of it becomes automatic.

# Concept Map

## §7.1 The Lorentz Factor and Relative Velocity

- **[[Def - Lorentz Factor and Relative Velocity]]**
	- The Lorentz factor of a particle $\mathcal{P}$ relative to an observer $\mathcal{O}$ is the ratio of their proper-time increments, $\mathrm{d}\tau = \Gamma\,\mathrm{d}\tau'$, and equals the scalar product of their four-velocities, $\Gamma = U \cdot U_0$ (when the worldlines cross or $\mathcal{O}$ is inertial). The four-velocity of $\mathcal{P}$ decomposes orthogonally as $U = \Gamma(U_0 + V)$, where $V$ is the velocity of $\mathcal{P}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$, a spacelike vector in the local rest space with $V \cdot U_0 = 0$. In the observer's own frame $U_0 = e_0$ so $\Gamma = u^0$, the time component of $U$; this is a compound page defining both the factor and the decomposition because neither is usable without the other.

- **[[Thm - Time Dilation (General Observer)]]**
	- The Lorentz factor satisfies $\Gamma \geq 1$, with equality if and only if $\mathcal{P}$ has the same four-velocity as $\mathcal{O}$; equivalently $\mathrm{d}\tau \geq \mathrm{d}\tau'$, so the proper time $\mathcal{O}$ ascribes to a segment of $\mathcal{P}$'s worldline exceeds the proper time $\mathcal{P}$ records on its own clock. This is the general-observer version of [[Thm - Time Dilation|time dilation]] from Special Relativity II: there the two events lay on the observer's own worldline, here they lie anywhere, made comparable by the [[Def - Einstein-Poincaré Simultaneity|Einstein–Poincaré simultaneity]] of the previous chapter. The proof is one line: in the observer's frame $\Gamma = u^0 = \sqrt{1 + \sum_i (u^i)^2} \geq 1$.

- **[[Def - Velocity Relative to an Observer]]**
	- The velocity of $\mathcal{P}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$ is the proper-time derivative $\mathbf{V} := \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}/\mathrm{d}\tau$ of $\mathcal{P}$'s position vector in $\mathcal{O}$'s rest space, equivalently the rest-space vector $V = (1/\Gamma)\perp_{U_0} U$ obtained by projecting the particle's four-velocity onto $U_0^\perp$ and dividing by $\Gamma$. It is spacelike and orthogonal to $U_0$, lives in the three-dimensional local rest space, and its Euclidean norm $|\mathbf{V}| = \sqrt{-V\cdot V}$ is the speed measured by $\mathcal{O}$. In components in the observer's frame, $V^\alpha = (0, V^1, V^2, V^3)$ with $V^i = \mathrm{d}x^i/\mathrm{d}\tau$, and the four-velocity is $u'^\alpha = (\Gamma, \Gamma V^1, \Gamma V^2, \Gamma V^3)$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Energy and Momentum Relative to an Observer *(from Relativistic Dynamics)*
> Multiply the four-velocity decomposition $U = \Gamma(U_0 + V)$ by the rest mass $m$ and you have the [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momentum]] $P = mU$ split into the energy $E = P \cdot U_0 = \Gamma m$ measured by $\mathcal{O}$ and the spatial momentum $\mathbf{p} = \Gamma m \mathbf{V}$. The whole of **Special Relativity XIII** is this chapter with a factor of $m$ attached: $E = \Gamma m$ is the relativistic energy whose low-speed expansion $\Gamma m \approx m + \tfrac{1}{2}m|\mathbf V|^2$ contains the rest energy and the kinetic energy.

- **[[Ex - Lorentz factor as the scalar product of four-velocities]]** (⭐)
	- Compute the Lorentz factor of a uniformly moving particle two ways — as the scalar product $\Gamma = U \cdot U_0$ and as the time component $u^0$ in the observer's frame — and confirm they agree, then extract the relative velocity from the orthogonal decomposition and derive the reciprocity $\Gamma = U \cdot U_0 = U_0 \cdot U$ from the symmetry of the inner product.

- **[[Ex - The cosmic-ray muon reaches the ground]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Resolve why atmospheric muons survive the descent to the ground both in the lab frame (time dilation: lifetime $\Gamma\tau_0$, full distance) and in the muon frame (length contraction: lifetime $\tau_0$, contracted distance $L/\Gamma$), show the two accounts give the identical condition with one factor of $\Gamma$ each, and check consistency with the Frisch–Smith measurement $\Gamma = 8.4 \pm 2.0$ at $V \approx 0.995c$.

- **[[Ex - Components of the relative velocity and the four-velocity]]** (⭐⭐)
	- For uniform circular motion, differentiate the trajectory for the relative velocity (constant speed $R\Omega$), assemble the four-velocity as $(\Gamma, \Gamma V^i)$ and verify its unit norm, then show the four-velocity is non-constant — the particle is accelerated despite constant speed, because a transverse acceleration turns the velocity without changing its magnitude — with $\Gamma \to \infty$ as the rim speed $R\Omega \to 1$.

## §7.2 Relative Acceleration

- **[[Def - Acceleration Relative to an Observer]]**
	- The acceleration of $\mathcal{P}$ relative to $\mathcal{O}$ is the second proper-time derivative of $\mathcal{P}$'s position vector in $\mathcal{O}$'s rest space, $\boldsymbol{\gamma} := \mathrm{d}^2\mathbf{x}/\mathrm{d}\tau^2 = \mathrm{d}\mathbf{V}/\mathrm{d}\tau$, a spatial vector in the local rest space. It is the relativistic analogue of Newtonian acceleration, but — unlike its four-vector cousin — it is *observer-dependent*: even for an inertial observer it changes when you switch to a differently-moving inertial observer, because the relative velocity it differentiates is itself observer-dependent. For an accelerated or rotating observer it acquires extra centripetal and Coriolis terms, exactly as a Newtonian acceleration acquires inertial forces in a non-inertial frame.

- **[[Thm - Expression of the Four-Acceleration]]**
	- The four-acceleration $A = \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}\tau'$ of $\mathcal{P}$ is expressible in terms of its relative acceleration $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$, relative velocity $V$, and the observer's four-velocity; for an inertial observer, $A = \Gamma^2\big[\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \Gamma^2(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{V})(V + U_0)\big]$, and at the instant $\mathcal{P}$ is momentarily at rest relative to $\mathcal{O}$ (so $\mathbf{V} = 0$, $\Gamma = 1$) this collapses to $A = \boldsymbol{\gamma}$. The four-acceleration is therefore the acceleration measured by the *momentarily comoving inertial observer* — the observer whose four-velocity equals $\mathcal{P}$'s at that event — which is the precise, frame-independent meaning of "proper acceleration". Its norm is $|A| = \Gamma^2\sqrt{|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|^2 + \Gamma^2(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{V})^2}$, constant for uniformly accelerated and uniform circular motion.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Relativistic Equation of Motion *(from Relativistic Dynamics)*
> The four-acceleration is what Newton's second law acts on relativistically: the [[Def - Four-Force|four-force]] is $f = mA$, and projecting it onto the observer's rest space gives the three-force $\mathbf{f} = \mathrm{d}\mathbf{p}/\mathrm{d}\tau$. Because $A = \boldsymbol{\gamma}$ only at momentary rest, the three-force and the relative acceleration $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ are *not* simply proportional at general speeds — the parallel and transverse "relativistic masses" $\Gamma^3 m$ and $\Gamma m$ are the components of this mismatch, worked out in **Special Relativity XIII**.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Uniformly Accelerated (Hyperbolic) Motion *(from Accelerated Observers)*
> A particle with *constant proper acceleration* — constant $|A|$ — is the relativistic uniformly accelerated observer, and its worldline is a hyperbola $x^2 - t^2 = 1/a^2$ rather than a parabola. The expression of the four-acceleration is the kinematic input to **Special Relativity XVI**: setting $|A| = a$ constant and integrating gives hyperbolic motion, the Rindler horizon, and the Unruh effect.

- **[[Ex - Maximum relative velocity and why tachyons exceed c]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Derive the speed bound $|\mathbf V| < c$ as the reality condition on the Lorentz factor (at $|\mathbf V| = c$, $\Gamma$ diverges; beyond, $\Gamma^2 < 0$), show a spacelike-worldline tachyon would have $|\mathbf V| > c$, distinguish a closing speed (a coordinate difference, allowed up to $2c$) from a relative speed (a four-velocity quantity, bounded), and explain via rapidity why no finite chain of boosts reaches $c$.

- **[[Ex - The four-acceleration at momentary rest is the proper acceleration]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute the collinear proper acceleration $\|A\|_g = \Gamma^3|\dot V|$ and its momentary-rest value $|\boldsymbol\gamma|$, integrate the constant-proper-acceleration condition $\Gamma V = at$ for $V(t)$, derive the hyperbolic worldline $(x+1/a)^2 - t^2 = 1/a^2$ and the Rindler horizon, and explain why the observed acceleration $\dot V = a/\Gamma^3$ falls to zero as the speed asymptotes to $c$ while the felt acceleration stays $a$.

- **[[Ex - Proper acceleration of circular motion and its norm]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Compute the centripetal relative acceleration $\boldsymbol\gamma = -\Omega^2\overrightarrow{OM}$, apply the transverse-case norm formula $\|A\|_g = \Gamma^2 R\Omega^2$, re-derive the same proper acceleration independently from the raw four-acceleration components as a metric-sign check, and explain via the $\Gamma^4$ scaling of radiated power why circular accelerators (synchrotrons) radiate so fiercely at high energy.

## §7.3 Photon Motion and the Velocity of Light

- **[[Def - Photon Propagation Direction and Velocity]]**
	- A photon has no four-velocity — its tangent is a null vector that cannot be normalised — so one adapts a null vector $\ell$ to the observer by the condition $\ell \cdot U_0 = 1$, giving the orthogonal decomposition $\ell = U_0 + N$ where $N$ is the **propagation direction**, a unit spatial vector in the local rest space ($N \cdot U_0 = 0$, $|N| = 1$). The velocity of the photon relative to $\mathcal{O}$ is then $\mathbf{V} = N$ (with $c$: $\mathbf{V} = c\mathbf{N}$) whenever the photon crosses the observer's worldline or $\mathcal{O}$ is inertial. The propagation direction is the unit vector along the photon's spatial track; its norm being exactly $1$ is the geometric source of the constancy of the speed of light.

- **[[Thm - Invariance of the Velocity of Light]]**
	- The velocity of light measured by *any* observer at a point of their own worldline has norm exactly $c$: $\|\mathbf{V}\|_g = c$, regardless of the observer's state of motion. This is a *derived* result here — it follows from photons travelling on null geodesics and the decomposition $\ell = U_0 + N$ with $|N| = 1$ — whereas Einstein took it as a postulate; the two famous experimental confirmations are Michelson–Morley (1887), which shows the speed of light is independent of the *direction* of the observer's motion, and Kennedy–Thorndike (1932), which shows it is independent of the *magnitude*. For an accelerated or rotating observer measuring light at a distance the simple result can fail, which is the kinematic seed of light-bending in curved spacetime.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Aberration and the Doppler Effect *(from Change of Observer)*
> The propagation direction $N$ is observer-dependent: a second observer moving relative to the first sees the same photon arrive from a different direction (**aberration**) and at a different frequency (**Doppler shift**). Transforming $N$ and the photon's frequency between two observers is the entire content of **Special Relativity VIII** — the [[Thm - Aberration of Light|aberration]] and [[Thm - The Doppler Effect|Doppler]] laws are what happens to $\ell = U_0 + N$ under a change of $U_0$.

> [!tip] Unlocked: The Celestial Sphere and Möbius Transformations *(from the Spinor Map)*
> The set of propagation directions $N$ an observer can receive is the unit sphere in the rest space — the **celestial sphere** $\mathbb{S}^2 \cong \mathbb{CP}^1$ — and a change of observer acts on it by a **Möbius transformation**, the action of $SL(2,\mathbb{C})$ on the Riemann sphere. Aberration is then a conformal map of the sky; this is the geometric punchline of **Special Relativity XI**, where the double cover $SL(2,\mathbb{C}) \to SO^+(1,3)$ realises the Lorentz group as the conformal group of $\mathbb{S}^2$.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The exercises of frame-relative kinematics chase a small, recognisable set of goals, and the first is to **compute a kinematic quantity relative to a given observer** — the Lorentz factor, the speed, the relative acceleration, the propagation direction of a photon — starting from the two four-velocities. The skill is to recognise that each of these is a specific scalar product or projection: $\Gamma$ is $U \cdot U_0$, the speed comes from $-V \cdot V$ with $V = (1/\Gamma)\perp_{U_0}U$, the propagation direction is $\perp_{U_0}\ell$. A second target is to **prove an inequality or a bound**: that $\Gamma \geq 1$ (time dilation), that $|\mathbf V| < c$ (the speed ceiling), that the proper acceleration is at least the relative acceleration. These are not coordinate computations but consequences of the indefinite metric — the reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the unit-norm constraint $U \cdot U = 1$ do all the work. A third target is to **establish an invariant or an observer-independent fact**: that the four-acceleration is the proper acceleration measured by the comoving inertial observer, that the speed of light is $c$ for every observer at a point of their worldline. A fourth, more physical, is to **predict the outcome of an experiment** — the surviving muon flux at ground level, the absence of a fringe shift in an interferometer — and confront it with measurement, which is what converts the geometry into physics. A fifth is **structural**: to verify that a quantity which *looks* observer-independent (the relative acceleration $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$) is in fact observer-dependent, or that one which looks observer-dependent (the proper acceleration $|A|$) is in fact invariant. Across all five the unifying remark is that you understand a frame-relative situation once you know which quantities are projections (hence observer-dependent: $\Gamma$, $V$, $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$, $N$) and which are full four-vector invariants (hence observer-independent: $U \cdot U$, $A \cdot A$, the null character of a photon's tangent).

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The hypotheses are equally stereotyped. **Two four-velocities are given**, or extractable — the observer's $U_0$ and the particle's $U$ — and immediately supply $\Gamma = U \cdot U_0$ and, by projection, the relative velocity; this is the richest source, because the orthogonal decomposition $U = \Gamma(U_0 + V)$ converts any pair of four-velocities into observer-relative kinematics. **A worldline is specified in coordinates**, $x^i(t)$ in the observer's reference space, from which $V^i = \mathrm{d}x^i/\mathrm{d}t$ and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}^i = \mathrm{d}^2x^i/\mathrm{d}t^2$ follow by differentiation, and $\Gamma$ from $u^0 = \sqrt{1 + \sum (u^i)^2}$. **The particle is a photon**, which means its tangent is null and unnormalisable, so the four-velocity is replaced by the adapted null vector $\ell$ with $\ell \cdot U_0 = 1$, and everything spatial is carried by $N = \perp_{U_0}\ell$. **The observer is inertial** ($A_0 = 0$, $\boldsymbol{\omega} = 0$), which kills the centripetal, Coriolis, and acceleration corrections and reduces the general expressions to their simplest form — this is the assumption to look for whenever a formula has a $1 + A_0 \cdot \overrightarrow{OM}$ or an $\boldsymbol\omega \times \overrightarrow{OM}$ that you would like to drop. **The particle is momentarily at rest relative to the observer** ($\mathbf V = 0$, $\Gamma = 1$), which collapses the four-acceleration to the relative acceleration, $A = \boldsymbol{\gamma}$ — the definition of proper acceleration. The recurring move is to route a source to a target: two four-velocities route through a scalar product to a Lorentz factor; a coordinate worldline routes through differentiation to the relative velocity and acceleration; the unit-norm constraint routes through algebra to the speed ceiling; a null tangent routes through the adaptation $\ell \cdot U_0 = 1$ to the propagation direction. The [[Special Relativity VII — Kinematics I, Motion Relative to an Observer#Problem-Solving Strategy|Problem-Solving Strategy]] section makes these routes explicit.

# Legal Operations

These are the moves almost every problem in the chapter is assembled from. The recurring discipline is to *decide which observer measures which quantity* and to keep four-vectors (invariant) cleanly separated from their projections (observer-dependent). When stuck, scan the list and try each one.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Read the Lorentz factor as a scalar product of four-velocities.** Whenever you have an observer's four-velocity $U_0$ and a particle's $U$, the Lorentz factor is $\Gamma = U \cdot U_0$ (for crossing worldlines or an inertial observer); see [[Def - Lorentz Factor and Relative Velocity]]. *Trigger:* you need $\Gamma$ and you know both four-velocities. The payoff is that a scalar product is computed in *any* convenient frame — usually the observer's own, where $U_0 = e_0$ and $\Gamma = u^0$ is just the time component of $U$. This single identity turns time dilation, energy, and the speed ceiling into algebra.

2. **Decompose the four-velocity orthogonally with respect to the observer.** Split $U = \Gamma(U_0 + V)$ into its part along $U_0$ and its part in the rest space $U_0^\perp$; see [[Def - Velocity Relative to an Observer]]. *Trigger:* you want the relative velocity, or you want to separate "time" from "space" as the observer sees them. *Pattern:* "project onto $U_0$ for the Lorentz factor, project with $\perp_{U_0}$ for the velocity." This is the workhorse of the chapter — almost every result is this decomposition differentiated, normed, or specialised.

3. **Project onto the local rest space with $\perp_{U_0}$.** The orthogonal projector $\perp_{U_0} = \mathrm{Id} - (\,\cdot\,U_0)\,U_0$ extracts the spatial part of any four-vector as the observer sees it; see [[Def - The Orthogonal Projector onto the Local Rest Space]]. *Trigger:* you have a four-vector and want the three-vector an observer measures — the velocity $V = (1/\Gamma)\perp_{U_0}U$, the propagation direction $N = \perp_{U_0}\ell$. The projector is what makes "the spatial part" a frame-independent operation: it depends only on $U_0$, not on a choice of spatial axes.

4. **Use the unit-norm constraint $U \cdot U = 1$.** Every four-velocity is a future-directed timelike unit vector; imposing this on a decomposition pins down the Lorentz factor and bounds the speed; see [[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration]]. *Trigger:* you have an expression for $U$ in terms of $\Gamma$ and $V$ and need a relation between them. Substituting $U = \Gamma(U_0 + V)$ into $U \cdot U = 1$ and using $V \cdot U_0 = 0$ gives $\Gamma^2(1 - |\mathbf V|^2) = 1$, the master relation behind both $\Gamma = (1 - |\mathbf V|^2)^{-1/2}$ and $|\mathbf V| < 1$.

5. **Compute in the observer's own frame.** Choose the orthonormal frame with $e_0 = U_0$; then $U_0$ has components $(1,0,0,0)$, the rest space is spanned by $e_1, e_2, e_3$, and every projection becomes "drop or keep components". *Trigger:* a scalar product or projection looks abstract — pick the adapted frame and it becomes arithmetic. In this frame $\Gamma = u^0$, $V^i = u^i/\Gamma$, and the speed is $|\mathbf V| = \sqrt{\sum (V^i)^2}$. This is the relativistic analogue of choosing convenient coordinates.

6. **Differentiate the relative velocity to get the relative acceleration; differentiate the four-velocity to get the four-acceleration.** The relative acceleration is $\boldsymbol{\gamma} = \mathrm{d}\mathbf V/\mathrm{d}\tau$ ([[Def - Acceleration Relative to an Observer]]); the four-acceleration is $A = \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}\tau'$ ([[Def - Four-Velocity and Four-Acceleration]]). *Trigger:* the problem asks about acceleration. Keep the two straight: $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ is differentiated with respect to the *observer's* time and lives in the rest space; $A$ is differentiated with respect to the *particle's* proper time and is a four-vector. They coincide only at momentary rest.

7. **Specialise to the simplest case, then add corrections.** The general formulas carry terms in $A_0 \cdot \overrightarrow{OM}$ (observer acceleration) and $\boldsymbol\omega \times \overrightarrow{OM}$ (observer rotation); set the observer inertial to kill them, set the worldlines crossing to kill $\overrightarrow{OM}$. *Trigger:* a formula is more complicated than the question warrants. *Pattern:* "is the observer inertial? is the particle at the observer's location? is it momentarily at rest?" — each "yes" deletes a term. The full formulas are needed only for rotating observers (**Special Relativity XVII**) and accelerated frames (**Special Relativity XVI**).

8. **Adapt a null vector to the observer for a photon.** A photon's tangent cannot be normalised, so impose $\ell \cdot U_0 = 1$ instead of unit norm, giving $\ell = U_0 + N$ with $|N| = 1$; see [[Def - Photon Propagation Direction and Velocity]]. *Trigger:* the moving object is light or a massless particle. The condition $\ell \cdot U_0 = 1$ is the photon's replacement for the unit-norm constraint, and it is exactly what makes the spatial part $N$ a unit vector — the source of $\|\mathbf V_{\mathrm{light}}\| = c$.

9. **Evaluate a four-vector invariant in any frame to settle it in all.** The norms $U \cdot U$, $A \cdot A$, and the null condition $\ell \cdot \ell = 0$ are observer-independent; compute them where they are easiest. *Trigger:* you want a frame-independent number such as the proper acceleration $|A| = \sqrt{-A \cdot A}$. Evaluate in the comoving inertial frame, where $A = \boldsymbol{\gamma}$ and the answer is the Newtonian-looking $|\boldsymbol{\gamma}|$, then trust invariance to carry it everywhere.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating the relative acceleration as observer-independent
> In Newtonian physics the acceleration of a particle relative to *any* inertial observer is the same vector — acceleration is absolute, only velocity is relative. It is tempting to carry this over and assume the relativistic relative acceleration $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ is likewise the same for all inertial observers. It is not. The formula $A = \Gamma^2[\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \Gamma^2(\boldsymbol{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf V)(V + U_0)]$ ([[Thm - Expression of the Four-Acceleration]]) shows $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ depends on the relative velocity $\mathbf V$, which is itself observer-dependent — so two inertial observers in relative motion measure *different* relative accelerations for the same particle, even though they agree on the four-acceleration $A$. The frame-independent object is $A$, not $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$. The operation becomes legal exactly when you ask about the four-acceleration, or when you restrict to a single observer.

> [!warning] 2. Adding the velocity of the source to the velocity of light
> The Galilean instinct is that a photon emitted by a source moving at $\mathbf V_s$ travels at $c + \mathbf V_s$ in the source's direction — that light, like a thrown ball, inherits its emitter's velocity. This is the hypothesis $\mathbf V_{\mathrm{light}} = \mathbf V_0 + k\mathbf V_s$ with $k = 1$, and it is false: relativity gives $k = 0$ ([[Thm - Invariance of the Velocity of Light]]). The concrete counterexample is binary stars — if light from the approaching and receding halves of an orbit travelled at different speeds, their images would arrive scrambled, yet de Sitter's analysis bounds $|k| < 0.002$ and Brecher's X-ray pulsars bound $|k| < 2\times 10^{-9}$. The operation is legal only in the $c \to \infty$ Galilean limit, where it is the addition of velocities for *massive* projectiles — never for light.

> [!warning] 3. Assuming the speed of light is $c$ for an accelerated observer at a distance
> The theorem $\|\mathbf V_{\mathrm{light}}\| = c$ holds for any observer *at a point of their own worldline* — when the photon crosses the observer's position, $\overrightarrow{OM} = 0$. It is tempting to drop the qualification and assert that an accelerated observer always measures $c$ for light, however far away. The general formula $\mathbf V = (1 + A_0 \cdot \overrightarrow{OM})\,c\,N - \boldsymbol\omega \times \overrightarrow{OM}$ shows this fails when $\overrightarrow{OM} \neq 0$ and the observer is non-inertial: a uniformly accelerated observer assigns a *position-dependent* coordinate speed to distant light, less than $c$ behind them. This is not a violation of relativity — it is the same phenomenon that, in a gravitational field, bends light and produces the Shapiro delay. The operation is legal exactly when the observer is inertial, or when the measurement is local ($\overrightarrow{OM} = 0$).

> [!warning] 4. Using $A = \boldsymbol{\gamma}$ at general speeds
> The clean identity "the four-acceleration equals the relative acceleration" holds *only* at the instant the particle is momentarily at rest relative to the observer, $\mathbf V = 0$. It is tempting to use $A = \boldsymbol{\gamma}$ as if it held always, which would make the proper acceleration equal to the observed relative acceleration at every speed. The counterexample is uniformly accelerated motion: a particle with constant proper acceleration $|A| = a$ has a relative acceleration $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ that *decreases* as its speed grows ($|\boldsymbol{\gamma}| = a/\Gamma^3$ for collinear motion), because the observer sees the particle's velocity asymptote to $c$ and stop changing. The full relation is $A = \Gamma^2[\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \cdots]$; the identity $A = \boldsymbol{\gamma}$ is its $\mathbf V = 0$ special case, legal only there.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems of this chapter are won the moment you classify what is being asked and decide *which observer measures which quantity*, keeping invariant four-vectors cleanly apart from their observer-dependent projections. Begin by identifying the two four-velocities — the observer's $U_0$ and the particle's $U$ — because almost every quantity in the chapter is a scalar product or projection of these two.

If the problem **asks for a kinematic quantity relative to an observer** — a Lorentz factor, a speed, a propagation direction — the decisive first move is to express that quantity as a scalar product or projection and then evaluate it in the observer's own frame. For the Lorentz factor, write $\Gamma = U \cdot U_0$ ([[Def - Lorentz Factor and Relative Velocity]]) and read it off as the time component $u^0$ in the frame where $U_0 = e_0$. For the relative velocity, project: $V = (1/\Gamma)\perp_{U_0}U$ ([[Def - Velocity Relative to an Observer]]), and its components are $V^i = u^i/\Gamma$. For a photon, the four-velocity is unavailable, so adapt the null vector by $\ell \cdot U_0 = 1$ and take $N = \perp_{U_0}\ell$ ([[Def - Photon Propagation Direction and Velocity]]). The reason this always works is that the observer's frame turns every projection into "keep or drop components", and the answer in that frame is the physical measurement.

If the problem **asks to prove a bound** — that $\Gamma \geq 1$, that the speed is below $c$, that the proper acceleration exceeds the relative acceleration — the instrument is the unit-norm constraint $U \cdot U = 1$ together with the future-directed condition $u^0 > 0$. The route is: write the four-velocity components in the observer's frame, impose $u^0 = \sqrt{1 + \sum (u^i)^2}$, and the bound falls out — $u^0 \geq 1$ is time dilation ([[Thm - Time Dilation (General Observer)]]), and substituting into $\Gamma^2(1 - |\mathbf V|^2) = 1$ gives $|\mathbf V| < 1$ ([[Thm - Maximum Relative Velocity is c]]). The bounds in this chapter are not coordinate accidents; they are the indefinite metric asserting itself through the unit-norm condition, and the reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $U \cdot U_0 \geq 1$ for future timelike unit vectors is their common ancestor.

If the problem **involves acceleration**, the strategy is to keep the two accelerations rigorously separate and to specialise. The four-acceleration $A = \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}\tau'$ is a frame-independent four-vector; the relative acceleration $\boldsymbol{\gamma} = \mathrm{d}\mathbf V/\mathrm{d}\tau$ is an observer-dependent rest-space vector ([[Def - Acceleration Relative to an Observer]]). To find the proper acceleration — the physically meaningful invariant — use [[Thm - Expression of the Four-Acceleration]] and specialise to momentary rest, where $A = \boldsymbol{\gamma}$; the norm $|A| = \sqrt{-A\cdot A}$ is then the acceleration felt by the particle, the reading of an accelerometer it carries. When the observer is non-inertial, the relative acceleration acquires centripetal ($\boldsymbol\omega \times (\boldsymbol\omega \times \overrightarrow{OM})$) and Coriolis ($2\boldsymbol\omega \times \mathbf V$) terms, exactly as in a rotating Newtonian frame; identify whether the observer rotates or accelerates and add only the terms you need.

If the problem **is about a photon or the speed of light**, the strategy is to replace "four-velocity" by "adapted null vector" everywhere and to remember the qualification on the constancy theorem. The propagation direction $N$ is a unit vector, so $\|\mathbf V_{\mathrm{light}}\| = c$ ([[Thm - Invariance of the Velocity of Light]]) — but only at a point of the observer's worldline, $\overrightarrow{OM} = 0$. For an experiment, the job is to translate the null result into a bound on a deviation parameter: Michelson–Morley bounds the *directional* dependence, Kennedy–Thorndike the *magnitude* dependence, and source-independence tests (binary stars, $\pi^0$ decay) bound the parameter $k$ in $\mathbf V_{\mathrm{light}} = \mathbf V_0 + k\mathbf V_s$. The unifying view is that each experiment isolates one way the speed of light *could* have depended on the observer or the source, and finds it does not.

Threading through all of this is one meta-principle: **every kinematic quantity is a scalar product or a projection of the two four-velocities, and its relativity is the statement that the projection depends on $U_0$.** The single question of the chapter is "what does the geometry of $U$ and $U_0$, with the indefinite metric, force?" — and the answer to almost every exercise is to write the relevant scalar product, project onto $U_0^\perp$, and impose $U \cdot U = 1$.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Def - Lorentz Factor and Relative Velocity|The Lorentz factor as a scalar product]]**: $\Gamma = U \cdot U_0$. This is the single most-used fact in the chapter, because it is free and frame-independent: any time two worldlines are present and you need their relative Lorentz factor, it is the scalar product of their four-velocities, computable in whichever frame is convenient. Reach for it whenever you need $\Gamma$ and know both four-velocities; in the observer's frame it is simply the time component $u^0$ of the particle's four-velocity, and it instantly delivers time dilation ($\Gamma \geq 1$), the relativistic energy ($E = \Gamma m$), and — once you impose the unit norm — the speed ceiling. Its disguised use is as a definition of "relative speed" without ever mentioning coordinates.

- **The orthogonal decomposition** $U = \Gamma(U_0 + V)$: the structural backbone of the chapter. Its recognisable setup is any situation where you must separate "what the observer calls time" from "what the observer calls space" for a moving particle. The reusable content is that it is *orthogonal* — $V \cdot U_0 = 0$ — so the unit-norm constraint $U \cdot U = 1$ separates into a time piece and a space piece, $\Gamma^2 - \Gamma^2|\mathbf V|^2 = 1$, with no cross term. Differentiate it for the four-acceleration, attach a mass for the four-momentum, take a null limit for a photon: the whole chapter and much of relativistic dynamics is this one decomposition manipulated.

- **[[Def - The Orthogonal Projector onto the Local Rest Space|The orthogonal projector]]** $\perp_{U_0} = \mathrm{Id} - (\,\cdot\,U_0)\,U_0$: the operation that extracts the spatial part of a four-vector as a specific observer sees it. Its typical use is to *define* observer-relative spatial quantities frame-independently — the velocity $V = (1/\Gamma)\perp_{U_0}U$, the propagation direction $N = \perp_{U_0}\ell$, the relative acceleration. The reusable skill is recognising that "the spatial part" is not a choice of axes but the action of this projector, which depends only on $U_0$; whenever a problem says "the part of $X$ that the observer measures", apply $\perp_{U_0}$. Note the sign: in mostly-minus the projector is $\mathrm{Id} - (X \cdot U_0)U_0$ because $U_0 \cdot U_0 = +1$.

- **[[Thm - Maximum Relative Velocity is c|The unit-norm constraint]]** $U \cdot U = 1$: the relativistic budget equation. Imposed on the decomposition $U = \Gamma(U_0 + V)$, it yields $\Gamma = (1 - |\mathbf V|^2)^{-1/2}$ and the bound $|\mathbf V| < 1$. Its recognisable trigger is any expression for a four-velocity in terms of $\Gamma$ and $V$ — substitute and the unknown relation appears. The reusable content is that the indefinite metric makes this constraint *restrictive*: a timelike unit vector cannot have an arbitrarily large spatial part, which is the geometric origin of the speed limit, and it is why rapidity (unbounded) is the better coordinate than velocity (bounded by $c$).

- **[[Thm - Expression of the Four-Acceleration|The four-acceleration as proper acceleration]]**: $A = \boldsymbol{\gamma}$ at momentary rest. Its typical use is to compute the physically meaningful, frame-independent acceleration of a particle — the accelerometer reading — by evaluating it in the comoving inertial frame where it is just the Newtonian-looking relative acceleration. The reusable principle is that the four-acceleration is invariant while the relative acceleration is not, so to get an invariant you specialise to the frame ($\mathbf V = 0$) where the two coincide and then trust $\sqrt{-A\cdot A}$ to hold in all frames. This is the kinematic input to uniformly accelerated motion, where constant $|A|$ defines hyperbolic worldlines.

# Bridges

1. **Relativistic dynamics — the four-momentum is the four-velocity decomposition with a mass attached.** The orthogonal decomposition $U = \Gamma(U_0 + V)$ is the kinematic skeleton of **Special Relativity XIII**. Multiply through by the rest mass $m$ to form the [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass|four-momentum]] $P = mU = \Gamma m(U_0 + V)$, and project onto the observer: the component along $U_0$ is the energy $E = P \cdot U_0 = \Gamma m$, and the rest-space part is the spatial momentum $\mathbf p = \Gamma m\mathbf V$. The Lorentz factor that this chapter computes as a scalar product is therefore the *energy per unit mass* an observer measures, and the famous expansion $E = \Gamma m = m + \tfrac{1}{2}m|\mathbf V|^2 + \cdots$ — rest energy plus Newtonian kinetic energy plus corrections — is nothing but the Taylor expansion of the function $\Gamma(|\mathbf V|)$ that appears here. The constancy of $\Gamma$ for uniform motion, the bound $\Gamma \geq 1$, the divergence $\Gamma \to \infty$ as $|\mathbf V| \to c$: each becomes a statement about energy once the mass is attached, and the structure of the dynamics is fixed entirely by the kinematics of this chapter.

2. **Riemannian and Lorentzian geometry — the projector is the induced metric on a hypersurface.** The [[Def - The Orthogonal Projector onto the Local Rest Space|orthogonal projector]] $\perp_{U_0}$ onto the rest space is, in the language of submanifold geometry, the operator that induces a metric on the spacelike hyperplane $U_0^\perp$ from the ambient Minkowski metric. Restricted to that hyperplane it is positive-definite — the rest space is a copy of Euclidean $\mathbb{R}^3$ — which is why the speed $|\mathbf V| = \sqrt{-V\cdot V}$ and the propagation direction $|N| = 1$ are ordinary Euclidean lengths even though the ambient metric is indefinite. This is the flat, single-tangent-space instance of the general construction in [[Riemannian Geometry I — Connections and Covariant Differentiation]], where one projects the ambient connection onto a hypersurface to get its intrinsic geometry plus a second fundamental form; here the hypersurface is the instantaneous rest space, and the "second fundamental form" data — how the rest space tilts as the observer moves — is precisely the observer's four-acceleration and four-rotation. The bridge makes the local rest space the special-relativistic ancestor of the spatial slices of a general spacetime.

3. **General relativity — the position-dependent speed of light is the seed of gravitational light-bending.** The qualification on [[Thm - Invariance of the Velocity of Light]] — that the speed of light is $c$ only *at a point of the observer's worldline*, and that a non-inertial observer assigns a position-dependent coordinate speed $\mathbf V = (1 + A_0\cdot\overrightarrow{OM})\,c\,N$ to distant light — is not a defect of special relativity but the first glimpse of gravity. By Einstein's equivalence principle a uniformly accelerated observer is locally indistinguishable from one at rest in a uniform gravitational field, so the position-dependent coordinate speed of light that an accelerated observer measures is, in the gravitational reading, the statement that light slows and bends in a gravitational potential. This is exactly the **Shapiro time delay** and the bending of starlight by the Sun, the two classic tests that carried physics from [[Special Relativity III — Minkowski Spacetime and the Metric|Minkowski space]] to the curved metric $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ of [[General Relativity I — Einstein's Equations and Schwarzschild]]. The factor $1 + A_0 \cdot \overrightarrow{OM}$ is, to first order, the gravitational redshift factor $1 + \Phi$ with $\Phi$ the Newtonian potential; the chapter's accelerated-observer kinematics is the local model from which the global theory of gravitation is built.

4. **Particle physics — time dilation is what lets unstable particles reach a detector.** The cosmic-ray muon calculation of this chapter is not a toy: it is the everyday working tool of every accelerator and cosmic-ray experiment. A particle with proper lifetime $\tau_0$ produced at speed $V$ travels a mean distance $d = \Gamma V \tau_0$ in the laboratory before decaying, $\Gamma$ times farther than the naive $V\tau_0$, because its internal clock — and a decay is a clock, governed by the same proper time — runs slow by exactly the Lorentz factor this chapter computes. This is why pions ($\tau_0 \sim 26\,\mathrm{ns}$) can be steered down a beamline tens of metres long, why muons ($\tau_0 \sim 2.2\,\mu\mathrm{s}$) survive the kilometres from the upper atmosphere to a ground detector, and why a relativistic unstable particle's decay length is a direct measurement of its $\Gamma$. The Frisch–Smith and CERN storage-ring experiments turn the geometric fact $\mathrm{d}\tau = \Gamma\mathrm{d}\tau'$ into a quantitative, percent-level confirmation, and the same formula sets the design length of every secondary-beam line in particle physics.

5. **Conformal geometry — the celestial sphere and the Lorentz group as Möbius transformations.** The propagation directions $N$ that an observer can receive form the unit sphere in the rest space, the celestial sphere, and a change of observer maps this sphere to itself by a conformal (angle-preserving) transformation — a Möbius transformation when the sphere is identified with the Riemann sphere $\mathbb{CP}^1$. This is the bridge to **Special Relativity XI** and the spinor formalism: the double cover $SL(2,\mathbb{C}) \to SO^+(1,3)$ realises every Lorentz transformation as an action on $\mathbb{CP}^1$ by $z \mapsto (az+b)/(cz+d)$, and the aberration of starlight — the way the pattern of the sky distorts when you boost — is exactly this Möbius action made visible. The fact that aberration is *conformal* (it preserves the angles between nearby stars, distorting the sky like a fish-eye lens rather than shearing it) is a geometric statement about the propagation direction $N$ that this chapter introduces, and it is the reason the natural home of the Lorentz group is the conformal automorphisms of the two-sphere.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: kinematics is the geometry of two four-velocities.** The single idea that organises the whole chapter is that every quantity an observer measures about a moving particle is a *scalar product or projection of the two four-velocities*, $U_0$ (observer) and $U$ (particle), taken with the indefinite metric. The Lorentz factor is the scalar product $U \cdot U_0$. The relative velocity is the projection $(1/\Gamma)\perp_{U_0}U$. The relative acceleration is the projected second derivative. The propagation direction of light is the projection of an adapted null vector. Once this is seen, the "relativity" of these quantities is no longer mysterious: a quantity is observer-dependent precisely because it involves $U_0$, and changing the observer changes $U_0$. The invariant quantities — the norms $U \cdot U = 1$ and $A \cdot A$, the null character of a photon's tangent — are exactly the ones built without reference to any particular $U_0$. This is the same lesson as the rest of relativity (invariants are the objects, frame-dependent quantities are their shadows) sharpened to a procedure: to compute what an observer measures, write the relevant scalar product or projection against that observer's four-velocity; to find an invariant, build a scalar that mentions no observer.

**The true name of the Lorentz factor is "the scalar product of the two four-velocities".** In [[Special Relativity II — Simultaneity, Time Dilation and Length Contraction|Special Relativity II]] the Lorentz factor was $\gamma = (1 - v^2)^{-1/2}$, a function of a relative velocity that presupposed two inertial frames and a boost between them. That formula is right but parochial — it cannot even be written for an accelerated observer, who has no global frame and no single relative velocity. The operational characterisation, the one that generalises and that makes the proofs one-liners, is $\Gamma = U \cdot U_0$: the Lorentz factor is the Minkowski scalar product of the particle's four-velocity with the observer's. From this single identity, time dilation is the reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality ($U \cdot U_0 \geq 1$ for future timelike unit vectors), the symmetry of the effect (each sees the other's clock slow) is the symmetry of the scalar product, and the energy of relativistic dynamics is $\Gamma m = mU \cdot U_0$. When you see "Lorentz factor", do not picture the velocity formula — picture the scalar product of two unit timelike vectors, and the velocity formula becomes the special case where you have chosen one of them to be a coordinate axis.

**The constancy of the speed of light is a theorem about a unit vector, not a postulate.** Einstein assumed it; here it is derived, and the derivation pinpoints *why* it is true. A photon travels on a null geodesic, so its tangent $\ell$ is a null vector. Adapt it to an observer by $\ell \cdot U_0 = 1$ — the photon's substitute for the unit-norm condition a massive particle's four-velocity obeys — and decompose orthogonally: $\ell = U_0 + N$. The null condition $\ell \cdot \ell = 0$, expanded with $U_0 \cdot U_0 = 1$ and $N \cdot U_0 = 0$, forces $N \cdot N = -1$, i.e. $|N| = 1$ in the Euclidean rest-space metric. The propagation direction is automatically a *unit* vector, and the speed of light is its norm, exactly $1 = c$. The whole content of the constancy of light is this algebraic fact — that the spatial part of a null vector normalised by $\ell \cdot U_0 = 1$ has Euclidean length one — and it holds for any $U_0$ whatsoever, which is why every observer at a point of their worldline measures $c$. The experiments do not test a postulate; they test whether the spatial part of the photon's null tangent really has length one independent of the observer's direction (Michelson–Morley) and magnitude (Kennedy–Thorndike) of motion, and it does.

**Acceleration sorts cleanly into one invariant and one observer-dependent shadow.** A subtle but high-leverage observation is that the two "accelerations" of the chapter sit on opposite sides of the invariant/observer-dependent divide, and confusing them is the most common error. The four-acceleration $A = \mathrm{d}U/\mathrm{d}\tau'$ is a genuine four-vector: it mentions no observer, its norm $\sqrt{-A\cdot A}$ is the proper acceleration that an accelerometer carried by the particle reads, and all observers agree on it. The relative acceleration $\boldsymbol{\gamma} = \mathrm{d}\mathbf V/\mathrm{d}\tau$ is a projection: it is differentiated with respect to the *observer's* time and lives in the *observer's* rest space, so it depends on the observer, and two inertial observers in relative motion measure different $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ for the same particle. They coincide only at the instant of momentary rest, $\mathbf V = 0$, where the comoving inertial observer's rest space and the particle's tangent align — and that coincidence is the operational definition of proper acceleration. The reusable diagnostic: if a problem's answer should be the same for all observers, it must be built from $A$, not $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$; if it is what a *particular* observer's instruments read, it is $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$, and you must say which observer.
