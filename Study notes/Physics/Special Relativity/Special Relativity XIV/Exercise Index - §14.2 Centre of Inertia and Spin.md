## §14.2 Centre of Inertia and Spin — Exercises

The exercises of §14.2 confront the chapter's two genuinely relativistic surprises: that the centre of mass depends on who is looking, and that a spinning body cannot be arbitrarily small. The centroid $\overrightarrow{OG_\mathcal{O}} = \frac1E\sum_a E_a\overrightarrow{OM_a}$ is an energy-weighted mean position, and *both* the positions (taken in a rest space that tilts with the observer) and the weights (energies, which transform) are observer-relative — so two observers in relative motion place the centre of mass of the *same* system at different events, displaced by $\vec\sigma\times\vec V/(mc^2)$. The intrinsic point, the centre of inertia $G$, exists only because one selects the comoving (barycentric) observers and agrees to use their answer. Out of this comes the spin: the angular momentum vector relative to a barycentric observer turns out to be *independent of the reference point* $C$, and that common value is the spin $\vec\sigma$, whose four-vector form is the [[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group|Pauli–Lubanski vector]] divided by the mass, $S^\mu = W^\mu/(mc)$, satisfying $S\cdot U = 0$. The König theorem packages this as "total $=$ spin $+$ orbital", and the Møller bound $R \geq \|\vec\sigma\|/(mc)$ turns the observer-dependence of the centroid into a floor on the size of anything that spins. The unifying skill is to keep the intrinsic (the spin, the centre of inertia, the Møller radius) rigidly apart from the observer-relative (the centroid, the energy, the dipole), and to work in the barycentric frame where the intrinsic objects are cleanest.

- [[Ex - Frame-dependence of the centre of inertia]] — compute the centroid $\overrightarrow{OG_\mathcal{O}} = \frac1E\sum_a E_a\overrightarrow{OM_a}$ of a simple two-particle system in two different inertial frames and show the results are genuinely different events, trace the discrepancy to the two observer-relative ingredients (the tilting rest space and the transforming energies), and confirm the displacement is $\overrightarrow{GG_\mathcal{O}} = \frac1{mc^2}\vec\sigma\times\vec V_\mathcal{O}$ — the single most surprising fact of the chapter, with no Newtonian shadow (⭐⭐⭐) ([[Def - Centre of Inertia]], [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass]], [[Def - Observer and Local Rest Space]], [[Def - Spin Four-Vector]]).

- [[Ex - The spin four-vector and the Pauli-Lubanski vector]] — show that for an isolated system the angular momentum vector relative to a barycentric observer is independent of the reference point $C$, identify this common value with the spin, and prove the spin four-vector equals the Pauli–Lubanski vector over the mass, $S^\mu = W^\mu/(mc)$, with $W^\mu = -\frac12\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}J_{\nu\rho}P_\sigma$, deducing $S\cdot U = 0$ from $W\cdot P = 0$ — the classical skeleton of the second Poincaré Casimir (⭐⭐) ([[Def - Spin Four-Vector]], [[Def - Casimir Invariants of the Poincaré Group]], [[Def - Angular Momentum Four-Tensor]], [[Def - The Levi-Civita Tensor]]).

- [[Ex - The König decomposition into spin and orbital parts]] — prove the relativistic König theorem $J_C = S + \overrightarrow{CG}^\flat\wedge P$, splitting the total angular momentum about any point into the point-independent spin $S$ (the angular momentum about the centre of inertia) and the orbital part carried by a fictitious point particle at $G$ with the total four-momentum, and verify all the $C$-dependence lives in the orbital term — the relativistic upgrade of the Newtonian centre-of-mass theorem (⭐⭐) ([[Thm - König Theorem (Relativistic)]], [[Def - Centre of Inertia]], [[Def - Spin Four-Vector]], [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass]]).

- [[Ex - The Møller radius and the minimal size of a spinning body]] — from the centroid displacement $\overrightarrow{GG_\mathcal{O}} = \frac1{mc^2}\vec\sigma\times\vec V_\mathcal{O}$ show the set of all possible centroids fills a disk of radius $R_0 = \|\vec\sigma\|/(mc)$ perpendicular to the spin, conclude the body's radius satisfies $R \geq R_0$ (a spinning system cannot be arbitrarily small, because holding fixed spin in a shrinking body forces the constituents past the speed of light), and promote the bound to the quantum Compton wavelength $R_0 = \sqrt{s(s+1)}\,\lambda_C$ (⭐⭐⭐) ([[Thm - Minimal Size of a Spinning System]], [[Def - Centre of Inertia]], [[Def - Spin Four-Vector]], [[Def - Four-Momentum and Rest Mass]]).
