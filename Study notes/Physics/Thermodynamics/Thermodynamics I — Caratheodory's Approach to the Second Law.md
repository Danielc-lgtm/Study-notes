# Notation Registry

A standing convention for the whole topic: **we work with a single thermodynamic system in equilibrium, with state space a smooth manifold $M$ of dimension $n+1$**. For a simple gas in a fluid bag, $M$ is two-dimensional; for a collection of $n$ bags of fluid separated by diathermous (heat-permeable, fluid-impermeable) membranes, $M$ has dimension $n+1$, with one coordinate for each volume and a single internal-energy coordinate $U$ pinning down the temperature. We assume the system is **simple** in the sense of Caratheodory: every state can be reached from every other state by some combination of quasistatic and non-quasistatic processes. Units throughout are SI; entropies have units of joules per kelvin.

- $M$, $M^{n+1}$ — the thermodynamic state space; an $(n+1)$-dimensional smooth manifold
- $V^n$ — the **mechanical manifold** or volume-coordinate manifold (the base of the submersion $\pi : M \to V$ projecting away the energy coordinate)
- $V, v^i$ — volume coordinates on $V^n$; for a simple gas, $V$ alone
- $U$ — the **internal energy**, a globally defined function $U : M \to \mathbb{R}$
- $p$, $p_i$ — pressure(s) of the system; for a simple gas, $p$ alone
- $T$ — **absolute temperature**, a globally defined function $T : M \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$
- $S$ — **entropy**, a globally defined function $S : M \to \mathbb{R}$
- $\delta W$, $W^1$ — the **work 1-form**; for a simple gas, $\delta W = p\, dV$; in general $\delta W = \sum_i p_i\, dv^i$
- $\delta Q$, $Q^1$ — the **heat 1-form**; a smooth 1-form on $M$ that vanishes nowhere
- $dU$ — the differential of $U$, an exact 1-form
- $dS$ — the differential of $S$, an exact 1-form
- $\delta Q = T\, dS$ — the central conclusion of the topic; $T$ is the integrating factor and $S$ the integral
- $\delta Q \wedge d(\delta Q)$ — the Frobenius obstruction; vanishing means the kernel of $\delta Q$ is integrable
- $H = U + pV$ — the **enthalpy**
- $F = U - TS$ — the **Helmholtz free energy**
- $G = U + pV - TS = H - TS$ — the **Gibbs free energy**
- $\gamma : [0,1] \to M$ — a quasistatic process (a smooth path); $\dot\gamma$ its tangent vector
- $\ker \delta Q$ — the **adiabatic distribution**, an $n$-plane field on $M^{n+1}$
- $\mathcal{D}$, $\Delta^k$ — generic notation for a smooth $k$-dimensional distribution on a manifold

The notation $\delta Q$ and $\delta W$ (rather than $dQ$ and $dW$) is used to **emphasise that $\delta Q$ and $\delta W$ are not differentials** of any state function — they are merely 1-forms that fail to be exact. Many older physics texts write $\bar d Q$ instead; we follow Frankel and use the unambiguous $\delta Q$, $\delta W$. The first law is then $dU = \delta Q - \delta W$, with $dU$ exact and $\delta Q$, $\delta W$ generally not.

> [!warning] Convention: sign of the work form
> We use $\delta W = +p\, dV$, so that the work done **by the system** on its surroundings appears with a positive sign and the first law reads $dU = \delta Q - \delta W$. Some texts (especially in chemistry) define $\delta W$ as work done **on** the system and write $dU = \delta Q + \delta W$. To convert, flip every sign on $W$ throughout. Heat $\delta Q$ is always heat **added to** the system.

# Motivation

Here is the entire topic in one sentence: **adiabatic inaccessibility forces the heat 1-form to be integrable, and integrability forces the existence of absolute temperature and entropy.** That single sentence is the content of Caratheodory's 1909 rederivation of the second law of thermodynamics, and the rest of this chapter unpacks each clause: what "adiabatic" means as a geometric condition, what "inaccessibility" means as a topological condition, and what the [[Thm - The Frobenius Theorem|Frobenius theorem]] gives you once you put them together.

Classical thermodynamics, as the nineteenth century developed it, is a tangle of cycles and heat reservoirs and idealised engines, with the second law stated in several mutually equivalent but conceptually awkward forms — Kelvin's, Clausius's, Planck's — each prohibiting some specific impossible process. Caratheodory, in a paper written at the urging of Max Born, asked: what is the *geometric* content of these prohibitions? He found a single answer. The state space of a simple thermodynamic system is a smooth manifold $M$, the quasistatic processes are smooth paths in $M$, and the differential of heat added is a 1-form $\delta Q$ on $M$ — but a 1-form that is *not* exact, because heat is not a state function. The adiabatic processes — those in which no heat is exchanged — are paths along which $\delta Q$ vanishes, the integral curves of the **adiabatic distribution** $\ker \delta Q$. Caratheodory's version of the second law is the geometric statement that this distribution is **integrable**, and the entropy is the function that locally labels its leaves.

The pivotal mathematical input is a contrapositive theorem about non-integrable distributions, due in modern form to Chow. If a distribution on a connected manifold is *not* integrable — and in fact is "very non-integrable", with brackets of vector fields spanning the whole tangent space — then any two points are connected by a horizontal path tangent to the distribution. The contrapositive is what we need: if there exist nearby states that are *not* connected by any horizontal (adiabatic) path, then the distribution must be integrable. Caratheodory's principle asserts exactly this: in every neighbourhood of every equilibrium state there are other states adiabatically inaccessible from it. Combined with Chow, this forces $\ker \delta Q$ to be involutive, and by the [[Thm - The Frobenius Theorem|Frobenius theorem]] equivalently to be integrable; and an integrable codimension-one distribution defined by a 1-form $\delta Q$ admits an integrating factor — there exist functions $T$ and $S$ with $\delta Q = T\, dS$. Identifying $T$ as absolute temperature and $S$ as entropy requires one more physical input (combining two systems in thermal contact, which fixes $T$ up to a universal constant), but the bulk of the work is done by the geometry.

The structural backbone of the chapter is the implication chain

$$\text{Caratheodory's principle (inaccessibility)} \implies \ker\delta Q \text{ involutive} \stackrel{\text{Frobenius}}{\iff} \ker\delta Q \text{ integrable} \iff \delta Q \wedge d(\delta Q) = 0 \iff \delta Q = T\, dS,$$

and every section of the chapter belongs to one step of this chain. §1.1 sets up the state space and the first law, defining $\delta Q$ and $\delta W$ as Pfaffian (non-exact) 1-forms and contrasting them with the exact differential $dU$ of internal energy. §1.2 states Caratheodory's principle and proves the Caratheodory–Frobenius theorem: inaccessibility forces involutivity. §1.3 extracts $T$ and $S$ from the integrating-factor representation and proves the entropy-increase principle for irreversible (non-quasistatic) processes. §1.4 introduces the four standard thermodynamic potentials $U$, $H$, $F$, $G$ obtained by Legendre transforms, and recovers the **Maxwell relations** as the closedness condition $d^2 = 0$ applied to each potential's differential.

This is, with one caveat, a complete and self-contained derivation of the second law: the caveat is that thermodynamics says nothing about *why* entropy increases at the microscopic level — that is the province of **statistical mechanics**, where $S$ becomes Boltzmann's $S = k_B \log W$ counting microstates compatible with a macrostate, and the irreversibility of "stirring" becomes a statement about phase-space measure. The reader is assumed to know smooth manifolds, differential forms, the exterior derivative, the wedge product, and especially the Frobenius theorem and integrability of distributions — all of [[Differential Geometry VIII — Differential Forms|DG VIII]] and [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius|DG X]]. A reader who has done [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics]] will see the Legendre-transform structure of §1.4 immediately, since it is the same construction that takes a Lagrangian on $TQ$ to a Hamiltonian on $T^*Q$.

# Concept Map

## §1.1 State Space and the First Law

- **[[Def - Thermodynamic State Space]]**
	- The thermodynamic state space of an equilibrium system is a smooth manifold $M^{n+1}$ whose points are equilibrium states, with $n+1$ independent state variables — for a simple gas in one bag, $M$ is two-dimensional with coordinates $(V, T)$ or $(V, p)$ or $(V, U)$ interchangeably. There is a submersion $\pi : M \to V^n$ onto a mechanical manifold of pure volume coordinates, and the internal energy $U : M \to \mathbb{R}$ is a globally defined function that parametrises each fibre $\pi^{-1}(v)$. The quasistatic processes are smooth paths $\gamma : [0,1] \to M$; non-quasistatic processes (stirring, sudden expansion) jump between non-adjacent equilibrium states and are represented as dashed connections, not paths in $M$.

- **[[Def - Quasistatic Process]]**
	- A quasistatic process is a smooth path $\gamma : [0,1] \to M$ in the state space, interpreted physically as a transition slow enough that the system remains arbitrarily close to equilibrium at every instant — every intermediate point of the path is itself an equilibrium state. Real processes are never quasistatic, but reversible idealisations (slow compression, slow heating) are well-approximated by them. The work and heat 1-forms can be integrated along quasistatic paths; for non-quasistatic transitions (stirring, free expansion), $\int \delta Q$ and $\int \delta W$ are meaningless because no path exists. Quasistatic ≠ reversible; a quasistatic process with friction is irreversible but still represented by a path in $M$.

- **[[Def - Heat 1-Form and Work 1-Form]]**
	- The **work 1-form** $\delta W$ and **heat 1-form** $\delta Q$ are smooth 1-forms on $M$, never zero, such that for any quasistatic process $\gamma$ the line integrals $\int_\gamma \delta W$ and $\int_\gamma \delta Q$ give the work done by the system and the heat absorbed by the system along $\gamma$. For a simple gas, $\delta W = p\, dV$; in general $\delta W = \sum p_i\, dv^i$ where $(v^1, \ldots, v^n)$ are coordinates on the mechanical manifold $V^n$. Neither $\delta W$ nor $\delta Q$ is exact in general — they are Pfaffian forms, and their line integrals depend on the path, not just the endpoints. This path dependence is exactly what makes heat and work "transit quantities" rather than state functions, and is captured by the notation $\delta$ rather than $d$.

- **[[Def - The First Law of Thermodynamics]]**
	- The first law of thermodynamics is the equation $dU = \delta Q - \delta W$ of 1-forms on the state space $M$: along any quasistatic process, the change in internal energy equals heat absorbed minus work done. The crucial structural content is that $dU$ is **exact** — it is the differential of the globally defined state function $U$ — while $\delta Q$ and $\delta W$ are Pfaffian forms that are individually **not exact**. Heat and work, separately, are path-dependent; their algebraic difference is not. This is the conservation-of-energy axiom of thermodynamics, and the *exactness* of $dU$ is the substantive content: it says energy is a state function (a property of the state, not of how the state was reached).

> [!tip] Unlocked: Closed Differential Form *(from Differential Geometry)*
> The first law is the assertion that $\delta Q - \delta W = dU$ is **exact**, even though neither summand is. This is the simplest physical instance of the distinction between [[Def - Closed and Exact Forms|closed and exact forms]]: heat and work are non-exact 1-forms whose difference is exact, and the failure of $\delta Q$ to be closed ($d(\delta Q) \neq 0$ in general) is precisely the obstruction Caratheodory must overcome via Frobenius.

- **[[Ex - The Heat 1-Form for an Ideal Gas]]** (⭐)
	- Compute $\delta Q$ explicitly for a simple ideal gas $pV = nRT$, $U = \frac{f}{2} nRT$, expressing it in $(V, T)$ coordinates and verifying that $\delta Q$ is not closed but admits the integrating factor $1/T$.

- **[[Ex - Verifying dU is Exact for an Ideal Gas]]** (⭐)
	- Verify directly that the differential $dU$ of the internal energy is exact (cross-partial test) for an ideal gas, and verify path-independence of $\int dU$ along two different connecting paths. Illustrates the first law's geometric content.

- **[[Ex - Work Done in Isothermal vs Isobaric Expansion]]** (⭐)
	- Compute the work done by an ideal gas along an isothermal, isobaric, and two-leg path between the same endpoints, illustrating that work is path-dependent while $\Delta U$ is not.

## §1.2 Caratheodory's Principle and the Second Law

- **[[Def - Adiabatic Process and Adiabatic Distribution]]**
	- An **adiabatic process** is a quasistatic path $\gamma$ along which no heat is exchanged: $\delta Q(\dot\gamma) = 0$ at every instant — equivalently, $\gamma$ is an integral curve of the **adiabatic distribution** $\Delta := \ker \delta Q \subset TM$, the codimension-one plane field annihilated by $\delta Q$. Since $\delta Q$ never vanishes, $\Delta$ is a smooth distribution of rank $n$ on $M^{n+1}$. The question Caratheodory poses is whether $\Delta$ is integrable — whether the adiabatic processes locally fill out submanifolds — and the second law will turn out to be exactly the assertion that it is.

- **[[Def - Caratheodory's Principle (Inaccessibility)]]**
	- **Caratheodory's principle.** In every neighbourhood of every equilibrium state $x \in M$ there exist states $y$ that are *not* accessible from $x$ by any quasistatic adiabatic path. This is Caratheodory's 1909 axiomatic statement of the second law of thermodynamics — purely geometric, with no mention of cycles, engines, or heat reservoirs. It is implied by Kelvin's statement (no cyclic process converts heat entirely into work) by a short argument involving cooling at constant volume, but it is logically *weaker*, and the Caratheodory program is to show it is nevertheless strong enough to derive entropy and absolute temperature.

- **[[Thm - Chow's Connectivity Theorem (Statement)]]**
	- If $\Delta$ is a smooth bracket-generating distribution on a connected manifold $M$ — meaning the iterated Lie brackets of vector fields in $\Delta$ eventually span the whole tangent space — then any two points of $M$ are connected by a piecewise smooth path tangent to $\Delta$. The contrapositive is the form we use: if there exist arbitrarily nearby points not joinable by a $\Delta$-tangent path, then $\Delta$ is *not* bracket-generating, and in the codimension-one case (which is our setting) it must in fact be involutive. Chow's theorem is the heart of sub-Riemannian geometry and control theory; it provides the engine that converts Caratheodory's "inaccessibility" hypothesis into a hard integrability condition on $\ker \delta Q$.

- **[[Thm - Caratheodory's Theorem on the Second Law]]**
	- **The central theorem.** Let $\theta$ be a smooth nowhere-vanishing 1-form on a manifold $M^{n+1}$, and suppose Caratheodory's inaccessibility principle holds for $\theta$: in every neighbourhood of every point, there exist points not connected by any piecewise smooth $\theta$-horizontal path. Then $\theta \wedge d\theta = 0$ — the distribution $\ker \theta$ is involutive, and by Frobenius, integrable. Hence locally $\theta = T\, dS$ for some functions $T$ (an integrating factor, nonzero where $\theta$ is) and $S$ (a local entropy). Applied to $\theta = \delta Q$, this proves the existence of local absolute temperature and entropy from Caratheodory's version of the second law alone. The one-liner mechanism is **adiabatic inaccessibility = integrability = existence of an integrating factor = existence of entropy.**

> [!tip] Unlocked: Sub-Riemannian Geometry *(from Control Theory and Sub-Riemannian Geometry)*
> The geometric setup of Caratheodory's theorem — a distribution on a manifold, asking which points are connected by horizontal paths — is the foundation of **sub-Riemannian geometry** and **nonholonomic mechanics**. Chow's theorem is its fundamental connectivity theorem; the Heisenberg group is its prototypical example. The same dichotomy that distinguishes holonomic (integrable) from nonholonomic (bracket-generating) constraints in [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius|DG X]] is the dichotomy that distinguishes "entropy exists" from "entropy does not exist" in thermodynamics — Caratheodory's principle is the thermodynamic input that selects the holonomic side.

- **[[Ex - Non-Integrability of a Hypothetical Adiabatic Distribution (Counterexample)]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Take the hypothetical "heat 1-form" $\theta = y\, dx - x\, dy + dz$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ — Frankel's Heisenberg-like form — and compute $\theta \wedge d\theta = -2\, dx \wedge dy \wedge dz \neq 0$. Conclude $\ker \theta$ is not integrable, and verify directly via Chow's theorem that every point of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is reachable from the origin by piecewise horizontal paths — so a system with this $\delta Q$ would *violate* Caratheodory's principle and admit no entropy.

- **[[Ex - Kelvin Implies Caratheodory]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Prove that Kelvin's statement of the second law (no cyclic process converts heat from a single reservoir entirely to work) implies Caratheodory's principle (inaccessible nearby states exist). The proof constructs a cycle using an isochore and a hypothetical adiabatic return, showing the negation of Caratheodory leads to a Kelvin violation.

- **[[Ex - Adiabatic Equation of State for Ideal Gas]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Derive $pV^\gamma = \text{const}$ for an ideal gas adiabatic process, with $\gamma = C_p/C_V = (f+2)/f$. Sets $\delta Q = 0$ in the heat 1-form, separates variables, and integrates. Illustrates how the adiabatic distribution's integral curves are computed in the simplest case (2D state space).

## §1.3 Entropy and Absolute Temperature

- **[[Thm - The Heat 1-Form is Integrable]]**
	- Given Caratheodory's principle, the heat 1-form $\delta Q$ satisfies $\delta Q \wedge d(\delta Q) = 0$ on $M$ — the adiabatic distribution $\ker \delta Q$ is involutive, hence by [[Thm - The Frobenius Theorem|Frobenius]] integrable, and the integral submanifolds are the **adiabatic surfaces** (codimension-one leaves of the adiabatic foliation). The integrability condition is equivalent to the existence, locally, of smooth functions $T \neq 0$ and $S$ with $\delta Q = T\, dS$. This is the immediate corollary of [[Thm - Caratheodory's Theorem on the Second Law]] specialised to $\theta = \delta Q$.

- **[[Thm - Existence of Integrating Factor for an Inaccessible Pfaffian]]**
	- For any smooth nowhere-vanishing 1-form $\theta$ on $M^{n+1}$ satisfying $\theta \wedge d\theta = 0$, there exist locally smooth functions $\lambda \neq 0$ and $f$ with $\theta = \lambda\, df$. This is the standard reformulation of [[Thm - The Frobenius Theorem|Frobenius]] for a single 1-form, and it is what allows the entropy and integrating factor to be extracted from integrability. Globally, the integrating factor and the antiderivative $f$ exist on a single Frobenius chart but may fail to extend over $M$ if the foliation has nontrivial leaf-space topology — the leap from local entropy to global entropy requires an extra physical assumption (every adiabatic leaf meets a fixed transversal "heating curve").

- **[[Def - Absolute Temperature and Entropy]]**
	- Once $\delta Q = T\, dS$ globally with $T > 0$, the function $T$ is the **absolute temperature** and $S$ the **entropy** of the system. They are not unique: $S$ is defined up to additive and multiplicative constants and $T$ correspondingly. To pin them down universally one combines two systems in thermal contact and demands that the integrating factor be a function of empirical temperature alone, identical for both subsystems — this fixes $T$ up to a universal multiplicative constant (the choice of unit, e.g. kelvin) and identifies $T$ as the *thermodynamic temperature* common to all systems in equilibrium. The entropy then satisfies the **second law in Caratheodory form**: $dS \geq \delta Q / T$ with equality for quasistatic processes, and $\Delta S \geq 0$ for any adiabatic process whether quasistatic or not.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Statistical Mechanics *(from Statistical Mechanics)*
> The thermodynamic entropy $S$ defined here is purely macroscopic and tells you nothing about the microscopic origin of irreversibility. **Statistical mechanics** derives $S$ from microscopic dynamics: Boltzmann's $S = k_B \log W$ counts the number of microstates compatible with a given macrostate, and identifies the thermodynamic temperature $T$ as $1/T = \partial S/\partial U$. This identification rests on the assumption that the system explores microstates uniformly (the **ergodic hypothesis**) and the integrating factor $T$ of Caratheodory becomes the inverse of the **Lagrange multiplier** in the maximum-entropy derivation of the **Gibbs measure**.

- **[[Ex - Carnot Cycle in Pressure-Volume and in Temperature-Entropy]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Draw the Carnot cycle in $(p, V)$ coordinates as two isotherms joined by two adiabats; redraw it in $(T, S)$ coordinates as a rectangle; compute the efficiency $\eta = 1 - T_c/T_h$ from the rectangle's geometry. The $(T,S)$ picture *defines* the integrating factor by demanding that adiabats become straight vertical lines.

- **[[Ex - Compute the Entropy of an Ideal Gas]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Integrate $dS = \delta Q / T = (C_V/T)\, dT + (p/T)\, dV$ for an ideal gas to obtain $S(T, V) = C_V \log T + nR \log V + \text{const}$. Verify $dS$ is exact, identify the constant up to the third law, and notice that "additive constant" is precisely the freedom Boltzmann fixes via $S = k_B \log W$.

- **[[Ex - Entropy Change in Free Expansion (Irreversible)]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Compute the entropy change $\Delta S = nR \log(V_2/V_1) > 0$ for an ideal gas in irreversible free expansion through a valve into vacuum. Demonstrates that $\Delta S$ is computed via state-function differences, not $\int \delta Q_{\text{actual}}/T$ (which would give zero for this adiabatic process), and verifies the second-law inequality with strict inequality.

## §1.4 Thermodynamic Potentials

- **[[Def - Thermodynamic Potential (U, H, F, G)]]**
	- A **thermodynamic potential** is a state function whose differential, restricted to its natural variables, encodes the equation of state. The four standard potentials for a simple gas are: **internal energy** $U(S, V)$ with $dU = T\, dS - p\, dV$; **enthalpy** $H(S, p) = U + pV$ with $dH = T\, dS + V\, dp$; **Helmholtz free energy** $F(T, V) = U - TS$ with $dF = -S\, dT - p\, dV$; and **Gibbs free energy** $G(T, p) = U + pV - TS$ with $dG = -S\, dT + V\, dp$. Each is obtained from $U$ by a [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics|Legendre transform]] swapping one conjugate pair $(S, T)$ or $(V, p)$. The natural variables matter — only when each potential is expressed in its own natural variables does its differential give the full equation of state.

- **[[Thm - Maxwell Relations from Closedness]]**
	- For each thermodynamic potential, the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives (equivalently, $d^2 = 0$ applied to the potential's differential) gives a **Maxwell relation** linking partial derivatives of the conjugate variables. From $dU = T\, dS - p\, dV$: $(\partial T/\partial V)_S = -(\partial p/\partial S)_V$. From $dH$: $(\partial T/\partial p)_S = (\partial V/\partial S)_p$. From $dF$: $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial p/\partial T)_V$. From $dG$: $(\partial S/\partial p)_T = -(\partial V/\partial T)_p$. These let you convert hard-to-measure derivatives (involving $S$) into easy-to-measure ones (involving $T, p, V$), and they are simply the closedness condition $d^2 = 0$ applied to each potential.

> [!tip] Unlocked: Legendre Transform in Mechanics and Convex Analysis *(from Geometric Mechanics and Convex Analysis)*
> The construction $F = U - TS$ that takes the energy $U(S, V)$ to the Helmholtz free energy $F(T, V)$ is the **Legendre transform** swapping $S$ for its conjugate variable $T = \partial U/\partial S$. This is the same construction that takes a Lagrangian $L(q, \dot q)$ to a Hamiltonian $H(q, p)$ in [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics]], and the same construction that takes a convex function $f$ to its **convex conjugate** $f^*(p) = \sup_x [px - f(x)]$ in convex analysis. The signs are chosen so that $F$ is minimised in equilibrium at fixed $(T, V)$, just as $H$ is conserved along Hamiltonian flow — the involution-and-sign structure is identical.

- **[[Ex - Maxwell's Relation from dG=0]]** (⭐)
	- Starting from $dG = -S\, dT + V\, dp$, compute $d^2 G = 0$ to derive $(\partial S/\partial p)_T = -(\partial V/\partial T)_p$. Use it to evaluate the entropy change of a gas under isothermal compression in terms of the experimentally accessible thermal expansion coefficient $\alpha = V^{-1}(\partial V/\partial T)_p$.

- **[[Ex - Joule-Thomson Coefficient from Thermodynamic Identities]]** (⭐⭐⭐)
	- Compute the Joule-Thomson coefficient $\mu_{JT} = (\partial T/\partial p)_H$ governing the temperature change of a gas in a throttling (isenthalpic) expansion. Use $dH = T\, dS + V\, dp = 0$ and a Maxwell relation to express $\mu_{JT}$ in terms of $T$, $V$, $p$, and the heat capacity $C_p$, and verify that for an ideal gas $\mu_{JT} = 0$ but for a Van der Waals gas it is nonzero — the inversion point separates heating from cooling.

- **[[Ex - Mayer's Relation Cp Minus CV from First Law]]** (⭐⭐)
	- Derive the general identity $C_p - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T$ relating the heat capacity difference to the thermal expansion coefficient and isothermal compressibility, using a Maxwell relation and the cyclic relation. Specialise to an ideal gas to obtain Mayer's relation $C_p - C_V = nR$.

# Sources and Targets

**Targets — what do we usually try to prove?**

The recurring goals in this topic fall into five types, each tied to one step of the implication chain in the [[Thermodynamics I — Caratheodory's Approach to the Second Law#Motivation|Motivation]] section. The most common is **identifying a state function from its differential**: given a 1-form representing some physical transit quantity, decide whether it is closed (so a potential exists) or merely admits an integrating factor (so a potential exists after multiplication by $1/T$ or similar). The second is **deriving Maxwell-type identities**: equating mixed partial derivatives of a thermodynamic potential gives relations among partial derivatives of state variables, and almost every "useful" identity in classical thermodynamics is a Maxwell relation in disguise. The third is **computing entropy changes along specified processes** — isothermal, isobaric, isochoric, adiabatic — by integrating $dS = \delta Q / T$ piecewise, or in the irreversible case bounding $\Delta S$ from above and below. The fourth is **computing efficiencies of cyclic processes** (Carnot, Otto, Stirling) in $(T, S)$ coordinates where the cycle becomes a rectangle and the efficiency is a ratio of areas. The fifth is **demonstrating integrability or non-integrability of a candidate 1-form** by computing the Frobenius obstruction $\theta \wedge d\theta$; this is the most directly geometric target and the one that links the chapter to [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius|DG X]]. These five — identify potential, derive Maxwell, compute $\Delta S$, compute cycle efficiency, decide integrability — are the targets, and they are recurring because each tests one face of the central object: $\delta Q = T\, dS$ in five different lighting conditions.

**Sources — what assumptions do we usually leverage?**

The assumptions are equally stereotyped. **An equation of state is given** — $pV = nRT$ for an ideal gas, the Van der Waals equation, a tabulated $p(V, T)$ — and this lets you express $\delta Q$ in coordinates and integrate it explicitly. **A specific process is specified** — isothermal, isobaric, isochoric, adiabatic — and the process specification picks out one of the four standard 1-form restrictions: $dT = 0$, $dp = 0$, $dV = 0$, or $\delta Q = 0$. **A heat capacity is known** — $C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_V$ or $C_p = (\partial H/\partial T)_p$ — and this is the bridge between temperature changes and heat exchange. **A cyclic process is given** by a closed path in $M$, and the integral $\oint \delta Q$ around the cycle is the net heat absorbed (which equals net work done, since $\oint dU = 0$). **The system is in thermal contact with a reservoir at fixed $T$** — an isothermal source — and this lets you replace $\int \delta Q$ by $T \Delta S$ across the relevant arc. The recurring routes: an equation of state plus a process specification routes through $\delta Q = T\, dS$ to an entropy computation; a thermodynamic potential plus $d^2 = 0$ routes through Maxwell relations to an identity among partial derivatives; a cyclic process plus $\oint dU = 0$ routes through the first law to a net work computation; a candidate Pfaffian plus the wedge $\theta \wedge d\theta$ routes through Frobenius to a yes/no integrability verdict.

These are the moves that nearly every problem in this topic is assembled from. When stuck, scan the list. Each operation is self-contained, with the wikilink targets explaining anything not defined here.

**Legal operations:**

1. **Split a 1-form into exact and non-exact parts using the first law.** Whenever $\delta Q$ or $\delta W$ appears, use $\delta Q = dU + \delta W$ (the first law solved for $\delta Q$) or $\delta Q = T\, dS$ (the second law statement) to convert it into expressions involving exact differentials of state functions. *Trigger:* you see $\delta Q$ in an integrand. *Pattern:* substitute, expand, and observe which terms integrate path-independently.

2. **Compute the Frobenius obstruction $\theta \wedge d\theta$ to test integrability.** For any 1-form $\theta$ on a manifold of dimension $\geq 3$, the wedge $\theta \wedge d\theta$ is a 3-form, and $\theta \wedge d\theta = 0$ identically is equivalent (by [[Thm - Frobenius Theorem in Forms Language|Frobenius in forms language]]) to the existence of locally defined functions $\lambda, f$ with $\theta = \lambda\, df$. *Trigger:* you are asked whether $\theta$ admits an integrating factor. *Pattern:* compute $d\theta$, wedge with $\theta$, simplify, set equal to zero.

3. **Restrict a 1-form to a process by pulling back along the path.** A process $\gamma$ is a path in $M$; the heat absorbed is $\int_\gamma \delta Q = \int_0^1 \delta Q(\dot\gamma(t))\, dt$, computable once $\delta Q$ is given in coordinates and $\gamma$ is parametrised. *Trigger:* the problem specifies a particular process and asks for a thermodynamic quantity. *Pattern:* parametrise $\gamma$, evaluate $\delta Q$, integrate.

4. **Use the closedness $d^2 = 0$ on a potential to extract Maxwell relations.** For any thermodynamic potential $\Phi$ with $d\Phi = A\, dx + B\, dy$ in some natural coordinates, applying $d$ again gives $0 = dA \wedge dx + dB \wedge dy$, which equates a mixed partial derivative of $A$ with one of $B$. *Trigger:* you are asked to relate two partial derivatives that "look unrelated". *Pattern:* identify which potential has those two derivatives as its natural conjugate coefficients, apply $d^2 = 0$, read off the relation.

5. **Legendre-transform a potential to change natural variables.** From $U(S, V)$ with $dU = T\, dS - p\, dV$, define $F = U - TS$; then $dF = -S\, dT - p\, dV$ has $T, V$ as natural variables. The transform is involutive (up to sign) and swaps one conjugate pair while leaving the other fixed. *Trigger:* the problem fixes an unnatural variable for the potential at hand. *Pattern:* identify the swap needed, perform the algebraic substitution, recompute the differential.

6. **Integrate along an adiabat by setting $\delta Q = 0$.** Along any adiabatic process $\gamma$, the constraint $\delta Q(\dot\gamma) = 0$ reduces the first law to $dU = -\delta W$ — internal energy change equals minus work done. For an ideal gas this integrates to $pV^\gamma = \text{const}$ (the adiabatic equation of state). *Trigger:* the problem says "adiabatic" or "no heat exchanged". *Pattern:* set the heat form to zero, solve the resulting ODE for the process path.

7. **Bound entropy change for irreversible processes from below.** For any process (quasistatic or not) connecting equilibrium states $x$ and $y$ with no net heat exchanged with the environment, $\Delta S = S(y) - S(x) \geq 0$, and the equality holds iff the process is reversible. For a process exchanging heat $Q$ with a reservoir at temperature $T_{\text{res}}$, $\Delta S \geq Q/T_{\text{res}}$ (Clausius's inequality). *Trigger:* the problem mentions an irreversible step like stirring, mixing, or free expansion. *Pattern:* bracket the irreversible step between two quasistatic arcs and use the bound.

8. **Recognise a state function by the line integral being path-independent.** A 1-form $\omega$ is exact (the differential of a state function) iff $\oint_\gamma \omega = 0$ for every closed loop $\gamma$, equivalently iff $\omega$ is closed (on a simply connected manifold). The internal energy $U$ and the entropy $S$ are state functions; heat $\int \delta Q$ and work $\int \delta W$ are not. *Trigger:* you are asked whether some combination of thermodynamic differentials defines a new state function. *Pattern:* test exactness by computing $d\omega$.

9. **Use Chow's theorem (contrapositive) to convert inaccessibility into involutivity.** Given a distribution $\Delta$ on a connected manifold, if every neighbourhood of every point contains points unreachable by $\Delta$-horizontal paths, then $\Delta$ must be involutive. *Trigger:* you are asked to deduce integrability from an inaccessibility statement. *Pattern:* state the inaccessibility, invoke Chow's theorem (contrapositive), conclude involutivity, apply Frobenius.

**Illegal but tempting operations:**

> [!warning] 1. Treating $\delta Q$ as the differential of a function $Q$
> It is tempting to write $\int_a^b \delta Q = Q(b) - Q(a)$ and call $Q$ "the heat content". This fails because $\delta Q$ is **not exact** — there is no function $Q$ on $M$ whose differential is $\delta Q$. Concretely: along a closed cycle, $\oint \delta Q$ is the net heat absorbed and is nonzero (this is the engine principle), but for any state function $Q$, $\oint dQ = 0$ identically. The repair condition is to multiply by the integrating factor $1/T$: $\delta Q / T = dS$ *is* exact, and $S$ *is* a state function — that is the entire content of the second law.

> [!warning] 2. Assuming every adiabatic 1-form admits an integrating factor
> A general nowhere-vanishing 1-form $\theta$ on $M^{n+1}$ with $n+1 \geq 3$ need **not** admit any local integrating factor. The concrete counterexample is Frankel's contact form $\theta = y\, dx - x\, dy + dz$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$, for which $\theta \wedge d\theta = -2\, dx \wedge dy \wedge dz \neq 0$, so $\ker \theta$ is not integrable and no $\lambda, f$ exist with $\theta = \lambda\, df$. The repair condition is exactly Caratheodory's principle: the *physical* assumption of adiabatic inaccessibility forces $\theta \wedge d\theta = 0$, and only then does the integrating factor exist. Integrability is a nontrivial fact about $\delta Q$, not a feature of arbitrary 1-forms.

> [!warning] 3. Applying the equation of state to a non-equilibrium state
> The relation $pV = nRT$ is meaningful only at points of $M$, i.e., at equilibrium states. During stirring or free expansion the system passes through states *not in* $M$, and "$T$" and "$p$" are not even defined at intermediate times. The repair condition is that the equation of state applies at the initial and final equilibrium states, and the first law applied between them gives a constraint that bypasses the intermediate non-equilibrium dynamics. This is why Joule's free-expansion experiment computes $\Delta U$ from initial and final states without ever computing $\int \delta W$ along the (undefined) path.

> [!warning] 4. Identifying thermodynamic entropy with Shannon entropy
> Once $S = -\sum p_i \log p_i$ is the "**information-theoretic entropy**" of a probability distribution, it is tempting to equate it with thermodynamic $S$ and treat them interchangeably. They are related — in equilibrium statistical mechanics with the Gibbs measure, thermodynamic entropy *equals* Shannon entropy of the equilibrium distribution times $k_B$ — but they are conceptually distinct: thermodynamic $S$ is a function on the macroscopic state space $M$, while Shannon $S$ is a functional on probability distributions over microstates. The repair condition is the **maximum-entropy principle**: at fixed energy, the Gibbs distribution maximises Shannon entropy, and this maximised value equals the Caratheodory entropy — but treating them as the same object in a problem where the distinction matters (e.g. **Maxwell's demon**, the **Jarzynski equality**, or finite-rate processes) is a category error.

# Problem-Solving Strategy

The problems in this topic divide cleanly along the chapter's implication chain, and recognising which step of the chain a problem is testing is more than half the work.

If the problem **gives you an equation of state and asks for a thermodynamic quantity** — entropy change, work done, heat absorbed along a specified path — then your route runs through the first law and the integrating factor. Express $U(V, T)$ (or whatever potential the problem makes natural) from the equation of state and the heat capacities; write $\delta Q = dU + p\, dV = C_V\, dT + p\, dV$, divide by $T$ to get $dS = (C_V/T)\, dT + (p/T)\, dV$; verify exactness if needed by checking $\partial_V(C_V/T) = \partial_T(p/T)$; then integrate $dS$ along the path. For most processes one of the variables is held fixed (isothermal, isobaric, isochoric, adiabatic) and the line integral collapses to an ordinary integral. The key habit is to *always* express integrands in terms of exact differentials of state functions before integrating — otherwise you risk integrating a non-exact form along a complicated path and getting the wrong answer.

If the problem **asks you to derive an identity among partial derivatives** that "looks unrelated" — say, relating $(\partial S/\partial V)_T$ to $(\partial p/\partial T)_V$ — then you are being asked to derive a [[Thm - Maxwell Relations from Closedness|Maxwell relation]]. The route is: identify which of the four potentials $U, H, F, G$ has those two partial derivatives appearing in its differential, write down that potential's $d\Phi = A\, dx + B\, dy$, and apply $d^2 \Phi = 0$ to extract $(\partial A/\partial y)_x = (\partial B/\partial x)_y$. The trick is the identification step: $S$ paired with $V$ at fixed $T$ points to $F$; $S$ paired with $p$ at fixed $T$ points to $G$; $T$ paired with $V$ at fixed $S$ points to $U$; $T$ paired with $p$ at fixed $S$ points to $H$. The pairing rule is "two variables that conjugately appear with opposite sign in the same $d\Phi$".

If the problem **asks for the efficiency of a cycle** — Carnot, Otto, Stirling, Diesel — then your route is to draw the cycle in $(T, S)$ coordinates and compute areas. The cycle in $(p, V)$ is geometrically complicated; in $(T, S)$ it is a rectangle (Carnot), a parallelogram (Stirling), or other simple shape. The first law gives $\oint dU = 0$, so $\oint \delta Q = \oint \delta W$, meaning the net heat absorbed equals the net work done. The net heat absorbed is the $(T, S)$-area enclosed; the efficiency $\eta = W/Q_h$ is then a ratio of two such areas. For Carnot, the rectangle has width $\Delta S$ and heights $T_h$ and $T_c$, giving $\eta = (T_h - T_c)/T_h = 1 - T_c/T_h$. The meta-strategy is to **choose coordinates in which adiabats and isotherms are straight lines**.

If the problem **asks you to decide integrability of a candidate 1-form** $\theta$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ or a manifold of dimension $\geq 3$, the answer is encoded in $\theta \wedge d\theta$: integrable iff this vanishes identically, non-integrable iff nonzero anywhere. Compute $d\theta$ in coordinates, wedge with $\theta$, look at the coefficient of the volume form. If it vanishes, find the integrating factor by solving $\theta = \lambda\, df$ for $\lambda, f$ (often by inspection — for $\delta Q$ on an ideal gas, $\lambda = 1/T$). If it does not vanish, invoke Chow's theorem to conclude every point is accessible from every point by horizontal paths, hence no state function $S$ exists with $\delta Q \propto dS$.

If the problem **involves an irreversible step** (stirring, mixing, free expansion), the trick is to **replace the irreversible path with a reversible one between the same endpoints**. Entropy is a state function: $\Delta S$ depends only on initial and final equilibrium states, regardless of the path. So compute $\Delta S$ along a reversible substitute path where $dS = \delta Q / T$ can be integrated, and bound the heat exchanged along the irreversible path using Clausius's inequality $\Delta S \geq Q_{\text{actual}}/T_{\text{reservoir}}$. The reversible substitute is a computational fiction; the *physical* process is irreversible, but its endpoints lie on the equilibrium manifold and that is all you need.

A meta-strategy threads through all the above: **every quantity in this chapter is either a state function (path-independent, integral of an exact form) or a transit quantity (path-dependent, integral of a Pfaffian)**, and at every step in a calculation you should know which kind you are looking at. The first law is the conversion rule: $\delta Q - \delta W = dU$. The second law is the second conversion rule: $\delta Q / T = dS$. Together they say that out of the three Pfaffians $dU, \delta Q, \delta W$, exactly one ($dU$) is exact, and the other two become exact after the modifications $\delta Q \to \delta Q / T = dS$ and $\delta W \to dU - \delta Q$. The entire chapter is the story of those two conversions.

# Most Reusable Properties

- **[[Thm - The Frobenius Theorem|Frobenius theorem]] in forms language: $\theta \wedge d\theta = 0$ iff $\theta = \lambda\, df$ locally.** This is the most reusable single fact in the chapter, because it is the bridge between "a 1-form" and "a state function up to an integrating factor". The recognizable setup is a Pfaffian equation $\theta = 0$ representing a constraint or a vanishing flux; the question is whether it admits an integral. Reach for it whenever a 1-form needs to be tested for exactness modulo a multiplier, whenever an "effective potential" is sought for a non-conservative flow, and whenever the question is whether some distribution on a manifold integrates to a foliation by submanifolds.

- **[[Def - Closed and Exact Forms|Closedness of exact differentials (d-squared = 0)]] applied to thermodynamic potentials.** The mixed-partial symmetry $(\partial^2 \Phi / \partial x \partial y) = (\partial^2 \Phi / \partial y \partial x)$ is a triviality in calculus, but applied systematically to the four thermodynamic potentials it generates all the Maxwell relations. The typical use is to convert a hard-to-measure partial derivative (anything involving $S$) into an easy-to-measure one (involving $T$, $p$, $V$). The principle generalises: in any geometric setting where a 1-form is exact, its coefficients in any coordinate system satisfy mixed-partial identities.

- **[[Def - The First Law of Thermodynamics|First law as an exactness statement]]: $dU = \delta Q - \delta W$, with $dU$ exact.** The first law is reusable precisely because $U$ is a state function. The pattern is: between two equilibrium states $x$ and $y$, $\Delta U = U(y) - U(x)$ is path-independent and equals $\int_\gamma (\delta Q - \delta W)$ along *any* connecting quasistatic path; conversely if you know two of $\Delta U$, $\int \delta Q$, $\int \delta W$ you know the third. This is what lets Joule's free-expansion experiment determine $U(T, V)$ without ever computing $\int \delta Q$ or $\int \delta W$ separately along the (ill-defined) irreversible path.

- **Legendre transform symbolised as "swap conjugate variables".** Going from $U(S, V)$ to $F(T, V) = U - TS$ is an algebraic substitution that swaps $S$ for its conjugate $T = \partial U/\partial S$. The reusable principle is that *which* potential is appropriate depends on *which* variables you can hold fixed experimentally: $F$ minimised at fixed $(T, V)$, $G$ minimised at fixed $(T, p)$. The same trick — Legendre-transform to make the natural variables coincide with the experimentally controlled variables — is the entire content of the Lagrangian-to-Hamiltonian transition in [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics|geometric mechanics]] and the construction of convex conjugates in convex analysis.

- **The integrating factor $1/T$ converts $\delta Q$ to $dS$.** The single transformation that organises the entire second-law machinery is multiplication by $1/T$: it takes the Pfaffian $\delta Q$ to the exact differential $dS$. The pattern is that whenever a non-exact 1-form $\theta$ on a low-dimensional manifold needs to be integrated and the answer should be path-independent, look for an integrating factor — solve $d(\theta/\lambda) = 0$ for $\lambda$. In thermodynamics the integrating factor is the absolute temperature; in geometric optics it is the eikonal; in the calculus of variations it is the conserved quantity along an extremal.

# Bridges

1. **Differential geometry — Frobenius integrability and the Caratheodory–Frobenius theorem.** The mathematical content of the second law is exactly [[Thm - The Frobenius Theorem|Frobenius's theorem]] applied to a single codimension-one Pfaffian, with Chow's theorem supplying the converse implication "non-integrable $\implies$ all points horizontally accessible". A 1-form $\theta$ on $M^{n+1}$ defines a codimension-one distribution $\ker \theta$; this distribution is integrable iff $\theta \wedge d\theta = 0$ identically, iff $\theta = \lambda\, df$ locally for some smooth $\lambda \neq 0$ and $f$. In the thermodynamic application, $\theta = \delta Q$, $\lambda = T$ (absolute temperature), and $f = S$ (entropy). The leaves of the resulting foliation are the **adiabatic surfaces** — codimension-one submanifolds of $M$ on which $S$ is constant. The construction is identical to the one in [[Differential Geometry X — de Rham Cohomology, Distributions, and Frobenius|DG X §10.3]], specialised to a single 1-form rather than a system.

2. **Geometric mechanics — Legendre transforms and conjugate variables.** The four thermodynamic potentials $U, H, F, G$ are related by Legendre transforms swapping conjugate pairs $(S, T)$ and $(V, p)$: $H = U + pV$ swaps $V \leftrightarrow p$; $F = U - TS$ swaps $S \leftrightarrow T$; $G$ swaps both. This is the same construction that takes a Lagrangian $L(q, \dot q)$ on $TQ$ to a Hamiltonian $H(q, p)$ on $T^*Q$ in [[Geometric Mechanics I — Symplectic Manifolds and Hamiltonian Dynamics|geometric mechanics]], where $p = \partial L/\partial \dot q$ is the conjugate momentum. The reason both contexts use Legendre transforms is the same: in each case you have a function on a tangent space whose natural variables (positions and velocities, or entropy and volume) are not the variables you can experimentally fix (positions and momenta, or temperature and volume), and the Legendre transform produces the function on the cotangent space whose natural variables *are* the experimentally fixed ones.

3. **Convex analysis — concavity of the entropy function.** The thermodynamic entropy $S(U, V)$ is, on physical grounds, a **concave** function of its arguments — this is the precise content of the second law for combined systems, and it is what ensures that the Legendre transform producing $F(T, V)$ is well-defined and involutive. Concave conjugacy in convex analysis is the systematic theory of Legendre transforms for convex functions; the convex conjugate $f^*(p) = \sup_x[px - f(x)]$ satisfies $f^{**} = f$ when $f$ is convex (lower semicontinuous), and the bijection between $f$ and $f^*$ is exactly the conjugate-variable swap. Wightman's introduction to Israel's *Convexity in the Theory of Lattice Gases* develops this systematically; the upshot is that the four thermodynamic potentials sit at the corners of a Legendre cube indexed by which variables are fixed and which are free.

4. **Statistical mechanics — entropy as $S = k_B \log W$.** The thermodynamic entropy $S$ defined here is purely macroscopic — a function on the manifold $M$ of equilibrium states. **Statistical mechanics** reconstructs it from microscopic dynamics: $S(E) = k_B \log W(E)$ where $W(E)$ is the number of microstates of the system with energy $\leq E$ (the Boltzmann entropy), and the absolute temperature satisfies $1/T = \partial S/\partial U$. The bridge is the maximum-entropy principle: at fixed expected energy, the probability distribution over microstates that maximises Shannon entropy $-\sum_i p_i \log p_i$ is the **Gibbs measure** $p_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}$ with $\beta = 1/k_B T$. Evaluated at this maximum, Shannon entropy equals $S/k_B$ — the thermodynamic Caratheodory entropy of this topic. This is the bridge from classical thermodynamics to **information theory** and ultimately to **algorithmic information theory** and **Maxwell's demon and algorithmic thermodynamics**, where the information content of a microstate description becomes a thermodynamic quantity in its own right.

5. **Sub-Riemannian geometry and nonholonomic mechanics — Chow's theorem.** The geometric setup of Caratheodory's argument — a distribution on a manifold, asking which points are connected by horizontal paths — is identical to the setup of sub-Riemannian geometry and of nonholonomic mechanics (rolling balls, parallel-parking constraints). [[Thm - Chow's Connectivity Theorem (Statement)|Chow's theorem]] is the fundamental connectivity result: a bracket-generating distribution makes all points horizontally accessible. The contrapositive supplies the integrability step in Caratheodory's argument. In nonholonomic mechanics the same theorem explains why a parallel-parking car can reach any configuration despite instantaneously having only two degrees of freedom (forward motion and steering); the difference from the thermodynamic case is the sign of the answer — nature gives us a bracket-generating distribution in parking and an integrable one in thermodynamics, because **Caratheodory's principle is the experimental input that distinguishes the two**.

# Insights

**The unifying frame: thermodynamics is the geometry of a single non-exact 1-form, and the second law is its Frobenius integrability condition.** The first law assigns each equilibrium state an internal energy $U$ — a state function — and asserts that heat plus work add up to the change in $U$ along any path. The second law says more: the heat 1-form $\delta Q$, though non-exact, admits an integrating factor, and the integrating factor is universal (the same for every system in thermal equilibrium). Everything in classical thermodynamics — entropy, temperature, free energies, Maxwell relations, Carnot efficiency — is a consequence of this single geometric fact applied to one specific 1-form. The reason Caratheodory's reformulation matters is that it identifies the **content** of the second law as a Frobenius integrability condition, rather than as a prohibition on engines. The engine statements (Kelvin, Clausius) are then *consequences* of integrability, not axioms.

**The true name of entropy is "the function whose level surfaces are the adiabats".** The textbook definition — $dS = \delta Q / T$ — is the right computational rule but the wrong picture. The geometric meaning of entropy is that adiabatic surfaces (the integral leaves of $\ker \delta Q$) are level surfaces of $S$, and the integrating factor $T$ measures how compressed those level surfaces are in the direction of $\delta Q$. To go from one adiabat to a nearby adiabat *requires* exchanging heat, and the amount of heat needed (per unit change in $S$) is the temperature. This makes the second law geometrically transparent: irreversible processes (stirring, mixing) move you from one adiabat to a later one, never back. Reversible adiabats are level sets; the foliation structure is the second law made visible.

**A trigger-reaction pattern: see a non-exact 1-form, look for an integrating factor.** Any time you encounter a 1-form $\theta$ that is "almost" the differential of a function — a flux, a transit quantity, an action — but is not closed, the question "does $\theta$ admit an integrating factor?" is one of the first to ask. Compute $\theta \wedge d\theta$; if it vanishes, $\theta = \lambda\, df$ for some $\lambda, f$, and you have a new state function $f$ defined up to constants. This pattern recurs all over physics: $\delta Q$ becomes $T\, dS$; the optical "eikonal" relating path length and frequency in inhomogeneous media; the action 1-form in mechanics admits the integrating factor that produces a Hamilton-Jacobi function. The trigger is "non-exact 1-form in a context where path-independence ought to hold"; the reaction is "compute $\theta \wedge d\theta$, then Frobenius".

**Inheritance: entropy inherits its existence from the integrability of $\ker \delta Q$, which inherits its integrability from Caratheodory's principle, which inherits its truth from Kelvin's statement of the second law, which inherits its truth (in statistical mechanics) from the overwhelming preponderance of high-multiplicity macrostates.** Each link in this chain is doing real work, and being clear about which link is the "source" of entropy clarifies what would change if any link broke. If Kelvin were false, Caratheodory would fail and integrability would fail and entropy would not exist. If Caratheodory were false but Kelvin true, entropy might exist locally but not globally. If $\delta Q$ were a different 1-form (e.g. with a non-vanishing Frobenius obstruction), no integrating factor would exist and the "second law" would be a different statement. The chain isolates what about $\delta Q$ is necessary for entropy: not its specific form, but the integrability condition $\delta Q \wedge d(\delta Q) = 0$.

**Why thermodynamic entropy and information-theoretic entropy are *related* but *distinct*.** Boltzmann's $S = k_B \log W$, Shannon's $H = -\sum p_i \log p_i$, and Caratheodory's $S = \int \delta Q / T$ are *not* the same definition — they live in different ambient categories. Caratheodory's $S$ is a function on the macroscopic state manifold $M$; Boltzmann's $W$ counts microstates of a coarse-grained macrostate; Shannon's $H$ is a functional on probability distributions. They coincide *numerically* in equilibrium when the probability distribution is the Gibbs measure, but the conceptual content differs: Caratheodory's $S$ depends on the choice of which observables count as "macroscopic", Boltzmann's $W$ depends on a coarse-graining of phase space, Shannon's $H$ depends on a probability distribution one chooses to associate with the system. The active research program around **Maxwell's demon and algorithmic thermodynamics** asks what entropy *is* when one allows the observer to perform computations on the microstate description — and finds that the answer involves **Kolmogorov complexity** and goes beyond classical statistical mechanics. The takeaway for this chapter is that the Caratheodory entropy is one specific incarnation of "entropy", arising from a specific geometric construction; treating it as the definition of "entropy" in general is a category mistake that leads to confusion in any setting where information-theoretic considerations matter (the **Jarzynski equality**, **fluctuation-dissipation theorem**, **black hole thermodynamics** with Bekenstein-Hawking entropy, **algorithmic information theory**).
