# Notation

$M$ is a smooth manifold (Hausdorff, second-countable, without boundary unless said otherwise). $\Omega^k(M)$ denotes smooth $k$-forms on $M$; $d$ is the exterior derivative; $\iota_X$ is the [[Def - Interior Product (Contraction with a Vector Field)|interior product]] with a vector field $X$. A $2$-form $\omega \in \Omega^2(M)$ assigns to each $p \in M$ an antisymmetric bilinear form $\omega_p$ on the tangent space $T_pM$.

**Standing convention.** Symplectic manifolds always have even dimension $\dim M = 2n$; we write $n = \dim M / 2$ throughout.

# Axiom Motivation

We want to globalize the notion of a [[Def - Symplectic Vector Space|symplectic vector space]] — a vector space carrying a closed-form-free notion of antisymmetric, nondegenerate pairing — into a manifold setting where the pairing varies smoothly from point to point. The naïve attempt is: an even-dimensional smooth manifold with a smooth $2$-form that is nondegenerate at every point (an **almost-symplectic structure**). This is genuinely different from a symplectic structure, and the difference comes from the closedness axiom $d\omega = 0$. The motivation question is: **why demand closedness?**

There are three independent answers, each of which would alone be enough to force the axiom.

First, **Liouville's theorem**. We want the dynamics generated by a Hamiltonian function $H : M \to \mathbb{R}$ via $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ to preserve the symplectic form, hence the symplectic volume $\omega^n$. By Cartan's magic formula, $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d\iota_{X_H}\omega + \iota_{X_H}d\omega = d(dH) + \iota_{X_H}(d\omega) = \iota_{X_H}(d\omega)$. The first term vanishes by $d^2 = 0$; for the whole expression to vanish for *every* Hamiltonian $H$, we need $\iota_{X_H}(d\omega) = 0$ for every $X_H$. Since $H \mapsto X_H$ is a bijection between functions and vector fields (by nondegeneracy), every vector field arises as $X_H$ for some $H$, so we need $\iota_X(d\omega) = 0$ for every vector field $X$. This forces $d\omega = 0$. Without closedness, Liouville's theorem fails — Hamiltonian dynamics would not preserve phase-space volume.

Second, **the Jacobi identity for the Poisson bracket**. Define $\{f, g\} := \omega(X_f, X_g)$. We want this bracket to satisfy Jacobi $\{f, \{g, h\}\} + \{g, \{h, f\}\} + \{h, \{f, g\}\} = 0$ so the algebra of observables $(C^\infty(M), \{\cdot, \cdot\})$ is a Lie algebra. A direct computation shows the Jacobi identity is equivalent to $d\omega = 0$ — exactly the closedness of $\omega$ is the necessary and sufficient condition. Without it, the bracket is antisymmetric and Leibniz but **not** Jacobi, so the observable algebra fails to be a Lie algebra and the entire formalism of canonical quantization breaks down.

Third, **Darboux's theorem**. Closedness $d\omega = 0$ is what makes the symplectic structure **locally rigid**: every symplectic manifold of dimension $2n$ is locally isomorphic to $(\mathbb{R}^{2n}, \sum dp_i \wedge dq^i)$. Without closedness, an almost-symplectic structure has nontrivial local invariants (the components of $d\omega$ are local quantities), and the theory has the same kind of local richness as Riemannian geometry. The Moser-trick proof of [[Thm - Darboux's Theorem|Darboux's theorem]] uses the closedness essentially: it builds a time-dependent vector field whose flow conjugates the structure to standard form, and the integrability of this vector field requires the deformation path $\omega_t$ to consist of *closed* forms.

Now the nondegeneracy axiom. **Why nondegenerate?** Because we want $H \mapsto X_H$ to be a well-defined bijection between functions and Hamiltonian vector fields. Concretely, we want $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ to have a unique solution $X_H$ for any choice of $H$. The map $X \mapsto \iota_X\omega = \omega(X, \cdot)$ goes from sections of $TM$ to sections of $T^*M$; it is an isomorphism if and only if $\omega$ is nondegenerate pointwise. Without nondegeneracy, some functions would generate no dynamics, and some vector fields would be Hamiltonian for many distinct Hamiltonians — neither is acceptable.

What if we *weaken* the axioms — say to $d\omega = 0$ alone, without nondegeneracy? Such a structure is called a **presymplectic** form, and it arises naturally in constrained mechanics (Dirac's formalism) and in field theory. It is not nothing, but it lacks the dynamics-from-energy bijection. What if we drop closedness? **Almost-symplectic** structures exist but lack Liouville's theorem and Darboux's theorem, and the Poisson bracket fails Jacobi. The classical theory of mechanics specifically requires both axioms.

Why **even dimension**? This is not a separate axiom; it follows from nondegeneracy. An antisymmetric matrix of odd dimension $2n+1$ has determinant $\det A = \det A^T = \det(-A) = (-1)^{2n+1}\det A = -\det A$, hence $\det A = 0$. So nondegeneracy is impossible in odd dimension. Symplectic manifolds are automatically of even dimension.

In summary, **closedness gives volume conservation, Jacobi for the Poisson bracket, and local rigidity (Darboux)**; **nondegeneracy gives well-defined energy-to-flow dynamics**; **even dimension follows from nondegeneracy**. Drop closedness and you lose Liouville, Jacobi, and Darboux all at once; drop nondegeneracy and you lose the very notion of Hamiltonian vector field.

# The Definition

A **symplectic manifold** is a pair $(M, \omega)$ where $M$ is a smooth manifold and $\omega \in \Omega^2(M)$ is a differential $2$-form satisfying:

1. **Closedness.** $d\omega = 0$.
2. **Nondegeneracy.** For every $p \in M$, the bilinear form $\omega_p : T_pM \times T_pM \to \mathbb{R}$ is nondegenerate. Equivalently, the bundle map $\omega^\flat : TM \to T^*M$, $X \mapsto \iota_X\omega$, is an isomorphism.

A $2$-form satisfying only (2) is called **almost-symplectic**; satisfying only (1) and being a form of rank $2n$ everywhere with $\dim M = 2n + k$ is called **presymplectic**.

A direct consequence of (2) is that $\dim M$ is even, $\dim M = 2n$. Each tangent space $(T_pM, \omega_p)$ is then a [[Def - Symplectic Vector Space|symplectic vector space]] of dimension $2n$.

The top wedge power $\omega^n := \omega \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega$ ($n$ factors) is a nowhere-vanishing top form on $M$, called the **Liouville form** or **symplectic volume form** (often divided by $n!$). It defines a canonical orientation: every symplectic manifold is orientable.

A **symplectic structure** on $M$ is a choice of such $\omega$; a manifold may admit no, one, or many symplectic structures.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

A symplectic manifold is a **smooth manifold equipped with a closed nondegenerate $2$-form**, which can be reformulated as a **smooth manifold equipped with a symplectic structure on every tangent space, varying smoothly and integrating to a global cohomology class $[\omega] \in H^2_{dR}(M; \mathbb{R})$**.

The natural morphisms in the category of symplectic manifolds are **symplectomorphisms** (also called **canonical transformations** in classical mechanics): smooth maps $\varphi : (M_1, \omega_1) \to (M_2, \omega_2)$ with $\varphi^*\omega_2 = \omega_1$. These are necessarily local diffeomorphisms (by nondegeneracy and the rank theorem), and the invertible ones form the **symplectomorphism group** $\mathrm{Symp}(M, \omega)$, an infinite-dimensional Fréchet Lie group.

In the language of $G$-structures, an almost-symplectic structure is a reduction of the frame bundle of $M$ from $\mathrm{GL}(2n, \mathbb{R})$ to the **symplectic group** $\mathrm{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{R})$. The integrability condition for this $G$-structure — the requirement that the structure be locally equivalent to the standard one — is exactly $d\omega = 0$. So **a symplectic manifold is an integrable symplectic $G$-structure**, and Darboux's theorem is the integrability theorem.

---

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A symplectic manifold is the **antisymmetric, closed analogue of a [[Def - Riemannian Metric|Riemannian manifold]]**. A Riemannian metric is a symmetric, positive-definite $(0,2)$-tensor field, with no closedness condition; a symplectic form is an antisymmetric, nondegenerate $2$-form with closedness imposed. The contrast in structure is dramatic and informative. Riemannian manifolds have rich local invariants — sectional curvature, Ricci tensor, scalar curvature — and the local geometry depends on these tensor fields in detail. Symplectic manifolds have **no local invariants** — by Darboux's theorem, all symplectic manifolds of equal dimension are locally identical. The interesting structure is global: topology of $M$, cohomology class $[\omega] \in H^2_{dR}(M)$, symplectic capacity, Floer homology.

From the algebraic side, a symplectic manifold is **the geometric incarnation of a Poisson algebra**: the space of smooth functions $C^\infty(M)$ becomes a Poisson algebra under the bracket $\{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g)$, and the symplectic structure is the geometric data that defines this bracket. More general **Poisson manifolds** (Poisson algebras of smooth functions arising from a possibly degenerate Poisson bivector $\pi$) generalize symplectic manifolds; the nondegenerate Poisson manifolds are exactly the symplectic manifolds.

**True name:** the true name of a symplectic manifold is **"phase space"** — the geometric arena in which classical mechanics takes place, where each smooth function $H$ generates a flow $X_H$ via $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ and the flow preserves the structure $\omega$. The official definition (closed nondegenerate $2$-form) is the minimal abstraction of "the geometry that makes Hamiltonian mechanics work".

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: $(\mathbb{R}^{2n}, \omega_0)$ with $\omega_0 = \sum_i dp_i \wedge dq^i$.** The standard example, exact ($\omega_0 = -d\theta_0$ with $\theta_0 = p_i dq^i$), closed automatically, nondegenerate because the matrix of $\omega_0$ in canonical coordinates is the standard symplectic matrix. This is the local model for every symplectic manifold by Darboux's theorem.

**Is an instance: every cotangent bundle $T^*Q$.** With $\omega = -d\theta$ where $\theta$ is the [[Def - The Canonical Symplectic Form on a Cotangent Bundle|tautological 1-form]]. Closed (exact, in fact), nondegenerate, with canonical coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$ in which $\omega = dp_i \wedge dq^i$. This is the natural phase space of a mechanical system with configuration manifold $Q$.

**Is an instance: every oriented surface $\Sigma$ with its area form $\omega = dA$.** A nowhere-zero $2$-form on a $2$-dimensional manifold is automatically symplectic: closedness is automatic (every $2$-form on a $2$-manifold is closed because $d\omega \in \Omega^3 = 0$), and nondegeneracy is the assumption that $\omega$ is nowhere-zero. So $S^2$ with its round area form, $\mathbb{T}^2$ with $dx \wedge dy$, any closed orientable surface — all are symplectic. Note: among these, only $\mathbb{R}^2$, $S^1 \times \mathbb{R}$ (cylinder), and $\mathbb{T}^2 \setminus \text{pt}$ are cotangent bundles; the rest have non-exact symplectic forms (since their second cohomology is nontrivial and $\omega$ represents a non-zero class).

**Is an instance: $\mathbb{CP}^n$ with the Fubini–Study form.** Complex projective space is a Kähler manifold; the Kähler form is symplectic. Explicitly on $\mathbb{CP}^1 = S^2$, the Fubini–Study form is (up to scale) the round area form on the sphere.

**Is an instance: every Kähler manifold.** A Kähler manifold is a complex manifold $M$ with a Hermitian metric $h$ whose imaginary part $\omega = -\mathrm{Im}(h)$ is closed; the imaginary part is then a symplectic form. So $\mathbb{C}^n$, $\mathbb{CP}^n$, complex tori $\mathbb{C}^n/\Lambda$, complex projective varieties, and many more are all symplectic.

**Is NOT an instance: $(\mathbb{R}^3, \omega)$ for any $2$-form.** Odd-dimensional manifolds cannot be symplectic — nondegeneracy of an antisymmetric form requires even dimension.

**Is NOT an instance: $(S^4, \omega)$.** The $4$-sphere admits no symplectic structure. This is a nontrivial topological obstruction: a closed symplectic manifold $(M^{2n}, \omega)$ has $[\omega]^n \neq 0 \in H^{2n}_{dR}(M)$ (since $\omega^n$ is a nonzero top form on a closed manifold), so $H^2_{dR}(M) \neq 0$. But $H^2_{dR}(S^4) = 0$, ruling out any symplectic structure. **Most closed simply connected manifolds with the right dimension are not symplectic** — symplectic structures impose genuine cohomological constraints.

**Is NOT an instance: $(\mathbb{R}^4, dx^1 \wedge dx^2)$.** The form $dx^1 \wedge dx^2$ on $\mathbb{R}^4$ is closed but degenerate at every point — its kernel includes $\partial_{x^3}$ and $\partial_{x^4}$. The rank is $2$, not $4$. This is a **presymplectic** form of constant rank $2$, not symplectic.

**Is NOT an instance: an almost-symplectic non-symplectic form.** On $S^6$ (the $6$-sphere) there exists a nondegenerate $2$-form (the imaginary part of the *almost*-complex Cayley structure) but it is **not closed**, so it is almost-symplectic without being symplectic. Whether $S^6$ admits any symplectic structure is a famous open question (closely related to the question of whether $S^6$ admits an integrable complex structure).

**Corollary (orientability).** Every symplectic manifold is canonically oriented by the nowhere-vanishing top form $\omega^n/n!$. So non-orientable manifolds (Möbius strip, Klein bottle, $\mathbb{RP}^2$) are not symplectic.

**Corollary (cohomology class).** On a closed symplectic manifold, the cohomology class $[\omega] \in H^2_{dR}(M; \mathbb{R})$ is non-zero, and $[\omega]^n = [\omega^n] \neq 0$. So $H^{2k}_{dR}(M) \neq 0$ for all $0 \leq k \leq n$.

**Corollary (Liouville volume).** The integral $\int_M \omega^n/n!$ (on a closed manifold) is a topological invariant of the symplectic structure, the **total symplectic volume**.

**Calibration check.** If you can do these three things, you have understood the definition. First, verify that the area form on $S^2$ satisfies both axioms (closedness is automatic on a $2$-manifold; nondegeneracy follows from non-vanishing). Second, explain why no symplectic structure exists on $S^3$, on $S^4$, or on the torus $T^3$. Third, give the formula for the symplectic form on $T^*S^1 = S^1 \times \mathbb{R}$ in terms of the angle $\theta$ on $S^1$ and the momentum coordinate $p$, and check that it equals $dp \wedge d\theta$.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Symplectic Reduction (Marsden–Weinstein Quotient) *(from Hamiltonian Group Actions)*
> When a Lie group $G$ acts on $(M, \omega)$ preserving $\omega$ with a moment map $\mu : M \to \mathfrak{g}^*$, the level set $\mu^{-1}(0)$ is a coisotropic submanifold, and the quotient $M//_\mu G := \mu^{-1}(0)/G$ inherits a canonical symplectic structure of dimension $\dim M - 2\dim G$. This is the central construction of **geometric mechanics with symmetry**: it lets you reduce the dimension of a phase space by quotienting out continuous symmetries while preserving the symplectic dynamics. The reduced phase space is where the dynamics "really lives" after the symmetry is exploited.

> [!tip] Kähler and Calabi–Yau Manifolds *(from Complex Geometry)*
> When a symplectic manifold $(M, \omega)$ also has a compatible integrable complex structure $J$ (so that $\omega(\cdot, J\cdot) = g(\cdot, \cdot)$ is a Riemannian metric), the structure is **Kähler**. Kähler manifolds combine symplectic, complex, and Riemannian geometry into one framework. **Calabi–Yau manifolds** are Kähler manifolds with vanishing first Chern class (equivalently a nowhere-zero holomorphic volume form, equivalently a Ricci-flat Kähler metric by Yau's theorem). They are the spaces on which string theory's "compactification dimensions" are required to live, and their **mirror symmetry** (a duality exchanging symplectic and complex data of paired Calabi–Yau spaces) is the deepest known link between symplectic and algebraic geometry.

> [!tip] Floer Homology and the Arnold Conjecture *(from Symplectic Topology)*
> Despite Darboux's local triviality, symplectic manifolds carry deep global invariants. **Floer homology**, invented by Andreas Floer to attack Arnold's conjecture, assigns to a symplectic manifold a graded module $HF^*(M, \omega)$ computed from a chain complex generated by fixed points of a Hamiltonian symplectomorphism, with differential given by counts of Gromov pseudo-holomorphic strips connecting them. **Arnold's conjecture** asserts that the number of fixed points of any nondegenerate Hamiltonian symplectomorphism of a closed symplectic manifold is at least $\sum_i \dim H^i_{dR}(M; \mathbb{R})$ — the sum of Betti numbers, which lower-bounds the number of critical points of any Morse function. Floer's theory proves the conjecture in many cases by identifying $HF^*$ with the singular cohomology $H^*(M; \mathbb{R})$.
