# Notation

$Q$ is a smooth manifold (the **configuration space**) of dimension $n$. $TQ$ is its [[Def - The Tangent Bundle|tangent bundle]] of dimension $2n$, with **bundle coordinates** $(q^i, \dot q^i)$ adapted to a chart $(U, q^i)$ on $Q$: a tangent vector at $q$ is $\dot q^i \partial_{q^i}$. A **Lagrangian** is a smooth function $L : TQ \to \mathbb{R}$, or more generally $L : TQ \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ when explicit time-dependence is allowed. The action of a curve $\gamma : [a, b] \to Q$ is the integral $S[\gamma] = \int_a^b L(\gamma(t), \dot\gamma(t), t)\, dt$.

---

# Axiom Motivation

A **Lagrangian function** is the variational dual of a Hamiltonian: a function on the *tangent* bundle (position-and-velocity space) rather than the *cotangent* bundle (position-and-momentum space), from which the dynamics is generated by a variational principle rather than by a symplectic flow. The motivating question is: **why introduce a function on $TQ$ separate from the Hamiltonian on $T^*Q$, and what does Lagrangian mechanics give that Hamiltonian mechanics doesn't?**

The historical answer comes from Newton and Euler. For a particle in a potential, Newton's law $m\ddot q = -\nabla V(q)$ can be written as the variational condition $\delta\int L\,dt = 0$ where $L = T - V$ — kinetic energy minus potential energy. This is **Hamilton's principle**: the actual trajectory $q(t)$ is a stationary point of the action functional $S[\gamma] = \int L(\gamma, \dot\gamma)\,dt$ among all curves with fixed endpoints. The equations of motion (Euler–Lagrange) are derived from this principle by integration by parts.

So a Lagrangian is, primarily, **the integrand of an action functional whose stationary points are the trajectories**. The minus sign between $T$ and $V$ is what makes Hamilton's principle agree with Newton's law: the work-energy theorem turns the variation of $\int T\,dt$ into one term and the variation of $\int V\,dt$ into another, with opposite signs, so $\delta\int(T - V)dt = 0$ produces $m\ddot q + \nabla V = 0$, which is Newton.

The deeper motivation is that **the Lagrangian framework generalizes better than the Hamiltonian framework**. In particular:

1. **Constraints.** If a system has holonomic constraints — surfaces in configuration space that the system is restricted to — the Lagrangian formalism handles them automatically: just restrict $L$ to the constraint manifold and apply Hamilton's principle there. The Hamiltonian formalism requires the more involved **Dirac–Bergmann constraint analysis**.

2. **Field theory.** In classical field theory, the configuration space is infinite-dimensional (a space of fields), and the action is the integral of a Lagrangian density over spacetime: $S[\phi] = \int \mathcal{L}(\phi, \partial_\mu\phi)\, d^4x$. The Euler–Lagrange equations become PDEs. Electromagnetism, Yang–Mills theory, general relativity, and the Standard Model are all formulated this way.

3. **Coordinate freedom.** The Lagrangian $L = T - V$ is naturally expressed in any coordinates on $Q$, with the kinetic energy involving the Riemannian metric: $T = \tfrac{1}{2}g_{ij}(q)\dot q^i\dot q^j$. The Euler–Lagrange equations $d/dt(\partial L/\partial \dot q^i) - \partial L/\partial q^i = 0$ are manifestly tensorial — they transform correctly under any coordinate change on $Q$. The Hamiltonian formalism, while equally coordinate-free in principle, looks more like "the same thing in different coordinates" rather than the geometrically natural object.

4. **Symmetries.** Noether's theorem in Lagrangian form is essentially trivial: a continuous symmetry of the Lagrangian produces a conserved current. The Hamiltonian version (moment map) is more refined but requires more setup.

**What about the question of which $L$ to choose?** For non-relativistic mechanics with potential forces:

$$L(q, \dot q, t) = T(q, \dot q) - V(q, t) = \tfrac{1}{2}g_{ij}(q)\dot q^i\dot q^j - V(q, t).$$

For relativistic mechanics, the Lagrangian becomes the proper-time integrand $L = -mc^2\sqrt{1 - |\dot q|^2/c^2}$ for a free particle, or more generally a manifestly Lorentz-invariant expression. For electromagnetism (a field theory), $\mathcal{L} = -\tfrac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ is the Lagrangian density. In each case, the choice of $L$ is dictated by physics (symmetries, observed equations of motion), but the variational structure is universal.

**What constraints are there on $L$?** The most important is **regularity**: the Hessian matrix $\partial^2 L/\partial \dot q^i \partial \dot q^j$ should be invertible. For $L = \tfrac{1}{2}g_{ij}\dot q^i\dot q^j - V$, this Hessian is just $g_{ij}$, which is invertible because $g$ is a Riemannian metric (nondegenerate). Regularity is what makes the Legendre transform $\mathbb{F}L : TQ \to T^*Q$ a (local) diffeomorphism, allowing passage between the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian pictures.

For **singular** Lagrangians — those with degenerate Hessian — the Legendre transform is not a diffeomorphism, and additional **constraints** arise on the cotangent side. These are the **Dirac constraints** of gauge theories, and they signal the presence of gauge freedom that must be quotiented out in the Hamiltonian formalism. Electromagnetism (with its gauge freedom $A \to A + d\chi$) is a paradigm example.

What if we **drop the variational character**, defining a Lagrangian as just any function on $TQ$? Then we lose Hamilton's principle as the generating mechanism for the equations of motion, and the Euler–Lagrange equations would just be a coordinate-dependent ODE without geometric meaning. The variational principle is what gives the Lagrangian formalism its conceptual clarity.

# The Definition

Let $Q$ be a smooth manifold (the **configuration space**) of dimension $n$. A **Lagrangian** is a smooth function

$$L : TQ \to \mathbb{R}, \quad \text{or more generally} \quad L : TQ \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$$

(the latter for explicit time-dependence). In bundle coordinates $(q^i, \dot q^i)$ on $TQ$, $L = L(q, \dot q)$ (or $L = L(q, \dot q, t)$).

The **action functional** of a smooth curve $\gamma : [a, b] \to Q$ is

$$S[\gamma] = \int_a^b L(\gamma(t), \dot\gamma(t), t)\, dt.$$

**Hamilton's principle** asserts that the physical trajectory $\gamma$ is a **stationary point** of $S$ among all variations $\gamma_s$ with fixed endpoints $\gamma_s(a) = \gamma(a)$, $\gamma_s(b) = \gamma(b)$:

$$\frac{d}{ds}\bigg|_{s = 0} S[\gamma_s] = 0 \text{ for all variations}.$$

By integration by parts, this is equivalent to the **Euler–Lagrange equations** holding along $\gamma$:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i} - \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i} = 0, \quad i = 1, \dots, n.$$

A Lagrangian is **regular** if the Hessian matrix $\partial^2 L/\partial \dot q^i \partial \dot q^j$ is invertible everywhere; equivalently the **Legendre transform** $\mathbb{F}L : TQ \to T^*Q$, $(q, \dot q) \mapsto (q, \partial L/\partial \dot q)$, is a local diffeomorphism (see [[Def - The Legendre Transform]]).

**Standard Lagrangian in mechanics.** For a mechanical system with configuration space $(Q, g)$ a Riemannian manifold and potential energy $V : Q \to \mathbb{R}$:

$$L(q, \dot q) = T(q, \dot q) - V(q) = \tfrac{1}{2}g_{ij}(q)\dot q^i\dot q^j - V(q).$$

This is **kinetic minus potential energy**, and it is regular because the Hessian $\partial^2 L/\partial \dot q^i\partial \dot q^j = g_{ij}$ is positive-definite. The corresponding Hamiltonian via Legendre is $H = T + V$, the total energy.

# Categorical / Structural Definition

A Lagrangian function lives on the tangent bundle of configuration space, $TQ$, which has no canonical symplectic structure. So the Lagrangian framework is fundamentally *variational* (about extremizing an integral functional), not *symplectic*.

There is, however, an **induced symplectic structure on $TQ$** for any regular Lagrangian: the pullback $\omega_L := (\mathbb{F}L)^*\omega_{T^*Q}$ of the canonical symplectic form on $T^*Q$ via the Legendre transform. In coordinates, $\omega_L = (\partial^2 L/\partial \dot q^i\partial \dot q^j)\, d\dot q^j \wedge dq^i + \ldots$ (extra terms involving the position derivatives of the Hessian). With this induced structure, the Lagrangian dynamics on $TQ$ becomes Hamiltonian for the **energy function** $E_L := \dot q^i (\partial L/\partial \dot q^i) - L$ (which equals $H \circ \mathbb{F}L$, the Hamiltonian pulled back to $TQ$).

In categorical language: the assignment $L \rightsquigarrow (TQ, \omega_L, E_L)$ sends a regular Lagrangian on $TQ$ to a symplectic manifold with a distinguished energy function — equivalent (via $\mathbb{F}L$) to the Hamiltonian picture $(T^*Q, \omega, H)$. The two are equivalent **categories of mechanical systems**, related by the Legendre transform functor.

For **field theories**, the Lagrangian framework generalizes: the configuration "space" $Q$ becomes a space of sections of a bundle over spacetime, the action is the integral of a Lagrangian density over spacetime, and the Euler–Lagrange equations become PDEs. The variational formulation generalizes much more cleanly than the Hamiltonian (which requires a choice of foliation by spacelike hypersurfaces and breaks manifest Lorentz invariance).

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A Lagrangian is the **classical-mechanical analogue of an action functional in field theory**, with $TQ$ replaced by an infinite-dimensional space of fields and the action being a spacetime integral. The variational structure is universal: from the **principle of least action** (or stationary action, more accurately), the entire dynamical content of any field theory — classical mechanics, classical field theory, general relativity, the Standard Model — is encoded in the choice of Lagrangian.

From the calculus-of-variations perspective, a Lagrangian is the **integrand of a first-order variational problem** on the space of curves $\gamma : [a, b] \to Q$. The Euler–Lagrange equations are the necessary conditions for a stationary point, and they form a second-order ODE on $Q$ (or PDE for fields). Higher-order Lagrangians (involving $\ddot q, \dddot q, \ldots$) give rise to higher-order Euler–Lagrange equations, but in classical mechanics first-order Lagrangians are the standard.

In **quantum mechanics**, the Lagrangian appears in **Feynman's path integral** formulation: the quantum amplitude is a sum over all paths weighted by $e^{iS/\hbar}$, with $S = \int L\,dt$ the classical action. The classical trajectory emerges as the stationary-phase point in the $\hbar \to 0$ limit (the **WKB approximation**). The Lagrangian is the natural object in the path integral; the Hamiltonian is the natural object in the canonical (operator) formalism.

**True name:** the true name of a Lagrangian is **"the function whose action integral is stationary at physical trajectories"**, with the second-order Euler–Lagrange equations being its operational expression. The choice $L = T - V$ for mechanical systems is what makes Hamilton's principle reproduce Newton's law.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: free particle.** $L(q, \dot q) = \tfrac{1}{2}m|\dot q|^2$ on $T\mathbb{R}^n$. Euler–Lagrange: $\partial L/\partial q^i - d/dt(\partial L/\partial \dot q^i) = 0 - d/dt(m\dot q^i) = -m\ddot q^i$, so $\ddot q^i = 0$ — straight-line motion.

**Is an instance: particle in a potential.** $L(q, \dot q) = \tfrac{1}{2}m|\dot q|^2 - V(q)$. Euler–Lagrange gives $m\ddot q^i = -\partial V/\partial q^i$ — Newton's second law.

**Is an instance: free relativistic particle.** $L(q, \dot q) = -mc^2\sqrt{1 - |\dot q|^2/c^2}$ on $T\mathbb{R}^3$. The action $S = \int L\,dt = -mc^2 \int \sqrt{1 - |\dot q|^2/c^2}\,dt = -mc \int ds$ is proportional to proper time, and stationary action is the geodesic principle in Minkowski space. The Euler–Lagrange equations recover relativistic motion at constant 4-velocity.

**Is an instance: geodesic Lagrangian.** $L(q, \dot q) = \tfrac{1}{2}g_{ij}(q)\dot q^i\dot q^j$ on $TM$ for a Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$. The Euler–Lagrange equations are the geodesic equations $\ddot q^k + \Gamma^k_{ij}\dot q^i\dot q^j = 0$ — see [[Riemannian Geometry II — Geodesics, the Exponential Map, and Variational Principles]].

**Is an instance: rigid body Lagrangian.** For a rigid body rotating about a fixed point, the Lagrangian on $TSO(3)$ is the rotational kinetic energy, expressed in terms of the angular velocity. In body frame, $L = \tfrac{1}{2}(I_1\Omega_1^2 + I_2\Omega_2^2 + I_3\Omega_3^2)$.

**Is an instance: charged particle in EM field.** $L(q, \dot q, t) = \tfrac{1}{2}m|\dot q|^2 - e\phi(q, t) + e A(q, t) \cdot \dot q$, where $(A, \phi)$ is the electromagnetic four-potential. The "minimal coupling" $A \cdot \dot q$ term in $L$ produces the magnetic Lorentz force via Euler–Lagrange.

**Is an instance: electromagnetism (field theory).** $\mathcal{L}(\phi, \partial\phi) = -\tfrac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ where $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$. The configuration "space" is the space of one-form potentials $A_\mu$. Euler–Lagrange gives Maxwell's equations $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0$.

**Is NOT an instance: a constant Lagrangian $L \equiv c$.** Then $\partial L/\partial q = \partial L/\partial \dot q = 0$, so Euler–Lagrange is trivially satisfied by every curve. There is no dynamics. Constant Lagrangians are degenerate (the Hessian is zero matrix, the most singular case).

**Is NOT an instance: a Lagrangian with degenerate Hessian.** Take $L = \dot q^1\dot q^2$ on $T\mathbb{R}^2$. The Hessian $\partial^2 L/\partial\dot q^i\partial\dot q^j = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ — actually invertible! Try instead $L = (\dot q^1)^2$ on $T\mathbb{R}^2$: Hessian is $\begin{pmatrix}2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, degenerate. This is a singular Lagrangian, and the dynamics is incomplete (only $q^1$ is determined; $q^2$ is unconstrained gauge). Such Lagrangians are common in gauge theories.

**Corollary (Euler–Lagrange equations).** A curve $\gamma : [a, b] \to Q$ is stationary for the action $S[\gamma] = \int L(\gamma, \dot\gamma, t)dt$ among fixed-endpoint variations iff it satisfies the EL equations $\partial L/\partial q^i - d/dt(\partial L/\partial \dot q^i) = 0$ for all $i$.

**Corollary (energy function).** For an autonomous Lagrangian $L = L(q, \dot q)$ (no explicit $t$-dependence), the **energy** $E_L := \dot q^i (\partial L/\partial \dot q^i) - L$ is conserved along EL trajectories. For $L = T - V$ (standard mechanical), $E_L = T + V$ — total energy. This is the variational origin of energy conservation, prior to and independent of the symplectic formulation.

**Corollary (Noether's theorem).** Every continuous symmetry of the Lagrangian — a $1$-parameter family of transformations $\gamma_s(q)$ with $L(\gamma_s(q), \dot\gamma_s(\dot q)) = L(q, \dot q)$ for all $s$ — produces a conserved quantity along EL trajectories. Translation symmetry gives momentum; rotation gives angular momentum; time-translation gives energy. This is the variational version of Noether; the symplectic version uses the moment map.

**Corollary (gauge ambiguity).** Adding a total derivative $dF/dt$ to $L$ — i.e., $L' = L + dF(q, t)/dt$ for some smooth $F : Q \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ — does not change the Euler–Lagrange equations. The action changes by a boundary term $\int (dF/dt)\,dt = F(\gamma(b), b) - F(\gamma(a), a)$, which vanishes under fixed-endpoint variation. So Lagrangians related by total time-derivatives produce the same physics.

**Calibration check.** If you can do these three things, you have understood the definition. First, write down the Lagrangian for a simple pendulum of mass $m$ and length $\ell$ in a uniform gravitational field; derive the equation of motion from Euler–Lagrange and verify it agrees with the small-angle limit $\ddot\theta = -(g/\ell)\theta$. Second, verify the energy function for $L = \tfrac{1}{2}m\dot q^2 - V(q)$ equals $T + V$, i.e., the total energy. Third, show that $L = m\dot q^2/2$ and $L = m\dot q^2/2 + d(q^2)/dt$ give the same Euler–Lagrange equations (the second differs by a total derivative).

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Noether's Theorem in Variational Form *(from Classical Mechanics)*
> Every continuous symmetry of the action $S[\gamma]$ — a $1$-parameter family of transformations of $Q$ (or of fields, in field theory) under which $L$ is invariant, possibly up to a total derivative — produces a conserved current. For a symmetry generated by the vector field $\xi$ on $Q$, the conserved quantity is $J_\xi = \xi^i (\partial L/\partial \dot q^i) - F_\xi$, where $F_\xi$ is the total-derivative term (if any). This is the **variational version** of Noether's theorem; the symplectic version uses the moment map on phase space. Both are equivalent for regular Lagrangians.

> [!tip] Path Integrals and Feynman Quantization *(from Quantum Mechanics)*
> Feynman's **path integral** formulation of quantum mechanics expresses the propagator $\langle q_f | e^{-i\hat H T/\hbar}|q_i\rangle$ as an infinite-dimensional integral over all paths $\gamma$ from $q_i$ to $q_f$, weighted by the exponential of the action $e^{iS[\gamma]/\hbar}$ with $S = \int L\,dt$. In the classical limit $\hbar \to 0$, the integral is dominated by the stationary-phase points — exactly the solutions of Euler–Lagrange — recovering classical mechanics. The path integral is the natural quantization in the Lagrangian framework, dual to the canonical operator quantization in the Hamiltonian framework. For field theory, the path integral is the standard tool: it preserves manifest Lorentz invariance and provides the Feynman-rules generating functional.

> [!tip] Calculus of Variations and Optimal Control *(from Applied Mathematics)*
> The Euler–Lagrange equations are the central tool of the **calculus of variations**, a subject that applies to far more than mechanics: minimal surfaces (Euler–Lagrange equation gives mean-curvature-zero condition), brachistochrone (gives cycloid), optimal control (Pontryagin maximum principle is the Hamiltonian version of EL for control problems), economics (utility maximization), and shape optimization. The Lagrangian formalism is the universal language for any "find the curve/surface/field minimizing this integral" problem, with the Euler–Lagrange equations as the universal first-order condition.
