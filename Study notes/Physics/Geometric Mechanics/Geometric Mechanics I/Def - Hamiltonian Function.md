# Notation

$(M, \omega)$ is a symplectic manifold. $H : M \to \mathbb{R}$ is a smooth function — the **Hamiltonian**. The associated [[Def - Hamiltonian Vector Field|Hamiltonian vector field]] $X_H$ is defined by $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$. The flow of $X_H$, when it exists, is the **Hamiltonian flow** $\phi^H_t$. In canonical (Darboux) coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$, $H = H(q, p)$ (possibly with explicit time dependence $H = H(q, p, t)$).

---

# Axiom Motivation

A **Hamiltonian function** is, formally, just a smooth function on a symplectic manifold. But the choice of which function to call "the Hamiltonian" is dictated by physics: it should be the **total energy** of the mechanical system, written as a function of position and momentum. The motivating question is: **why does the energy play a privileged role in classical mechanics?**

The historical answer comes from Newton's mechanics. For a single particle of mass $m$ in a potential $V(q)$, the equation of motion is $m\ddot q = -\nabla V(q)$. The energy $E = \tfrac{1}{2}m|\dot q|^2 + V(q)$ — kinetic plus potential — is **conserved**: $dE/dt = m\dot q \cdot \ddot q + \nabla V \cdot \dot q = \dot q \cdot (m\ddot q + \nabla V) = 0$ by Newton's law. So the energy is the simplest conserved quantity of the dynamics, and it determines the dynamics completely (in the autonomous case): the level sets $\{E = c\}$ are the trajectories.

The geometric reformulation is **Hamilton's discovery**: convert the velocity $\dot q$ into the **canonical momentum** $p = m\dot q$ (a covector), express the energy as a function $H(q, p) = |p|^2/(2m) + V(q)$ on the cotangent bundle, and observe that Newton's equations can be rewritten as the symmetric pair

$$\dot q^i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}.$$

These are **Hamilton's equations**. Their geometric content is that the trajectory $(q(t), p(t))$ in phase space is the integral curve of the vector field $X_H$ defined by $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$.

So the role of the Hamiltonian is dual: physically it is the total energy; geometrically it is the generator of dynamics. The two perspectives are unified by the construction $H \mapsto X_H \mapsto \phi^H_t$.

**Why must $H$ be a function on $M$ — why not a more general object?** Because the dynamics is generated by the equation $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$, and the right-hand side is the exterior derivative of a function. A more general $1$-form on the right would not necessarily be exact, and the equation might have no solution (if the $1$-form is not in the image of $X \mapsto \iota_X\omega$) or infinitely many (if it is in the image but with non-trivial kernel). The function $H$ — equivalently the exact $1$-form $dH$ — is what makes the dynamics well-defined and "comes from" an observable.

**Why smooth?** Because we want $X_H$ to be a smooth vector field, with a well-defined smooth flow. Non-smooth Hamiltonians (e.g., piecewise smooth, or distributional) arise in idealized contexts (a particle in a hard-wall potential is described by an $H$ with discontinuities at the wall) but require additional care in the formulation of the dynamics.

**Can $H$ depend explicitly on time?** Yes, and many physically relevant Hamiltonians do — for example, a charged particle in a time-varying electromagnetic field. The formalism handles this by treating $H : M \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ as a function of phase-space variables and time, defining a *time-dependent* Hamiltonian vector field $X_{H_t}$, and integrating it as a time-dependent flow. Energy is no longer conserved when $\partial H/\partial t \neq 0$, but the symplectic-geometric machinery still works.

**What is the natural class of Hamiltonians for a given mechanical system?** Physics dictates: $H$ should be the total energy, $H = T + V$, with $T$ the kinetic energy (a function of momenta, possibly with position-dependent coefficients via a metric) and $V$ the potential energy (a function of positions). In coordinates on $T^*Q$ for a Riemannian configuration space $(Q, g)$:

$$H(q, p) = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{ij}(q)p_ip_j + V(q).$$

This is the **standard mechanical Hamiltonian**, and it arises from the Lagrangian $L = T - V$ via the [[Def - The Legendre Transform|Legendre transform]] (see [[Thm - Equivalence of Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Formalisms]]).

In summary: the choice of $H$ as a smooth function $M \to \mathbb{R}$ — geometrically dictated by the equation $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ requiring a function on the right-hand side, physically dictated by $H$ being the total energy — packages the entire dynamical content of the mechanical system into a single scalar field on phase space.

# The Definition

Let $(M, \omega)$ be a symplectic manifold. A **Hamiltonian function** (or simply **Hamiltonian**) is any smooth function

$$H : M \to \mathbb{R}.$$

When the Hamiltonian depends explicitly on time, it is a smooth function $H : M \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, written $H(p, t)$ or $H_t(p)$, depending on the convention.

**Associated structures:**

1. The **Hamiltonian vector field** $X_H \in \Gamma(TM)$ defined by $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ (see [[Def - Hamiltonian Vector Field]]).
2. The **Hamiltonian flow** $\phi^H_t : M \to M$, the time-$t$ map of the flow of $X_H$ (when defined).
3. **Hamilton's equations** in canonical coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$:

$$\dot q^i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}.$$

**Standard Hamiltonian in mechanics.** For a mechanical system with configuration space $(Q, g)$ (a Riemannian manifold) and potential energy $V : Q \to \mathbb{R}$, the **mechanical Hamiltonian** on $T^*Q$ is

$$H(q, p) = T(q, p) + V(q) = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{ij}(q)p_i p_j + V(q),$$

the total energy expressed in $(q, p)$ coordinates. This $H$ is the Legendre transform of the Lagrangian $L = T - V$ (see [[Def - The Lagrangian Function]] and [[Def - The Legendre Transform]]).

**Properties:**
- $H$ is **constant along its own flow** when autonomous (no explicit time-dependence): $dH/dt = X_H(H) = \omega(X_H, X_H) = 0$ by antisymmetry. This is **energy conservation**.
- Adding a constant to $H$ does not change $X_H$: $X_{H+c} = X_H$ since $d(H+c) = dH$. The energy zero-point is gauge.
- Smooth functions on $M$ form a Poisson algebra under the bracket $\{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g)$ (see [[Def - Poisson Bracket]]).

---

# Categorical / Structural Definition

The smooth functions $C^\infty(M)$ form a commutative associative $\mathbb{R}$-algebra under pointwise operations, and the symplectic structure $\omega$ promotes this to a **Poisson algebra** via the bracket $\{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g)$.

The choice of a Hamiltonian $H$ is the choice of an element of this algebra; the assignment $H \mapsto X_H$ is an $\mathbb{R}$-linear map $C^\infty(M) \to \Gamma(TM)$ whose image is the Lie subalgebra $\mathfrak{ham}(M, \omega) \leq \mathfrak{symp}(M, \omega)$ of Hamiltonian vector fields. The kernel of $H \mapsto X_H$ is the locally constant functions; on a connected $M$, this is $\mathbb{R}$ (the constant functions).

There is no privileged choice of Hamiltonian abstractly — the symplectic manifold $(M, \omega)$ alone does not pick out a Hamiltonian. The choice is made by the physical situation: the energy of the mechanical system. The structural role of $H$ is to **select a 1-parameter group of symplectomorphisms** $\phi^H_t \in \mathrm{Symp}(M, \omega)$, namely the flow of $X_H$. So a Hamiltonian is equivalent to a choice of "direction" inside the infinite-dimensional Lie group of symplectomorphisms.

# Relate to Other Fields / Compression

A Hamiltonian function is the **classical limit of a quantum Hamiltonian operator**. In quantum mechanics, the Hamiltonian is a self-adjoint operator $\hat H$ on a Hilbert space, and the dynamics is generated by $i\hbar \,d\psi/dt = \hat H\psi$ (Schrödinger) or $i\hbar \,d\hat A/dt = [\hat A, \hat H]$ (Heisenberg). In the semiclassical limit $\hbar \to 0$, the operator $\hat H$ corresponds to a classical Hamiltonian $H \in C^\infty(M)$ on phase space $M$, with the **principal symbol** of $\hat H$ being $H$. The Heisenberg equation $\dot{\hat A} = [\hat A, \hat H]/i\hbar$ classicalizes to $\dot f = \{f, H\}$, the Hamilton's equation for the observable $f$ in Poisson-bracket form.

From the Lagrangian perspective, a Hamiltonian is the **Legendre transform of a Lagrangian**: given a regular Lagrangian $L : TQ \to \mathbb{R}$, the corresponding Hamiltonian is $H(q, p) = p_i\dot q^i - L(q, \dot q)$ with $\dot q$ expressed in terms of $(q, p)$ via $p_i = \partial L/\partial \dot q^i$. The Hamiltonian thus arises as a derived object from the more fundamental Lagrangian in constrained-mechanics or field-theory settings; in pure symplectic mechanics, the Hamiltonian is taken as primary.

**True name:** the true name of a Hamiltonian is **"the function whose level sets are the dynamical trajectories' constants of motion, equivalently the function whose Hamiltonian vector field is the dynamics"**. In autonomous systems, the level set $\{H = E\}$ is the (energy-)constant-motion submanifold, and the Hamiltonian flow runs along these level sets.

# Examples / Corollaries

**Is an instance: the harmonic oscillator.** $H(q, p) = \tfrac{1}{2}(p^2 + \omega_0^2 q^2)$ on $T^*\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^2$, where $\omega_0$ is the angular frequency (not to be confused with the symplectic form $\omega$!). Hamilton's equations give $\dot q = p$, $\dot p = -\omega_0^2 q$, with solutions $(q(t), p(t)) = $ ellipses in phase space.

**Is an instance: the free particle.** $H(q, p) = |p|^2/(2m)$ on $T^*\mathbb{R}^n$. Hamilton's equations give $\dot q^i = p_i/m$, $\dot p_i = 0$, so $q(t) = q(0) + (p/m)t$ — straight-line motion at constant velocity.

**Is an instance: particle in a potential.** $H(q, p) = |p|^2/(2m) + V(q)$ on $T^*\mathbb{R}^n$. Hamilton's equations recover Newton's second law $m\ddot q = -\nabla V(q)$.

**Is an instance: geodesic Hamiltonian.** $H(q, p) = \tfrac{1}{2}g^{ij}(q)p_ip_j$ on $T^*M$ for a Riemannian manifold $(M, g)$. The Hamiltonian flow projects to geodesics of $g$, see [[Ex - Geodesic Flow on a Riemannian Manifold is Hamiltonian]].

**Is an instance: charged particle in an electromagnetic field.** $H(q, p, t) = |p - eA(q, t)|^2/(2m) + e\phi(q, t)$ on $T^*\mathbb{R}^3 \times \mathbb{R}$, where $(A, \phi)$ is the electromagnetic four-potential and $e$ is the charge. Hamilton's equations recover the Lorentz force law $m\ddot q = e(E + \dot q \times B)$.

**Is an instance: rigid body Hamiltonian.** For a free rigid body with principal moments of inertia $(I_1, I_2, I_3)$, the rotational kinetic energy is $H = \tfrac{1}{2}(L_1^2/I_1 + L_2^2/I_2 + L_3^2/I_3)$ in body-frame angular momentum coordinates. The phase space is the cotangent bundle of the rotation group $T^*SO(3)$, reduced by rotational symmetry.

**Is an instance: any constant function $H \equiv c$.** This is a valid Hamiltonian, but $dH = 0$ so $X_H = 0$ — the dynamics is trivial. Constants generate no flow.

**Is NOT an instance: a $1$-form $\eta$ on $M$.** A $1$-form is the wrong type for a Hamiltonian; only functions are. If you want to define dynamics from a $1$-form $\eta$, you can — by $\iota_{X_\eta}\omega = \eta$, defining a **symplectic vector field** $X_\eta$ — but the resulting $X_\eta$ is Hamiltonian (i.e., of the form $X_H$ for some function $H$) if and only if $\eta$ is exact, $\eta = dH$.

**Is NOT an instance: a non-smooth $H$.** Hamiltonians must be smooth ($C^\infty$) for the vector field $X_H$ to be smooth and for the flow to be smooth. Non-smooth Hamiltonians (hard-wall potentials, delta-function potentials) are usually treated as idealized limits of smooth ones, or via measure-theoretic generalizations.

**Corollary (energy conservation for autonomous $H$).** When $H$ does not depend explicitly on time, $dH/dt = X_H(H) = \omega(X_H, X_H) = 0$, so $H$ is constant along its own flow. Energy is conserved.

**Corollary (level sets are invariant).** The Hamiltonian flow preserves the level sets $\{H = c\}$ for autonomous $H$. So the dynamics is confined to a $(2n - 1)$-dimensional **energy shell** $\Sigma_E := \{H = E\}$.

**Corollary (gauge ambiguity).** Adding a constant to $H$ does not change $X_H$, so the dynamics is invariant under $H \mapsto H + c$. The energy zero-point is unphysical and may be chosen freely.

**Calibration check.** If you can do these three things, you have understood the definition. First, write down the Hamiltonian for a pendulum of mass $m$ and length $\ell$ in a uniform gravitational field, in cotangent-bundle coordinates on $T^*S^1$. Second, verify that energy is conserved by computing $X_H(H) = \omega(X_H, X_H)$ and noting that it vanishes by antisymmetry. Third, give an example of a time-dependent Hamiltonian for which energy is *not* conserved (e.g., a particle in a time-varying potential), and check that $dH/dt = \partial H/\partial t$ along the flow.

# Unlocked by This

> [!tip] Energy-Momentum and Noether Theorem *(from Hamiltonian Group Actions)*
> The Hamiltonian function plays the role of "time-evolution generator" in classical mechanics. By the symplectic version of Noether's theorem, **every continuous symmetry of the Hamiltonian produces a conserved quantity**: if $G$ acts on $(M, \omega)$ preserving both $\omega$ and $H$, the moment map $\mu : M \to \mathfrak{g}^*$ gives $\dim G$ conserved quantities. Time-translation invariance of an autonomous $H$ gives back $H$ itself as conserved (energy conservation); spatial translations give linear momentum; spatial rotations give angular momentum. The general statement is encoded in the **moment map**, the geometric incarnation of Noether's theorem in symplectic form.

> [!tip] Quantization and the Hamiltonian Operator *(from Quantum Mechanics)*
> In quantum mechanics, the classical Hamiltonian $H \in C^\infty(M)$ is "promoted" to a self-adjoint operator $\hat H$ on a Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$. **Canonical quantization** prescribes the substitution $q^i \to \hat q^i$, $p_i \to -i\hbar\partial_{q^i}$ (in the position representation), with $H \to \hat H = H(\hat q, \hat p)$ — modulo ordering ambiguities. The Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\partial_t\psi = \hat H\psi$ governs the quantum dynamics, with the classical limit $\hbar \to 0$ recovering Hamilton's equations. **Geometric quantization** is the systematic program for constructing $\hat H$ from $H$ in a coordinate-free, functorial way, with the obstructions (Maslov classes, anomalies) capturing the structural difference between classical and quantum.

> [!tip] Hamiltonian Field Theory and the Stress-Energy Tensor *(from Mathematical Physics)*
> In classical field theory (electromagnetism, general relativity, Yang–Mills, etc.), the configuration space $Q$ is an infinite-dimensional space of fields, and the Hamiltonian becomes a **Hamiltonian density** integrated over space. The conserved energy-momentum is encoded in the **stress-energy tensor** $T^{\mu\nu}$, whose components are conserved due to translational symmetry of the Lagrangian: $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ is the spacetime version of the conservation of total energy and momentum. In general relativity, $T^{\mu\nu}$ becomes the source of the Einstein field equations $G^{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G T^{\mu\nu}$ — the energy-momentum content of matter determines the geometry of spacetime.
